# FACT PACK — How the American Parties Came to Swap Coalitions

**Status:** source-of-truth for the gated authoring pipeline. NOT reader-facing. No prose. Accuracy + completeness only. Zero-hallucination bar is absolute on this politically sensitive topic.

**Chapter scope this pack serves:** an ANCHOR chapter that runs *origins → Reconstruction* in full, then *teases* the 20th-century realignment. Sections 1–4 are the body; Section 5 is grounding for a teaser only (the chapter does not narrate the full 20th-century swap).

**Confidence key:** [HIGH] = corroborated, uncontroversial, hard date/number. [MED] = correct in substance but figures vary slightly by source or are approximate. [DISPUTED] = scholars actively disagree, or the claim is contested/apocryphal — see the DO-NOT-ASSERT section.

---

## ORIENTATION

The reader's confusion is real and the answer is genuinely surprising: in the 1860s the **Republicans were the party of the North, of anti-slavery, of strong federal power and economic modernization (the Hamiltonian heir), and of Black voters**; the **Democrats were the party of the white South, slavery, states' rights, and the planter class.** Over roughly a century-plus, the two parties' regional and ideological coalitions came to look roughly inverted. The single most important honesty point for the author: **this inversion was GRADUAL (decades, not an event) and MULTI-CAUSAL.** "The parties switched overnight" / "they just flipped" is a cartoon. Also be careful: the modern words "liberal" and "conservative" do NOT map cleanly onto the 1860s parties — applying them anachronistically is its own error (see DO-NOT-ASSERT).

## 1. THE FIRST PARTY SYSTEM (1790s–1820s)

- The First Party System ran roughly **1792–1824**. [HIGH] (Wikipedia, *First Party System*)
- Two parties: the **Federalist Party** (organized largely by **Alexander Hamilton**, coalescing **c. 1789–1791** around Hamilton's fiscal program) and the **Democratic-Republican Party** (a.k.a. Jeffersonian Republicans; formed **1792–1793** by **Thomas Jefferson and James Madison**, absorbing many former Anti-Federalists). [HIGH]
- **Federalist core positions:** strong centralized federal government; broad/"implied" constitutional powers; a **national bank**; **federal assumption of state debts**; **protective tariffs**; promotion of commerce and manufacturing; closer ties to **Britain**. [HIGH]
- **Democratic-Republican core positions:** **limited federal power**, **strict construction** of the Constitution, **states' rights**, an **agrarian** republic of independent farmers, **low tariffs**, opposition to the national bank as unconstitutional, sympathy for **revolutionary France**. [HIGH]
- Federalists dominant **until 1800**; Democratic-Republicans dominant **after 1800** (Jefferson elected 1800). [HIGH]
- **Why the Federalists collapsed:** the party was crippled by the **Hartford Convention** (held **Dec 15, 1814 – Jan 4, 1815**, Hartford, Connecticut), a gathering of New England Federalists protesting Madison and the War of 1812. Its report was overshadowed by the **Treaty of Ghent** (ratification news reaching the U.S. early 1815) and **Andrew Jackson's victory at the Battle of New Orleans (Jan 8, 1815)**; rumors of secessionist talk branded Federalism as disloyal/regional-separatist amid apparent victory, gutting its national credibility. [HIGH] (American Battlefield Trust; American History Central)
- **Era of Good Feelings:** roughly **1815–1825**; one-party (Democratic-Republican) dominance; **James Monroe** elected **1816** and **1820** (1820 essentially unopposed). The label captures national unity after the War of 1812; the Federalists effectively ceased to be a national party. [HIGH]
- KEY FRAMING for the author: the deep, durable thread is **Hamiltonian (strong-federal, pro-commerce, pro-bank, pro-tariff, modernization) vs. Jeffersonian (states'-rights, agrarian, strict-construction)**. This is the spine the reader should track — the *party names* attached to each pole change over the next century, the *poles* are more stable. [HIGH — interpretive framing, standard in the literature]

## 2. THE SECOND PARTY SYSTEM (1830s–1850s)

- The Second Party System ran roughly **1828–1854**. [HIGH] (Wikipedia, *Second Party System*)
- Two parties: the **Democratic Party** (the modern Democratic Party's lineage; built around **Andrew Jackson**, growing out of the Democratic-Republicans) vs. the **Whig Party** (existed **1833–1854**; assembled by **Henry Clay** from National Republicans, Anti-Masons, and anti-Jackson Democrats). [HIGH]
- **Jacksonian Democrat core positions:** the "common man" vs. entrenched elites; expansion of the vote to non-landowning white men; hostility to concentrated economic power (notably the **Second Bank of the United States** — Jackson vetoed its recharter 1832 and killed it); a strong populist executive; generally **low tariffs**; aggressive **westward expansion**. [HIGH]
- **Whig core positions:** opposition to Jackson's "executive tyranny"; support for the **national bank**; **protective tariffs**; **federally funded internal improvements**; "rule of law" and protection of minority interests against majority tyranny. [HIGH]
- **Henry Clay's "American System"** (the Whig economic program): (1) a **national bank** for a uniform currency; (2) a **protective tariff** to foster domestic manufacturing; (3) federal subsidy of **internal improvements** (roads, canals). [HIGH]
- Note the inheritance: the **Whig/Clay "American System" is the Hamiltonian pole** (strong-federal, pro-commerce, pro-tariff, modernization); the **Jacksonian Democrats are the Jeffersonian pole** (states'-rights-leaning, agrarian-populist, anti-bank). This is the bridge the author uses to carry the Section-1 spine forward. [HIGH — standard framing]
- **Why slavery broke the Whigs:** the Whigs were a **cross-sectional** party (Northern and Southern wings) held together by economics, not by a slavery position. As slavery moved to the center of national politics in the early 1850s, the party could not hold its Northern (increasingly anti-slavery) and Southern (pro-slavery) wings together. The **Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854** was the breaking blow (see Section 3). Whig leaders scattered into the nativist **Know-Nothings (American Party)** and the new **Republicans**. **Henry Clay died 1852; Daniel Webster died 1852** — the loss of the founding leadership compounded the collapse. [HIGH] (History.com, *Why the Whig Party Collapsed*; Wikipedia)

## 3. THE 1850s CRUCIBLE (the heart of the chapter)

### The Kansas-Nebraska Act
- **Kansas-Nebraska Act** signed **May 30, 1854** (sponsored by Democratic Sen. **Stephen A. Douglas** of Illinois). [HIGH]
- It organized the Kansas and Nebraska territories under **"popular sovereignty"** — letting settlers vote slavery up or down — which **effectively repealed the Missouri Compromise of 1820** (the 36°30′ line barring slavery in northern Louisiana Purchase territory). [HIGH]
- Consequence: it reopened the slavery-in-the-territories question, triggered **"Bleeding Kansas"** (guerrilla violence over whether Kansas would be slave or free), **destroyed the Whig Party, split the Democrats, and catalyzed the founding of the Republican Party.** [HIGH] (Wikipedia; U.S. Senate; Lumen Learning)

### Founding of the Republican Party (when / where / who)
- **Earliest local meeting** to propose the name "Republican" for a new anti-slavery party: a schoolhouse in **Ripon, Wisconsin, March 20, 1854.** [HIGH] (multiple; Ripon's own claim)
- **First statewide convention** to adopt a platform and nominate candidates under the Republican name: **"Under the Oaks," near Jackson, Michigan, July 6, 1854** (a mass open-air meeting; ~10,000 attendees per local accounts). [HIGH / the attendance figure is [MED] — local/promotional]
- BOTH Ripon and Jackson claim "birthplace" status; the honest formulation is **Ripon = earliest local meeting/the name; Jackson = first state convention + first statewide slate.** The author should name both and not pick a single "birthplace." [HIGH] (Michigan Enjoyer; Jackson County GOP; Ripon League of Women Voters)
- **The founding coalition:** **ex-Whigs (the Northern, anti-slavery wing) + Free-Soilers (the Free Soil Party) + anti-slavery ("anti-Nebraska") Democrats**, united on one plank: **stopping the EXPANSION of slavery into the territories** (NOT, at founding, abolition where it already existed). [HIGH]
- IMPORTANT precision: the founding Republican position was **anti-slavery-EXPANSION / free-soil**, not nationwide abolitionism. Lincoln's 1860 platform pledged not to interfere with slavery in existing slave states; it opposed slavery's spread. Don't overstate the 1860 GOP as an abolition party. [HIGH]

### The Democrats split (1860)
- **1860 Democratic National Convention, Charleston, SC, April 23 – May 3, 1860:** deadlocked. Douglas led on the first ballot but couldn't reach the **two-thirds** required. After a pro-slavery platform plank was rejected (minority/Northern report adopted **April 30, 165–138**), roughly **50 Southern delegates walked out.** [HIGH]
- The party reconvened in **Baltimore (June 1860)** and split into two tickets: **Northern Democrats nominated Stephen A. Douglas** (popular sovereignty / moderate on slavery); **Southern Democrats nominated Vice President John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky** (pro-slavery, states'-rights platform). [HIGH]
- A fourth party, the **Constitutional Union Party**, nominated **John Bell** (TN) on a "preserve the Union, dodge slavery" platform. [HIGH]

### What each party WAS at the Civil War
- **1860 presidential election results:** **Lincoln (Republican) 180 electoral votes**, **Breckinridge (Southern Dem) 72**, **Bell (Constitutional Union) 39**, **Douglas (Northern Dem) 12.** [HIGH] (Wikipedia; National Archives Electoral College)
- **Popular vote:** Lincoln **~39.8%** (one of the **lowest popular-vote shares ever for a winner** — but NOT the lowest; **John Quincy Adams won the 1824 election with ~30.9%**, lower. Safe phrasing: "one of the lowest," never "the lowest ever."); Douglas **~29.5%**; Breckinridge **~18%**; Bell **~12.6%**. Douglas + Breckinridge combined **~47.6%.** [HIGH — round-number precision varies slightly by source; treat the percentages as ~]
- **Geography:** Lincoln swept the **North** + Pacific (CA, OR), winning **zero** slave states (he wasn't even on many Southern ballots); Breckinridge took most of the **South** + DE, MD; Bell took KY, TN, VA; Douglas won only **Missouri** (+ split NJ) — the only candidate to win electoral votes in **both** free and slave states. [HIGH]
- **Republicans (1860s) = ** the **North**, **anti-slavery-expansion**, **Lincoln**, and the **Hamiltonian heir**: strong federal government, economic modernization (during the war the GOP Congress passed the Homestead Act, Pacific Railway Act, National Banking Acts, protective tariffs). [HIGH]
- **Democrats (1860s) = ** the **South** (the secessionists were Democrats) **PLUS Northern Democrats**, who split into **War Democrats** (backed the war) and **"Copperheads" / Peace Democrats** (wanted a negotiated peace). [HIGH]
- **Copperheads:** Northern anti-war Democrats, strongest in the **Midwest (Ohio, Indiana, Illinois)**; explicitly invoked **Jefferson and Jackson**; opposed Lincoln's centralization/emancipation as "strangling the Constitution." [HIGH]
- **1864 election:** Democrats nominated Gen. **George B. McClellan** (pro-war) for president with a **Copperhead-written platform calling the war a failure and demanding peace negotiations**; McClellan repudiated the peace plank. **Sherman's capture of Atlanta (Sept 1864)** undercut the peace argument; **Lincoln won re-election decisively.** [HIGH]

## 4. RECONSTRUCTION — THE MIRROR IMAGE (the inversion-from-today, made explicit)

This is where the author makes the "opposite of today" point precise.

- **The Reconstruction Amendments were written and pushed by REPUBLICANS, over DEMOCRATIC opposition:** [HIGH]
  - **13th Amendment** — abolished slavery. Passed Congress **Jan 1865**; **ratified December 6, 1865.** (Lincoln did not live to see ratification.) [HIGH]
  - **14th Amendment** — birthright citizenship, due process, equal protection. **Ratified July 9, 1868.** [HIGH]
  - **15th Amendment** — voting rights regardless of "race, color, or previous condition of servitude." **Ratified February 3, 1870.** [HIGH]
- After the war, **freedmen voted overwhelmingly Republican** — the Republicans became the **"party of Lincoln"** and the party of **Black civil rights and Black voters.** The first Black members of Congress (e.g., Hiram Revels, Joseph Rainey, both 1870) were **Republicans.** [HIGH]
- **First Black votes:** African American men voted in significant numbers for the first time in **1867** (under the Reconstruction Acts) — the basis of the famous "First Vote" image (Section: images). [HIGH]
- **Democrats became the party of the white South:** [HIGH]
  - **"Redemption"** — between roughly **1868 and 1877**, white Southern Democrats ("**Redeemers**") retook Southern state governments, in significant part through **paramilitary violence and voter suppression** (the **Ku Klux Klan**, founded 1865/66; the **White League** and **Red Shirts** from ~1874, described by historians as "the military arm of the Democratic Party"). [HIGH]
  - **Reconstruction ended with the Compromise of 1877** (resolving the disputed 1876 Hayes–Tilden election; federal troops withdrawn from the South). [HIGH]
  - Redeemer Democratic governments then built **Jim Crow** — legalized segregation and the systematic **disenfranchisement of Black voters** (poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses), entrenched **1890s–1900s.** [HIGH]
- **The "Solid South":** for roughly a **century** (post-Reconstruction into the mid-20th c.), the white South voted **reliably Democratic** at every level. [HIGH]
- **THE EXPLICIT INVERSION (the reader's "aha"):** in this era the **Republicans** are the party of Black Americans, civil-rights amendments, and federal enforcement; the **Democrats** are the party of the white South, "states' rights" as a shield for white supremacy, and Jim Crow. **This is the mirror image of the parties' racial coalitions today** — and it is true, precise, and the starting point the 20th-century realignment had to move *away* from. [HIGH]
- HONESTY GUARDRAIL: this fact is sometimes weaponized ("the Democrats were the party of slavery/Jim Crow, therefore..."). The author's job is to state it accurately AND to then explain the realignment (Section 5) that moved the coalitions — *not* to leave the reader at 1900 implying today's parties hold those same positions. The mirror is the setup; the swap is the payoff. [framing guidance]

## 5. THE 20th-CENTURY SWAP — GROUNDING FOR THE TEASER ONLY

The anchor chapter only *teases* this. The pack grounds the teaser and flags the landmines. Two engines:

### Engine A — The New Deal economic realignment (1930s)
- **Franklin D. Roosevelt** and the **New Deal** built a new Democratic majority — the **"New Deal coalition"** — crystallizing in the **1936 election**: Southern white Protestants **+** Northern urban ethnics, Catholics, Jews, organized labor, **and Black voters in the North.** [HIGH]
- **Black voters in the North began shifting Democratic on economics:** in **1936, roughly 71–75% of Black voters backed FDR** — a dramatic break from their historic Republican ("party of Lincoln") allegiance, driven by New Deal relief programs and Eleanor Roosevelt's visible anti-discrimination stance. [HIGH — the exact % varies 71–76 by source]
- **The unstable marriage:** the New Deal coalition now held BOTH the segregationist white Solid South AND a growing bloc of Northern Black voters — inside the same party. This is the internal contradiction that the civil-rights era would eventually rupture. [HIGH — interpretive but standard]
- Marker of the South still being "in": **FDR carried every former Confederate state all four times (1932–44); no Democrat has carried the entire former Confederacy since 1944.** [HIGH]

### Engine B — The civil-rights racial realignment (1948 → ~1990s)
- **1948:** at the Democratic convention, a strong civil-rights plank (championed by **Hubert Humphrey**) was adopted; **~35 Southern delegates walked out.** They met in **Birmingham, Alabama, July 17, 1948**, formed the **States' Rights Democratic Party ("Dixiecrats")**, and nominated **Gov. Strom Thurmond (SC)** for president and **Gov. Fielding Wright (MS)** for VP, on a pro-segregation platform. The Thurmond–Wright ticket carried **Alabama, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina — 39 electoral votes, ~1.17 million popular votes.** **Truman won anyway**, helped by Black votes in the North. [HIGH]
- **1964:** **President Lyndon B. Johnson** signed the **Civil Rights Act of 1964 on July 2, 1964.** Republican nominee **Sen. Barry Goldwater (AZ)** had **voted against** the Act (on stated constitutional/federalism grounds). In the **1964 election**, LBJ won a national landslide **but Goldwater carried his home state (AZ) plus the five Deep South states** (Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia, South Carolina) — **states that had not voted Republican for president since Reconstruction.** [HIGH]
- **1965: Voting Rights Act** (signed Aug 6, 1965). Registered Black voters in the former Confederacy **rose by nearly 1 million between 1964 and 1968.** [HIGH]
- **Nixon's "Southern Strategy" (1968, 1972):** in **1968**, Nixon allied with the now-Republican **Strom Thurmond** and won most of the South while segregationist independent **George Wallace** took the Deep South; in **1972**, with no Wallace, Nixon **swept the former Confederacy**, winning ~**80% of the Southern white vote.** [HIGH]
- **The arc:** the white South's migration from solidly Democratic to solidly Republican unfolded over roughly **30+ years**, "essentially complete by the 1990s." Conversely, **Black voters became overwhelmingly Democratic** (now routinely ~85–90%+). [HIGH — the South-goes-GOP "by the 1990s" framing is standard; treat the Black-Democratic % as a modern approximation, not a 19xx datum]
- CRITICAL: this was **gradual and multi-level** — presidential voting flipped first (1964–72); state and local offices and party identification took **decades longer** (well into the 1990s–2000s in many places). The author must not compress it into one election or one decision. [HIGH]

## DISPUTED / DO-NOT-ASSERT

1. **The apocryphal LBJ quote — "We have lost the South for a generation."**
   - DO NOT present this as a verified Johnson quote. [DISPUTED]
   - Sourcing reality: the line is **not documented contemporaneously.** It is sometimes attributed via aide **Bill Moyers**, but the earliest traceable reference is a **1986/87 Moyers recollection (~20+ years later)**, and Moyers' own wording was softer/different (something like *"I think we may have lost the South for your lifetime — and mine"*), which he later mused may have been *"merely a jest."* As a hard direct quote with the "for a generation" wording, it **first appears in print around 2002–2004**, ~40 years after the fact, with **no first-hand witness citation.** Fact-checkers rate the exact quote effectively fabricated. [DISPUTED — well-documented as apocryphal]
   - What the author MAY safely say: that the realignment of the South is *often summed up* by an apocryphal line attributed to LBJ, **flagging it as disputed/likely never said in that form**; OR skip the quote entirely. NEVER quote it as established fact.

2. **The race-vs-economics scholarly debate (the South's shift to the GOP).** [DISPUTED — genuine, ongoing scholarly disagreement; present BOTH sides]
   - **Race-centered account (the more mainstream / dominant view):** the white South left the Democrats primarily as a **backlash against the national Democratic Party's embrace of civil rights.** Empirical anchor: **Kuziemko & Washington (2018, *American Economic Review*, "Why Did the Democrats Lose the South?")** find that **racially conservative whites account for essentially all** of the 1958–1980 decline in white Southern Democratic identification, and find **"essentially no role for income growth"** in that defection. Also in this camp: Earl & Merle Black (*The Rise of Southern Republicans*), and the broad civil-rights-realignment literature.
   - **Economics/Sunbelt-growth account (the notable revisionist challenge):** **Byron Shafer & Richard Johnston, *The End of Southern Exceptionalism* (Harvard, 2006; V.O. Key Award)** argue the shift was driven primarily by **postwar economic development, suburbanization, and the rise of a Southern middle/upper class** that became Republican on **class/economic** grounds — with race a smaller driver than commonly assumed; early GOP gains came among **economic elites**, not the most racially conservative.
   - What the author MAY safely say: present this as a **real, non-partisan scholarly debate over the RELATIVE WEIGHT of race vs. economics/growth** — both factors were present; the disagreement is about proportion and mechanism. Do NOT declare the debate "settled" in either direction. (Fair summary: the **weight of recent quantitative work leans toward race as the primary driver of voter-level defection**, while the economics/growth account remains a serious, award-winning challenge especially for *elite* and *down-ballot* timing.) Avoid implying either "it was all racism" or "race had nothing to do with it" — both are misreadings.

3. **"The parties switched overnight / they just flipped."** [DO-NOT-ASSERT as stated]
   - This is a cartoon. The realignment was **gradual (decades), multi-causal, and uneven across levels** (presidential first, then state/local, then party ID). Say "gradual and multi-causal," never "flipped" or "switched overnight."

4. **Modern-label traps — "liberal" vs. "conservative."** [DO-NOT-ASSERT mapping]
   - Do NOT call 1860s Republicans "liberals" or 1860s Democrats "conservatives" in the modern sense. The modern liberal/conservative axis (welfare state, social policy, etc.) did not exist in that form. The **stable** threads to track are **regional coalition** (North/South), the **race/civil-rights** position, and the **Hamiltonian-strong-federal vs. Jeffersonian-states'-rights** axis — NOT modern ideological labels. (Note even the federal-power axis isn't clean: 1860s Republicans championed strong federal power, but so did 20th-c. liberal Democrats — the federal-power valence itself shifted.)

5. **"The party of Lincoln" / "the party of slavery" rhetorical traps.** [HANDLE WITH CARE — facts are true, the spin is the danger]
   - The factual claims (1860s Republicans = anti-slavery/civil-rights; 1860s–1900s Democrats = slavery/Jim Crow) are TRUE and must be stated. The trap is using them to imply **today's** parties hold those positions. The author MUST pair the historical fact with the realignment so the reader is not left with a false present-day inference. State the mirror; then deliver the swap.

6. **Attendance / popular-vote precision.** The "10,000 Under the Oaks" figure and the exact 1860 popular-vote percentages vary slightly by source — present as approximate ("about 40%," "roughly 10,000"), don't assert false precision. [MED]
