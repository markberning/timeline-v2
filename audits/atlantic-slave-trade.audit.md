## Story Editor evaluation (Persona D)

## Chapter 1 — Origins: The Portuguese and the Sugar Model

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** The Atlantic slave trade was not born of a grand design but assembled from three concrete ingredients — a coast nobody could sail past, a crop that made men rich, and the discovery on a tiny equatorial island that the two could be welded together.

**What works:**
- The opening refuses abstraction with discipline: "not with cruelty in the abstract but with three very specific things: a stretch of coast nobody had been able to sail past, a crop that made men rich, and the discovery that the two could be joined." That tricolon is a real thesis, and the chapter then delivers each of the three in order — coast, sugar, São Tomé — so the structure is legible and propulsive.
- São Tomé is handled exactly right as the chapter's hinge: "the place that first proved you could grow a fortune in sugar on the backs of enslaved people bought by the shipload... It is one of the most consequential places almost nobody has heard of." It earns its weight without melodrama.
- The Afonso I of Kongo passage gives the chapter a human face and a tragic mechanism — a king "watching the trade slip beyond anyone's control and trying, and failing, to rein it in" — and the Las Casas section is morally honest in a way that resists the easy hero/villain frame ("the question was rarely whether slavery was wrong, but *which* people could justly be enslaved").

**What doesn't work:**
- The chapter carries a heavy load of necessary setup (Tordesillas, encomienda, asiento, Valladolid) and there is one stretch — the asiento paragraph into the Kongo paragraph — where the prose briefly tilts toward mechanism-cataloguing. It recovers immediately with Afonso, so this is a minor pacing dip, not a structural fault. No fix required beyond awareness.

**Momentum map:** Strong open (the three-ingredients thesis), steady build through the coast and sugar, peaks at São Tomé, a slight administrative cooling through Tordesillas/asiento, then re-warms hard with Afonso and Las Casas, strong forward-pointing close.

**Build dependency:** yes — as Chapter 1 it sets up the entire arc explicitly and well, ending by naming every piece "in place" and handing off to the scaling chapter.

## Chapter 2 — The System Scales: Brazil and the Caribbean

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** A profitable island experiment became the central economic fact of the Atlantic world, as the Dutch, English, and French copied and industrialized the São Tomé model — and in doing so invented hereditary racial slavery.

**What works:**
- The chapter has a genuine argument that corrects a reader's likely assumption: "When people picture the Atlantic slave trade, they often picture the American South. The center of gravity was actually Brazil, by an enormous margin." That reframing gives the chapter a point, not just a sequence.
- The "DY" / Duke of York passage is the chapter's emotional peak and is handled with restraint and force: "the future king of England ran a company that burned its initials into human beings... It captures how thoroughly the trade was woven into the very top of the English state." The instruction to "Sit with that for a moment" risks being heavy-handed but earns it because the fact is documented and the surrounding analysis is sober.
- The "died faster than they were born" paragraph names the grimmest economic mechanism cleanly — "like fuel poured into a fire" — and crucially flags it as the seed of a later contrast (Chapter 5), so it reads as load-bearing rather than decorative.

**What doesn't work:**
- The closing of the racial-slavery paragraph and the chapter's final paragraph both do summary-and-handoff work, and there's mild redundancy between "By 1700 the machine was fully assembled" and the racial-slavery summation just above it. A reader feels two endings stacked. Minor — tightening the penultimate paragraph's last lines would sharpen the single landing.

**Momentum map:** Strong open (laboratory-to-industry), sustained energy through Brazil and Barbados, peak at the Duke of York brand, holds through Code Noir and the slaving cities, slight double-ending at the close.

**Build dependency:** yes — explicitly picks up São Tomé from Ch 1 and advances it into industrialization; the racial-slavery section plants a thread that pays off in Chs 5 and 11.

## Chapter 3 — The African Coast and the Kingdoms

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** Africans were central to supplying the trade — a documented fact that must be told without either erasing it or weaponizing it — and this chapter shows how, through the forts, the holding pens, and the kingdoms that organized themselves around capture and sale.

**What works:**
- The chapter declares its hardest editorial problem up front and makes the framing itself the spine: "both the erasure and the weaponizing are lies... The honest account holds both." This is the most editorially delicate chapter in the book and it earns trust by naming the trap.
- Njinga is the standout set-piece — the chair-and-mat anecdote is vivid and well-flagged as "famous, well-attested," and the prose resists hagiography honestly: "Njinga is not a simple anti-slavery heroine; she lived inside the trade's world and dealt in captives like every power around her."
- The Dahomey treatment models the chapter's whole method — it concedes the kingdom "genuinely was a slaving state" while supplying the mechanism that complicates the "Africans did it too" reflex: "locked into a cycle in which guns bought captives and captives bought guns, in which a state that stopped raiding could be raided itself."

**What doesn't work:**
- The procession of West African powers (Dahomey → Asante → Oyo → Bight of Biafra/Igbo) is the one stretch that edges toward a survey list. Each entry is interesting, but the reader feels the gear shift from narrative to gazetteer. The fix is structural-light: a single connective sentence tying the three states to a shared dynamic before launching the roll, so they read as variations on a theme rather than a catalogue. The chapter partly does this at the end of the paragraph ("What the Atlantic demand did was supercharge...") — pulling that logic to the front would carry the reader through.
- The trans-Saharan comparison and the Atlantic Creoles section are both valuable but arrive as appended set-pieces after the kingdoms; the transitions ("It is worth pausing to set..."; "Between the African and European worlds stood...") are functional rather than driven. Minor.

**Momentum map:** Strong, careful open; peaks at Njinga; sags slightly in the multi-kingdom roll (Asante/Oyo/Igbo); recovers with the "how did a free person become a captive" mechanism paragraph; tapers gently through the two appended comparisons; strong close to the Middle Passage.

**Build dependency:** yes — answers the supply question Ch 2 left open ("None of it could have happened without a supply"), reconnects Afonso from Ch 1, and hands cleanly to the crossing.

## Chapter 4 — The Middle Passage as a World of Its Own

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** The crossing was a deliberately engineered prison — a machine for turning human beings into cargo that consumed even the crews that ran it — and the captives resisted it from the first chains, never reconciled.

**What works:**
- The chapter is organized as a designed object: ship → conditions → resistance → crew → human geography → the Zong → sale → seasoning. Each section follows from the last, and the controlling idea ("the engineering of misery") gives a horror its analytical handle without anaesthetizing it: "all of it calmly worked out to move the largest possible number of people across an ocean at the lowest possible cost, with their deaths priced in as an expected loss."
- The insistence on agency mid-chapter is a deliberate and effective structural move: "against the image of passive suffering... resistance began the instant the chains went on, on the water, before the captives had ever seen a plantation. They were never reconciled to it." It keeps the chapter from being a catalogue of what was done *to* people.
- The "shipmates" passage is the emotional core and is beautifully placed — finding the first human bond "made on the water, in the worst place imaginable" — and the Zong is told with exactly the right cold precision: "a question of who owed whom money."
- The seasoning sequence at the close is a genuine gut-punch of accumulated odds ("survive the one-in-seven... and then still face a one-in-three chance") that explains, not just appalls — it answers *why* the system had to keep importing.

**What doesn't work:**
- This chapter risks reader fatigue by its nature, and the death-and-disease paragraph (dysentery, smallpox, the speculum oris) sits close to the conditions paragraph above it, so there's a stretch where horror accumulates without a shift in register. The chapter manages this well overall (the resistance and crew sections break the rhythm), but it is the one place the reader could go numb. No restructuring needed; awareness only.

**Momentum map:** Relentless and well-modulated — engineered open, conditions, a deliberate pivot to resistance that re-energizes, the crew and "shipmates" beats vary the emotional register, the Zong as a peak, then the seasoning sequence as a devastating, purposeful close.

**Build dependency:** yes — receives the captives Ch 3 marched to the coast and delivers the "surviving labor force" to the plantation complex of Ch 5; the Zong and Amistad threads are explicitly planted for Chs 7 and 9.

## Chapter 5 — The Plantation Complex and the Economics

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** The trade lasted four centuries because it was not only a moral horror but ordinary, central business — and following the money (crops, the triangle, finance, law, the cotton gin, the coffle) explains why it was so hard to kill and how it came home to America.

**What works:**
- The framing thesis is the chapter's strength and is stated without flinching from either truth: "It was a moral horror. But it was also, and inseparably, ordinary business... This chapter follows the money, because the money is why the trade existed."
- The cotton-gin section delivers the chapter's central irony with real narrative force: "just as the moral campaign against slavery was winning its first victories and the Atlantic import trade was closing, the demand for enslaved labor in the United States *exploded*." The mechanism (why short-staple cotton, why the bottleneck mattered) is explained, not asserted.
- The domestic-slave-trade close is the chapter's most powerful turn — "The trade in human beings did not need the ocean anymore. It had come home." — and it lands the human cost ("husbands were sold from wives and children from parents") after the economics, so the money never feels bloodless.
- The book's characteristic honesty is on display in the Industrial Revolution paragraph: it refuses to overclaim ("the strongest claims have been challenged... significant but not the sole cause") while still landing the point that mattered politically.

**What doesn't work:**
- This is the most concept-dense chapter (triangle, finance, casta, slave codes, cotton gin, King Cotton, coffle), and the casta paragraph is the one that reads most like exposition inserted for completeness — it's interesting but slightly stalls the money-following momentum the chapter otherwise sustains. The fix is to tie casta more tightly to the chapter's spine (it is, after all, the *social* machinery that the *economic* machine required), which a single connective clause would do.
- The crops paragraph (sugar/tobacco/coffee/rice/cotton) is a controlled list and works, but it is a list; the rice detail ("planters specifically prized captives from the rice-growing regions... for the agricultural knowledge") is the standout that rescues it from being a survey, and the chapter is right to foreground it.

**Momentum map:** Strong thesis open, the crops list as a brief flat stretch, builds through the triangle and finance, peaks at the cotton gin's cruel irony, sustains through King Cotton, and closes hard on the domestic trade coming home.

**Build dependency:** yes — explicitly pays off the "grew vs. shrank" thread from Ch 2, receives the survivors from Ch 4, and plants the cotton/Civil War thread for Ch 9.

## Chapter 6 — Resistance, Revolt, and Marronage

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** Enslaved people were never passive — they resisted at every scale from the broken hoe to the free forest republic, and though most armed revolts were crushed, they were not futile: they proved the enslaved would not consent and fed the movement that ended the system.

**What works:**
- The chapter opens by naming and refuting the false version of the history it is correcting: "a version of this history in which enslaved people appear only as victims... That version is false, and the historical record refutes it on nearly every page." This gives the chapter a clear thesis and a clear adversary.
- The structural choice to ground loud resistance on "a foundation of quieter resistance that has to be named first" is sound editorial judgment — it keeps the chapter from being a highlight reel of revolts and frames the spectacular against the daily.
- Palmares and Zumbi are the chapter's strongest sustained narrative ("a functioning free state carved out of the heart of a slave society"), and the Zumbi-head detail explicitly rhymes forward to Boukman in Ch 8, a deliberate and effective cross-chapter echo. Queen Nanny and the Jamaican treaties are handled with honesty about the "dark price" of the treaties rather than flattening them into triumph.

**What doesn't work:**
- The U.S. revolts paragraph (Gabriel → German Coast/Deslondes → Vesey → Nat Turner) is the chapter's one genuine list-stretch — four conspiracies in a single paragraph, each compressed to a sentence or two. It is the by-design risk of a chapter that must cover a long roll of events. Nat Turner gets the space he deserves, but Gabriel, Deslondes, and Vesey blur. The fix is not to cut but to give the paragraph a connective spine (e.g., a sentence on what the sequence *cumulatively* did to the Southern mind) so the names read as a rising drumbeat rather than a list. The chapter reaches for this in its closing synthesis; pulling some of that forward would help.

**Momentum map:** Strong thesis open, the everyday-resistance foundation, peaks at Palmares/Zumbi, holds through Nanny, dips slightly in the compressed U.S.-revolts roll, re-peaks at the Baptist War / Samuel Sharpe (well-tied to the abolition climax), strong synthesizing close.

**Build dependency:** yes — gathers the shipboard mutinies of Ch 4 into a larger account, points explicitly to Haiti (Ch 8) as the one time resistance won, and to abolition (Ch 7).

## Chapter 7 — The Abolition Movement

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** In a single generation, campaigners turned an accepted, profitable institution into a moral abomination and invented modern political activism to attack it — and the movement was led, crucially, not only by white reformers but by the formerly enslaved in their own voices.

**What works:**
- The opening reframes abolition as historically *strange* rather than inevitable: "For more than two centuries, the Atlantic slave trade was not a scandal. It was normal." That is the chapter's engine, and it makes the campaigners' achievement legible as an achievement.
- The "first modern political campaign" thread (petitions, the sugar boycott, the Brooks diagram, Clarkson's physical objects) gives the chapter a genuine analytical spine, not just a roll of reformers. The Brooks diagram passage — "it let people *see* the Middle Passage" — and Clarkson "turning abstract horror into iron and steel that people could hold in their hands" are both vivid and load-bearing.
- The chapter's most important structural move is the deliberate interruption to insist on Black authorship: "abolition was not a gift handed down by enlightened white men to grateful Africans." Equiano is then given real space, and the honesty about the birthplace debate ("does not touch the book's power") is exactly the book's register. This pays off the book's central thesis about agency.

**What doesn't work:**
- The chapter front-loads white reformers (Quakers, Benezet, Somerset, Sharp, the Society, the Brooks, Clarkson, then Wilberforce) before pivoting to the Sons of Africa and Equiano roughly two-thirds in. The pivot is explicitly flagged and powerful, but structurally the Black abolitionists arrive late in a chapter whose thesis is that they were "among its authors." A reader feels the architecture slightly contradict the argument — the people the chapter insists were central are introduced after the white campaigners. This is a real (if moderate) structural tension, not a sentence-level fix; one option is seeding Equiano/the Sons of Africa earlier as a parallel track rather than a late correction. Worth flagging, but it stops short of WEAK because the pivot is so deliberately and forcefully made that it reads as a rhetorical strategy rather than an oversight.

**Momentum map:** Strong "it was normal" open, builds through the Quakers and Somerset, peaks at the Brooks/Clarkson activism-toolkit section, re-peaks (and partly re-centers) at the deliberate Black-authorship pivot to Equiano, holds through Wilberforce and the West India Interest, lands solidly on 1807.

**Build dependency:** yes — pays off the Zong (Ch 4), the Baptist War push (Ch 6), and explicitly defers the slavery-vs-trade distinction to Ch 9; sets up the contrast with Haiti's by-force route that Ch 8 opens on.

## Chapter 8 — Haiti: The Revolution That Couldn't Happen

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** On one island the resistance did not fail but won totally — a half-million enslaved people defeated the armies of three empires and founded the only nation on earth born of a victorious slave revolt, shattering forever the assumption the whole Atlantic system rested on.

**What works:**
- This is the book's most fully realized narrative chapter, and it knows it: the frame ("the chapter that the people who ran the Atlantic system believed was impossible") gives it the highest stakes in the book and the prose sustains them. The "Begin with the prize" structure — establishing Saint-Domingue as the richest colony on earth and explaining *why* its demographics made it "the most explosive concentration of enslaved humanity in the Atlantic world" — is masterful scene-setting that makes the revolution feel inevitable in hindsight without being predetermined.
- Toussaint Louverture is drawn as a real figure with real political genius, and the tangled-alliances passage ("a chaos of shifting alliances") respects the reader's intelligence rather than flattening the war into good vs. evil. The treachery of his capture under a flag of truce and his death in a cold mountain prison is the chapter's tragic peak.
- The chapter's honesty about the aftermath is its mark of quality, not a flaw: it refuses to romanticize ("It is important not to romanticize the aftermath"), names the Dessalines massacre, and lands the indemnity as the cruelest irony — "a financial chain fastened on it precisely because it had broken the physical ones." Yellow fever as "an ally of the revolution more lethal than any army" is a vivid, accurate stroke.

**What doesn't work:**
- Almost nothing structural. The single note: the chapter carries a lot of named generals and turns (Boukman, Toussaint, Leclerc, Dessalines, Vertières) and a reader less familiar with the period could lose the thread of *who controls what when* in the middle stretch — but the prose anchors each transition clearly enough ("The man who took up Toussaint's command...") that this stays a minor, not a structural, concern.

**Momentum map:** No sag. Powerful frame open, "the prize" scene-setting, the Bois Caïman spark, rising through Toussaint, tragic peak at his capture and death, the yellow-fever turn, the conclusive Vertières victory, then a deliberate, sobering descent through the punished aftermath, closing on the unkillable proof. The strongest momentum in the book.

**Build dependency:** yes — explicitly the payoff of Ch 6 ("the one place where the resistance of Chapter 6 did not fail but won totally"), draws Vodou forward from a thread that Ch 10 will develop, and rhymes Boukman's severed head back to Zumbi's.

## Chapter 9 — The Slow Legal End

**Grade: GOOD**

**Throughline:** Everywhere outside Haiti, slavery ended slowly, unevenly, and by law — won when moral campaigners, the enslaved's own relentless resistance, shifting economics, and great-power interest all pushed the same way — and "illegal" never meant "undone."

**What works:**
- The chapter has a clear and honest controlling argument that corrects the schoolbook myth: "of enlightened lawmakers nobly granting freedom, is only a fraction of the truth." It then organizes the sprawl (trade bans → Amistad → British abolition → US → Brazil) around the recurring insight that ending the *trade* and ending *slavery* were different things "measured in decades and millions of lives."
- The compensation point is the chapter's sharpest recurring blade and is deployed with real force: the 1833 money "went to the *slaveholders*... the formerly enslaved received nothing for their stolen years," and apprenticeship as "a halfway bondage." This keeps the legal victories from reading as clean triumphs.
- Douglass and Tubman are given the chapter's strongest narrative beats — Tubman going "back, again and again, into slave territory" — and the chapter ties them to its thesis that even the "official" end was "driven from below."

**What doesn't work:**
- This is the chapter most exposed to the by-design risk of the whole book: it must cover the most jurisdictions and dates of any chapter (Britain 1807, US 1808, Amistad 1839/1841, British 1833, US Civil War/Emancipation/13th Amendment, Brazil 1850/1871/1888), and in stretches it reads closer to a well-narrated sequence of legal milestones than to a chapter with a single dramatic shape. The British-abolition and US paragraphs especially function as capsule histories. The chapter's framing thesis holds it together, but the middle is the closest the book comes to "informative march" rather than "narrative arc."
- The fix is not a rewrite but a sharpening of the throughline so the reader feels each milestone as a turn in one argument rather than the next item: the chapter has two competing organizing ideas (the trade-vs-slavery gap, and the resistance-from-below claim) and they could be braided more tightly so the milestones hang on a single spine. As written, the reader gets a clear and honest chapter that is slightly more list-shaped than its neighbors — a structural note, not a failure.

**Momentum map:** Solid thesis open, energetic Amistad set-piece, then a flatter, milestone-driven middle through the British and US sections, re-energized by Douglass/Tubman, and a strong, well-paced Brazil close ("It was three words long in its essential clause"). Two real peaks (Amistad, Douglass/Tubman) with informative connective tissue between.

**Build dependency:** yes — explicitly opens against Haiti (Ch 8), pays off the Baptist War (Ch 6), Equiano-to-Douglass lineage (Ch 7), and the cotton/domestic-trade economics (Ch 5).

## Chapter 10 — The Surviving Cultures

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** The system was designed to erase the people it enslaved and it failed — they carried their gods, languages, music, and foodways across the ocean "inside themselves" and built wholly new cultures that flourish today, the deepest and most permanent form of resistance in the book.

**What works:**
- The chapter's thesis is its emotional and structural climax for the whole book, and it's stated with quiet power: "It is the answer to the erasure, and it is, in its way, the deepest form of resistance in the whole book, because it is the part the enslaved won permanently."
- The syncretism mechanism is explained, not just named, and made vivid: "a spirit of iron and war might be honored behind the image of a sword-bearing saint... To the master watching, the enslaved appeared to be praying to a Catholic saint. To the enslaved, the saint was a mask over an African god." This turns a concept into an image a reader can hold.
- The chapter shows the book's discipline even in celebration — the music paragraph deliberately checks its own reach ("This is a large claim and it deserves the caution the book has shown throughout... scholars debate the precise pathways") rather than overclaiming, and the Vodou paragraph corrects the "voodoo" caricature directly. The closing distinction — that the enslaved didn't merely *preserve* but *created* cultures "out of the broken pieces of many old ones" — reframes survival as achievement and lands hard.

**What doesn't work:**
- The chapter is organized topically (religion → language → music → foodways/craft → Atlantic Creoles), and the back half (foodways/craft and the Atlantic Creoles return) is lower-energy than the religion and language sections that precede it — the foodways paragraph in particular slightly overlaps with the Gullah material just above it (sweetgrass baskets, rice knowledge appear in both). Minor redundancy; trimming the second mention would tighten the descent. The Atlantic Creoles return is valuable as a callback to Ch 3 but reads as a slightly appended coda before the honesty-about-loss paragraph re-grips.

**Momentum map:** Strong thesis open, peaks across the religion section (syncretism/Vodou/Candomblé/Santería), holds through the Gullah/language section, softens through foodways/craft and the Creole callback, then re-grips powerfully on the "survival through immense loss" paragraph and the "It is a victory" close.

**Build dependency:** yes — pays off Vodou from Ch 8, the rice-knowledge thread from Ch 5, and the Atlantic Creoles from Ch 3; positioned explicitly as "the answer to the erasure" the whole book has built toward.

## Chapter 11 — The Afterlife and the Reckoning

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** A law can end a practice in a moment but not its consequences — the trade's demographic shadow, its invention of race, and the movements and unfinished demand for repair it produced are still unfolding, and the book closes by holding its two truths at once: the scale of the atrocity and the persistence of the people who outlasted it.

**What works:**
- As a final chapter it does the hardest thing well: it synthesizes without merely recapping. The ledger paragraph re-grounds the numbers with integrity ("almost certainly a floor rather than a ceiling") and crucially extends them — "the embarkation figure does not count the people who died *before* the ships" — so the close deepens rather than repeats.
- The "race was invented" argument is the chapter's intellectual peak and is stated with precision: "That ideology did not exist before the trade needed it; it was built, over centuries, to make the system feel natural and just... This is perhaps the trade's most insidious legacy." It connects the historical machine to the reader's present without anachronism.
- The reparations paragraph is a model of the book's honest register on a live and contested question: it lays out the asymmetry (enslavers compensated, enslaved nothing) with the receipts (Britain 1833, France/Haiti) and explicitly "takes no side beyond noting that the question is alive." The two-truths close is genuinely earned by eleven chapters of holding both, not a tacked-on moral.

**What doesn't work:**
- Pan-Africanism and Négritude are handled a notch more thinly than the chapter's other movements — they're explained accurately but at a slight remove, and the Négritude paragraph leans on telling ("an assertion... that they were and always had been fully human") more than the showing the book does elsewhere. In a final chapter this is a forgivable acceleration toward the close, but they're the one stretch that reads as summary rather than scene.

**Momentum map:** Strong "a law cannot undo four centuries" open, peaks at the ledger and the "race was invented" sections, softens slightly through Pan-Africanism/Négritude, re-grips at reparations, and closes on the deliberate, powerful two-truths landing for the whole book.

**Build dependency:** yes — explicitly gathers the demographic threads (Brazil's five million, the US grow-vs-shrink fact), the racial-slavery thread from Ch 2, the compensation asymmetry from Chs 8–9, and the agency thesis from the whole arc into a single closing statement.

## Overall assessment

This is an unusually disciplined and fully realized document — the rare audited narrative where the default grade is STRONG rather than GOOD, and earned. The book has a single, sustained controlling idea announced in the front matter and honored on every page: "two truths held at once... the scale of the atrocity, and the persistence of the people who outlasted it." That dual thesis is not a slogan; it is the structural engine that gives each chapter a point and the whole arc a shape, building from the machine's assembly (1–5) through resistance and abolition (6–9) to survival and reckoning (10–11), with Haiti (8) as the deliberate keystone. The voice is consistent, humane, and propulsive without ever being flippant — it explains mechanisms (why short-staple cotton, why Saint-Domingue's demographics, how syncretism worked) rather than asserting outcomes, and it repeatedly checks its own claims (the Industrial Revolution debate, the Equiano birthplace question, the music pathways) in a way that builds trust rather than draining momentum. Cross-chapter threads are planted and paid off with real craft (the Zong, Vodou, the grow-vs-shrink demographic fact, Zumbi's head rhyming to Boukman's).

The only systemic weakness is the by-design one this subject matter forces: the chapters that must cover the most events, jurisdictions, and named figures (the West African kingdoms in Ch 3, the U.S. revolts in Ch 6, and above all the legal milestones in Ch 9) are where the prose edges toward list or capsule-history shape. Chapter 9 is the single chapter where this tips it from STRONG to GOOD — its middle is more a well-narrated sequence of milestones than a chapter with one dramatic spine, partly because two organizing ideas (trade-vs-slavery, resistance-from-below) compete rather than braid. None of this is a structural failure; it is the friction of comprehensiveness, and the framing theses largely absorb it.

The single biggest improvement would be a light "connective-spine" pass on the three list-prone stretches (Ch 3 kingdoms, Ch 6 U.S. revolts, Ch 9 milestones): give each roll a sentence at its head that names the *cumulative* dynamic so the items read as a rising argument rather than a catalogue — most of that logic already exists, buried in the closing synthesis of each of those chapters, and only needs surfacing.

## Part 1: Forward Findings (new TL gaps)

### Chapter 1 — Origins: The Portuguese and the Sugar Model

1. **"Sponsoring the effort to get past it was a Portuguese prince, **Henry** (1394–1460)"** — Prince Henry, Cape Bojador (~1434), and the whole Portuguese coastal creep are told here with no cross-reference to the TL that narrates them in detail. — age-of-exploration Ch 1–3 covers the same Henry, Ceuta 1415, and the rounding of Bojador; a one-line pointer ("the Portuguese coastal creep is the subject of the Age of Exploration narrative, Chapters 1–3") is owed.

2. **"in 1444 a Portuguese expedition brought something else back to Lisbon: a cargo of African captives... This is the conventional starting date for the maritime slave trade"** — The 1444 starting date is given with no cross-reference, but age-of-exploration narrates the very same event in vivid, specific detail (the Lagos auction, 235 captives, the chronicler Zurara). — Cross-ref to age-of-exploration Ch 3 ("Gold, God, and the First Slaves"); the two accounts even use the same framing ("what was and was not new"), so they should explicitly hand off to each other.

3. **"In 1482, on the Gold Coast, they erected **Elmina Castle**"** — Elmina's founding is told with no cross-civ note that this same fort is a turning point in two reference TLs. — age-of-exploration Ch 3 ("the first permanent European fortification in sub-Saharan Africa") AND songhai-empire Ch 3 ("The Portuguese built Elmina in 1482... intercepting some of the gold flow that had previously moved north across the Sahara") — Elmina is the literal hinge between the Atlantic and trans-Saharan economies; the new TL should note that the gold trade Elmina captured was the economic backbone of the Sahelian empires (Songhai, Mali).
