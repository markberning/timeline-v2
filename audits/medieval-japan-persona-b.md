## Chapter 1 — Two Capitals, Two Powers

1. `[CHECK]` **"drowned by his own grandmother rather than be captured, died the child-emperor Antoku, eight years old"** — the drowning-by-grandmother story comes from the *Tale of the Heike* (the chapter itself flags the Heike as polished literature); the specific detail and the exact age "eight years old" should be checked against what is actually documented rather than the epic's telling.
2. `[CHECK]` **"He went under holding the legend that his grandmother told him a capital waited beneath the waves"** — this is a famous, very literary line from the Heike; verify it is being presented as the epic's invention (which the surrounding text does gesture at) and that the wording is not an over-smooth paraphrase of a quote that may not read this cleanly in the source.
3. `[SOFT]` **"longer than the gap between Columbus and the present day"** — a rhetorical comparison; the "nearly seven hundred years" figure for the shogunate-in-some-form is fine, but the Columbus framing is the writer's flourish, not a sourced fact. Low priority.

## Chapter 2 — The Power Behind the Power

1. `[CHECK]` **"the aging nun Masako is said to have gathered them and reminded them ... in a speech the chronicle preserves and surely improved"** — the text is appropriately hedged ("said to," "surely improved"), but the famous Masako speech is exactly the kind of too-perfect set-piece that should be verified as coming from the Azuma Kagami and flagged as the chronicle's likely embellishment, which it is here; confirm the substance.
2. `[CHECK]` **"confiscated the estates of every courtier and warrior who had backed the rebellion, some three thousand of them"** — "some three thousand" confiscated estates is a suspiciously specific round-ish number for the Jōkyū War aftermath; worth checking the figure against the actual documented count of redistributed estates.
3. `[SOFT]` **"Yasutoki himself reportedly said it was written for rough warriors who could not read the court's classical Chinese codes"** — "reportedly said" is hedged, but this attributed sentiment/quote to Hōjō Yasutoki about the Jōei Code should be verified as a real attribution and not a tidy paraphrase invented to make the point.

## Chapter 3 — A New Religion for a New Age

1. `[CHECK]` **"each over twenty-six feet tall"** — the height of the Niō guardian figures at the Nara temple gate (Tōdai-ji's Nandaimon); a specific measurement worth confirming (commonly cited around 8.4 m / ~28 ft).
2. `[CHECK]` **"around 1252 the people of Kamakura cast in bronze a giant seated figure ... over forty feet tall"** — the Great Buddha of Kamakura date (1252) and height ("over forty feet") are specific; verify the figure (the statue is usually given as ~11.3 m / ~37 ft, which would make "over forty feet" an overstatement).

## Chapter 4 — The Divine Wind

1. `[CHECK]` **"a combined Mongol, Chinese, and Korean army of perhaps twenty to thirty thousand"** — first-invasion (1274) troop figure; appropriately hedged with "perhaps," but verify the range is the conventional scholarly estimate and not invented.
2. `[STRONG]` **"the sources put at something like 140,000 men, very probably the largest seaborne invasion attempted anywhere in the world up to that time, not exceeded in sheer numbers until well into the twentieth century"** — the 140,000 figure for 1281 is a traditional source number worth flagging, but the bigger smell is the sweeping superlative claim that it was unexceeded "until well into the twentieth century"; this is the kind of confident-sounding global comparison that may be the writer's own and not anchored to any source.
3. `[CHECK]` **"a barrier of stone and packed earth running for miles along the beach, roughly six to nine feet high"** — the Hakata Bay defensive wall dimensions ("six to nine feet high," "miles"); specific measurements that should be checked against the excavated remains.
4. `[CHECK]` **"commissioned around 1293 an illustrated scroll ... depicting his own deeds"** — the Takezaki Suenaga scroll (Mōko Shūrai Ekotoba) date "around 1293"; verify the dating, which is itself debated.
5. `[SOFT]` **"Marine archaeology off Kyushu has since recovered wreckage of the lost fleet from the seabed, hard physical confirmation of the disaster"** — broadly true (Takashima wrecks), but check the confident "hard physical confirmation" framing isn't overstating what the marine archaeology actually demonstrates about the typhoon vs. the fleet's loss.

## Chapter 5 — The Emperor Who Wanted It All

1. `[CHECK]` **"smuggled out, the Taiheiki says, hidden under a pile of dried fish in a boat"** — explicitly attributed to the Taiheiki and flagged as such, which is the right handling; still a too-perfect anecdote worth a verification note that this is the chronicle's colorful version.
2. `[CHECK]` **"the legend ... is that Nitta found the land approaches impassable and so prayed at the shore and threw his sword into the waves ... whereupon the tide drew back"** — properly labeled "the legend, recorded in the Taiheiki"; confirm the story is correctly attributed and not presented as fact.
3. `[CHECK]` **"the Taiheiki gives the brothers a famous parting exchange, vowing to be reborn seven times over"** — the "seven lives for my country" (shichishō hōkoku) line; the text correctly calls it "almost certainly the chronicle's invention," but verify the attribution and the famous phrasing.
4. `[CHECK]` **"With that mass suicide, 148 years of Kamakura rule ended"** — "148 years" is an oddly precise span (1185–1333); arithmetic-wise it is defensible, but confirm the start date being counted (1185 vs 1192) matches the "148" figure rather than reading as invented precision.
5. `[SOFT]` **"his agents were caught and his closest advisers exiled, while the emperor himself escaped blame by insisting ... that he had known nothing about it"** — the 1324 (Shōchū) plot detail of Go-Daigo's straight-faced denial is a tidy anecdote; low-priority verification that it isn't smoothed past the actual record.

## Chapter 6 — Two Emperors, One Throne

1. `[CHECK]` **"From 1336 to 1392 — fifty-six years"** — the Nanboku-chō span figure; arithmetic checks (1336–1392 = 56), so this is fine, but flagged only to confirm the endpoint dates are the conventional ones.
2. `[CHECK]` **"his moss-carpeted garden at the temple of Saihō-ji in Kyoto is one of the masterpieces of the form"** — attributing the Saihō-ji (moss temple) garden's design to Musō Soseki is a traditional attribution that is actually contested by modern scholars; verify the claim that he designed it.
3. `[SOFT]` **"the Ashikaga in 1342 fitted out official trade ships and sent them to China"** — the "Tenryū-ji ships" of 1342; date and characterization worth a quick check.

## Chapter 7 — The Golden Pavilion

1. `[STRONG]` **"by Kan'ami's beautiful young son, who performed alongside him. The shogun took the troupe under his personal patronage ... and (the sources are fairly frank about this)"** — the claim that Yoshimitsu was sexually/romantically captivated by the boy Zeami, asserted as something "the sources are fairly frank about," is a strong, specific characterization of a relationship; this should be verified, because "the sources are fairly frank" is the kind of confident sourcing claim that can be invented or overstated.
2. `[CHECK]` **"In 1404 he ... formally accepted from the Ming emperor the title 'King of Japan'"** — the 1404 date for the tally-trade agreement / "King of Japan" title; verify (the "King of Japan" acceptance is often dated to 1402–1404).
3. `[CHECK]` **"only to be burned to the ground in 1950 by a disturbed young monk"** — the 1950 arson of Kinkaku-ji is real and well documented, but the date and "disturbed young monk" framing should be confirmed (this is outside the narrative period and easy to get slightly wrong).
4. `[SOFT]` **"In the 1370s one such troupe came to perform before the young shogun"** — the date of the famous Imagumano sarugaku performance before Yoshimitsu (traditionally 1374/1375); verify the decade.

## Chapter 8 — Tea, Ink, and Emptiness

1. `[CHECK]` **"the man usually credited with pointing it in its decisive direction was a monk and tea adviser to the shogun named Murata Jukō"** — Jukō (Shukō) as a "tea adviser to the shogun [Yoshimasa]" is a traditional claim that modern scholarship treats as largely legendary; verify the asserted connection to Yoshimasa's court.
2. `[CHECK]` **"from any single spot at its edge, you can see at most fourteen of the fifteen rocks; one is always hidden"** — the famous Ryōan-ji "fifteenth hidden rock" claim; the chapter hedges its meaning, but the factual assertion that one rock is always hidden from any viewing spot is frequently repeated and frequently challenged — worth a verification flag.
3. `[CHECK]` **"holding just fifteen rocks arranged in small groups"** — the count of fifteen rocks at Ryōan-ji is standard, but pair-check with the "fourteen visible" claim above.
4. `[SOFT]` **"Kenkō wrote that things left unfinished are more beautiful than things complete ... the moon glimpsed through cloud"** — these are real and famous Tsurezuregusa sentiments, but they are paraphrased closely enough to a quotation that it is worth confirming the paraphrase is faithful and not a smoothed invention.

## Chapter 9 — The Capital Burns

1. `[CHECK]` **"he had been a monk; the succession was literally drawn by lot at a shrine"** — the claim that Ashikaga Yoshinori's succession was decided by lottery at a shrine (Iwashimizu Hachimangū) is a striking, story-perfect detail; verify it is documented rather than legend.
2. `[CHECK]` **"By the start of 1467 there were something like a hundred thousand troops camped in and around the capital"** — "a hundred thousand troops" for the Ōnin War muster; a large round number worth checking against scholarly estimates.
3. `[SOFT]` **"Yamana Sōzen and Hosokawa Katsumoto, the two principals, both died in 1473 — within months of each other"** — the both-died-in-1473 detail is dramatically convenient; it is in fact correct, but flagged for confirmation given how neat it is.
4. `[SOFT]` **"called the Red Monk for his complexion and his shaved head"** — the nickname/origin for Yamana Sōzen ("Aka-nyūdō," red lay-priest); verify the gloss is accurate and not invented color.

## Chapter 10 — A Country of Warlords

1. `[CHECK]` **"In 1526 the Iwami silver mine ... began to be worked with a new refining technique ... Japanese mines may have been pouring out a substantial fraction of all the silver mined on earth"** — the 1526 date for Iwami's takeoff (the cupellation/haifuki technique is usually dated to 1533) and the "substantial fraction of all the silver on earth" claim both warrant checking; the global-share superlative is the kind of impressive-sounding figure that can be inflated.
2. `[CHECK]` **"In 1488, in Kaga province ... they rose against the shugo ... drove him to suicide, and took over the province themselves ... for nearly a century"** — the 1488 Kaga Ikkō-ikki date and the "nearly a century" of peasant self-rule are specific; verify both (the rule lasted to ~1580, so "nearly a century" is roughly right, but confirm).
3. `[SOFT]` **"the code of the Imagawa house, for instance, reads like the constitution of a small independent country"** — the Imagawa Kana Mokuroku as the showcase bunkokuhō; fine as an example, but the characterization is the writer's flourish; low priority.
4. `[SOFT]` **"recent scholarship has shown he was rather better connected than the rags-to-rule story claims"** — invokes "recent scholarship" generically about Hōjō Sōun's origins; this is accurate in broad terms, but the vague appeal to unnamed recent scholarship is worth a confirmation note.

## Chapter 11 — Tigers and Dragons

1. `[CHECK]` **"His battle standard carried four phrases borrowed from the ancient Chinese strategist Sun Tzu ... 'swift as the wind, silent as the forest, fierce as the fire, immovable as the mountain'"** — the Fūrinkazan attribution to Takeda Shingen's standard and the translation are standard, but the precise wording and the direct Sun Tzu sourcing should be confirmed (it is a real Sun Tzu passage, but verify the translation isn't over-polished).
2. `[CHECK]` **"the seated Takeda lord parrying the Dragon's sword strokes with nothing but his iron war fan"** — the fourth-Kawanakajima (1561) single-combat scene; the text explicitly calls it likely "later invention," which is the right handling, but flag the famous war-fan detail for verification as legend.
3. `[CHECK]` **"Between 1553 and 1564 they fought there five separate times"** — "five separate times" at Kawanakajima between specific years; the "five battles" framing is traditional and somewhat schematic — verify the count and the date range.
4. `[SOFT]` **"handed a single arrow, each could snap it easily; handed three arrows bound together, none could break the bundle"** — the Mōri Motonari "three arrows" parable; the text labels it "is said to have taught" and even undercuts it, which is correct; flagged only because the bundle-of-arrows story is a wandering folk motif that may be apocryphal.
5. `[SOFT]` **"the 'one-eyed dragon' — he had lost an eye to childhood smallpox ... once sent an embassy of his own all the way across the Pacific and on to Europe"** — the Date Masamune smallpox-eye and the Hasekura embassy are real, but verify the eye was lost to smallpox specifically (the cause is sometimes given differently).

## Chapter 12 — The Black Ships and the Demon King

1. `[CHECK]` **"one of his own senior retainers committed suicide in protest at his behavior, hoping the shock might reform him"** — the Hirate Masahide suicide-as-remonstrance over young Nobunaga; a story-perfect anecdote; verify it is documented rather than legendary embellishment.
2. `[CHECK]` **"by the traditional figure, three thousand of them ... organized his gunners into rotating ranks"** — the Nagashino three-thousand-gun / three-rank volley; the text explicitly cautions it is "stylized rather than literal," which is exactly right; flag the figure for verification, as the chapter itself does.
3. `[CHECK]` **"the Japanese took to the matchlock ... possibly producing more firearms than any single European state"** — the "more firearms than any single European state" claim is a striking superlative; widely repeated but worth verification as it can be overstated.
4. `[CHECK]` **"the Japanese, by their own counts, hundreds of thousands of Japanese ... For a few decades there were more practicing Christians in Japan than in some European countries"** — Jesuit convert numbers ("hundreds of thousands") are self-reported (the text says "by their own counts," good), and the "more Christians than some European countries" comparison is a soft superlative worth checking.
5. `[CHECK]` **"burned the entire complex to the ground, killing ... thousands of monks, along with women and children"** — the 1571 Mount Hiei massacre casualty scale; the text hedges ("by the accounts that survive, which may exaggerate"), which is correct; flag the "thousands" figure as a contested traditional number.
6. `[CHECK]` **"He installed Ashikaga Yoshiaki ... the Ashikaga shogunate ... came to its formal end after 237 years"** — "237 years" (1338–1573 by the text's count = 235; or 1336–1573 = 237) is an oddly precise span; verify the arithmetic against the founding date the book itself uses (it elsewhere dates the Ashikaga shogunate to 1338, which would give 235, not 237).
7. `[SOFT]` **"the Demon King (Dairokuten Maō ... a title Nobunaga ... is said to have rather enjoyed applying to himself)"** — the claim Nobunaga embraced the "Demon King of the Sixth Heaven" label for himself is traditional; "is said to have" is appropriately hedged; low-priority verification.
8. `[SOFT]` **"eleven days short of finishing the job"** — "eleven days" is oddly precise rhetorical framing for how close Nobunaga was to unification at Honnō-ji; flag as invented-sounding precision (likely echoing Hideyoshi's eleven-day return march rather than any real "days to unification").

## Chapter 13 — The Peasant Who Ruled Japan

1. `[CHECK]` **"Within roughly eleven days of Nobunaga's death he had caught and crushed the murderer Mitsuhide"** — the famous "Chūgoku Great Return" (Chūgoku Ōgaeshi) timing; "roughly eleven days" to Yamazaki is the traditional figure — verify it.
2. `[CHECK]` **"He entered Nobunaga's service as a lowly attendant, a sandal-bearer by the famous story"** — the sandal-bearer origin is explicitly flagged as "the famous story," which is correct handling; flag as legend to verify.
3. `[CHECK]` **"In 1590 Hideyoshi brought a colossal army, by some accounts over two hundred thousand strong, and simply surrounded Odawara"** — the 200,000+ figure for the Odawara siege; a large traditional number worth checking, though the text hedges with "by some accounts."
4. `[STRONG]` **"even bringing tea masters, courtesans, and entertainers so his men could enjoy themselves while the defenders' will collapsed"** — the "leisurely siege with tea masters and courtesans" at Odawara is a colorful, too-perfect anecdote of the kind AI-history invents; verify it is documented and not embroidered.
5. `[CHECK]` **"In 1591 ... Hideyoshi ordered Sen no Rikyū to commit seppuku ... a quarrel over a statue of Rikyū placed above a temple gate"** — the date (1591) and the famous Daitoku-ji gate-statue explanation are standard; the text rightly says the reasons "are still debated," but verify the statue-over-the-gate detail.
6. `[CHECK]` **"the sword hunt (katanagari) of 1588 ... the collected metal ... would be melted down for the bolts and nails of a giant statue of the Buddha"** — the 1588 date and the Great Buddha (Hōkō-ji) pretext are standard; verify both, especially the specific "bolts and nails of a giant Buddha" justification (which is a real edict claim worth confirming).
7. `[CHECK]` **"In 1592 he launched a massive invasion, perhaps 150,000 to 160,000 men"** — the first Korean invasion (Imjin War) troop figure; verify the "150,000 to 160,000" range (commonly cited around 158,000).
8. `[STRONG]` **"twenty-six Christians ... executed by crucifixion at Nagasaki. The Twenty-Six Martyrs ... were the first such killings"** — the 1597 Twenty-Six Martyrs are real and well documented (number, place, method are correct); flagged STRONG only to verify the date is 1597 (sometimes given as Feb 1597) and the "first such killings" claim, since the surrounding text places it within the Korean-war years.
9. `[SOFT]` **"the carrying-off of Korean potters whose skills founded famous Japanese ceramic traditions"** — broadly true (Arita/Satsuma ware origins), but the sweeping "founded famous Japanese ceramic traditions" is a tidy causal claim worth a light check.

## Chapter 14 — The Gambler's Victory

1. `[CHECK]` **"A Japanese saying about the three unifiers ... Nobunaga says 'kill it,' Hideyoshi says 'make it want to sing,' and Ieyasu says 'wait.'"** — the famous "cuckoo that won't sing" senryū is real and well known, but verify the wording/attribution; it is an Edo-period invention (not the unifiers' own words), and the text presents it as a "saying," which is acceptable — confirm the rendering.
2. `[CHECK]` **"Each side fielded somewhere around eighty thousand men — together perhaps the largest battle ever fought on Japanese soil"** — the ~80,000-per-side Sekigahara figures and the "largest battle on Japanese soil" superlative; verify the troop numbers and the superlative (Sekigahara totals are debated and the "largest" claim contestable).
3. `[STRONG]` **"Ieyasu ... finally ordered his own gunners to fire a volley at Kobayakawa's position — a furious demand to commit or be treated as an enemy"** — the dramatic "Ieyasu fires on the wavering Kobayakawa to force his hand" episode; the text itself notes "some modern historians doubt this dramatic detail," which is correct handling — flag for verification as a likely later embellishment.
4. `[CHECK]` **"rebuilt around 1601 onward into the soaring white complex nicknamed the White Heron"** — Himeji Castle's major rebuild date (the famous Ikeda-era reconstruction is usually dated ~1601–1609); verify the "around 1601" start.
5. `[CHECK]` **"William Adams the shipwrecked Englishman whom Ieyasu kept as an advisor and even made a samurai"** — the William Adams claim (advisor, made a samurai/hatamoto) is real and documented; flag only to confirm "made a samurai" is the accurate characterization (he was granted hatamoto status and a fief).
6. `[SOFT]` **"the famous duelist Miyamoto Musashi ... undefeated in dozens of duels and author of a treatise on strategy"** — "undefeated in dozens of duels" rests largely on Musashi's own self-report in the Book of Five Rings; the "dozens" and "undefeated" are traditional self-claims worth a light flag.
