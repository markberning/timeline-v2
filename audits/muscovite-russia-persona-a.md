## Chapter 1 — The World the Mongols Broke

1. **"the senior title of grand prince passed around by a confusing rotation"** — names the rotation as confusing but never says how it worked — How did the title actually rotate? Who got it next, and by what rule, if not father-to-son?
2. **"a postal relay system, siege engineers borrowed from conquered China"** — "borrowed from conquered China" is dropped in without saying when or how the Mongols conquered China — When did the Mongols conquer China, and is that a separate story from the Rus' conquest?
3. **"a city of stone cathedrals built in white limestone in the previous century"** — "the previous century" is vague for a newcomer with no fixed timeline in their head — Roughly what years does "the previous century" mean here — the 1100s?
4. **"the Mongols caught and destroyed that army, and Yuri with it, at the Sit River"** — the death of the grand prince is stated as a result with no sense of how a whole army plus its leader was destroyed — Was this a battle, an ambush, a massacre? What actually happened at the Sit River?
5. **"the spring thaw, turning the roads back to mud, spared the far north"** — earlier the text said winter froze the roads into highways; here the thaw "spares" the north, which seems to contradict the idea that mud stops armies only in spring/summer — So in winter the frozen rivers were the Mongols' roads, but the thaw stopped them — is the point that they ran out of frozen-road season?

## Chapter 2 — Living Under the Yoke

1. **"the Rus' princes were now the tax-paying tributaries of this state"** — the term "tributaries" is used before "tribute" is defined a few paragraphs later — What is a tributary here — someone who pays, someone who's a vassal, or both?
2. **"a system historians call lateral or collateral succession"** — this term and its full explanation actually appear in Chapter 5, but the confusing rotation it describes was already raised in Chapter 1 without a name — (note: this is the missing mechanism from Ch.1 finding #1) Why is the rotation rule named and explained three chapters after it first confused the reader?
3. **"the great formal split between the Catholic and Orthodox churches dates to 1054"** — the split is given a date but no cause or content — What did the two churches actually split over? Why did they stop being one church in 1054?
4. **"a brotherhood of warrior-monks, sworn to celibacy and to holy war, who had carved out a crusading state along the Baltic"** — "crusading state" assumes the reader knows what a crusade is and that there were crusades in the Baltic, not just the Holy Land — Were there crusades in the Baltic? Who were they fighting, and was this connected to the famous crusades to Jerusalem?

## Chapter 3 — The Rise of the Moneybag

1. **"the manipulation of the khan's favor"** — listed as a method of expansion alongside purchase and marriage, but how you "manipulate" a khan's favor to gain land is left abstract — What does manipulating the khan's favor look like in practice — bribery, informing on rivals, what?
2. **"his see — his official seat — had historically been at Kiev and then at Vladimir"** — "see" is glossed as "official seat," but it's unclear whether a Metropolitan physically lived there or it was just a legal title — Did the Metropolitan actually live where his "see" was, and why could he move it so easily?
3. **"the great plague pandemic that swept out of Asia and across Eurasia in the mid-1300s"** — the Black Death's mechanism (what it was, how it spread, what it did to a body) is given only as "killed indiscriminately" — What was the Black Death — a disease? carried how? what did it do to people?
4. **"a lavra being a great senior monastery"** — fine as a gloss, but the parenthetical also says "today it is the spiritual heart of the Russian Church," a forward jump with no anchor — Is this monastery still standing and important today, and if so, what does "spiritual heart" mean concretely?

## Chapter 4 — Kulikovo and the Idea of Resistance

1. **"the senior title of grand prince passed around by a confusing rotation"** — (recurring) the rotation problem keeps being referenced obliquely — see Ch.1 #1
2. **"the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, a vast and rapidly expanding power (originally pagan, later Catholic)"** — Lithuania appears as a major rival with almost no scene-setting beyond "vast" — Where was Lithuania, how did a pagan state become so powerful, and how did it end up holding Ukraine and Belarus?
3. **"Its formidable ruler Olgerd (Algirdas) besieged Moscow more than once in the late 1360s"** — Olgerd is introduced and then dropped; the reader doesn't know if the sieges succeeded or failed beyond "the walls held" — Did Olgerd ever take Moscow, or just fail repeatedly? What happened to him and to Lithuania's threat?
4. **"power fell into the hands of a strongman named Mamai, who was not himself of the royal blood of Genghis Khan"** — the rule that only Genghis's blood can be khan is asserted but never explained — Why could only descendants of Genghis Khan legitimately rule? Who made that rule and why did everyone honor it?
5. **"consciously modeled on the older epic of The Tale of Igor's Campaign"** — a source/text named with zero introduction — What is The Tale of Igor's Campaign — when was it written, what is it about, why would a battle-poem imitate it?
6. **"exactly as Kiswahili distilled an African language out of borrowed Arabic words"** — a cross-civ comparison to Kiswahili dropped in with no explanation of what Kiswahili is — What is Kiswahili and where is it from? The reader has no idea what this comparison is reaching for.
7. **"the Central Asian world-conqueror Timur (also called Tamerlane) would smash Tokhtamysh"** — Timur is introduced here in one clause but given a full introduction in Chapter 5; here he's a name with no scene — Who is Timur and where did he come from? (he's properly introduced a chapter later, which is backwards for the reader)

## Chapter 5 — The Gathering of the Lands

1. **"He smashed the army of the khan Tokhtamysh (the same Tokhtamysh who had burned Moscow in 1382), sacked the Horde's great trading cities on the Volga"** — Timur's wrecking of the Horde is described entirely by result (smashed, sacked) — How did Timur beat the Horde? Was this one battle, a campaign? Why did he attack them at all?
2. **"the once-unified Horde began to splinter into smaller successor khanates — Kazan, Astrakhan, Crimea, the Great Horde — each a fragment of the old terror"** — four new states named in one breath with no individual sense of where they are or how they differ — Where are these four khanates, and is "the Great Horde" the leftover core or just another fragment?
3. **"Blinding — deliberately destroying a rival's eyes — was a standard piece of medieval political surgery, used across the Orthodox world and especially in Byzantium"** — "political surgery" and "used across the Orthodox world" assert a widespread practice with one example — Was blinding really common, or is Byzantium the only real example? How was it physically done?
4. **"At the Council of Florence in 1439 ... the Byzantine delegation ... agreed to it"** — the Council of Florence is named as "a great gathering of Western and Eastern churchmen in Italy" but the reader doesn't know who called it or why it had authority — Who organized the Council of Florence, and why would the Byzantines travel to Italy to negotiate their own faith away?
5. **"This was the moment the Russian Church became, in effect, autocephalous"** — well-glossed, but the reader may wonder whether Constantinople accepted this or fought it — Did Constantinople recognize Russia electing its own metropolitan, or did this cause a rift?
6. **"the city Russians simply called Tsargrad, 'the city of the Caesar'"** — fine gloss, but it sits right beside the earlier claim that "tsar" comes from "Caesar," and the reader may want the connection drawn — Is "Tsargrad" the same root as "tsar"? (the text could confirm this link it's clearly implying)

## Chapter 6 — Ivan the Great and the Third Rome

1. **"it controlled a fur-trading empire stretching toward the Arctic and the Urals"** — "fur-trading empire" is asserted but the mechanism (who trapped, who bought, why furs were valuable) is empty — Who actually did the trapping and trading, and why were furs valuable enough to call it an empire?
2. **"a major partner of the Hanseatic League (the powerful confederation of northern German trading towns that dominated commerce across the Baltic)"** — the Hanseatic League is glossed but the reader doesn't know what Novgorod traded with them or how the partnership worked — What did Novgorod and the Hansa actually exchange? Furs for what?
3. **"the Bolognese engineer Aristotele Fioravanti built the Dormition Cathedral"** — "Bolognese" assumes the reader knows Bologna is in Italy — Where is Bologna, and is this the same as the Italian engineers mentioned in the same paragraph?
4. **"those red walls and swallowtail battlements, which look so quintessentially Russian, were in fact built by Italians and resemble the fortress walls of Milan and Verona"** — "swallowtail battlements" is a specific architectural term with no image given — What is a swallowtail battlement? The reader can't picture the shape being compared to Milan and Verona.
5. **"closer to slavery than to medieval Western feudalism"** — "Western feudalism" is invoked as a contrast the reader is assumed to understand — What is medieval Western feudalism, and how is serfdom different from it? The reader may not know either term.
6. **"a peasant could leave his landlord only during a two-week window around St. George's Day in the autumn"** — earlier the same paragraph says "two-week window," but Chapter 9 calls it "the week around St. George's Day" — Is it a week or two weeks? The two chapters disagree on the size of the window.

## Chapter 7 — The First Tsar

1. **"Ivan's mother, Elena Glinskaya, ran it briefly and then died in 1538, possibly poisoned"** — Elena Glinskaya appears and dies in a single clause with no sense of who she was or where she came from — Who was Elena Glinskaya? Was a woman running a regency unusual, and why?
2. **"a priest named Sylvester, a low-born official named Alexei Adashev, and the metropolitan Macarius among them"** — three advisers named in a list with no sense of what each did — What did each of these men actually do? Why does a priest, a low-born official, and the metropolitan form a governing circle?
3. **"the creation of the streltsy (STRELT-see, 'shooters'), Russia's first permanent corps of firearm-equipped infantry"** — well-defined, but "first permanent corps" raises the question of what the army was before — If the streltsy were the first permanent infantry, who fought Russia's wars before them — only the gentry cavalry?
4. **"a 'holy fool,' a kind of revered religious eccentric, buried there"** — "holy fool" is glossed only as "religious eccentric," which doesn't explain why such a person would be revered or have a cathedral named for him — What is a holy fool — why would an eccentric be holy, and why name Russia's greatest cathedral after one?
5. **"the first direct trade link between Russia and Western Europe, formalized in the English Muscovy Company"** — but Novgorod's Hanseatic trade (Chapter 6) and the German Quarter foreigners suggest Russia already traded with the West — Wasn't Russia already trading with the West through Novgorod and the Hansa? How is this the "first direct" link?
6. **"more than a century after Gutenberg's press had transformed Western Europe"** — Gutenberg and his press are named as a known reference — Who was Gutenberg and what did his press do? The reader may know the name vaguely but not the date or impact being compared to.

## Chapter 8 — The Terror

1. **"the death in 1560 of Ivan's first wife, Anastasia Romanovna, of the Romanov family"** — Anastasia is introduced mainly as a future dynasty-founder's relative; who she was to Ivan beyond "steadied" him is thin — Who was Anastasia and why would her death specifically unhinge him? What does "steadied him" mean concretely?
2. **"He split Russia in two. One part, the zemshchina, was the ordinary land ... The other part, the oprichnina ... eventually perhaps a third of the entire country"** — the reader can't picture how you "split a country in two" administratively while people still live everywhere — How does splitting a country into two zones actually work — were the zones contiguous, scattered, did people have to move?
3. **"they sniffed out and swept away the tsar's enemies"** — the dog's head and broom emblems are explained, but how the oprichniki "sniffed out" enemies (informers? denunciations? random terror?) is empty — How did they actually identify "traitors"? Was there any process, or just accusation?
4. **"his exhumed remains showed high mercury levels, though that may have been from medicine"** — mercury "from medicine" is dropped in with no explanation of why anyone took mercury — Why would mercury be used as medicine, and what were they treating?
5. **"a band of Cossacks — free, semi-independent frontier warriors of the southern steppe"** — Cossacks get a fuller introduction in Chapter 10, but they first appear here with a thin one-line gloss — Where did the Cossacks come from? Why are there "free" warriors living outside the state at all? (the real introduction comes two chapters later)
6. **"the famous, harrowing painting by Ilya Repin"** — Repin is named as a source/artist with no introduction — Who was Ilya Repin and when did he paint this? The reader has no idea this is a 19th-century painting, centuries after the event.

## Chapter 9 — The Dynasty Burns Out

1. **"the line of Rurik (ROO-rik), the half-legendary Norse warlord whom every prince of Moscow claimed as ancestor, had ruled the Russian lands for roughly seven hundred years"** — Rurik is named as a "half-legendary Norse warlord" but the reader doesn't know how a Norse (Viking?) warlord became the ancestor of all Russian princes — Who was Rurik, when did he live, and how did a Norse warlord end up founding the Russian ruling line?
2. **"the week around St. George's Day in late autumn"** — (recurring) Chapter 6 called this a "two-week window" — see Ch.6 #6
3. **"Moscow paid its cavalry not in cash ... but in land, the pomestie (poh-MYES-tyeh) estate granted to a man in return for military service"** — clear gloss, but it raises the unanswered question of who actually worked these estates before serfdom locked peasants down — If peasants could freely leave, how did the pomestie system ever function before they were bound?
4. **"this was very likely the global climate aftershock of a colossal volcanic eruption far away, at Huaynaputina in Peru in early 1600"** — the mechanism (ash dimming the sun) is given, which is good; but "in Peru" assumes the reader connects a South American volcano to Russian frost — How does a volcano in Peru cause crop failure in Russia? (the ash/sulfur sentence does explain it — borderline, but the geographic leap is large)
5. **"the figures are guesses built on the horrified accounts of survivors"** — good hedging, but the "two million people, perhaps a third of the population of the affected regions" precedes it and the reader can't tell what "affected regions" means — Which regions were affected — all of Russia, or just the north? A third of what total?

## Chapter 10 — The Time of Troubles

1. **"a defrocked monk named Grigory Otrepyev ... who had taken vows, picked up an alarming amount of knowledge about the old court"** — "defrocked" is used without gloss — What does "defrocked" mean — that he was a monk who quit, or was thrown out?
2. **"He won the backing of Polish-Lithuanian magnates, the immensely powerful regional lords of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth"** — "magnates" glossed as "regional lords," but how lords could back a private invasion independent of their king is unexplained — Could Polish lords really raise armies and invade a neighbor on their own, without the king? How did that work?
3. **"He secretly converted to Catholicism, which his Polish backers wanted"** — the stakes of a would-be Orthodox tsar secretly turning Catholic are huge but stated flatly — Why did the Poles want him Catholic, and why did he think he could hide it from Orthodox Russia?
4. **"built around the famous winged hussars (heavy lance-armed cavalry who wore towering wooden frames feathered like wings rising over their backs)"** — vividly described, but "why" they wore wings is left dangling as "terrifying and genuinely effective" — Why did wings make the cavalry effective — did they frighten horses, make noise, just look terrifying?
5. **"Shuisky was overthrown and packed off to a monastery, the Russian custom for disposing of inconvenient men by forcing them to take monastic vows, which legally ended their claim to anything worldly"** — good gloss; but it's the third time forced tonsure appears (Filaret, Sophia later) and the reader may want to know whether the victim could ever come back — Could a man forced into monastic vows ever return to power, or was it permanent? (Filaret's later return seems to contradict "legally ended their claim")
6. **"a council of leading boyars took charge of Moscow, a body history remembers as the Seven Boyars"** — "Seven Boyars" named but the reader doesn't know if it's literally seven men or a nickname — Was it literally seven boyars? Who chose them, and what authority did they have to offer the throne away?

## Chapter 11 — Rebuilding and the Great Schism

1. **"His father, Filaret ... forced into monastic vows by Boris Godunov to remove him as a rival ... When he was finally released in 1619"** — a man forced to become a monk is later "released" and made Patriarch, which seems to contradict Chapter 10's claim that vows "legally ended their claim to anything worldly" — How could Filaret be a forced monk yet return to become co-ruler? Doesn't that break the rule that tonsure was permanent and final?
2. **"the disciplined, drilled, firearm-wielding infantry of Western Europe"** — Western military superiority is asserted repeatedly but the reader never learns what "drilled" infantry actually did differently — What did European infantry do — volley fire, formations? — that Russian levies couldn't, and why was it so much better?
3. **"its Jewish estate-managers onto a hostile Orthodox peasantry"** — Jewish estate-managers appear suddenly with no explanation of why Jews managed Polish estates in Ukraine — Why were Jews managing Polish lords' estates in Ukraine? How did that arrangement come about?
4. **"the name of Jesus was to be respelled in the service books with an extra letter"** — concrete and good, but the reader can't tell what the change was or why a spelling could damn a soul — What was the spelling change (from what to what), and why would adding a letter to a holy name be a matter of salvation?
5. **"the court painter Simon Ushakov, who began softening the flat, otherworldly Russian icon with Western shading and lifelike modeling"** — Ushakov is named with a clear contribution, but "flat, otherworldly" icons were described earlier as deliberately so — Why was the Russian icon deliberately flat, and why was making it lifelike a "revolution" rather than just an improvement?
6. **"the emancipation of 1861, two centuries later"** — a forward reference to a payoff that is explicitly in the next book, not this one — Does this book ever return to 1861, or is the reader being pointed at something they won't see here?

## Chapter 12 — The Young Peter and the End of Muscovy

1. **"could bend a silver plate with his bare hands"** — a vivid physical claim presented as fact in the opening — Is this a documented fact or a legend? The book is usually careful to flag tall tales, but here it's stated plainly.
2. **"the Streltsy (STRELT-see), the hereditary corps of musketeers who garrisoned Moscow, a privileged, conservative, increasingly mutinous force, half soldiers and half a tradesmen's guild"** — the streltsy were introduced in Chapter 7 as "shooters"/first permanent infantry; here "hereditary" and "half a tradesmen's guild" are new — How did a corps of soldiers become "hereditary" and run trades on the side? Did sons inherit the job?
3. **"the German Quarter (the Nemetskaya Sloboda), the walled suburb of Moscow where foreign merchants, soldiers, engineers, and adventurers, Dutch, German, Scots, English, were required to live, segregated"** — good scene-setting, but "German Quarter" housing Dutch, Scots, and English is confusing without noting that "German" was a generic Russian word for foreigners — Why is it called the German Quarter if Dutch, Scots, and English lived there too?
4. **"new artillery (cast, the story goes, partly from melted-down church bells)"** — "the story goes" flags it as possibly apocryphal, which is good, but why bells specifically is unexplained — Why would they melt church bells for cannon — was bronze that scarce, and didn't the Church object?
5. **"he had to buy his army's escape by handing Azov, his first conquest, back to the Turks"** — "buy his army's escape" — was this a literal payment, a treaty, a bribe? — How do you "buy" an escape from an army that has you surrounded? What were the actual terms?
6. **"recast the entire nobility's relationship to the state with the Table of Ranks in 1722"** — the chapter and the book repeatedly say Peter declared the empire in 1721 as the closing event, but the Table of Ranks is dated 1722, after that endpoint — Is the Table of Ranks (1722) inside this book's timeframe or after it? The book's stated end is the 1721 imperial proclamation.
