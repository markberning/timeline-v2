## Story Editor evaluation (Persona D)

## Chapter 1 — The Colonial Pressure Cooker

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about how three centuries of racial hierarchy, economic extraction, and creole resentment packed an empire full of explosive pressure that needed only an outside trigger to detonate.

**What works:**
- The controlling metaphor — "This chapter is about the packing" — gives the whole chapter a thesis and a shape, so the parade of topics (casta, silver, Bourbon reforms, early revolts) all reads as evidence for a single argument rather than a survey.
- The counterintuitive opening claim ("It was not seething with independence plots") creates immediate tension and a question the reader wants answered, and it pays off in the closing line that the trigger "will not come from inside it at all."
- The three early sparks are not just listed — they are organized around a real pattern ("revolt from the top wanted self-rule and kept the social order; revolt from the bottom wanted to overturn the social order itself"), which turns a potential list into an argument.

**What doesn't work:**
- The middle "ideas" paragraph (Humboldt, Mutis, Nariño) is the closest thing to an achievement-list stretch — it introduces three figures in quick succession and the momentum dips slightly. It recovers with the strong "smuggled cargo" image, but the Mutis sentence in particular is the one beat the chapter could lose without cost.

**Momentum map:** Strong thesis-driven open, steady build through casta → silver → Bourbon reforms, a slight dip in the mid "ideas" paragraph, strong "picture the pressure cooker in 1807" close that hands off to Chapters 2 and 3.

**Build dependency:** yes — as Chapter 1 it sets up the central arc explicitly (the empire held by a single thread, the question "who rules when the king is gone") and plants the two triggers (Haiti, Napoleon) that the next two chapters detonate.

## Chapter 2 — Haiti Lights the Fuse

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about how an enslaved population did the literally unthinkable — defeating France and founding the first nation born of a slave revolt — and thereby became both the inspiration and the terror that shaped every cautious calculation the Spanish-American liberators would make.

**What works:**
- The chapter earns its place in the book by repeatedly tying Haiti back to the main story ("what it *meant* to everyone watching"), so it never reads like a detour into a different revolution — it reads like the emotional and strategic engine of everything to come.
- The framing of the sugar economy as "a death machine" rather than generic slavery delivers exactly the visceral stakes the persona looks for; the reader feels the horror, not just registers a fact.
- The closing turn — Haiti as the literal, material secret backer who would arm Bolívar on the abolition condition — sets up a genuine narrative debt (the bargain) that the book will collect in Chapter 5, giving the reader a reason to keep going.

**What doesn't work:**
- Louverture's arc and Dessalines's arc are both strong, but the transition between them ("Because by then the revolution no longer needed Louverture") slightly rushes past the strategic handoff; a reader briefly wonders how leadership passed. Minor — it self-corrects within the sentence.

**Momentum map:** Strong "cane knives" open, builds through the death-machine economy and the unimaginability of victory, peaks emotionally at Louverture's betrayal and the 1804 declaration, and closes on the highest-stakes note (the specter of Haiti + the secret bargain). No dead stretches.

**Build dependency:** yes — it explicitly advances from Chapter 1's "two triggers" setup, delivering the first one, and it plants the abolition bargain and the "specter of Haiti" that recur as causal forces through Chapters 5, 8, and 9.

## Chapter 3 — The Empire Decapitates Itself

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about how Napoleon's removal of the Spanish king turned a loyalist crisis into a revolutionary one, because the very argument the colonists used to stay loyal ("sovereignty reverts to the people") contained the logic of independence.

**What works:**
- The intellectual climax is genuinely thrilling for a history chapter: the chapter shows a *trap springing* — the loyalist junta argument carrying inside it a "radioactive" question it "could not stop itself from asking." That is a real piece of dramatic logic, not a fact-dump.
- The Portugal/Spain contrast ("Portugal's empire bent in the storm. Spain's snapped") is a crisp, memorable hinge that simultaneously explains this chapter and pre-loads Chapter 9.
- The Bayonne sequence has narrative drive — the feuding father and son, Napoleon playing them off, the crown handed to a brother "with barely a shot fired at the top" — concrete and vivid where it could have been procedural.

**What doesn't work:**
- The Cortes of Cádiz / 1812 Constitution paragraph is the most expository stretch, and it briefly slows the momentum between the Bayonne drama and the "trap springs" climax. It is necessary setup (it pays off in Chapter 4 and Chapter 8), but it is the one passage that reads more like explanation than story.

**Momentum map:** Strong "held together by a person, not an idea" open, dramatic Portugal-flight and Bayonne middle, a slight expository dip at Cádiz, then a rising intellectual climax ("here is where the trap springs") and a hand-off close to Chapter 4.

**Build dependency:** yes — it delivers the second trigger Chapter 1 promised and Chapter 2 anticipated, and it sets the central question ("who rules when the king is gone") that Chapters 4–7 spend the war answering.

## Chapter 4 — The Juntas and the First Republics

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about why the first wave of revolutions failed almost everywhere — and how that failure taught its survivors the three brutal lessons (central authority, the masses, Spanish cruelty as recruiter) that the winning second wave would be built on.

**What works:**
- The chapter is explicitly framed as a chapter of *failure* ("this chapter is not a triumph"), which is a bold and effective structural choice — it gives a sprawling, multi-front year a single emotional throughline and makes the reader understand defeat as setup.
- The Boves/llanero passage delivers the book's sharpest reversal of expectation (the masses fighting *for* the king against the creole republic) and ties it directly back to Haiti's lesson "from the other direction" — a genuinely illuminating cross-chapter linkage.
- The Morillo paragraph lands a real irony with stakes ("Spanish brutality was the revolution's best recruiting officer"), turning a reconquest that looks like total defeat into the seedbed of victory.

**What doesn't work:**
- This is the most populous chapter (Buenos Aires, Caracas, Miranda, Bolívar, Belgrano, Artigas, Boves, Morillo), and the Belgrano/Artigas paragraph is where it comes closest to a multi-front survey. The throughline holds, but a reader tracking names is working hardest here; tightening the Artigas digression (which mostly seeds Chapter 10) would sharpen the focus on the failure thesis.

**Momentum map:** Strong thesis open, builds through the Venezuela collapse and the Cartagena Manifesto, peaks at the Boves reversal, dips slightly into the multi-front Belgrano/Artigas survey, recovers hard at Morillo and the three-lessons close.

**Build dependency:** yes — it pays off the juntas Chapter 3 set in motion, and it explicitly forges the three lessons that motivate Bolívar's transformation in Chapter 5; the "washed up in Jamaica" close is a direct handoff.

## Chapter 5 — Bolívar's Northern Campaign

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about how a beaten exile became the Liberator of half a continent by learning the three lessons of failure — accepting a strong hand, buying his army with Haiti's abolition bargain, and winning the plainsmen — and gambling it all on the impossible Andes crossing.

**What works:**
- The frame ("Eleven years later he would be the master of an area larger than the conquests of Napoleon... begins not with a battle but with a letter and a bargain") sets a clear transformation arc and then delivers it beat by beat.
- The Boyacá sequence is the best action writing in the book: the Spanish certainty that "no army would attempt that crossing," Bolívar doing "exactly that," the men who "had never seen snow simply died on the mountain," and the two-hour battle that "broke Spanish power in New Granada in a single afternoon." Real stakes, real momentum.
- The Manuela Sáenz paragraph is well-placed — it both enriches the cast and plants a debt ("the Liberator of the Liberator") collected in Chapter 10, and the "remember the name" beat on Sucre does the same. The chapter manages its forward references like a novelist seeding payoffs.

**What doesn't work:**
- The Gran Colombia paragraph and the Carabobo/Maracaibo/Pichincha paragraph stack three victories and a state-founding in quick succession; the energy of the Boyacá set-piece dissipates a little into a "completing the liberation" list. It is saved by the "dream and its fatal flaw were born in the same breath" line, but the run of battle names is the chapter's one flat patch.

**Momentum map:** Strong transformation-frame open, character study of Bolívar, builds through the Jamaica Letter and Haiti bargain, peaks at the Andes/Boyacá set-piece, sags slightly through the follow-on victories, recovers at Sucre/Sáenz and the two-liberators-converging close.

**Build dependency:** yes — it collects the abolition bargain (Chapter 2) and the three lessons (Chapter 4), and its close sets up the convergence on Lima that structures Chapters 6 and 7.

## Chapter 6 — San Martín and the Southern Crossing

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about the anti-Bolívar — the cold professional who methodically engineered the liberation of the south and then, at the summit of triumph, handed the whole prize to his rival and walked into oblivion.

**What works:**
- The fire/ice framing against Bolívar ("If Bolívar was the revolution's fire... then the man who freed the southern half... was its ice") gives the chapter an instant identity and makes San Martín's eventual renunciation land as the logical expression of his character rather than a mystery.
- The strategic insight — Lima cannot be taken overland, so first free Chile, build a fleet, sail north — is presented as "the intellectual heart of his campaign," which turns logistics into narrative drive; the reader follows a *plan unfolding* rather than a sequence of battles.
- The Guayaquil meeting is handled with real editorial discipline and dramatic power: the chapter leans into the unknowability ("famous precisely because nobody knows what was said") rather than inventing a scene, and the catalogue of unanswerable questions makes the silence itself dramatic. The renunciation is genuinely moving.

**What doesn't work:**
- The Chacabuco → Cancha Rayada → Maipú run is necessary but is the chapter's most conventional battle sequence; it briefly reads as a campaign summary before the chapter re-engages emotionally at Lima and Guayaquil. Minor — the methodical-soldier framing keeps it from feeling like a list.

**Momentum map:** Strong ice/renunciation open, character-driven through the professional-soldier setup and the strategic insight, steady through the crossing and Chilean battles, peaks emotionally at the Guayaquil walk-away and the obscurity-in-France close.

**Build dependency:** yes — it runs parallel to Chapter 5 and converges with it (the two armies marching on Lima), and the Guayaquil handoff explicitly sets up Bolívar finishing the war in Chapter 7; San Martín's exile seeds Chapter 10.

## Chapter 7 — The Final Reckoning

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about the hunting-down and destruction of the last Spanish field army in two high-altitude battles that ended three centuries of empire — and the immediate, uneasy realization that winning the war had not begun the peace.

**What works:**
- The chapter knows it is short and uses brevity as a virtue: it is taut and propulsive, framed as a manhunt ("how that last army was hunted down and destroyed... in two battles fought above 11,000 feet").
- The Junín detail — a cavalry battle fought "in near silence, with the lance and the saber... Not a shot was fired" — is exactly the kind of arresting, specific image that makes a battle memorable, and the chapter is self-aware that it "sounds invented but is well attested."
- The closing move is the chapter's best work: rather than ending on triumph, it pivots to the unanswered questions (borders, citizenship, slavery, the generals with armies) that "would dominate the rest of the century," giving the military climax real thematic weight and a strong pull toward Chapters 8–11.

**What doesn't work:**
- The Bolivia / president-for-life paragraph, while important (it seeds the Chapter 10 Bolívar-Santander argument), slightly diffuses the momentum right after the Ayacucho climax — the chapter peaks at "the continent was free," then has to step back down into constitutional theory before the strong stock-taking close.

**Momentum map:** Lean manhunt open, builds through Junín's silent cavalry to the Ayacucho climax ("the continent was free"), a brief step-down into the Bolivia constitution, then a strong "Ayacucho had ended the war. It had not begun the peace" close that hands off to the back half of the book.

**Build dependency:** yes — it completes the war arc the previous chapters built toward, collects Sucre ("remember the name," Chapter 5), and explicitly poses the unanswered questions that structure Chapters 10 and 11.

## Chapter 8 — Mexico's Two-Stage Revolution

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about the great paradox of Latin American independence — that Mexico broke from Spain not because radicals overthrew conservatives but because the conservatives finally found independence safer than a newly liberal Spain, so the revolution literally ran backward.

**What works:**
- The "revolution that ran backward" thesis is the strongest single organizing idea in the book, and the chapter delivers it with total clarity, framing the two stages as "aimed in opposite directions" and paying it off in the closing "mirror image" contrast with Brazil.
- The Hidalgo material has real emotional and visual power (the bell, the 80,000-strong "flood," the severed head in an iron cage for a decade) and is explicitly tied back to the specter of Haiti — the creoles who "saw their own throats."
- The Iturbide about-face and the Plan of Iguala are explained as "a masterpiece of having-it-both-ways," which makes a complex political settlement legible and even satisfying as a piece of cynical statecraft.

**What doesn't work:**
- The post-independence tail (Iturbide's empire, Casa Mata, Guadalupe Victoria, the Central American breakaway, the Church's enduring power) packs a lot of aftermath into the final third; it is well-handled but is the densest stretch and slightly tests the reader after the clean Iguala climax. The throughline survives because each beat reinforces "the war could change who ruled without changing the order they ruled over."

**Momentum map:** Strong paradox open, vivid and emotional through Hidalgo and Morelos, a determined-holdout beat with Guerrero, sharp pivot at the 1820 Spanish liberal revolt, climaxes at the Iguala compromise, dense but purposeful aftermath, strong "mirror image of Brazil" close.

**Build dependency:** yes — it depends on the 1820 Spanish liberal revolt established as a force in Chapters 3–4, draws explicitly on the Haiti specter (Chapter 2), and its closing contrast sets up Chapter 9; it stands apart from the Bolívar/San Martín thread by design and earns it.

## Chapter 9 — Brazil's Bloodless Break

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about why the same Iberian crisis that shattered Spanish America into a dozen warring republics instead produced, in Brazil, a single durable monarchy — because how an empire breaks depends on how it was held together.

**What works:**
- The chapter is built as a sustained, explicit comparison ("Everything about Brazil's independence is the opposite of Spanish America's"), which makes it the analytic payoff of the whole book; the reader has been primed across eight chapters and now gets the contrast cashed out.
- The "Why?" structure in the second half — laying out four converging reasons Brazil stayed one country — is genuinely satisfying expository architecture: it reads as an argument being built, not a list, because each reason is anchored to something the reader already knows from earlier chapters.
- The Dia do Fico and Cry of Ipiranga are given with the right balance of color and skepticism ("polished by a century of national art into something grander and cleaner than the messy reality"), keeping the house voice while still delivering the drama of a prince defying his own father.

**What doesn't work:**
- The chapter carries its arc past the natural climax (the unified empire) into Pedro I's later troubles, the Cisplatine War / Uruguay, the abdication, and Pedro II's reign — a lot of dynastic follow-through. It is handled gracefully and the "stable center that held" theme justifies it, but the final third is the one place the chapter's energy diffuses across many years.

**Momentum map:** Strong contrast-thesis open, builds through the court-in-Rio transformation and the Dia do Fico, peaks at the Cry of Ipiranga and the four-reasons analysis, then a long but purposeful glide through Pedro I's fall to Pedro II's stability and the strong "prince succeeded where the Liberator was about to fail" close.

**Build dependency:** yes — it is the explicit counterpoint to the entire Spanish-American arc, draws directly on the court's flight (Chapter 3) and the Haiti specter (Chapter 2), and its closing line sets up the Bolívar tragedy of Chapter 10 by contrast.

## Chapter 10 — The Broken Dream and the Caudillos

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about the morning after — how the same war that won independence hollowed out the new nations, dissolved the great dream of unity, killed the liberators heartbroken, and left a vacuum that the strongman filled for the rest of the century.

**What works:**
- The opening thesis ("defeating an empire is far easier than building a nation") reframes the whole book's triumphant arc into tragedy and gives the chapter immediate emotional stakes; the "plowed the sea" motif is deployed perfectly as the keynote.
- The Bolívar–Santander conflict is elevated from a personality clash to "the founding argument of Latin American politics... it has never entirely stopped," which gives the chapter intellectual heft and explains the caudillo era as the consequence of a real unresolved question.
- The Chile/Portales exception is used with real editorial skill — "the great exception is worth noting because it proves the pattern" — turning a counterexample into reinforcement of the thesis (institutions vs. strongmen), and the Sarmiento/"civilization and barbarism" close names the era's self-understanding.

**What doesn't work:**
- The chapter has a large cast of post-war figures (Santander, Páez, Rosas, Portales, Sarmiento, Bello) and the back third (Rosas → Portales → Sarmiento → Bello) is a sequence of national case studies; it holds together under the institutions-vs-strongmen thesis, but it is the chapter most at risk of reading as a tour of national politics rather than a single arc. The Bello sentence in particular is a dense aside.

**Momentum map:** Powerful tragic open, peaks emotionally at Bolívar's death and "plowed the sea," steady through the caudillo definition, then a case-study sequence (Rosas/Portales/Sarmiento) that holds the thesis but flattens slightly, recovering at the "unkept promises" close that hands off to Chapter 11.

**Build dependency:** yes — it collects nearly every thread (Gran Colombia from Chapter 5, San Martín from Chapter 6, the Bolivia constitution from Chapter 7, the unanswered questions from Chapter 7), and it explicitly sets up the unfinished business (slavery, inter-state war) of Chapter 11.

## Chapter 11 — The Unfinished Century

**Grade: STRONG**

**Throughline:** This chapter is about how independence's real conclusion was not the glorious 1820s battles but the bloody, century-long settling-up — nations warring on each other, slavery dismantled a half-century late, the last monarchy falling, and Europe's empire quietly replaced by a new hegemon to the north.

**What works:**
- The chapter is structured around a clear, escalating argument — the wars among the new nations, then slavery, then the fall of Brazil's monarchy, then the U.S. arrival — and it explicitly frames itself as following "independence to its real conclusion," giving a potentially sprawling closing chapter a firm spine.
- It practices the skepticism the book's framing note promised: the Paraguay casualty figures are flagged as "genuinely dispute[d]" rather than dramatized, which builds trust at exactly the moment a lesser chapter would reach for the most shocking number.
- The Panama/Monroe Doctrine close is a strong ending for the whole book — "One era of foreign domination was closing as another opened" — and the final paragraph's return to "plowed the sea" (correcting Bolívar — "He was too harsh — the nations were real") is a genuinely earned, resonant capstone.

**What doesn't work:**
- This chapter carries the heaviest load of discrete topics (Triple Alliance, War of the Pacific, guano, Brazilian abolition in stages, the fall of the monarchy, Cuba, Panama, Monroe Doctrine), and the stretch from the War of the Pacific through the staged abolition laws is where it most resembles a rapid survey. The thesis ("the unfinished century") holds it together, but it is the chapter working hardest to keep momentum across many subjects.

**Momentum map:** Strong "did not end gently" open, builds through the two great inter-state wars, steps into the staged-abolition sequence (the densest patch), recovers at the fall of the monarchy, and closes powerfully on the U.S. as new hegemon and the corrected "plowed the sea" capstone.

**Build dependency:** yes — it is the deliberate payoff of the unfinished-business theme seeded in Chapters 7 and 10, collects the Haiti abolition bargain (Chapters 2, 5), the Brazilian monarchy (Chapter 9), and the dream of unity (Chapter 5), and closes the arc the framing note opened.

## Overall assessment

This is an exceptionally well-built document — arguably one of the most structurally disciplined narratives in the corpus. It has a single, clear, repeatedly-restated controlling argument (the king-shaped hole at the center of the empire, and the gap between winning a war and building a nation) that every chapter advances rather than merely follows. The voice is consistent, confident, and genuinely literary: it uses controlling metaphors ("the packing," "fire and ice," "plowed the sea"), counterintuitive framing (Mexico's backward revolution, Brazil's bloodless monarchy), and disciplined honesty about its own evidence (the Guayaquil silence, the Paraguay figures). The chapters build on each other with unusual rigor — debts are planted and collected like a novel's (the Haiti bargain, "remember the name" Sucre, Manuela Sáenz, the unanswered questions of Chapter 7), and the two thematic "control" chapters (Mexico and Brazil) earn their departures from the main Bolívar/San Martín thread by serving the book's comparative argument. The single biggest thing that would improve the document is pacing in the back third of several chapters: a recurring pattern is a clean climax followed by a denser "what happened after" survey stretch (the follow-on victories in Ch 5, the post-Ayacucho constitution in Ch 7, the post-independence aftermath in Ch 8, the dynastic follow-through in Ch 9, the case-study sequence in Ch 10, the staged-abolition run in Ch 11). None of these sink a chapter or fall to GOOD — the throughlines hold throughout — but trimming or further dramatizing those survey passages is where the editing energy belongs.

## Part 1: Forward Findings (new TL gaps)

### Chapter 1 — The Colonial Pressure Cooker

1. **"the conquests of the Aztec and Inca empires in the following decades"** — the two conquests that founded this whole empire are named in a single clause with no anchor to the civilizations that have full narratives — the Aztec fall (1519–21, Cortés, smallpox + Tlaxcala) is covered in detail in aztec-empire.md Ch 8 and the Inca fall (1532, Cajamarca, Pizarro, 168 men) in inca-empire.md Ch 8; a one-line "(the side of the conquered is told in the Aztec and Inca narratives)" pointer would let the reader see the empire being founded from the other end.

2. **"the mountain of silver at Potosí ... a rotating draft inherited and twisted from the old Inca empire"** — the claim that the mita was inherited from the Inca is exactly right and is the same institution inca-empire.md Ch 8 describes ("the repartimiento system reorganizing the mit'a labor draft for Spanish silver mining at Potosí ... worked almost entirely by mit'a-conscripted indigenous laborers") — the new TL should note that the Inca side of this labor system (and the figure that Potosí produced ~60% of the world's silver, 1545–1825) is told in inca-empire.md, since the reader is being told a pre-Columbian institution survived without being shown where it came from.

3. **"a Quechua leader named José Gabriel Condorcanqui — who took the name Túpac Amaru II ... claiming descent from the last Inca emperor"** — Túpac Amaru II is given here as a colonial spark with no explanation of who Túpac Amaru I was; inca-empire.md Ch 8 covers BOTH (Túpac Amaru I, the last Sapa Inca, beheaded in Cusco 1572, and his descendant Túpac Amaru II, executed in the same plaza 1781 "209 years earlier"). The new TL should add a terse pointer — the name reaches back to the last independent Inca ruler — to anchor the "descent from the last Inca emperor" claim and connect the 1780 revolt to the inca narrative's own coverage of it.

4. **"the European Enlightenment ... gave colonial intellectuals a vocabulary for their grievances"** — the Enlightenment-ideas-crossing-the-Atlantic theme is presented as if Spanish America were the receiving end only; early-american-republic.md repeatedly frames the same Enlightenment vocabulary (natural rights, consent of the governed) driving the U.S. founding, and the new TL's own Ch 4 notes Miranda "had fought in the American Revolution." A "meanwhile" note that the same smuggled books had just produced an independent republic to the north would temporally anchor the creoles' inspiration.

5. **"inspired directly by the recent American Revolution, to break Brazil away from Portugal and found a republic"** (Inconfidência Mineira, 1789) — the American Revolution is named as a direct inspiration without any pointer to early-american-republic.md, which covers that revolution's success in full; a brief cross-reference would let the reader follow the model the Minas conspirators were copying.

### Chapter 2 — Haiti Lights the Fuse

6. **"It produced something like a third of the world's sugar and more than half its coffee"** — this entire chapter retells the Haitian Revolution, which is the subject of a dedicated reference chapter (atlantic-slave-trade.md Ch 8, "Haiti: The Revolution That Couldn't Happen"). The new TL covers Bois Caïman implicitly (the 1791 rising) but omits the Vodou ceremony, Boukman, and especially the post-independence French **indemnity** that atlantic-slave-trade.md Ch 8 stresses ("an enormous indemnity ... a crushing debt it spent generations paying off"). At minimum the new TL should cross-reference the slave-trade narrative's fuller Haiti chapter, since the two now overlap heavily.

7. **"it was cheaper to work an enslaved person to death within a few years and buy a replacement off a slave ship ... This was not the relatively self-reproducing slavery of the North American mainland"** — the new TL makes exactly the demographic contrast that atlantic-slave-trade.md Ch 11 and early-american-republic.md Ch 13 both make (the U.S. enslaved population "grew through births" while sugar zones "had to be constantly replenished"). The comparison is correct but uncited; a pointer to the U.S./mainland side would sharpen a contrast the new TL is already drawing.

8. **"the loss of Saint-Domingue helped convince him to give up on the Americas entirely and sell the enormous Louisiana Territory to the young United States in 1803"** — this is the exact causal link early-american-republic.md Ch 12 makes from the other side ("Napoleon had just lost a brutal war in the Caribbean colony of Saint-Domingue ... With his richest colony gone ... Napoleon abruptly offered to sell the entire Louisiana territory"). Both narratives independently assert the Haiti→Louisiana causation; the new TL should cross-reference the U.S. narrative so the reader can follow the consequence north.

9. **"Haiti was the first independent nation in Latin America ... the second independent republic in the Western Hemisphere, after the United States"** — a "first/second" claim made twice (here and again in Ch 7's recap). atlantic-slave-trade.md Ch 8 phrases it identically ("the second independent nation in the Americas, after the United States — and the first and only one ever created by a victorious revolt of the enslaved"). The claim is consistent across TLs but should carry a cross-reference, since "first independent nation" claims are exactly the class this audit flags for comparison.

### Chapter 3 — The Empire Decapitates Itself

10. **"the entire Portuguese court ... fled across the Atlantic to their colony of Brazil"** — the flight of the court is the hinge fact, but the new TL doesn't note that age-of-exploration.md established Portugal's distinct Atlantic-empire model (Ch 6 "A Line Drawn Around the World," the Tordesillas division that gave Portugal Brazil). A one-line pointer to why Brazil was Portuguese in the first place would close the loop for a reader who hasn't read the exploration narrative.

### Chapter 5 — Bolívar's Northern Campaign

11. **"the famous letters of Bolívar"** / **"with leaders like Simón Bolívar citing the United States as the proof that a colony could become a self-governing republic"** — the new TL never notes that the United States loomed as the explicit model AND the cautionary counter-model in Bolívar's own thought; early-american-republic.md Ch 17 states Latin American independence was "much of it explicitly inspired by the American and French revolutions, with leaders like Simón Bolívar citing the United States." The Jamaica Letter passage here (Bolívar arguing Spanish America was "not the disciplined citizenry of the United States") is the perfect spot for a cross-reference to the U.S. founding narrative.

12. **"the British Legion, thousands of veteran soldiers thrown out of work by the end of the Napoleonic Wars in Europe"** — the Napoleonic Wars are invoked as the supply of mercenaries without a pointer; early-american-republic.md Ch 15 defines and dates them ("the roughly 1803–1815 continent-spanning wars"). Minor, but the new TL leans on the Napoleonic Wars repeatedly (Chs 2, 3, 4, 5) and never anchors them to a narrative that covers them.

### Chapter 7 — The Final Reckoning

13. **"the Spanish empire on the continent of South America — which had begun with the conquest of the Inca three centuries before, almost in these same mountains — was finished"** — this is a deliberate full-circle line (Ayacucho near where the Inca fell) and it is precisely the moment to cross-reference inca-empire.md Ch 8, which ends the Inca state at Vilcabamba/Cusco in the same highlands. The symmetry the new TL is reaching for (empire begins with Cajamarca, ends at Ayacucho) is much stronger if it points the reader at the conquest narrative.

14. **"Cuba and Puerto Rico (and even those it would lose by the end of the century — Chapter 11)"** — fine internally, but the eventual loser is the United States, whose rise the new TL treats as the closing theme; no forward issue here beyond confirming the Ch 11 handling (see finding 17).

### Chapter 8 — Mexico's Two-Stage Revolution

15. **"the loss of half the national territory to the United States ... like Antonio López de Santa Anna"** — Santa Anna and the U.S. seizure of half of Mexico are named in a single forward-looking clause; antebellum-america.md Chs 11–12 cover this in full (Texas, the Alamo, Santa Anna, the Mexican War, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, "roughly half of Mexico's national territory"). This is the single richest missing cross-reference in the new TL: the post-independence catastrophe that befell Mexico is a whole two-chapter arc in a completed reference TL, and the new TL gestures at it in half a sentence. A pointer to antebellum-america.md is strongly warranted.
