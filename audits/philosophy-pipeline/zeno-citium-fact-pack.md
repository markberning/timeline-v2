## THE SOURCE PROBLEM — READ THIS FIRST (load-bearing)

Zeno of Citium wrote prolifically. None of it survives. Every claim in this fact pack — biographical, doctrinal, anecdotal — reaches us via:

1. **Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Eminent Philosophers* Book VII** (3rd century AD, ~500–600 years after Zeno's death). DL is THE primary source for nearly everything biographical and much that is doctrinal. He is one person, writing centuries later, quoting and summarizing a lost tradition. He is not reliable the way a contemporary document is. Every DL-sourced claim is tagged **DL-TRADITION** below.

2. **Stobaeus** (5th century AD, *Eclogae*) — doxographic anthology; used for the telos formula and ethics. Even further from Zeno than DL.

3. **Cicero** (*Academica*, *De Finibus*, *Tusculan Disputations*) — 1st century BC, ~200 years after Zeno. A near-contemporary of the school's later development, but still secondhand on Zeno himself. His fist-demonstration passage is the best single piece of "Zeno in action" we have (though Cicero is reconstructing what he was told Zeno did).

4. **Sextus Empiricus** (2nd–3rd century AD) — hostile source (Skeptic), preserves doctrinal material via refutation.

5. **Plutarch, Clement of Alexandria, and other later writers** — preserve fragments and critiques of the *Republic* especially.

**Practical rule for the author:** treat virtually everything biographical about Zeno as DL-tradition at best. Treat the school's *doctrines* as recoverable at the paraphrase level via multiple later sources (DL, Stobaeus, Cicero, Sextus all converge on the core positions). No verbatim quotation from Zeno ships as a direct quote in quotation marks unless verified against an identified original — and since all originals are lost, this means nothing ships in quote-marks as "Zeno said." The doctrines ship as reports: "the Stoics held," "Zeno is reported to have argued," "by the later tradition."

**This is content, not a disclaimer.** The silence IS the story. Contrast: Plato's *Republic* sits in every great library on earth. Zeno's *Republic* is gone. We know Stoicism as mediated through Cleanthes, Chrysippus, Cicero, Epictetus, and Marcus Aurelius — none of whom are Zeno. The thinker read must hold that seam visible throughout.

### The founding legend: shipwreck + bookshop (DL VII.2) — LATE-LEGEND (DL)

**Tell as the founding legend it is, not as fact.** By Diogenes Laertius' account: Zeno was shipwrecked "on a voyage from Phoenicia to Peiraeus with a cargo of purple." Having landed in Athens at roughly age thirty, he wandered into a bookseller's shop, picked up Xenophon's *Memorabilia* (the book about Socrates), began reading, and was captivated. He asked the bookseller where he could find men like the Socrates in the book. The bookseller pointed to the Cynic philosopher Crates, who happened to pass by at that moment. "Follow yonder man," the bookseller said. Zeno did.

DL himself gives a competing version: "some say he did not come to Athens as a result of the shipwreck at all, but that he sold all his cargo at Athens first, and then turned to philosophy." The wording in Hicks' translation (attalus.org, DL VII.2 — **VERIFIED**): "He was shipwrecked on a voyage from Phoenicia to Peiraeus with a cargo of purple" / "He went into a bookseller's shop and began to read the second book of Xenophon's Memorabilia… he asked where men like Socrates were to be found. Just then Crates passed by, and the bookseller pointed to him and said, 'Follow yonder man.'"

**Framing rule:** This is the school's own founding myth, preserved by a writer six centuries later. It has the shape of a founding legend (the journey, the wreck, the chance encounter with the book, the fateful question). The author must frame it as "by the tradition Diogenes Laertius preserves" or "as the story goes." Do not state flat.

## 2. Teachers (after Crates)

All via DL VII.2, tagged DL-TRADITION throughout:

1. **Crates of Thebes** (Cynic) — the first, the one from the bookshop legend. Crates was himself a student of Diogenes of Sinope; the Cynic line runs Antisthenes → Diogenes → Crates → Zeno.
2. **Stilpo of Megara** (Megarian school — logic + dialectic)
3. **Xenocrates** (Platonist — head of Plato's Academy after Plato's death)
4. **Polemo** (also Platonist — Xenocrates' successor as head of the Academy)
5. **Diodorus Cronus** (Megarian logician — for the study of dialectic)

The consistent picture: Zeno spent ~20 years studying under multiple schools before founding his own. He inherited the Cynics' ethical simplicity (virtue as the only good), the Megarians' logic, and engaged seriously with the Academy. When he opened his school at the Stoa Poikile (~300 BC, conventional date), he was synthesizing, not revolting.

**Lineage note for the data spine:** The greeks-fact-pack spine (item 12) gives `Socrates → Antisthenes → Diogenes (Cynics) → Crates → Zeno of Citium (Stoa)` — consistent. Add the Heraclitean physics inheritance separately (the logos/fire cosmology).

- *The Republic* (the most famous — see §5 below)
- *On Life According to Nature*
- *On Impulse, or Human Nature*
- *On Passions*
- *On Duty*
- *On Law*

**Physical:** *On Sight*, *On the Universe*, *On Signs*

**Logical:** *General Things*, *Homeric Problems*, *On the Verbal Study of Poetry*

**Other:** *On Being*, *On Nature*, *A Course of Exercises*

DL lists approximately 22 works. Every single one is lost. We have titles, short summaries, and paraphrases-via-hostile-or-sympathetic-later-writers. Nothing more.

## 4. The Three Parts of Philosophy (Logic, Physics, Ethics) — and the analogies

**Source:** DL VII.39–40, Hicks translation. **VERIFIED** via Wikisource and Perseus.

The Stoics (this is attributed to the school generally; whether it originated with Zeno himself or was formalized by Chrysippus is contested — DL attributes the schema to the school) divided philosophy into three parts: **logic, physics, and ethics**. DL records three analogies for how they fit together:

**The animal analogy (DL VII.40, Hicks):** "Logic corresponding to the bones and sinews, Ethics to the fleshy parts, Physics to the soul."

**The egg analogy (DL VII.40, Hicks):** "the shell is Logic, next comes the white, Ethics, and the yolk in the centre is Physics."

**The fertile field analogy (DL VII.40, Hicks):** "Logic being the encircling fence, Ethics the crop, Physics the soil or the trees."

Note: the SEP (section 2) cites the "fertile field" analogy as *orchard/wall/fruit/land or trees*; the DL Hicks translation gives "encircling fence / crop / soil or trees." These are the same image; small translation variation.

**Tag:** DL-TRADITION; attributed to the school collectively. Do not put as Zeno's personal formulation — he may well have introduced it, but the evidence that the analogies are specifically his vs. the school's general teaching is not clean.

**Framing for the thinker read:** The three-part structure is foundational to how Stoicism presents itself. The author can say "the school Zeno founded held that philosophy divides into three parts" with the DL tradition tag.

## 5. Key Doctrines — paraphrase-only, sourced

### 5a. The telos formula: "living in agreement" / "in agreement with nature"

**This is one of the most carefully documented attribution questions in the whole school.**

- **Zeno's original version** (per the tradition recovered via Stobaeus 63B and DL VII.87): the telos (goal/end) is "living in agreement" — *homologoumenōs* — sometimes rendered "living consistently." The SEP (§2 "Ethics") states: "It appears that Zeno's original articulation of the principle was 'live consistently' (*homologoumenōs*), to which Cleanthes added the clarifying clause 'with nature.'" Sources: Stobaeus 63B (for Cleanthes' version: "living in agreement with nature, *homologoumenōs tē physei*") and DL VII.87 (for the school's composite formula).

- **DL VII.87, Hicks translation** (VERIFIED via attalus.org): Zeno "designate[d] as the end 'life in agreement with nature,' which is the same as a virtuous life."

- **Cleanthes' addition:** Cleanthes, Zeno's first successor, is credited in the tradition with adding *tē physei* ("with nature") to yield the complete formula "living in agreement with nature." The IEP (stoicism entry) and SEP both make this attribution. Stobaeus 63B is the cited ancient source for the fuller version.

**The "nature" in "according to nature" is doing heavy lifting and must be explained in the read.** It means: (a) human nature — the rational animal living according to its own rational nature; (b) Nature as cosmic rational order (the logos, Heraclitean inheritance) — what the universe actually is. Living "according to nature" is not living simply or outdoors; it is aligning your judgments and will with what reason (both yours and the universal kind) actually demands.

**Paraphrase-only in the thinker read.** No quotation marks around the telos formula as Zeno's words — it is the school's reported position.

### 5b. Virtue as the only good; adiaphora (indifferents)

**Source:** DL VII.94ff (Hicks); SEP §2 "Ethics"; IEP "Stoicism."

The Stoics held that **virtue** (*aretē*) is the only true good, and **vice** the only true evil. Everything else — health, wealth, reputation, pleasure, even life itself — is *adiaphora* (indifferent): not good, not evil, neither here nor there for making a life genuinely good or bad.

The school then subdivided indifferents: some are *proēgmena* (preferred) — health, wealth — and some *apoproēgmena* (dispreferred) — sickness, poverty. The sage pursues preferred indifferents when reason allows, but does not treat getting or losing them as gains or losses that affect his fundamental wellbeing. Only the condition of his soul — his virtue — does that.

**Plain restatement for the read:** It's not that wealth doesn't matter at all; it's that it doesn't matter the way people think it does. Losing your health is bad — the kind of bad you should try to avoid — but it doesn't make you a worse person. Only poor character does that. The Stoic who goes to the doctor is acting rationally; the Stoic who falls apart when ill has confused a dispreferred indifferent for an actual evil.

**Tag:** Doctrinal consensus across DL + Stobaeus + Cicero. Paraphrase-only; do not quote-mark.

### 5c. The kataleptic impression — and Zeno's clenched-fist demonstration

**Source:** Cicero, *Academica* II.144, Rackham translation (Harvard University Press, 1967). **VERIFIED via Wikipedia's Katalepsis article** and the search-result Latin original.

This is the best single piece of "Zeno in action" we have, and it comes from Cicero — roughly 200 years after Zeno, writing in a philosophical dialogue — but the passage is widely cited and the Latin original is preserved:

**Cicero's account of Zeno's demonstration (paraphrase of Rackham, Acad. II.144):**  
Zeno would hold out his hand with fingers spread wide and say: "A visual appearance (*visum*, Greek *phantasia*) is like this." Then he would close his fingers a little: "An act of assent (*adsensus*) is like this." Then he would press his fingers together completely and make a fist: "This is comprehension (*comprensio*, Greek *katalēpsis*)." And this is why, Cicero says, Zeno coined the Greek term *katalēpsis* for it — from *katalambanein*, to grasp. Then finally, Zeno would bring his other hand over and clench the fist tight: "This is knowledge (*epistēmē*) — and only the wise man possesses it."

**Latin original (from the search-verified passage):**  
"nam cum extensis digitis adversam manum ostenderat, 'visum' inquiebat 'huius modi est'; dein cum paulum digitos contraxerat, 'adsensus huius modi'; tum cum plane compresserat pugnumque fecerat, comprensionem illam esse dicebat (qua ex similitudine etiam nomen ei rei, quod ante non fuerat, κατάληψιν imposuit)."

**Source URL:** Cicero, *Academica* II.144. The best accessible online text is via Perseus or the LacusCurtius Latin library; the Wikipedia "Katalepsis" article cites Rackham's Loeb Classical Library translation (Harvard UP, 1967). For a sourced English text: search Perseus for *Academica* — the relevant section is Book II (sometimes called *Lucullus*), around §144.

**The doctrine itself:** The Stoics made three grades between impression and knowledge. (1) *Phantasia* (appearance, impression): the mind receives an image of something. (2) *Synkatathesis* (assent): the mind affirms the impression. (3) *Katalēpsis* (cognitive / graspable impression): an impression that is clear, distinct, and arising from a real object — the kind that can't be false. Zeno defined it as an impression that "could not have come from something which is not the case." This is the criterion of truth for the Stoics. (4) *Epistēmē* (knowledge): systematic, organized kataleptic impressions — what the sage has, by definition.

**Why this matters:** The Academic Skeptics (Arcesilaus, then Carneades) targeted this criterion relentlessly: how do you ever know your impression *couldn't* be false? Their attack on the kataleptic impression is the central debate of Hellenistic epistemology. Zeno's fist is the pivot.

**Framing rule:** "Cicero reports that Zeno demonstrated his epistemology with his hand." Never state this as a direct Zeno quote. It is Cicero reconstructing a Zeno performance, in a dialogue, two centuries later.

### 5d. The passions as errors of judgment

**Source:** SEP §2 "Emotions" / IEP "Stoicism" / DL VII.110ff (Hicks). Consensus across multiple ancient sources.

The Stoics held that the emotions (*pathē*, passions) are not impulses over which we have no control — they are judgments. Specifically, each passion involves a false belief about the value of something:

- **Distress (*lupē*):** the false judgment that something present is a genuine evil (when it is only a dispreferred indifferent).
- **Fear (*phobos*):** the false anticipatory judgment that something future is a genuine evil.
- **Desire/Craving (*epithumia*):** the false judgment that something absent is a genuine good.
- **Pleasure (*hēdonē*):** the false judgment that something present is a genuine good.

The corrective is not suppression — it is *cognitive correction*. If you stop falsely judging sickness as a genuine evil (rather than a dispreferred indifferent), the distress loses its footing. The Stoic does not become numb; they become *un-deceived*. (This is why the greeks era narrative, ch6, uses Epictetus' "men are disturbed not by things but by the opinions they form about things" as the summary: it is a later formulation of Zeno's doctrine.)

The Stoic school also recognized "good emotions" (*eupatheiai*) — joy, caution, willing — which are rational responses available to the sage who has corrected his false valuations.

**Tag:** Doctrinal consensus. Paraphrase-only.

## 6. Zeno's *Republic* — honest framing required

**Sources:** DL VII.32–33 (Hicks) and §129–131 (for community of wives); Plutarch and Clement of Alexandria (preserved fragments via polemic); Cassius the Skeptic (hostile critique preserved via DL). Wikipedia, HandWiki "Republic (Zeno)" — cross-checked 2026-06-13.

Zeno's *Republic* was his most famous work and probably his earliest. It is entirely lost. What we know comes through:
- DL's own brief summary
- Critics (Cassius the Skeptic mounted the most detailed ancient polemic; his objections are preserved by DL and preserve content by negation)
- Later Stoics who were embarrassed by it

**Key reported contents (all DL-TRADITION, mostly hostile-source-mediated):**

1. **Community of wives** — Zeno reportedly "laid down community of wives, with free choice of partners," recognizing no marriage beyond voluntary union. DL VII.131. **DL-TRADITION.**
2. **No temples, law-courts, or gymnasia** — "he prohibits the building of temples, law-courts and gymnasia in cities." DL VII.33. **DL-TRADITION.**
3. **No need for money** — "we should not think it need be introduced either for purposes of exchange or for travelling abroad." DL VII.33. **DL-TRADITION.**
4. **Citizenship limited to the virtuous** — only the wise/virtuous should be counted citizens of the ideal city. Cassius' polemic via DL. **DL-TRADITION.**
5. **Written consciously in response to / opposition to Plato's Republic.** This is the scholarly consensus view; the text itself is lost so we cannot confirm intent from the source.

**Critical framing (REQUIRED):** The later Stoic school was embarrassed by the *Republic*. By the time of Chrysippus, there was a tradition claiming Zeno wrote it when he was "young and thoughtless" while still under heavy Cynic influence. Whether that apologia was accurate or retroactive damage-control is unknowable. The *Republic* belongs to Zeno's early, Cynic-adjacent period — before he had fully synthesized his own system. The thinker read must not let the *Republic* absorb Zeno's mature philosophy, nor should it be written out; both moves distort.

**The "utopian" label:** apply cautiously. The work describes an ideal community of sages governed entirely by virtue — less a political program than a thought-experiment about what a world of perfectly rational beings would look like. Its critics (then and now) point out that a city with no courts assumes everyone is already just, which is question-begging. Frame the *Republic* as a revealing early document showing Zeno thinking at the intersection of Cynic simplicity and Stoic virtue-ethics — interesting for what it shows about the school's roots, not a blueprint.

## 7. The school's evolution: Cleanthes and Chrysippus

### Cleanthes (c. 330–c. 230 BC)

Second head of the Stoa; Zeno's direct successor. Poetically inclined — the *Hymn to Zeus* survives, one of the very few Stoic texts we have in full (it is his, not Zeno's). Added "with nature" to the telos formula. A faithful transmitter rather than an innovator.

### Chrysippus (c. 279–c. 206 BC)

Third head of the Stoa; its great systematizer. The ancient saying: **"If Chrysippus had not existed, there would have been no Stoa"** — Greek: *ei mē gar ēn Chrysippos, ouk an ēn stoa* — source: **DL VII.183** (Hicks). **VERIFIED via Wikipedia "Chrysippus" article's footnote e.**

Chrysippus wrote over 700 works (almost all lost; known via DL's catalogue). He built the school a **propositional logic** (more sophisticated than Aristotle's syllogistic for complex conditionals), a worked-out physics grounding the ethics, and a detailed account of the passions. When we say "Stoic philosophy," we mean Chrysippean Stoicism as much as Zenonian Stoicism. The school's great advantage over its Platonic and Aristotelian competitors was its integration: the same cosmology that says the universe is rational logos also explains why living rationally is natural, which explains why virtue is the good. Chrysippus built the integrated architecture. Zeno founded, Chrysippus built.

**Thinker-read framing rule:** Do not let Zeno absorb Chrysippus' sophisticated logic-system or the fully elaborated four-passion taxonomy or the cylinder analogy for fate — those are Chrysippean developments. Zeno gave the school its ethical core and its name. The systematization is Chrysippus'.

## 8. Disambiguation — MUST include prominently

**Zeno of Citium** (c. 334–262 BC, Cyprus, founder of Stoicism) is a COMPLETELY DIFFERENT PERSON from:

**Zeno of Elea** (c. 490–430 BC, Elea in southern Italy, the paradoxes man — Achilles and the tortoise, the arrow). Zeno of Elea was a student of Parmenides and lived roughly 150 years before Zeno of Citium. The two are chronologically, geographically, and philosophically unrelated.

## 9. Lineage — consistent with greeks era read and data spine

### Answered (what Zeno inherited)

- **The Cynics** (Crates → Diogenes of Sinope → Antisthenes → Socrates): the ethical core — virtue as the only good; wealth and conventional success as irrelevant to a good life; the sharp division between what is "up to us" and what is not. Zeno inherited all of this. He softened the Cynics' contempt for convention into a doctrine of "preferred indifferents."
- **Heraclitus**: the *logos* — the rational principle pervading the universe; the idea that fire/energy is the underlying reality; the unity of opposites. Stoic physics (cosmic *logos*, *pneuma*, the universe as rational) is Heraclitean.
- **Megarian logic** (Stilpo, Diodorus Cronus): propositional logic, the "master argument," conditional reasoning. Chrysippus built on this.
- **Platonic/Academic influence** (Xenocrates, Polemo): engagement with the theory of knowledge; the categories debate; ethical teleology.

### Led to (what Zeno's school generated)

- **Cleanthes** → **Chrysippus** (built the system)
- **The Roman Stoa**: Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius — all explicitly Chrysippean-Zenonian. The *Meditations* exist because Zeno founded a school in a colonnade in Athens.
- **The Academic Skeptics**: Arcesilaus (Zeno's contemporary) targeted the kataleptic impression directly — the Stoic-Skeptic debate is among the era's most productive philosophical arguments.
- **Christian moral philosophy**: several Stoic doctrines (universal reason/logos, the cosmopolitan brotherhood of rational beings, the examination of the passions) were consciously appropriated by early Christian writers.

**Data spine edge:** `socrates → zeno-citium` runs through the Cynic line: Socrates → Antisthenes → Diogenes → Crates → Zeno. This is consistent with greeks-fact-pack item 12.

## 12. "Why this is a break" block material

**BEFORE:** The Cynics (Diogenes, Crates) had the ethical kernel right — virtue matters, wealth and reputation don't — but they expressed it as provocation and contempt: live in a jar, reject all convention, mock the king in public. It was a lifestyle demonstration, not a philosophy. It had no logic, no physics, no account of knowledge, no way to engage with Aristotle or the Academy on their own ground. And it was essentially individualist — each Cynic was their own project.

**AFTER:** Zeno took the Cynic kernel and built a complete, integrated, *arguable* system. The same claim — virtue is the only good — now stood inside a cosmology (the universe is governed by rational *logos*; human reason is a piece of that logos; living rationally is therefore living in accordance with what the universe actually is), an epistemology (the kataleptic impression as the criterion of truth), and a logic (inherited from the Megarians, systematized by Chrysippus) that could hold its own in debates with the Academy and the Skeptics. Stoicism could be *argued* as well as lived. That is the break: from countercultural lifestyle philosophy to complete, integrated, *systematic* philosophical tradition — the most influential single tradition in the ancient world after Plato and Aristotle.

**The concrete move:** Zeno's founding claim — that virtue is *sufficient* for happiness, that nothing outside the self's rational agency can make or break a good life — is not a Cynic boast but a philosophical argument. It requires a theory of the good (what makes something genuinely valuable), a theory of knowledge (how the sage knows what is truly good), and a cosmology (why rational virtue tracks what the universe actually demands). Without those three, the claim is a slogan. With them, it becomes Stoicism.

## 14. Apocryphal quotes to exclude (blacklist)

Philosophy's DL-tradition problem is acute here. The following circulate widely and ship as paraphrase-only or not at all:

1. **"Man conquers the world by conquering himself."** — not verified in ancient sources; widely misattributed to Zeno of Citium online. Do not use.
2. **"We have two ears and one mouth, so we should listen more than we say."** — this is attributed to Epictetus in some sources, but also floats to Zeno. Neither attribution is verified; Diogenes Laertius (VI.78 area) gives a variant to Diogenes the Cynic ("nature gave us two ears and one tongue, to the end that we should hear more and talk less") — but this is DL on Diogenes the Cynic, not Zeno. Exclude or paraphrase with the DL-Diogenes attribution.
3. **Any quotation of Zeno in quote-marks.** Everything is lost. Use paraphrase with "the school held" / "Zeno is reported to have argued" / "by Diogenes Laertius' account."
4. **The "all things happen according to fate/providence/logos" formulation as a direct Zeno quote.** This is Stoic doctrine; it may originate with Zeno, but is transmitted via Chrysippus' systematization. Do not pin to Zeno personally.

## 15. Potted-summary traps to avoid

1. **"Stoic = emotionless stoic."** The lowercase modern meaning has hijacked the philosophy. Stoics are not numb; they have a rich theory of emotions (including "good emotions" the sage *should* have). The greeks era read already does the rescue ("a gritted-teeth statue who feels nothing" identified as the caricature). The thinker read deepens it.
2. **"Zeno = the full Stoic system."** The mature, systematized, defended Stoicism is Chrysippus'. Zeno founded; Chrysippus built. Don't let Zeno absorb the full school's later doctrines anachronistically (the passions taxonomy, the logic, the fate/compatibilism debates — all Chrysippean).
3. **"Citium is a Greek city."** It was a Phoenician-Greek city. The Phoenician heritage is part of the story and should not be erased in the telling.
4. **"The Republic was Zeno's mature position."** It was likely his earliest major work, written under heavy Cynic influence. The later school distanced itself from it. Date it and frame it correctly.
5. **"Without Chrysippus there would be no Stoa" as Zeno's own assessment.** This is a later saying reported by DL (VII.183). It is a report about Chrysippus, not a quote from Zeno about anything.
