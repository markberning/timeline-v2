## 1. Identity, dates, place

- **Name / place:** Parmenides of Elea (Greek *Elea*, Latin *Velia*), a Greek colony on the
  Tyrrhenian coast of southern Italy (Cilento, Campania). Founder of the **Eleatic school**.
  ✅ CONFIRMED (SEP, IEP, Wikipedia).
- **Dates:** **c. 515 – c. 450 BC** (the registry figure, `src/lib/philosophy-data.ts`).
  ⚠️ TWO COMPETING DATINGS — must be framed honestly, not asserted flat:
  - **Platonic dating → c. 515 BC.** Plato's *Parmenides* stages a meeting at which Parmenides is
    "about sixty-five years old" and Socrates "a very young man"; back-calculating from Socrates'
    age gives a birth ~515 BC and a meeting ~450 BC. Whether that meeting is historical or a
    Platonic literary device is itself debated. ✅ (Plato *Parmenides*, Jowett; SEP, IEP.)
  - **Apollodorus / Diogenes Laertius dating → c. 540 BC** (floruit / *acme* synchronized with the
    69th Olympiad, 504–501 BC; DL IX.23). ~25 years earlier. 🟡 the standard doxographic formula
    (acme = age 40 at a public event) — flag as the late-tradition convention it is.
  - House move: lead with the Platonic c.515 (matches the registry and the Plato-schools-Socrates
    story the read uses), name the c.540 alternative once, in one clause, and SAY the basis of each.
- **Death:** no reliable account survives. Do NOT assert a death scene. ✅ (absence confirmed.)

## 2. The single work — the poem *On Nature*

- Parmenides wrote ONE work: a single philosophical poem in **dactylic hexameter** (the meter of
  Homer and Hesiod), conventionally titled ***On Nature*** (*Peri Physeōs*) — though the title is a
  later convention applied to many pre-Socratics, not necessarily his own. ✅ CONFIRMED (SEP, IEP).
- It survives only in **fragments — roughly 150–160 lines** — quoted by later writers, above all
  the 6th-century-AD Neoplatonist commentator **Simplicius** (who quotes the longest stretches,
  including most of B8, precisely because by his day the poem was already rare). Sextus Empiricus
  and others preserve other pieces. ✅ CONFIRMED (SEP "the sources," IEP). Frame Simplicius as the
  reason we have as much as we do.
- Structure, as the goddess lays it out: a **proem** (the chariot ride, B1), then **two roads** —
  the **Way of Truth** (*alētheia*) and the **Way of Opinion / Seeming** (*doxa*). ✅ CONFIRMED.
- ⚠️ INTERPRETIVE DISPUTE (must be flagged, do not resolve): **what the Way of Opinion is FOR is
  genuinely contested.** Parmenides spends a large part of the poem giving a detailed cosmology
  (fire/light and night, the heavenly bodies) on the Opinion road — yet the goddess calls it
  deceptive and untrue. Scholars dispute whether it is (a) a best-possible-but-still-false account
  of the world of seeming, (b) a dialectical exercise, (c) the views of rival thinkers, or (d)
  something with real positive value. SAY it is debated; do not pick a winner. ✅ (SEP, IEP both
  flag this as unsettled.)

### B1 — the proem (Burnet fr. 1)  ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM
> "The car that bears me carried me as far as ever my heart desired, when it had brought me and set
> me on the renowned way of the goddess…"
> "…the daughters of the Sun, hasting to convey me into the light, threw back their veils from off
> their faces…"
> "There are the gates of the ways of Night and Day, fitted above with a lintel and below with a
> threshold of stone."
> The goddess's welcome: "Welcome, O youth, that comest to my abode on the car that bears thee
> tended by immortal charioteers!" She promises to teach him "the unshaken heart of well-rounded
> truth, as well as the opinions of mortals in which is no true belief at all."
- Use any of these verbatim, in quotation marks, attributed **DK 28 B1, trans. Burnet**. The
  read may also paraphrase the narrative arc (chariot → daughters of the Sun → gate of Night and
  Day → goddess) in house voice.

### B2 — the two ways (Burnet fr. 4, 5)  ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM
> "Come now, I will tell thee—and do thou hearken to my saying and carry it away—the only two ways
> of search that can be thought of. The first, namely, that *It is*, and that it is impossible for
> it not to be, is the way of belief, for truth is its companion. The other, namely, that *It is
> not*…"
- Attribute **DK 28 B2, trans. Burnet**. (Burnet prints the em-dashes; they are INSIDE the quote
  and stay.) This is the Way of Truth ("that it is, and that it cannot not be") vs the rejected way.

### B3 — thinking and being  🟡 USE THE BURNET LINE, NOT THE FAMOUS SLOGAN
- The famous rendering "to think and to be are the same" / "thinking and being are the same" is a
  DISPUTED, heavily-interpreted translation of B3 (`τὸ γὰρ αὐτὸ νοεῖν ἐστίν τε καὶ εἶναι`). It is
  one of the most contested lines in Greek philosophy and is NOT safe to quote as a flat fact.
- **House move:** do NOT quote "to think and to be are the same" as a verbatim quotation. Instead
  paraphrase carefully — Parmenides ties what *can be thought* to what *can be* — and lean on the
  verbatim B6 below, which makes the same move in Burnet's PD wording and is unambiguous.
  **PARAPHRASE ONLY for the B3 slogan.**

### B6 — what can be spoken and thought, is (Burnet fr. 6)  ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM
> "It needs must be that what can be spoken and thought is; for it is possible for it to be, and it
> is not possible for what is nothing to be."
- Attribute **DK 28 B6, trans. Burnet**. This is the load-bearing quote for "what is not cannot be
  thought or spoken" — use this one, not the contested B3 slogan.

### B7 — restrain thought from the way of "is not" (Burnet fr. 7)  ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM
> "For this shall never be proved, that the things that are not are; and do thou restrain thy
> thought from this way of inquiry."
- Attribute **DK 28 B7, trans. Burnet**.

### B8 — the signs of what-is (Burnet fr. 8)  ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM
> "One path only is left for us to speak of, namely, that *It is*. In this path are very many tokens
> that what is is uncreated and indestructible; for it is complete, immovable, and without end. Nor
> was it ever, nor will it be; for now *it is*, all at once, a continuous one."
> "Nor is it divisible, since it is all alike, and there is no more of it in one place than in
> another, to hinder it from holding together…"
> "Moreover, it is immovable in the bonds of mighty chains, without beginning and without end… It
> is the same, and it rests in the self-same place, abiding in itself."
- Attribute **DK 28 B8, trans. Burnet**. This is the core argument: what-is is **uncreated,
  indestructible, whole/complete, one, continuous, indivisible, unmoving, changeless.** All the
  "signs" the read enumerates come from here. The well-rounded-sphere simile is also in B8 (Burnet
  renders it as bounded "on every side"); SAFE to paraphrase the sphere image, attribute the
  bounded-on-every-side idea to B8.

## 4. Positions / claims (each tied to a source) — for the prose, not as quotes

- **Reason over the senses.** The core "break": pure deductive reason, pushed to its conclusion,
  says that what truly IS must be one, eternal, ungenerated, indivisible, unchanging — and therefore
  that the everyday world of change and plurality that the SENSES report is not the way things truly
  are. Parmenides trusts the argument over the eyes. ✅ CONFIRMED (SEP, IEP; B7 explicitly tells the
  hearer to restrain thought from the senses' "is not" road).
- **The birth of metaphysics / ontology.** Standard scholarly framing: Parmenides is the hinge at
  which the systematic study of *being as such* begins; later metaphysics is largely a set of
  responses to him. ⚠️ Frame as the widely-held scholarly judgment it is ("most historians treat…"),
  not as a flat "he invented metaphysics" first-claim (the philosophy first-ever trap). ✅ (SEP, IEP.)
- **Direct foil to Heraclitus.** Heraclitus (the flux/"everything flows" thinker, the Greeks era)
  makes change the fundamental truth; Parmenides makes changelessness the fundamental truth. They
  are the canonical opposed pair of the pre-Socratics. ✅ CONFIRMED — but ⚠️ NO ancient text records
  the two men debating or even reading each other; the "answer to Heraclitus" framing is a
  PHILOSOPHICAL opposition drawn by later readers, not a documented exchange. SAY so (do not write
  "Parmenides set out to refute Heraclitus"). 🟡 Some ancient sources read B6's "two-headed" mortals
  as aimed at Heraclitus, but this is interpretation, not fact — keep it hedged.

## 5. Zeno of Elea — the pupil and the paradoxes

- **Zeno of Elea** (c. 490 – c. 430 BC) was Parmenides' pupil and (per Plato's *Parmenides*) his
  much-younger companion. He defended Parmenides not by arguing FOR the One directly but by showing
  that the COMMON-SENSE alternative (that motion and plurality are real) leads to absurdity — the
  famous **paradoxes**. ✅ CONFIRMED (SEP "Zeno of Elea," Plato *Parmenides*).
- ⚠️ DISTINGUISH FROM ZENO OF CITIUM (the Stoic, ~3rd c. BC) — different man, the read should note
  it once, as the Socrates read does. (`zeno-citium` IS a separate thinker id in the registry.)
- The paradoxes (Achilles and the tortoise; the arrow; the dichotomy; the moving rows): ⚠️ our
  knowledge of them is **second-hand, chiefly through Aristotle's *Physics*** (who reports and
  refutes them) and later commentators. Zeno's own book survives only in fragments. The vivid
  "Achilles and the tortoise" labeling and framing is the later tradition's; Aristotle's own text
  speaks of "the slowest runner." 🟡 Frame the tortoise the way the Greeks era read already does
  (per this vertical's proof-run-1 catch): the named tortoise is the tradition, Aristotle says the
  slowest runner. ✅ (SEP "Zeno's Paradoxes"; the Greeks-era ledger.)
- The point for THIS read: the paradoxes are a DEFENSE of Parmenides — they make the world the
  senses report look incoherent, so the rational One looks less crazy by comparison. ✅ (SEP.)

## 6. The afterlife — later philosophy as a set of responses

- **The pluralists / atomists** (Empedocles, Anaxagoras, and especially Leucippus & Democritus)
  are standardly read as trying to SAVE the appearances of change while conceding Parmenides' core
  point that being can't come from non-being: the atomists' uncuttable atoms are each a little
  unchanging Parmenidean "what-is," and they introduced the VOID (a kind of "what-is-not") precisely
  to allow motion. ✅ CONFIRMED as the standard scholarly reading (SEP atomism; IEP). Frame as "the
  standard reading," lightly — keep it to the level the read needs.
- **Plato.** Two concrete, verifiable facts:
  1. Plato's dialogue ***Parmenides*** stages a meeting where the **elderly Parmenides (≈65) and
     Zeno question a very young Socrates**, exposing problems in the young Socrates' theory of Forms.
     ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM (Jowett, below). It is Plato's most respectful and most difficult
     handling of an earlier thinker.
  2. Plato's ***Sophist*** commits a "**parricide**" against "**father Parmenides**": to explain how
     false statements are even possible, the Eleatic Stranger argues that in some sense **not-being
     IS** — directly against Parmenides' ban on "what is not." ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM (Jowett, below).

### Plato *Parmenides* (Jowett) — the ages  ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM
> "…the former being at the time about sixty-five years old, aged but well-favoured—Zeno, who was
> said to have been beloved of Parmenides in the days of his youth, about forty, and very
> good-looking:—that they lodged with Pythodorus at the Ceramicus outside the wall, whither
> Socrates, then a very young man, came to see them"
- Source (HTTP-checked): `https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1687/pg1687.txt` (Plato, *Parmenides*,
  trans. Jowett, Project Gutenberg, PD). Use verbatim or paraphrase; attribute **Plato, *Parmenides*,
  trans. Jowett**.

### Plato *Sophist* (Jowett) — the parricide  ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM
> "Then I fear that I must lay hands on my father Parmenides; but do not call me a parricide; for
> there is no way out of the difficulty except to show that in some sense not-being is…"
- Source (HTTP-checked, string-matched in the file): `https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1735/1735-h/1735-h.htm`
  (Plato, *Sophist*, trans. Jowett, Project Gutenberg, PD). Attribute **Plato, *Sophist*, trans.
  Jowett** — spoken by the Eleatic Stranger. (Note: Burnet/Jowett both spell it the Stranger from
  Elea; "father Parmenides" is the Stranger's phrase.)

## 7. Biographical anecdotes — DL-sourced, ALL legend-framed 🟡

- DL IX (the *Lives*) reports Parmenides was wealthy/well-born; that he studied with the Pythagorean
  Ameinias; that he gave Elea laws. 🟡 LEGEND-FRAME-IT — all from Diogenes Laertius, ~700 years
  later. Use sparingly, framed as "the much-later biographer Diogenes Laertius reports…", or omit.
  Do NOT assert any of it flat. The read does not NEED these; the poem is the story.
- There is no reliable account of his appearance, his death, or his teaching beyond the poem and
  Plato's literary portrait. Do not invent any.
