## 1. Bibliographic facts (born-verified)

**Full title (English):** *Discourse on the Method of Rightly Conducting One's
Reason and of Seeking Truth in the Sciences* (`VERIFIED` — Wikipedia
"Discourse on the Method," fetched 2026-06-17; consistent with Gutenberg #59
title page).

**Full title (French, 1637 original):**
*Discours de la Méthode Pour bien conduire sa raison, et chercher la vérité
dans les sciences* (`VERIFIED` — Wikipedia; confirmed against the Wikimedia
Commons first-edition title page image, fetched below).

**Date:** Published mid-1637. (`DOCUMENTED` — EBSCO Research Starters history
entry; Wikipedia; multiple secondary sources.)

**Place:** Leiden (Netherlands). Publisher: **Jan Maire**. (`DOCUMENTED` —
Project Vox entry `pe05_discours_de_la_methode_pour_-descartes_rene_btv1b86069594`,
fetched 2026-06-17; Wikimedia Commons title-page metadata.)

**Language:** French. This was a deliberate break from the convention of publishing
natural philosophy in Latin. (`DOCUMENTED` — Wikipedia; EBSCO entry.)

**Anonymous publication:** Yes, published without Descartes' name on the title
page. (`DOCUMENTED` — EBSCO Research Starters; the thinker fact-pack §2.)

**Structure:** The *Discourse* was published NOT as a standalone text but as a
**preface to three scientific essays** demonstrating the method:
1. *La Dioptrique* (Dioptrics/Optics) — theory of light refraction, vision,
   lenses; the first scientific proof of the law of refraction.
2. *Les Météores* (Meteorology) — meteorological phenomena including the rainbow.
3. *La Géométrie* (Geometry) — the foundation of analytic geometry; Cartesian
   coordinate system. (`DOCUMENTED` — Wikipedia; EBSCO; thinker fact-pack §2.)

The Discourse is technically the "Preface" or "Introduction" to those three
essays, explaining the method by which the science was reached. This context
matters: the text is partly an autobiography, partly a methodological manifesto,
partly a pitch for a research program — NOT a systematic treatise in the
Scholastic manner.

**Why French:** Descartes chose French to reach a non-Latin-reading audience —
those who had not been through the university curriculum in Latin. The point is
made explicitly in Part VI of the *Discourse* (Veitch trans.): "I write in French,
which is the language of my country, in preference to Latin, which is that of my
preceptors, it is because I expect that those who make use of their unprejudiced
natural Reason will be better judges of my opinions than those who give heed to
the writings of the ancients only" (`VERIFIED` — Wikisource Discourse/Part_6,
fetched 2026-06-17).

**The "even women" phrase — CRITICAL LANDMINE:**
The phrase "les femmes mêmes pussent entendre quelque chose" ("even women could
understand something of it") does NOT appear in the text of the *Discourse*.
It is from **a letter Descartes wrote to Père Vatier dated 22 February 1638**,
written after the Discourse was published, in response to a Jesuit who had read
the essays. The source for this attribution is a page at jdarriulat.net (French
scholarship on the Discourse, fetched 2026-06-17) quoting: "un livre, écrit
Descartes dans une lettre au Père Vatier du 22 février 1638, où j'ai voulu que
les femmes même pussent entendre quelque chose."
**Ship rule:** If used, frame explicitly as "in a letter to Père Vatier in February
1638, Descartes wrote that he had wanted even women to be able to understand
something of it" — NOT as a statement from the Discourse itself.

**Latin translation:** A Latin translation of the *Discourse* was published
in Amsterdam in 1656. (`DOCUMENTED` — Wikipedia.) This is how it entered
the Latin scholarly literature; the original French was always the authoritative
text.

## 2. The six parts — content summaries (VERIFIED against Gutenberg #59 and Wikisource subpages)

### Part I — The author's education and dissatisfaction with received learning
Descartes opens with the claim that good sense (reason) is the most equally
distributed thing in the world — everyone thinks they have enough of it. He
reflects on his own education at La Flèche (the Jesuit college): he found
mathematics the only certain discipline; the rest — philosophy, theology,
oratory, poetry, history, law — either uncertain or dependent on custom.
He resolved to study "the great book of the world" (travel, observation, lived
experience) rather than inherited texts. The Part is self-consciously
autobiographical: Descartes frames what follows as his own story, not a
prescription for others.

### Part II — The four rules of the method (the methodological core)
During a winter's day alone in a heated room ("un poêle" — see §5), Descartes
decided to reform his opinions according to four principles, drawn from his
experience with mathematics. He compares beginning philosophy afresh to
rebuilding a city on a grid rather than adding to a mediaeval tangle. The four
rules are stated explicitly.

### Part III — Provisional morality
Before undertaking systematic doubt, Descartes sets up three moral maxims to
govern his conduct in the interim — so he can act while suspending judgment.
He compares the situation to a traveler in a forest: you must keep walking in
one direction even without certainty, or you will never leave. He also announces
his resolution to devote his life to philosophy.

### Part IV — The cogito, the existence of God, the soul
This is the philosophical heart of the text. Descartes employs methodic doubt,
resolves to treat all uncertain things as false, discovers the cogito as the
one indubitable certainty, then argues upward to God's existence (from the idea
of a more perfect being in his mind) and to the real distinction of mind and body.

### Part V — Physics, the heart, animal automata, and the human soul
Descartes summarizes key results from *Le Monde* (the treatise he suppressed in
1633 after Galileo's condemnation), including his mechanistic account of the
heart and blood circulation. He argues that animals are automata (machines with
no rational soul) and distinguishes them from humans by two tests: language use
and flexible reason. He argues the human rational soul could not arise from
matter and must be specially created.

### Part VI — Why he is publishing; the scientific program
Descartes explains his hesitation about publishing (the shadow of Galileo; he
notes that he does not want to suppress his work entirely but also does not want
controversy). He explains his choice of French. He describes his need for others'
assistance with experiments and articulates his hope that natural philosophy, if
properly conducted, will make humans "masters and possessors of nature" — with
medicine as the crowning application, the extension of healthy life.

### Q1 — Part I: The opening ("good sense") — VERIFIED

> "Good sense is, of all things among men, the most equally distributed; for
> every one thinks himself so abundantly provided with it, that those even who
> are the most difficult to satisfy in everything else, do not usually desire a
> larger measure of this quality than they already possess."

**French original (for cross-check):**
"Le bon sens est la chose du monde la mieux partagée ; car chacun pense en être
si bien pourvu, que ceux même qui sont les plus difficiles à contenter en toute
autre chose n'ont point coutume d'en désirer plus qu'ils en ont."
(Source: Duke University bilingual text, fetched 2026-06-17.)

**Author note:** "Good sense" = reason; Descartes uses "bon sens" and "raison"
interchangeably in the text. The joke is double-edged: universal confidence in
one's own reason is the opening premise of a text about how most people reason
badly.

### Q2 — Part I: Application is all — VERIFIED

> "For it is not enough to have a good mind; the main thing is to apply it well.
> The greatest souls are capable of the greatest vices as well as the greatest
> virtues; and those who proceed but very slowly can make much greater progress,
> if they always follow the right path, than those who hurry and stray from it."

(Veitch; confirmed via Wikisource Part 1 and the Cottingham/WIST cross-check at
https://wist.info/descartes-rene/349/ — Cottingham's rendering is similar but
in-copyright; the Veitch wording above is the PD text.)

### Q3 — Part II: The four rules of the method (four precepts) — VERIFIED

The rules are presented as a block in Part II. Veitch renders them:

**Rule 1 (clarity / certainty):**
> "never to accept anything for true which I did not clearly know to be such;
> that is to say, carefully to avoid precipitancy and prejudice, and to comprise
> nothing more in my judgment than what was presented to my mind so clearly and
> distinctly as to exclude all ground of doubt."

**Rule 2 (analysis / division):**
> "to divide each of the difficulties under examination into as many parts as
> possible, and as might be necessary for its adequate solution."

**Rule 3 (synthesis / order):**
> "to conduct my thoughts in such order that, by commencing with objects the
> simplest and easiest to know, I might ascend by little and little, and, as it
> were, step by step, to the knowledge of the more complex."

**Rule 4 (enumeration / review):**
> "in every case to make enumerations so complete, and reviews so general, that
> I might be assured that nothing was omitted."

**Author note on the rules:** The four rules are presented in one paragraph in
the original; for the work read they may be presented as a numbered list but
must not be re-labeled (the original text does not name them Rule 1 / Rule 2 /
etc.; Descartes simply lists them). The four-rule reduction was itself Descartes'
simplification: his earlier *Rules for the Direction of the Mind* (c. 1628,
posthumous) contained 21 rules; here he distills the essence to four.

### Q4 — Part III: First moral maxim — VERIFIED

> "The first was to obey the laws and customs of my country, adhering firmly to
> the Faith in which, by the grace of God, I had been educated from my childhood,
> and regulating my conduct in every other matter according to the most moderate
> opinions, and the farthest removed from extremes, which should happen to be
> adopted in practice with general consent of the most judicious of those among
> whom I might be living."

---

### Q5 — Part III: Second moral maxim — VERIFIED

> "My second maxim was to be as firm and resolute in my actions as I was able,
> and not to adhere less steadfastly to the most doubtful opinions, when once
> adopted, than if they had been highly certain."

---

### Q6 — Part III: Third moral maxim — VERIFIED

> "My third maxim was to endeavour always to conquer myself rather than fortune,
> and change my desires rather than the order of the world, and in general,
> accustom myself to the persuasion that, except our own thoughts, there is
> nothing absolutely in our power."

**Author note on provisional morality:** Descartes explicitly calls these
"provisional" — moral rules for getting through ordinary life while the method
of doubt is being applied to all his beliefs. The analogy he uses (paraphrase):
a traveler lost in a forest should pick a direction and walk steadily rather than
wander back and forth. The maxims allow action while certainty is being established.
The Stoic flavour of the third maxim (change desires, not the world) is noted
by scholars; Descartes does not cite the Stoics but the resemblance to Epictetus'
dichotomy (things in our power vs. not) is structural.

---

### Q7 — Part IV: Methodic doubt — VERIFIED

> "I was willing to suppose that there existed nothing really such as they
> presented to us; and because some men err in reasoning, and fall into
> Paralogisms, even on the simplest matters of Geometry, I, convinced that I was
> as open to error as any other, rejected as false all the reasonings I had
> hitherto taken for Demonstrations."

---

### Q8 — Part IV: The cogito — VERIFIED (CRITICAL — THE KEY PASSAGE)

> "But immediately upon this I observed that, whilst I thus wished to think that
> all was false, it was absolutely necessary that I, who thus thought, should be
> somewhat; and as I observed that this truth, I think, therefore I am (COGITO
> ERGO SUM), was so certain and of such evidence that no ground of doubt,
> however extravagant, could be alleged by the sceptics capable of shaking it,
> I concluded that I might, without scruple, accept it as the first principle of
> the philosophy of which I was in search."

(From the Veitch pg59 text; "COGITO ERGO SUM" appears in ALL-CAPS as Veitch's
own Latin gloss — see §4 below for the locus landmine.)

---

### Q9 — Part IV: The mind as wholly distinct from the body — VERIFIED

> "I thence concluded that I was a substance whose whole essence or nature
> consists only in thinking, and which, that it may exist, has need of no place,
> nor is dependent on any material thing; so that 'I,' that is to say, the mind
> by which I am what I am, is wholly distinct from the body."

---

### Q10 — Part IV: From the idea of perfection to God — VERIFIED (paraphrase confirmed)

Descartes argues from the idea of a more perfect being in his mind to God's
existence. The key step in Veitch's rendering:

> "For how would it be possible that I should know that I doubted, and that
> something was still wanting to me, if I did not know what was the thing in
> which I was deficient? ...I conceived the idea of a Being more perfect than
> myself...it was manifest to me that this nature had been placed in me by a
> Nature which was truly more perfect than mine, and which even possessed within
> itself all the perfections of which I could form any idea; that is to say, in
> a single word, which was God."

---

### Q11 — Part V: Animals as automata / the two tests — VERIFIED (paraphrase)

Descartes proposes two tests to distinguish a true human from a machine or
animal. Veitch:

**First test (language):**
> "they could never use words or other signs arranged in such a manner as is
> competent to us in order to declare our thoughts to others"

**Second test (flexible reason):**
> "although such machines might execute many things with equal or perhaps greater
> perfection than any of us, they would, without doubt, fail in certain others
> from which it could be discovered that they did not act from knowledge"

---

### Q12 — Part VI: The scientific dream — VERIFIED

> "...a practical [philosophy], by means of which, knowing the force and the
> action of fire, water, air, the stars, the heavens, and all the other bodies
> that environ us, as distinctly as we know the different crafts of our artisans,
> we might also apply them in the same way to all the uses to which they are
> adapted, and thus render ourselves the lords and possessors of nature."

**Author note:** "lords and possessors of nature" is one of the most-quoted
lines of the *Discourse* and of early modern science. Confirm at drafting time
against the Gutenberg source since this is a high-visibility quotation.

## 4. THE COGITO LOCUS LANDMINE (mandatory reading before drafting)

This is the most recurrent error in Descartes scholarship at the popular level.
The full ruling is in `descartes-meditations-fact-ledger.md` §CROSS-WORK and
`descartes-fact-pack.md` §3. Summary for this ledger:

| Text | Year | Language | Formulation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Discourse on the Method, Part IV** | **1637** | **French** | **"je pense, donc je suis"** — narrative discovery; Veitch's English adds "(COGITO ERGO SUM)" in parentheses as his own editorial gloss, NOT in Descartes' 1637 French |
| *Meditations on First Philosophy*, Med. II | 1641 | Latin | "ego sum, ego existo" — "I am, I exist"; NO syllogistic form "therefore"; not "cogito ergo sum" |
| *Principles of Philosophy*, Part I, Art. 7 | 1644 | Latin | "ego cogito, ergo sum" — the explicit Latin axiom as a formal philosophical statement; THE ONLY PLACE the full Latin formula appears as Descartes wrote it |

**Author instructions:**
1. In the *Discourse* work read, the cogito appears as "je pense, donc je suis"
   (French, 1637). When quoting Veitch, quote verbatim including the parenthetical
   "(COGITO ERGO SUM)" and note that the uppercase Latin is Veitch's gloss, not
   Descartes' text.
2. NEVER write "Cogito, ergo sum" as if Descartes wrote that in the Discourse.
   He wrote French; the Latin formula is Veitch's and belongs properly to the
   1644 Principles.
3. If the key-passage block cites the cogito, use Veitch's full sentence (Q8
   above), and note in the house-voice prose: the French reads "I think, therefore
   I am" and the Latin gloss Veitch adds — "COGITO ERGO SUM" — that everyone now
   quotes as if it were Descartes' own phrasing, actually belongs to a book
   Descartes wrote seven years later.
4. The *Meditations* (1641) says "I am, I exist" — not "therefore I am." The two
   texts present the same result via different philosophical framings.

## 5. The stove-heated room (poêle) — framing rules

**What Descartes says (DOCUMENTED — self-report in the Discourse, Part II):**
The *Discourse* itself is the source; Descartes says (in Veitch's rendering, as
found in the Marxists.org edition, which preserves the stove detail the Gutenberg
pg59 edition partly elides):
> "I remained the whole day shut up alone in a stove-heated room, where I had
> complete leisure to occupy myself with my own thoughts."

The Gutenberg pg59 version renders the same passage slightly differently:
> "I remained the whole day in seclusion, with full opportunity to occupy my
> attention with my own thoughts."

The French original: "je demeurois tout le jour enfermé seul dans un poêle, où
j'avois tout le loisir de m'entretenir de mes pensées" (confirmed in the Duke
bilingual text, fetched 2026-06-17). A *poêle* is a room heated by a stove, not
a stove itself; the tradition of saying "Descartes was inside a stove" is a
mistranslation.

**Year:** Winter of 1619–1620, while Descartes was in Germany (Bavaria) with the
army of Maximilian I. The *Discourse* gives the setting as "Germany"; the date
November 10, 1619 comes from private notebooks, not the *Discourse* itself.

**Framing rule:** Ship as self-report. "As Descartes tells it in the Discourse..."
or "in the autobiographical account he would publish eighteen years later..." The
1637 text presents the 1619 retreat as the origin story of the method. Whether
the philosophical revelation happened exactly as described, and in exactly those
circumstances, is the kind of thing a memoir tidies up. It is a literary
construction as well as a biographical claim.

**The three dreams (LATE-TRADITION — keep separate):**
The famous three revelatory dreams of November 10–11, 1619 do NOT appear in the
*Discourse*. Their source is **Adrien Baillet's biography** (1691), which drew on
Descartes' lost private notebook (*Olympica*, part of the *Cogitationes privatae*).
The notebook was seen by Leibniz but is now lost; we have Baillet's account only.
Baillet wrote seventy-one years after the event and was writing hagiography.
**Do NOT conflate the stove (Discourse, Part II, self-reported) with the three
dreams (Baillet 1691, from lost notebook, late tradition).** They are two different
things from two different sources.

## 6. Position summaries (each tied to a source)

### 6a. Methodic / hyperbolic doubt
**Doctrine:** Descartes resolves in Part IV to reject as absolutely false
anything about which he can find the least ground for doubt — including the
testimony of the senses and the apparent certainty of mathematics, because an
all-powerful deceiving being could in principle falsify even those. This is
explicitly a methodological move (not a sincere belief that everything is false)
designed to find what survives the strongest possible challenge.
**Source:** *Discourse*, Part IV (Gutenberg #59, Veitch). Consistent with the
*Meditations* treatment, which is more extended; the *Discourse* version is the
shorter, popular account.
**What is new:** The explicit methodological use of radical doubt as a tool for
establishing certainty, not as a position in its own right. The Academic Skeptics
(Arcesilaus, Carneades) used similar arguments to suspend judgment; Descartes
uses the same arguments to force a crisis that reveals what cannot be doubted.

### 6b. The cogito as the first indubitable truth
**Doctrine:** Even the most extreme doubt — even a deceiving demon falsifying
everything — cannot falsify the existence of the doubter, because doubting is
a form of thinking, and thinking requires a thinker. The cogito ("I think,
therefore I am") is the one thing that survives. From it, Descartes works upward.
**Source:** *Discourse*, Part IV (Q8 above). The same result, differently framed,
in the *Meditations* Med. II.
**Note for author:** The cogito in the *Discourse* is presented as a
**discovery** — "I observed that this truth..." — and as a **narrative** event
in Descartes' intellectual autobiography. The *Meditations* presents it as the
result of a six-day withdrawal from all prior opinion. The *Discourse* version is
more accessible (conversational French, personal "I"), the *Meditations* version
more rigorous (Latin, systematic, with Objections and Replies). The work read
is the *Discourse*; use the *Discourse* framing.

### 6c. The method's four rules
**Doctrine:** See Q3 above. Briefly: accept only what is clear and distinct
(certainty); divide problems into parts (analysis); work from simple to complex
(synthesis); enumerate completely (review). The rules are modeled on mathematical
demonstration — they describe how a geometer reasons applied to all inquiries.
**Source:** *Discourse*, Part II (Gutenberg #59, Veitch). Consistent with thinker
fact-pack §5a.

### 6d. The provisional morality
**Doctrine:** While doubt is being applied to all beliefs, Descartes sets up
three practical maxims to govern action (see Q4–Q6). He does NOT apply the method
to morality — he recognizes that life requires decision while certainty is being
sought. The word "provisional" is Descartes' own framing.
**Source:** *Discourse*, Part III (Gutenberg #59, Veitch).
**Note:** A fourth maxim is sometimes cited: devote your life to the pursuit of
truth. Descartes mentions this as a result of reflecting on his provisional
morality but it is less formally structured than the three. Ship as three maxims
unless the draft requires four.

### 6e. Proof of God and the external world (Part IV)
**Doctrine:** From the cogito, Descartes infers his own imperfection (he doubts;
doubting implies imperfection). He finds in his mind the idea of a perfect being.
An imperfect being cannot produce the idea of perfection from its own resources;
the idea must have been placed in him by a perfect being — God. God is not a
deceiver (deception is an imperfection). Therefore what he clearly and distinctly
perceives is true; therefore the external world exists.
**Source:** *Discourse*, Part IV (Gutenberg #59, Veitch). Shorter and less
rigorous than the *Meditations* versions (Trademark argument, Med. III; Ontological
argument, Med. V). The *Discourse* gives the gist; the *Meditations* gives the
full argument with the Objections and Replies.
**Note for author:** The *Discourse* version of the God-argument is a condensed
summary, not the full proof. The work read can present it as a sketch — "in the
*Meditations* he would spell it out more fully" is an appropriate forward pointer.

### 6f. Mind / body distinction
**Doctrine:** Descartes concludes from the cogito that he is "a substance whose
whole essence or nature consists only in thinking" (Q9 above), which requires no
body and is wholly distinct from body. This is Part IV's formulation. Part V
develops the body-as-machine view and the tests distinguishing humans from animals.
**Source:** *Discourse*, Parts IV and V (Gutenberg #59, Veitch). Extended in
`descartes-fact-pack.md` §5d.
**Potted-summary trap (mandatory flag):** "Descartes invented mind-body dualism"
is FALSE AS STATED. Plato's *Phaedo* separates soul and body explicitly; the
Scholastics (Aquinas, Ockham) all have soul-body doctrines. What is new in
Descartes: (a) two complete substances, each sufficient without the other; (b)
the body as literally a machine (mechanistic materialism); (c) this mechanical
body makes the interaction problem acute in a way it had not been before. Ship as:
"he did not invent the idea that mind and body are different; he invented the
problem of how they connect, by making the body so thoroughly a machine."

### 6g. The "I think" as foundation
**Doctrine:** The cogito is the first principle — "the first principle of the
philosophy of which I was in search" (Q8). Descartes makes first-person certainty
the foundation of all knowledge. This is the defining move of "modern" philosophy
as conventionally understood. Everything builds upward from this ground floor.
**Source:** *Discourse*, Part IV (Gutenberg #59).

## 7. Potted-summary traps (mandatory for fact-checker gate)

These textbook caricatures must not appear unqualified in the work read:

1. **"Descartes invented mind-body dualism"** — see §6f above. He invented the
   interaction problem in its mechanistic form. The dualism as concept has
   antecedents in Plato, Augustine, and the Scholastics.

2. **"The cogito ergo sum is in the Discourse"** — the FORMULA "cogito ergo sum"
   is Veitch's Latin gloss (parenthetical in the English translation) and belongs
   as a formal Latin axiom to the 1644 *Principles*. The *Discourse* (1637)
   contains "je pense, donc je suis" in French.

3. **"Descartes proved God's existence"** — he argued for it; the Cartesian circle
   (God guarantees clear perception; clear perception proves God) was noted before
   the ink was dry. Mersenne raised it in the Second Objections; Arnauld pressed
   it in the Fourth. Frame: "Descartes argued for God's existence, and the debate
   began in the same volume."

4. **"The stove-heated room was a stove"** — a *poêle* is a room heated by a
   stove, not the stove itself. Descartes was not sitting inside an oven.

5. **"The three dreams are in the Discourse"** — the three dreams come from Baillet
   1691 via the lost Olympica; they are not in the *Discourse*.

6. **"Descartes believed animals don't feel pain"** — more precisely: his position
   was that animals have no rational soul and therefore no SENSATION in the
   philosophically full sense; their reactions to damage are mechanical. Whether he
   thought there was "nothing it is like" to be an animal in pain is debated by
   scholars. Ship the documented position (animals are automata; two tests for
   distinguishing humans from machines) and avoid the stronger claim.

## 9. The break block (mandatory — why the Discourse is a break)

**BEFORE:** Natural philosophy written in Latin, addressed to scholars trained in
the Aristotelian-Scholastic tradition. Published works in the genre took positions
by citing authorities (Aristotle, Aquinas, Averroes), debating syllogistically,
and arguing within the framework of received learning. The audience was university-
educated clergy and aristocracy. French-language books existed; scholarly books
did not address the general reader.

**AFTER:** Descartes' *Discourse* does several things at once that were unusual
or unprecedented in combination:
(a) Written in French for a general educated reader, not the Latin-reading
professional. This is a statement about who philosophy's audience should be.
(b) Autobiographical in form — Descartes presents his method as something he
found by living and thinking, not as received doctrine. "Here are the paths I
have followed" (Part I). This is philosophically significant: the authority is
first-person experience, not tradition.
(c) A method-first approach: BEFORE stating any thesis about what exists, he asks
what procedure could yield certainty. The method is prior to the content. This
reverses the Scholastic order (start from authoritative content; use method to
organize it).
(d) The first person as foundation: Part IV makes "I think, therefore I am" the
first principle of the whole inquiry. The philosopher's own thinking is the ground
floor; everything else is built upward from there. This is the move from "what
does the world reveal to us?" to "what can I know?"

## 10. Soft spots — what this ledger could not fully verify

1. **The Jan Maire publisher's name** — confirmed in Wikimedia Commons
   title-page metadata and the Project Vox entry; not confirmed by fetching the
   original Baillet or an archival source. Secondary-source confirmed only;
   sufficiently documented for authoring purposes.

2. **The fourth maxim** — some scholars list four moral maxims in Part III
   (adding a maxim about devoting one's life to truth); others list three.
   The Veitch translation presents three formal maxims and then Descartes'
   reflection on what following them revealed. Count conservatively as three; note
   the scholarly discussion as a parenthetical if needed.

3. **The Vatier letter wording** — the phrase "les femmes mêmes pussent entendre
   quelque chose" is cited in French secondary scholarship (jdarriulat.net) as
   from the February 22, 1638 letter to Père Vatier. A full text of this letter
   in PD French or English was not located in this pass. The attribution to the
   Vatier letter is well-established in the scholarly tradition; if the letter is
   quoted, flag it as PARAPHRASE-ONLY until verified against the primary text
   (in the Adam-Tannery edition of Descartes' letters).

4. **The "lords and possessors of nature" passage (Q12)** — confirmed as present
   in the *Discourse*, Part VI, in secondary and tertiary sources; the exact Veitch
   wording was not string-matched against the Gutenberg file in this pass. Verify
   exact wording against Gutenberg #59 before committing to quotation marks.

5. **The *Dioptrique*'s proof of the law of refraction** — claimed as first
   scientific proof in §1 above; this is the standard claim in histories of optics
   (Snell had the result but did not publish; Descartes published in 1637). Flag
   for the fact-checker gate to confirm the "first published proof" wording.

## 11. Apocrypha blacklist (Discourse-specific additions)

These supplement the blacklists in `descartes-fact-pack.md` §11 and
`descartes-meditations-fact-ledger.md`.

1. **"Cogito ergo sum is the Discourse's famous line"** — the Discourse says
   "je pense, donc je suis." The Latin "Cogito, ergo sum" is Veitch's gloss and
   Descartes' 1644 Principles formulation, not the 1637 French text.

2. **"Descartes was sitting inside a stove"** — a mistranslation of *poêle*;
   he was in a room heated by a stove.

3. **"Descartes had his revelation when he dreamed in the stove-room"** — the
   three-dreams story is from Baillet 1691, not from the Discourse. The Discourse
   says only that he was alone in the room all day thinking.

4. **"The Discourse proves God's existence more rigorously than the Meditations"**
   — the reverse: the *Discourse* gives a popular sketch; the *Meditations* gives
   the full argument with Objections and Replies.

5. **"Descartes invented the scientific method"** — he proposed a method;
   Bacon (Novum Organum, 1620) proposed an empirical inductive method the same
   generation. Neither "invented" the scientific method; both are part of a wider
   17th-century movement to reform natural philosophy.

6. **"Even women could understand the Discourse" is in the Discourse** — this
   phrase is from a letter to Père Vatier (February 22, 1638), written after
   publication. Not in the Discourse text.
