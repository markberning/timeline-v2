## Quotes (every quoted line, source + location, all PD English)

### Bentham — *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789; written 1780, pub. 1789)
- **"Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure."**
  — ch. 1, §I. VERIFIED verbatim against the IPML ch.1 text (laits.utexas.edu IPML c01;
  cross-checked earlymoderntexts/rechtsphilosophie editions). Hero-chapter epigraph + ch.1 body.
- **"that principle which approves or disapproves of every action whatsoever. according to the
  tendency it appears to have to augment or diminish the happiness of the party whose interest is
  in question."** — ch. 1, §II (definition of the principle of utility). VERIFIED verbatim
  (laits.utexas.edu IPML c01). The lower-case "according" and the period after "whatsoever." are
  reproduced as printed in that edition. Ch.1 body.
- **"that property in any object, whereby it tends to produce benefit, advantage, pleasure, good,
  or happiness"** — ch. 1, §III (definition of utility). VERIFIED verbatim (laits.utexas.edu IPML
  c01). Ch.3 epigraph + ch.1 body.

### Bentham — *Anarchical Fallacies* (written c. 1795; pub. 1816 French / 1843 Bowring English)
- **"Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense,
  nonsense upon stilts."** — Article II (critique of the French *Declaration of the Rights of
  Man*). VERIFIED against ditext.com/bentham and davidmhart.com (Bowring 1843 English edition).
  NOTE: one online transcription (ditext) carries OCR typos ("nonscnse", a stray comma/dash); the
  canonical Bowring wording is reproduced here ("rhetorical nonsense, nonsense upon stilts"), which
  is the standard scholarly quotation. The em-dash that some editions print between "nonsense" and
  "nonsense upon stilts" is rendered as a comma in the read to keep the no-em-dash-in-narration
  rule clean while preserving wording; this is a punctuation-only normalization, flagged here.
  [VERIFY-PUNCT] — wording is solid; the single internal mark (comma vs dash) varies by edition.
  Ch.2 epigraph + ch.2 body.

### Mill — *Utilitarianism* (1863)
- **"a doctrine worthy only of swine"** — ch. 2 (Mill stating the critics' charge). VERIFIED
  verbatim (Gutenberg #11224, *Utilitarianism* ch.2). Ch.4 body (framed as the critics' jeer that
  Mill quotes and answers).
- **"The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness
  Principle, holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as
  they tend to produce the reverse of happiness."** — ch. 2 (the Greatest Happiness Principle
  definition). VERIFIED verbatim (Gutenberg #11224). Ch.4 body + ch.5 epigraph (epigraph uses the
  first clause through "promote happiness").
- **"It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates
  dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."** — ch. 2. VERIFIED verbatim (Gutenberg #11224; cross-check
  Wikisource 1863 djvu p.22). Ch.4 epigraph + ch.4 body. The continuation ("And if the fool, or
  the pig, are of a different opinion, it is because they only know their own side of the
  question. The other party to the comparison knows both sides.") is PARAPHRASED in the read, not
  quoted ("The pig and the fool dissent only because they know one side of the question; the human
  being and Socrates know both.").
- **"By happiness is intended pleasure, and the absence of pain"** — ch.2. This standard
  continuation is PARAPHRASED in the read ("by happiness he meant pleasure and the absence of
  pain"), NOT set in quote marks, so no verification burden beyond the well-attested sense.

### Mill — *On Liberty* (1859)
- **"The object of this Essay is to assert one very simple principle"** — ch. 1. VERIFIED verbatim
  (Gutenberg #34901, *On Liberty* ch.1). Ch.4 body.
- **"the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised
  community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."** — ch. 1. VERIFIED verbatim
  (Gutenberg #34901). Ch.4 body + ch.6 epigraph. British spelling "civilised" preserved.

### Harriet Taylor Mill — *Enfranchisement of Women* (Westminster Review, July 1851)
- **"admission, in law, and in fact, to equality in all rights, political, civil and social, with
  the male citizens of the community"** — VERIFIED verbatim (Gutenberg #73404, released 2024-04-16;
  the essay is PD). Ch.4 body. Authorship note: the essay long appeared under Mill's name and its
  exact division of labor is debated; the read attributes it to Harriet Taylor (standard modern
  attribution) and cross-links her hub `taylor` (= "Harriet Taylor").

### Sidgwick — *The Methods of Ethics* (1874)
- NO quotation. Named in prose; the **"dualism of practical reason"** is his term and is given as

  of the dualism (utilitarian principle vs. rational egoism, self-evident yet not always

## Dates / claims ledger (every non-quote factual assertion)
- **Bentham 1748–1832; Mill 1806–1873; Sidgwick 1838–1900; Harriet Taylor 1807–1858.** Confirmed
  against `src/lib/philosophy-data.ts` (bentham 1748–1832, mill 1806–1873, taylor 1807–1858) +
  standard reference (Sidgwick 1838–1900). CONFIRMED.
- **IPML written 1780, printed 1789** — standard (the read dates it 1789, the publication year,
  matching the work-card convention). CONFIRMED.
- **"greatest happiness of the greatest number" antecedents** — Francis Hutcheson (*Inquiry into
  the Original of Our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue*, 1725, "greatest happiness for the greatest
  numbers") and Joseph Priestley are the usual sources Bentham credited; the read names Hutcheson
  and Priestley and the Epicurean root for pleasure-as-good, and frames Bentham's originality as
  making happiness the SOLE standard + operationalizing it, NOT inventing the phrase. CONFIRMED
  (SEP "Bentham"; standard history). Framed as antecedent, not as Bentham's coinage.
- **"each to count for one, and none for more than one"** — attributed to Bentham as "a maxim
  later associated with Bentham" (Mill quotes it in *Utilitarianism* ch.5 and credits Bentham; the
  exact wording's provenance is debated). The read does NOT set it in quote marks and hedges the
  attribution ("A maxim later associated with Bentham captures it"). CONFIRMED-as-hedged.
- **Push-pin vs poetry** — Bentham's view that, quantity of pleasure equal, push-pin is as good as
  poetry, is from *The Rationale of Reward* (1825). The read gives it as Bentham's view, not a
  quotation. CONFIRMED (standard; the "push-pin... poetry" formulation is Bentham's in Rationale of
  Reward, Book III).
- **Philosophic Radicals** — the reforming circle around Bentham/James Mill (incl. names like
  Francis Place, George Grote, J.S. Mill); campaigned for franchise reform, secret ballot, penal
  and poor-law reform, codification. CONFIRMED (standard history; SEP "Bentham", "Mill").
- **Penal reform logic** (punishment as itself an evil, justified only to prevent greater evil,
  proportioned, deterrent not vengeful, no crimes without victims) — Bentham's penal theory,
  IPML + *Principles of Penal Law*. CONFIRMED (SEP "Bentham").
- **Panopticon** — Bentham's prison design (central inspector, self-policing inmates); the British
  government ultimately did not build it, a documented disappointment of his life. CONFIRMED
  (standard; Bentham *Panopticon*, 1791; Parliament's rejection / the 1813 compensation). Framed
  honestly: surveillance emblem in retrospect, intended as humane/cheap reform.
- **New Poor Law 1834** (deterrent workhouses) — associated with Benthamite/utilitarian reasoning
  (Edwin Chadwick, a Bentham secretary, was central to the 1834 reform). The read uses it as the
  "double edge" example: utilitarian reasoning underwrote BOTH humane reform AND harsh present
  suffering for a projected future sum. CONFIRMED (standard history; Chadwick's role).
  [VERIFY-NUANCE] — the read attributes the harshness to "cold" utilitarian calculation, which is
  the standard reading; some historians dispute how Benthamite the workhouse cruelty really was.
  Hedged in-text ("in colder hands") and flagged here.
- **Bentham Auto-Icon** (preserved body + waxed head, displayed at University College London) —
  CONFIRMED (UCL; standard). Used in the hero caption.
- **Mill's upbringing + breakdown** — James Mill's rigorous early education (Greek at three),
  Mill's mental crisis in his early twenties, recounted in his *Autobiography* (1873). CONFIRMED
  (standard; SEP "Mill"). Given as biography, not over-asserted.
- **On Liberty as joint work with Harriet Taylor** — Mill explicitly described *On Liberty* as a
  joint production and credited Taylor's thought as inseparable from his own (Dedication of *On
  Liberty*; *Autobiography*). The brief REQUIRES crediting her as a genuine partner, not a
  footnote; honored explicitly in ch.4. CONFIRMED.
- **Harm principle as utilitarian, not an external limit** — Mill's own framing (liberty serves
  the long-run general happiness; *On Liberty* ch.1, "utility in the largest sense, grounded on the
  permanent interests of man as a progressive being"). The read presents liberty as what
  utilitarianism correctly worked out demands, which is Mill's position, NOT the cartoon. CONFIRMED.
- **Rights given a utilitarian home; justice as a branch of utility** — Mill, *Utilitarianism*
  ch.5 ("Of the Connexion between Justice and Utility"). CONFIRMED. The read uses this to answer the
  "frame the innocent" cartoon.
- **Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics* (1874)** + the dualism of practical reason. CONFIRMED
  (Wikipedia "The Methods of Ethics"; Rawls/Smart praise is well-attested but NOT quoted in the
  read).
- **Act vs rule utilitarianism; demandingness; sacrifice-the-innocent / trolley** — these are
  STANDARD positions/objections in moral philosophy, presented as live problems (not attributed to
  any single named author, to stay at school altitude). The trolley problem traces to Philippa Foot
  (1967) and Judith Jarvis Thomson; the read uses it generically without claiming it is
  nineteenth-century. CONFIRMED as standard framing. No false attribution.
- **Welfare economics + cost-benefit analysis as utilitarianism's descendants** — standard
  intellectual-history claim (Pigou, and the broader treatment of well-being as measurable +
  maximizable). CONFIRMED as a general lineage claim, stated at the level of "most direct
  descendant", not a precise causal assertion.
- **20th-c. rights/contract revival defined against utilitarianism** — Rawls's *A Theory of
  Justice* (1971) is the obvious referent ("the separateness of persons"); the read does NOT name
  Rawls (school altitude, and to stay PD-era-safe) but describes the reaction accurately. CONFIRMED
  as a fair, unattributed characterization.

## Framing traps honored (per the era brief landmines)
- **Utilitarianism is NOT "the ends justify any means."** Ch.2 explicitly rejects the cartoon
  (Bentham defends rights-protections on utilitarian grounds; his quarrel is only with their
  pretended natural foundation). Ch.4 + ch.5 give Mill's and rule-utilitarianism's answers to the
  sacrifice-the-innocent charge. The strongest version of each objection is stated fairly before
  the answer (brief's "make the reader get it" floor).
- **Mill built in higher pleasures, rights, AND liberty.** All three are worked at idea-altitude
  with the verified quotes (swine/Socrates; the GHP restatement; the harm principle; justice as a
  branch of utility). Honored.
- **Harriet Taylor credited as a genuine partner, not a footnote** (Mill said so). Ch.4 states the
  joint authorship of *On Liberty*, her own *Enfranchisement of Women* with a verified quote, and
  links her hub. Honored per the brief's explicit instruction.
- **"Greatest happiness of the greatest number" is an inherited phrase**, not Bentham's coinage
  (Hutcheson/Priestley named); Bentham's originality framed correctly. Honored.
- **Bentham's harshness vs humaneness held in tension** — the read does not sanitize (New Poor Law
  / the double edge of consequence-morality is named) nor caricature (his anti-cruelty,
  egalitarian "each counts for one" is foregrounded). Honored.
- **Sidgwick named, not quoted; no fake quotation.** Honored.
- **Nothing named as translated** — all four sources are English originals; honored.

## Inline-defined terms (clarity floor — every term glossed on first use)
utilitarianism (greatest aggregate happiness as the criterion of right action); consequentialism
(rightness depends only on consequences); divine command; natural law; the principle of utility;
utility (the property of producing benefit/pleasure/happiness); felicific calculus (computing net
happiness by scoring + summing pleasures and pains); natural right (a right held simply by being
human, prior to law); Philosophic Radicals; Panopticon; higher vs lower pleasures; Greatest
Happiness Principle; harm principle; dualism of practical reason; act utilitarianism vs rule
utilitarianism; the demandingness objection; welfare economics.
