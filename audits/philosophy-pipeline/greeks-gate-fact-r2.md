### 1b. The One / emanation / soul's ascent summary [VERIFY]

**Draft text:** "Plotinus took Plato's world of Forms and crowned it with a single
ultimate source he called the One, beyond being and beyond description, from which
everything else overflows in descending levels, each fainter and further out; the
human soul is a spark that has descended into the material world and longs to climb
back up toward reunion with the One."

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED — accurate summary of standard Neoplatonism**

Verified against IEP "Plotinus" and SEP "Plotinus":

- **"The One"**: Confirmed. Plotinus's first principle is called the One, and it is
  "utterly uncompounded" and transcends all predication. IEP confirms: "no words can
  do justice to the power of the One" and it "is neither being nor essence, but the
  source, or rather, the possibility of all existence." The phrase "beyond being and
  beyond description" accurately captures this.

- **Emanation in descending levels**: Confirmed. The system operates through three
  hypostases in descending order: The One → Intellect (Nous) → Soul. Reality
  "overflows" from higher to lower levels without diminishing the source. The draft's
  "overflows in descending levels, each fainter and further out" is a fair
  characterization.

- **The soul's descent and ascent**: Confirmed. The soul descends into materiality but
  retains connection to its divine source; the path of philosophy is ascent back toward
  the One through contemplation. IEP confirms this dynamic.

One **small precision note**: The draft says the soul is "a spark that has descended."
Strictly speaking in Plotinus, the Soul (as the third hypostasis) generates the
material world and individual souls descend; the "spark" metaphor is a common
characterization and not wrong at era altitude, but is more evocative than technical.
At era altitude (not a thinker-deep page), this is acceptable. The overall summary
is accurate and will not mislead the reader.

**No change required.** The [VERIFY] tag can be removed.

### 1c. Early Christian thinkers connection [VERIFY]

**Draft text:** "the Neoplatonists gave the early Christian thinkers a way to picture a
single divine source, and so a pagan Greek's reworking of Plato became one of the
load-bearing beams of Christian theology."

**(The draft softened "Augustine" to "early Christian thinkers" to stay within pack
support; the [VERIFY] note flagged this for the fact-check, which can restore Augustine
if verified.)**

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED — and Augustine can be named**

The Augustine–Neoplatonism connection is one of the most thoroughly documented claims
in the history of Western philosophy. Verified across multiple sources:

- **SEP "Augustine"** (plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/): Augustine read "books of
  the Platonists" translated by Marius Victorinus c. 386 CE, which comprised treatises
  by Plotinus (including Enneads I.6, I.2, V.1, VI.4–5) and Porphyry. Augustine names
  Plotinus directly in *De beata vita*. Neoplatonism shaped his epistemology,
  metaphysics (God's transcendence, the soul's immateriality, ontological hierarchies),
  ethics (spiritual ascent and returning to God), and his conception of evil as
  privation.

- Additionally, Ambrose of Milan (who converted Augustine) was also strongly shaped by
  Plotinus. The Greek Fathers (Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Gregory of Nazianzus) were also
  substantially influenced.

The draft's cautious "early Christian thinkers" framing is accurate; but given the
verification, the draft could also name Augustine specifically. The coordinator may
choose to restore Augustine's name (the claim is solid), or keep "early Christian
thinkers" (also accurate and appropriately broad at era altitude).

**COORDINATOR OPTION (not a required fix):** The sentence may be upgraded to:
> "the Neoplatonists gave Augustine and the early Christian thinkers a way to picture a
> single divine source, and so a pagan Greek's reworking of Plato became one of the
> load-bearing beams of Christian theology."

This would be more concrete and historically accurate. The [VERIFY] tag can be removed
either way.

### 4d. Arabic-transmission names: al-Fārābī, Avicenna, Averroes — what-they-did clauses

**Draft text:** "al-Fārābī, Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā), and Averroes (Ibn Rushd) translated
Aristotle, wrote vast commentaries that thought *with* him and pushed his arguments
further, and kept him alive and working when the Latin West had nearly lost him, before
handing him back to Europe through the translation schools of Spain."

**Verdict: ⚠️ PARTIALLY ACCURATE — "translated Aristotle" is inaccurate for al-Fārābī
and Avicenna; Averroes is closer. SHOULD-FIX.**

Verified against SEP entries for al-Fārābī and Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna):

- **al-Fārābī**: Did NOT translate Aristotle. He wrote commentaries on existing Arabic
  translations and produced original philosophical syntheses. He was known as the
  "Second Teacher" (Aristotle being the first) for his elaborate commentaries.
  Translating Aristotle was not his role.

- **Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā)**: Did NOT translate Aristotle. He worked with existing Arabic
  translations and wrote original systematic philosophical encyclopedias (The Healing,
  The Salvation, Pointers and Reminders) that extended and sometimes corrected
  Aristotle. He was an independent philosopher building on the Aristotelian tradition,
  not a translator.

- **Averroes (Ibn Rushd)**: More accurately described as the great commentator. His
  exhaustive commentaries on all of Aristotle (he covered virtually the entire corpus)
  were translated into Latin and transmitted Aristotle to the Latin West. He was called
  "The Commentator" in the medieval West. The transmission-to-Europe framing applies
  most directly to Averroes.

The phrase "translated Aristotle, wrote vast commentaries" conflates two different
activities and misattributes translation work to al-Fārābī and Avicenna. The safer and
more accurate framing is that they "preserved, extended, and commented on Aristotle"
(or similar — no translator role claimed).

**SHOULD-FIX (not MUST-FIX because the deeper claim — they kept Aristotle alive and
handed him back to Europe — is accurate and the error is a scope over-claim, not a
hallucination). The draft is at era altitude and does not name specific texts, but the
"translated" verb should be softened.**

**Replacement wording:**
> "al-Fārābī, Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā), and Averroes (Ibn Rushd) preserved Aristotle,
> wrote vast commentaries that thought *with* him and pushed his arguments further,
> and kept him alive and working when the Latin West had nearly lost him, before
> handing him back to Europe through the translation schools of Spain."

*(Drop "translated Aristotle" and replace with "preserved Aristotle" — accurate at era
altitude, since the transmission chain involved Arabic-to-Latin translators in Toledo
working from texts the Arabic scholars had kept alive, and the commentaries were
themselves part of what was translated and transmitted.)*

## 6. Regression spot-check — original gate-1 MUST-FIXes

### MF-1: Protagoras / Meno 91d "ten sculptors" wording

**Draft text:** "according to Socrates in Plato's dialogue the *Meno*, he earned more
in fees than ten sculptors put together, the great Phidias among them."

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED — but wording slightly inverted vs. Jowett; SHOULD-FIX**

Verified against Jowett's translation of the *Meno* (Wikisource):

> "I know of a single man, Protagoras, who made more out of his craft than the
> illustrious Pheidias, who created such noble works, or any ten other statuaries."

The Jowett text says "more than the illustrious Pheidias … **or** any ten other
statuaries" — meaning more than Phidias, OR alternatively more than any ten other
sculptors. The draft says "more in fees than ten sculptors put together, the great
Phidias among them" — which implies Phidias is one of the ten, whereas Jowett's
structure names Phidias separately (as the obvious comparison) and then adds "or any
ten other statuaries" as a further intensifier.

A Loeb translation (W. R. M. Lamb) renders it: "more money than Pheidias — so famous
for the noble works he produced — or any ten other sculptors." This confirms the
structure: Phidias is the named exemplar; "ten other sculptors" is the amplifying
comparison.

The draft's phrasing "ten sculptors put together, the great Phidias among them" is a
reasonable prose compression, but it slightly misrepresents the structure of the
argument (Phidias is not one of a group of ten; he is the named star against whom
Protagoras is compared, with the ten as an additional intensifier). This is a minor
inaccuracy.

**SHOULD-FIX. Replacement wording:**
> "according to Socrates in Plato's dialogue the *Meno*, he earned more in fees than
> the celebrated Phidias himself, or any ten other sculptors"

*(Follows the actual sentence structure of Meno 91d: Phidias as the named comparison
first, then "any ten other sculptors" as amplification.)*
