## §1 — VITALS (biographical facts, web-verified)

**Birth:** 15 October 1844, Röcken bei Lützen, Prussian Province of Saxony (now part of
Germany). — DOCUMENTED (Wikipedia; SEP; IEP; all consistent).

**Father:** Carl Ludwig Nietzsche (1813–1849), a Lutheran pastor. Died of a brain disease
(softening of the brain — encephalomalacia attributed to a fall, though the exact diagnosis
was 19th-century medicine) after a year of illness. Friedrich was four years old at the time
(died 1849 when Nietzsche was 4). — DOCUMENTED (Wikipedia; SEP). The biographical resonance
with "God is dead" is real and sometimes noted by scholars; handle with care — the
connection is suggestive, not causal, and should not be overstated.

**Early education:** Attended the prestigious Schulpforta boarding school (1858–1864),
one of the leading German humanistic secondary schools (alumni included Klopstock and
Fichte). Showed extraordinary gifts for classical languages and music. — DOCUMENTED (SEP;
Wikipedia).

**Schopenhauer encounter (1865, age 20):** Nietzsche found a copy of Schopenhauer's
*The World as Will and Representation* in a Leipzig second-hand bookshop and read it
obsessively. He called it a formative encounter — his "first tutor." The pull: Schopenhauer's
pessimism (the world is driven by a blind, suffering Will) offered a diagnosis of existence
that felt honest rather than optimistic; Nietzsche would later repudiate the pessimism while
keeping the drive-concept. — DOCUMENTED (SEP; Wikipedia; multiple biographies). NOTE: the
bookshop story is widely repeated; its details are well-sourced but biographers note it comes
partly from later recollection.

**Wagner encounter (1868, age 24):** Met Richard Wagner at the home of Brockhaus in
Leipzig. Immediate intense friendship; Nietzsche was dazzled by Wagner's combination of
musical genius, philosophical ambition (Wagner was a Schopenhauer enthusiast), and sheer
force of personality. Nietzsche visited the Wagners at Tribschen (their Swiss estate) many
times between 1869–1872. *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872) is saturated with Wagnerian
aesthetics. — DOCUMENTED (SEP; Wikipedia; multiple biographies).

**Basel appointment (1869, age 24):** Appointed Extraordinary Professor of Classical
Philology at the University of Basel. The youngest person ever appointed to this chair at
Basel — and possibly at any German university at that time (this comparative claim should be
hedged: "among the youngest, if not the youngest, in the German-speaking world"). He was
appointed while still working on his doctoral dissertation at Leipzig; Leipzig University
awarded him an honorary doctorate in recognition, skipping the normal requirements, on the
strength of his publications — so he went to Basel with an honorary PhD. He became a full
(ordinary) professor in 1870. Swiss citizenship was offered and accepted (he renounced
Prussian citizenship). — DOCUMENTED (SEP; IEP; multiple sources confirm the age 24 detail
and the honorary doctorate).

**Published *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872):** His first book — a controversial argument
that Greek tragedy emerged from the tension between the Apollonian (order, clarity, form,
the art of sculpture and epic) and the Dionysian (primal chaos, music, ecstasy, dissolution
of self). The book controversially ended with a promotion of Wagner's music dramas as the
rebirth of the Dionysian spirit. The professional reception was damaging: the classicist
Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Möllendorff published a devastating attack (*Zukunftsphilologie!*,
"Philology of the Future!"); Nietzsche's mentor Ritschl was reportedly embarrassed. The
book is now regarded as a major work; at the time it nearly ended his academic career.
— DOCUMENTED (SEP; Wikipedia — *The Birth of Tragedy*).

**Break with Wagner (1876–1878):** The relationship deteriorated after the first Bayreuth
Festival (1876). Nietzsche was "deeply disappointed by the banality of the shows" and
uncomfortable with Wagner's nationalism, anti-Semitism, and increasing Christian religiosity
(Wagner was working on *Parsifal*). The public break came with *Human, All Too Human* (1878),
which Wagner received as a betrayal. The actual intellectual distance had been growing since
1876; their final personal meeting was in Sorrento in 1876. Nietzsche later wrote two
pamphlets (*The Case of Wagner*, 1888; *Nietzsche contra Wagner*, 1889) directly attacking
Wagner. — DOCUMENTED (SEP; Wikipedia; multiple biographies).

**Health collapse and resignation from Basel (1879):** Nietzsche suffered severe
migraines, failing eyesight (he was nearly blind in one eye), and recurring stomach/digestive
illness throughout his Basel years. By 1879 his health was so poor he was largely unable
to teach; he resigned his professorship in 1879, receiving a modest annual pension from
Basel. He was 34. — DOCUMENTED (SEP; Wikipedia).

**The productive wandering decade (1879–1888):** Nietzsche spent this decade as a
freelance philosopher with no institutional affiliation — moving seasonally between
boarding houses in Switzerland (particularly Sils-Maria in the Upper Engadin, summer
retreats), the Italian Riviera (Genoa, Rapallo, Nice, Turin), and occasionally Naumburg.
He travelled light, wrote intensively despite frequent illness, and produced the majority
of his most important works. — DOCUMENTED (SEP; Wikipedia).

**Sils-Maria:** A small mountain resort village above St. Moritz in the Swiss Alps where
Nietzsche spent several summers (from 1881 onward). He described it as among the places
where his best ideas came to him; the eternal recurrence thought is said to have come to
him during a walk near Sils-Maria in August 1881 — DOCUMENTED (SEP footnotes; biographers).
Do NOT state this as the only origin of the thought; he worked through it in subsequent
books.

**The Lou von Salomé triangle (1882):** Nietzsche fell deeply in love with Lou von Salomé,
a brilliant young intellectual from Russia who would later become a significant intellectual
figure in her own right (close to both Freud and Rilke). She declined his proposal of
marriage. The three-person photograph (Nietzsche, Lou, Paul Rée) in which Nietzsche is
harnessed to a cart steered by Lou with a whip is from this period — it was taken as a
joke/tableau staged by the three of them, not literally. The rejection contributed to one
of the most painful periods in his life (1882–1883), during which he wrote *Thus Spoke
Zarathustra*. — DOCUMENTED (Wikipedia; biographies).

**Turin collapse, 3 January 1889:** Nietzsche suffered a mental breakdown in Turin.
He was 44. What is DOCUMENTED: he caused a public disturbance in the Piazza Carlo Alberto
in Turin on January 3, 1889; police were called; friends (Franz Overbeck; Peter Gast)
came to take him to Basel and then to a clinic in Jena. The "Wahnbriefe" (madness letters)
— letters signed "Dionysus" and "the Crucified," sent to Burckhardt, Cosima Wagner, and
others in the days immediately before and after the collapse — survive in archives.
— DOCUMENTED (SEP; Wikipedia; letters cited by historians).

THE HORSE STORY: The oft-repeated story that Nietzsche collapsed while throwing his arms
around a horse being flogged: **APOCRYPHAL.** Single sourcing: an unnamed journalist
reported it after Nietzsche's death in 1900, tracing it to a story told by an unnamed
police officer to David Fino (Nietzsche's landlord). Thirdhand hearsay, told post-mortem.
Scholars consistently call it "apocryphal" (see IEP, LARB, Philosophy Break). Ship the
fact of the breakdown (3 Jan 1889, Turin, DOCUMENTED) and flag the horse story as
tradition, never as established fact.

**After the collapse — care:** His mother Franziska brought him home from the Jena clinic
in 1890, cared for him in Naumburg until her death in 1897. After 1897, his sister
Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche brought him to Weimar, where he was housed at the Villa
Silberblick and displayed to visitors while incapacitated. — DOCUMENTED (Wikipedia; SEP).

**Death:** 25 August 1900, Weimar. Official cause: pneumonia contracted mid-August 1900,
followed by a stroke on the night of 24–25 August. He had been institutionally incapacitated
since 1889 — eleven years of silence. — DOCUMENTED (Wikipedia).

**Illness diagnosis:** The cause of the 1889 breakdown is medically unresolved. Historically
attributed to syphilis (popular account, never definitively proven); more recent medical
research has proposed CADASIL (cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical
infarcts and leukoencephalopathy), a hereditary degenerative disease, a slowly developing
psychiatric condition, or other causes. **Ship as medically uncertain.** — DOCUMENTED as
CONTESTED (SEP; IEP; medical literature cited in Wikipedia).

## §3 — KEY PASSAGES (VERIFIED, PD translations)

### A. *The Gay Science* §125 — The Madman (Thomas Common, 1910)

**Work:** *The Joyful Wisdom* (= *The Gay Science*), Book Third, §125
**Translation:** Thomas Common, 1910 (Project Gutenberg #52124 / #52881)
**Status:** VERIFIED word-for-word by the era-5 fact pack coordinator, 2026-06-12,
against the Gutenberg source (HTTP 200, string-matched). The era-5 pack confirmed both
the Thomas Common wording AND a critical variant note (see below).

**Verified Common wording (verbatim; use EXACTLY):**
> "God is dead! God remains dead! And we have killed him! How shall we console
> ourselves, the most murderous of all murderers? ... who will wipe the blood from us?"
> — *The Joyful Wisdom* §125, trans. Thomas Common (Gutenberg #52124)

**Full passage summary (for author context — the key-passage block uses the excerpt above,
but the author must understand the whole):** A madman lights a lantern on a bright morning
and runs to the marketplace crying "I seek God! I seek God!" The crowd — already skeptical
believers who no longer believe — mock him. He turns on them: "God is dead! God remains
dead! And we have killed him!" Then he asks the horror questions: "How shall we console
ourselves... What festivals of atonement, what holy games shall we have to devise? Is not
the greatness of this deed too great for us? Must we not ourselves become gods?" He then
falls silent, smashes his lantern, and concludes: "I come too early... this tremendous
event is still on its way... This deed is still more distant from them than the most
distant stars — and yet they have done it themselves." He later enters churches and sings
a *Requiem aeternam deo* — a mass for the dead God.

**CRITICAL VARIANT NOTE (the line-wrap gotcha — carry forward from era-5 pack):**
The era-5 coordinator discovered that a Gutenberg line-wrap obscures "the most murderous
of all murderers." When section 125 is grep'd in single-line mode, only the first part
of the sentence appears, making the phrase appear absent. The full text (correctly wrapped)
contains "the most murderous of all murderers" in the Thomas Common translation.
**The phrase IS in Common. "Murderers of all murderers" (without "most") is the Kaufmann
wording. "Most murderous of all murderers" is the Common wording. Use the Common version.**

**Key interpretive frame (lock this for the prose — not to be simplified out):**
The declaration is NOT atheism as a boast or victory. It is a diagnosis of catastrophe:
European culture has effectively killed the Christian God (the Enlightenment did this
work), but the consequences — that the entire moral order of the West was grounded in
that God — have not been absorbed yet. The madman is "too early"; the news hasn't arrived.
The horror is the point.

**sourceUrl:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/52124

### B. *The Gay Science* §341 — The Greatest Weight / The Heaviest Burden (Thomas Common)

**Work:** *The Joyful Wisdom*, Book Fourth, §341
**Translation:** Thomas Common, 1910 (Gutenberg #52124)
**Status:** VERIFIED — content confirmed against multiple cross-referenced sources
(Wesleyan PDF of the section; Goodreads quotes cross-checked; quotepark.com).

**Verified wording (verbatim — cross-reference composite; author to re-check exact
Common wording against Gutenberg #52124 at authoring time; content confirmed, wording
confirmed from secondary sources quoting Common):**
> "What, if some day or night a demon were to steal after you into your loneliest
> loneliness and say to you: 'This life as you now live it and have lived it, you will
> have to live once more and innumerable times more; and there will be nothing new in
> it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and sigh and everything unutterably
> small or great in your life must return to you, all in the same succession and
> sequence — even this spider and this moonlight between the trees, and even this moment
> and I myself. The eternal hourglass of existence is turned upside down again and
> again — and you with it, speck of dust!' Would you not throw yourself down and gnash
> your teeth and curse the demon who spoke thus? Or have you once experienced a
> tremendous moment when you would have answered him: 'You are a god, and never have I
> heard anything more divine!'"

Note: the question at the end of the section is the test:
> "The question in each and every thing, 'Do you desire this once more and innumerable
> times more?' would lie upon your actions as the greatest weight!"

(This second clause's exact wording in Common varies slightly across editions;
the key phrase "greatest weight" is consistent. The "speck of dust" and "eternal
hourglass" lines are confirmed Common.)

**sourceUrl:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/52124

**Key interpretive frame (lock this):**
The thought is presented as a thought experiment, not a cosmological claim. Nietzsche
may not have believed it literally (scholars debate this). Its function is as a test:
could you will this life back, unchanged, infinitely? Every exit is blocked — no "next
time I'll do better," no edited replay. The weight is maximum. The person who could say
yes has achieved *amor fati* (love of fate). This connects to the Übermensch: the
overman is partly defined by the capacity to affirm eternal recurrence.

**Sils-Maria origin note:** Nietzsche reportedly had the eternal recurrence thought during
a walk near Sils-Maria in August 1881 — DOCUMENTED (biographies; SEP). He carried the
thought for years before publishing it in the Gay Science (1882) and developing it further
in Zarathustra.

### C. *The Gay Science* §343 — What Our Cheerfulness Signifies (Thomas Common)

**Work:** *The Joyful Wisdom*, Book Fifth (added in the 1887 second edition), §343
**Translation:** Thomas Common, 1910 (Gutenberg #52124)
**Status:** VERIFIED word-for-word by the era-5 coordinator, 2026-06-12 (HTTP 200,
string-matched against Gutenberg #52124; confirmed).

**Exact Common wording:**
> "*What our Cheerfulness Signifies.* The most important of more recent events — that
> 'God is dead,' that the belief in the Christian God has become unworthy of belief —
> already begins to cast its first shadows over Europe."
> — *The Joyful Wisdom* §343, trans. Thomas Common (Gutenberg #52124)

**Framing:** §343 is the CONSTRUCTIVE turn after §125's horror. The phrase "what our
cheerfulness signifies" is Nietzsche's own ironic register: yes, the death of God is
catastrophic — but for "free spirits" (the philosophically courageous), the destruction
of the old horizon opens a new one. He calls it "a new dawn." But the dawn is for the
few who can bear it; he is not announcing universal good news. The final section of Book
Five gestures toward the open sea — an image of dangerous, exhilarating freedom with no
shore in sight.

### D. *Thus Spake Zarathustra* — The Übermensch / Overman (Thomas Common, 1909)

**Work:** *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Prologue §3
**Translation:** Thomas Common, 1909 (Project Gutenberg #1998)
**Status:** VERIFIED (Prologue text confirmed at standardebooks.org; multiple sources).

**Exact Common wording (verbatim):**
> "I teach you the Superman. Man is something that is to be surpassed."

And immediately following:
> "What have ye done to surpass man?"

And shortly after:
> "Man is a rope stretched between the animal and the Superman — a rope over an abyss.
> A dangerous crossing, a dangerous wayfaring, a dangerous looking-back, a dangerous
> trembling and halting."

And:
> "What is great in man is that he is a bridge and not a goal: what is lovable in man
> is that he is an OVER-GOING and a DOWN-GOING."

**Translation note:** Thomas Common uses "Superman" for *Übermensch*. This was published
1909, before the comic-book character (1938). The modern preference, established by
Kaufmann (in copyright), is "overman." House voice should use **"overman"** and note
once that "Thomas Common's 1909 translation renders it 'Superman.'" Do not use "Superman"
in the narrative without this gloss; it misleads modern readers.

**sourceUrl:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1998

**What the Übermensch is NOT (lock this — the posthumous-misuse trap):**
- Not a specific race or people (the Aryan reading is Elisabeth's distortion).
- Not a biological type or a master race.
- Not Nietzsche's political program.

**What it IS:** An existential type or possibility — the kind of person who can bear the
death of God, create new values without divine backing, and affirm eternal recurrence.
It is a challenge, not a description of someone who already exists.

### E. *Twilight of the Idols* — "What Does Not Kill Me" (Anthony M. Ludovici, 1911)

**Work:** *Twilight of the Idols*, "MAXIMS AND MISSILES" (section title in Ludovici:
"MAXIMS AND MISSILES", not "Maxims and Arrows"), §8
**Translation:** Anthony M. Ludovici, 1911 (Project Gutenberg #52263)
**Status:** VERIFIED (era-5 fact pack; confirmed Ludovici text fetched).

**Exact wording (verbatim):**
> "From the military school of life. — That which does not kill me, makes me stronger."
> — *Twilight of the Idols*, "MAXIMS AND MISSILES" §8, trans. A. M. Ludovici (Gutenberg #52263)

**Apocryphal-quote status:** This IS genuinely Nietzsche's and is NOT apocryphal. The
phrase circulates widely in paraphrase ("what doesn't kill you makes you stronger") —
this is real, just modernized. The key: the "From the military school of life" tag is
load-bearing context — Nietzsche frames it as a compressed principle of hardship training,
not a cosmic reassurance or an endorsement of all harm.

**sourceUrl:** https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52263/52263-h/52263-h.htm

### F. *On the Genealogy of Morality* — The Slave Revolt (Horace B. Samuel, PD)

**Work:** *The Genealogy of Morals*, First Essay, §10
**Translation:** Horace B. Samuel (Project Gutenberg #52319)
**Status:** VERIFIED (fetched directly from Gutenberg #52319; text confirmed).

**Exact wording (verbatim):**
> "The revolt of the slaves in morals begins in the very principle of _resentment_
> becoming creative and giving birth to values — a resentment experienced by creatures
> who, deprived as they are of the proper outlet of action, are forced to find their
> compensation in an imaginary revenge."
> — *The Genealogy of Morals*, First Essay §10, trans. Horace B. Samuel (Gutenberg #52319)

And the contrast with aristocratic morality (same section):
> "While every aristocratic morality springs from a triumphant affirmation of its own
> demands, the slave morality says 'no' from the very outset to what is 'outside itself,'
> 'different from itself,' and 'not itself': and this 'no' is its creative deed."

**sourceUrl:** https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52319/52319-h/52319-h.htm

**Key interpretive frame (the steelman — lock this):** Nietzsche is making a GENEALOGICAL
claim — tracing the historical-psychological origins of moral vocabulary — not a normative
judgment that the masters were right or the weak are contemptible. His point: our current
moral feelings (pity, equality, humility as virtues) grew from reactive resentment, not
from creative affirmation. His challenge is to ask: are we holding these values because
we've examined them, or because we inherited them in our sleep? The real target is
life-hating morality in any form, not "the weak" as a class.

**Note on the French word:** Nietzsche uses the French *ressentiment* deliberately as
a technical term — not ordinary English resentment (a passing feeling) but a chronic,
bottled, creative-destructive psychic state. Keep the French word in prose.

### G. *Beyond Good and Evil* — Antisemitism passage (Helen Zimmern, 1907)

**Work:** *Beyond Good and Evil*, §251
**Translation:** Helen Zimmern, 1907 (Project Gutenberg #4363; also Marxists.org)
**Status:** VERIFIED (full text of §251 fetched from Marxists.org, which hosts the
Zimmern text).

**Key phrases from §251 (these are exact Zimmern wording):**
The section opens by listing German national "stupidities" including: "the anti-French
folly, the anti-Semitic folly, the anti-Polish folly." Later in the section he recommends
"it would perhaps be useful and fair to **banish the anti-Semitic bawlers out of the
country.**"

**Note on the full passage:** §251 is a complicated text. Nietzsche:
(a) Praises Jewish intellectual vitality ("the strongest, toughest, and purest race at
    present living in Europe");
(b) Describes the German instinct against further Jewish immigration as a real phenomenon;
(c) Then explicitly condemns antisemitism as one of the German "follies" and recommends
    expelling "the anti-Semitic bawlers."

The honest framing: Nietzsche held a complex, often self-contradicting set of views on
Jews — sharply different from antisemitism as an ideology, but not entirely free of
stereotyping. His opposition to antisemitism as a movement is explicit and documented.
His admiration for Jewish intellectual achievement is genuine in the text. His early
letters contain some casual anti-Jewish remarks (historians note these as "thoughtless
decoration rather than deep conviction" — Holub). The posthumous Nazi appropriation of
his work runs directly against his documented published positions.

**sourceUrl:** https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/nietzsche/1886/beyond-good-evil/ch08.htm

### H. *Beyond Good and Evil* §22 — Perspectivism (Helen Zimmern, 1907)

**Work:** *Beyond Good and Evil*, §22
**Translation:** Helen Zimmern, 1907 (Gutenberg #4363)
**Status:** VERIFIED (content confirmed; exact wording partially fetched from Marxists.org
and Gutenberg excerpts; author to re-confirm full §22 text at authoring time).

**Confirmed Zimmern wording fragment (partial — exact):**
> "Let me be pardoned, as an old philologist who cannot desist from the mischief of
> putting his finger on bad modes of interpretation, but 'Nature's conformity to law,'
> of which you physicists talk so proudly, as though — why, it exists only owing to
> your interpretation and bad 'philology.'"

**Published perspectivism locus:** BGE §22 is where Nietzsche publishes his perspectivism
in its clearest form. The argument: what physicists call "laws of nature" are not texts
read straight off reality — they are interpretations that reflect the interpreter's
grammar and values. The closing move: "we should not forget that this is also only
interpretation — and you will be eager enough to make this objection? — well, so much
the better." Even the counter-reading is an interpretation.

**The notebook fragment (DISTINGUISH THIS):**
The famous "there are no facts, only interpretations" line comes from Nietzsche's
**unpublished notebooks** (Nachlass, Summer 1886–Fall 1887), NOT a published text.
More precise wording from the notebooks: "facts are precisely what there is not, only
interpretations." Status: DOCUMENTED (confirmed as genuine Nietzsche; published in
vol. 17 of the Stanford Complete Works and other editions of the Nachlass), but it is a
**private note**, not a published claim. Ship the Nachlass version clearly labeled as
unpublished, and use the published BGE §22 as the anchor.

**sourceUrl:** https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4363/4363-h/4363-h.htm

### I. *The Gay Science* §270 — "Become what thou art" (Thomas Common)

**Work:** *The Joyful Wisdom*, §270
**Translation:** Thomas Common, 1910 (Gutenberg #52124)
**Status:** PARAPHRASE-ONLY for exact wording — the phrase "thou shalt become what thou
art" / "become the person you are" is VERIFIED as the content of §270 from multiple
secondary sources citing Common; exact full-text of §270 not independently fetched.

**The Pindar chain (MUST note this):** "Become who you are" is Nietzsche quoting (and
transforming) the ancient Greek poet **Pindar**. The source is Pindar's *Pythian Odes*
II.72 (c. 476 BC): "Become such as you are, having learned what that is" (trans. William
H. Race). Nietzsche uses it in §270 of the Gay Science and elsewhere, adapting it as an
imperative for self-creation. Nietzsche is not the originator of the phrase; he is its
greatest modern popularizer. The chain is: Pindar → Nietzsche. **Flag this in any
passage that attributes "become who you are" to Nietzsche without noting Pindar.**

## §4 — IDEAS AS DOCUMENTED POSITIONS (thinker-depth; complement to era-5 §2)

### The Death of God — diagnosis, not boast

The era-5 pack §2 and §10 (pinned) covers this thoroughly. Key additional thinker-depth
frame:

The Gay Science has THREE "God is dead" moments:
1. **§108** (Book Third): "God is dead; but given the way of men, there may still be
   caves for thousands of years in which his shadow will be shown — and we — we still
   have to vanquish his shadow, too." (VERIFIED Common wording, era-5 pack.) This is
   the quiet announcement.
2. **§125** (Book Third): The madman parable — the public, dramatic announcement;
   the diagnosis of horror and responsibility. "The most murderous of all murderers."
3. **§343** (Book Fifth, added 1887): "What our Cheerfulness Signifies" — the
   constructive response; the "free spirits" can experience this as liberation.

The philosophical argument beneath all three: for over a millennium, European morality's
authority derived from the Christian God. Enlightenment reason has eroded the credibility
of that God. But morality cannot simply detach from its theological roots and survive:
it borrowed its authority from the theology. When the theology goes, the entire structure
of values loses its foundation. The result is nihilism — the absence of any ground for
saying anything matters. Nietzsche is NOT a nihilist cheerleader; he is diagnosing the
coming crisis and asking what could possibly replace the foundation.

### The Will to Power — corrected account

The era-5 pack §2 covers this. Thinker-depth additions:

The concept is Nietzsche's correction of Schopenhauer's "will to live" (Wille zum Leben).
Schopenhauer held that a blind, suffering will drives everything; the right response is
to quiet the will. Nietzsche accepts the idea of a deep driving force but rejects:
(a) That the primary drive is survival/persistence — it is expansion, overcoming;
(b) That the right response is quieting or flight — it is affirmation.

The will to power in Nietzsche's published works is primarily a psychological/biological
force, not a political program:
- In BGE §13 (Zimmern): "A living body's cardinal instinct is above all to discharge its
  strength — self-preservation is only one of the indirect and most frequent results thereof."
- In BGE §36 (Zimmern): "...the world seen from within... would simply be 'Will to Power,'
  and nothing else." (PARAPHRASE-ONLY for exact wording — content confirmed, full text
  needs author-stage verification.)
- The Genealogy applies it to the psychological mechanism of value creation (slaves
  turning resentment into a creative force — that too is a will to power).

**The *Will to Power* compilation TRAP (carry from era-5 §8):**
The book titled *The Will to Power* is NOT a work Nietzsche published. It is a posthumous
compilation by his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche (published 1901, expanded 1906),
assembled from his unpublished notebooks. The scholarly consensus (Kaufmann, Hollingdale,
Montinari, and the Stanford/Colli-Montinari critical edition) is that it does not reflect
Nietzsche's intent and was selectively constructed to support a nationalist reading.
Mazzino Montinari, the definitive editor of Nietzsche's Nachlass, called it "a forgery"
(though this is the strongest version of the claim — other scholars say "selective distortion").
**Never cite *The Will to Power* as a book Nietzsche wrote.**

### Eternal Recurrence — thought experiment, not cosmology

Thinker-depth additions to era-5 §2:

Nietzsche first mentions the thought in Gay Science §341 (1882). He develops it further
in *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (especially Part III, "The Vision and the Riddle" and "The
Convalescent"). In *Ecce Homo* he called the thought the "fundamental conception" of
*Zarathustra*.

**Whether Nietzsche literally believed it:** This is contested. Some scholars (e.g., Loeb)
argue he believed it as a cosmological hypothesis; others argue it was always a hypothetical
or thought experiment. Nietzsche did sketch some naturalistic arguments for it in his
notebooks. The philosophical function (the test of amor fati) does not depend on its
literal truth; the text presents it that way. Ship as: primarily a thought experiment with
contested cosmological status.

**Amor fati:** Latin "love of fate" — the response Nietzsche advocates to eternal
recurrence. Not mere acceptance but active affirmation of one's actual life, including
its worst moments, with nothing edited out. Appears in Gay Science §276: "my formula for
greatness in a human being is amor fati: that one wants nothing to be different, not
forward, not backward, not in all eternity." — PARAPHRASE-ONLY for exact Common wording;
content confirmed.

### Master/Slave Morality and Ressentiment — the steelman

Thinker-depth: the genealogical method is central.

Nietzsche does not argue normatively that "masters are better" or "slaves deserve
contempt." He argues GENEALOGICALLY: this is how our moral vocabulary got its shape.
The First Essay of the Genealogy traces the historical-psychological process:

1. "Good/bad" is the master's distinction — affirmative, first-person, un-resentful.
   "Good" = noble, vital, powerful; "bad" (German *schlecht*) = common, weak, low.
2. "Good/evil" is the slave's distinction — reactive, second-person, born from ressentiment.
   "Evil" = the master, revalued as villain; "good" = the slave's own weakness, revalued
   as virtue (humility, patience, meekness).
3. The slave revolt in morals SUCCEEDED: Christianity, and later democratic equality,
   are its children. We live inside the value vocabulary the slaves created.

**What Nietzsche is actually asking:** Are you holding your moral commitments because you
have examined them and affirm them freely? Or because you absorbed them in a tradition
that was shaped by resentment you weren't part of? This is uncomfortable regardless of
where you sit on the political spectrum.

**The potted-summary trap:** "Nietzsche thought weak people were bad and strong people
were good." Wrong framing. His targets are *reactive*, life-hating values — which the
powerful can hold just as much as the weak. The authentic artist or philosopher who
affirms life from genuine creative strength is his exemplar, not the political strongman.

### Perspectivism — the published version

No view from nowhere. Every interpretation is made from a situated standpoint.
**The published version** (BGE §22 — Zimmern verified): what we call "laws of nature"
are interpretations that reflect our grammar and values. Even the alternative
interpretation is still an interpretation — perspectivism does not undercut itself
by claiming to be the one true view.

**What perspectivism is NOT:** It is NOT relativism in the sense that "any opinion is
as good as any other." Nietzsche believed some interpretations were richer, more
life-affirming, more honest. The denial of a God's-eye view is not the same as
saying all views are equally valid. He explicitly attacked interpretations he considered
life-denying, impoverished, or dishonest.

**The notebook version** ("facts are precisely what there is not, only interpretations"):
Unpublished Nachlass. DOCUMENTED but NOT published by Nietzsche. Distinguish carefully.

### The Apollonian and the Dionysian (*The Birth of Tragedy*, 1872)

The earliest of Nietzsche's major concepts. The Apollonian (from Apollo, god of light
and form) represents clarity, order, individuation, measure, beautiful illusion — the art
of sculpture and epic. The Dionysian (from Dionysus, god of wine and ecstasy) represents
chaos, dissolution of individual boundaries, music, primal unity, suffering borne through
ecstasy. Greek tragedy, Nietzsche argues, was the supreme art form because it held both
in tension: the Dionysian chaos of music and myth given Apollonian form and structure.
The Socratic tradition (reason, optimism, dialectic) killed tragedy by privileging
rational analysis over aesthetic experience.

**Status of this concept in his later work:** Nietzsche substantially revised and moved
past the specific Apollonian/Dionysian framing of *Birth of Tragedy* — in his 1886
preface ("An Attempt at Self-Criticism") he criticizes the book's Schopenhauerian and
Wagnerian overlays. The concepts survive in modified form (the Dionysian as life-affirmation)
throughout his later work. State this revision history if the concept is covered.

### Revaluation of All Values (*Umwertung aller Werte*)

Nietzsche's announced project in his last productive year (1888). The task after the
death of God: all inherited values — which grew from the slave morality tradition —
need to be re-examined and re-evaluated from a life-affirming standpoint. He announced
it would be a multi-volume work; *The Antichrist* (1888) was published as a first
installment (or possibly as the entire first "Book" of this projected work — scholars
debate). He never completed the larger project; the breakdown intervened.

## §6 — HONESTY SECTIONS

### 6.A — The posthumous misuse block (REQUIRED — framing gate)

The facts, in order:

1. **Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche** (1846–1935) was Nietzsche's younger sister.
   In 1885 she married Bernhard Förster (1843–1889), a schoolteacher and virulent
   antisemitic agitator who had organized a petition against Jewish influence in German
   schools. Nietzsche broke with her over this marriage and mocked Förster explicitly
   in his letters. DOCUMENTED (Wikipedia; SEP; letters cited by biographers; Holub).

2. **Nueva Germania (1887–1893):** Elisabeth and Bernhard emigrated to Paraguay to
   found an Aryan German colony. Bernhard committed suicide there in 1889 (the same
   year as Nietzsche's breakdown). Elisabeth returned to Germany. DOCUMENTED.

3. **Control of the archive:** From 1894, Elisabeth took control of the Nietzsche-Archiv
   (established in Naumburg, later moved to Weimar) and shaped the reception of
   Nietzsche's work for the next four decades until her death in 1935. DOCUMENTED.

4. **The Will to Power compilation:** Elisabeth assembled *Der Wille zur Macht* from
   Nietzsche's unpublished notebooks, publishing it in 1901 and expanding it in 1906.
   She selected and ordered the material to support a nationalist, proto-fascist reading.
   This "book" is NOT a work Nietzsche wrote; it is her editorial construction.
   **The scholarly consensus (Kaufmann, Hollingdale, Montinari) is that it distorts
   Nietzsche's thought.** The motivation is debated (deliberate falsification vs.
   true-believer distortion vs. protective intent) — the distorting EFFECT is not
   disputed. DOCUMENTED. Ship as: "assembled... selecting and ordering them to support
   her own nationalist, antisemitic sympathies; the effect is documented, the intent
   still debated by scholars." (era-5 pack §10 item 3 wording — carry forward.)

5. **The Nazi appropriation:** The Nietzsche-Archiv became associated with the
   cultural-nationalist program. Hitler attended Elisabeth's funeral in 1935.
   DOCUMENTED (Wikipedia; multiple biographies). Do NOT say Hitler endorsed Nietzsche's
   published philosophy — he endorsed Elisabeth's curated version of it. The
   distinction matters.

6. **Nietzsche's own documented views:** His published works and private letters make
   his contempt for antisemitism and German nationalism explicit:
   - BGE §251 (Zimmern, VERIFIED): lists "the anti-Semitic folly" among German national
     stupidities and recommends banishing "the anti-Semitic bawlers out of the country."
   - *The Antichrist* attacks nationalist Christianity directly.
   - Letters to his sister: he broke off correspondence with her over her marriage to
     Förster, calling Förster derogatory names in letters to others. DOCUMENTED.
   - The "All anti-Semites ought to be shot" postcard: LATE/INFORMAL — real but
     informal; use the published-works evidence as the primary documentation; name
     the postcard as secondary color.

7. **The Übermensch is not an Aryan superman.** It is an existential type.
   Nietzsche explicitly rejected biological and racial readings. The "master race"
   reading is Elisabeth's distortion. DOCUMENTED (SEP; published scholarship).

### 6.B — Nietzsche's views on women (MUST name honestly)

Nietzsche wrote scattered remarks on women throughout *Beyond Good and Evil* (especially
Part 7, §231–239), *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, and the Genealogy. These range from
performative provocation to passages that read as plain contempt:

- BGE §232 (Zimmern): "What is truth to a woman! From the very first nothing has been
  more alien, repugnant, inimical to woman than truth — her great art is the lie, her
  supreme concern is appearance and beauty." (content VERIFIED from secondary sources
  citing Zimmern; exact wording to be confirmed at authoring time).
- BGE §239 (Zimmern): "Woman wants to be independent — and thereupon she begins to
  enlighten men about 'woman as such': this is one of the worst developments of the
  general uglification of Europe." (content VERIFIED from secondary sources; confirm
  exact wording.)

**Scholarly debate:** Some scholars read these passages as deliberate provocation or as
part of a broader critique of received ideas (the view that Nietzsche was mocking the
shallow versions of feminism his contemporaries were promoting, not women as such).
Others read them as expressions of actual contempt. The passages are genuinely contested.

**Ship this honestly:** Name the passages as a genuine surface in his work that is
difficult and contested; do not explain them away ("he was being ironic"), but do not
make them the whole man ("he was a misogynist and that's all") either. The era-5
narrative's formulation ("range from performative provocation to what reads as plain
contempt... a genuinely difficult surface in his work") is the correct register.

## §7 — INFLUENCE CHAIN (thinker-level; consistent with era-5 §6)

### Answered (what Nietzsche inherited and broke from):

| Source | Connection | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Schopenhauer** | Will to live → will to power; pessimism rebutted but drive-concept kept; *Birth of Tragedy* is Schopenhauerian; Nietzsche was a self-described disciple until ~1876 | Edge: schopenhauer → nietzsche |
| **Heraclitus** | Change, flux, and strife as the engine of existence; "the Dionysian" has Heraclitean roots; Nietzsche admired Heraclitus explicitly in early lectures | Edge: heraclitus → nietzsche |
| **Wagner** | Early inspiration for *Birth of Tragedy*; the Dionysian rebirth idea was partly Wagnerian; the break with Wagner was the break from Romanticism | Influence-then-reaction |
| **Hegel** | Nietzsche attacks the Hegelian system (Zarathustra's "spirit of gravity"; the "God is dead" event is in part the death of Hegelian idealism's God) | Reaction against |
| **Pre-Socratics generally** | Nietzsche taught classical philology; his first-love was the pre-Socratics, especially Heraclitus; early lecture notes (*Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks*) engage them directly | Intellectual inheritance |
| **French moralists (La Rochefoucauld, etc.)** | *Human, All Too Human*'s aphoristic style and psychological analysis owes something to the French moraliste tradition | Stylistic influence |

### Answered by (who Nietzsche fed):

| Receiver | How Nietzsche influenced them |
|---|---|
| **Heidegger** | *Being and Time* engages Nietzsche's problem of nihilism; Heidegger's Dasein has Nietzschean roots; his 4-vol *Nietzsche* lectures are foundational 20th-c Nietzsche scholarship |
| **Foucault** | Genealogy as method (power-knowledge genealogies of discipline, sexuality, madness) is directly Nietzschean; Foucault credits Nietzsche explicitly |
| **Existentialism** | Kierkegaard and Nietzsche are the two ancestors of 20th-c existentialism (Sartre, Camus, de Beauvoir); neither called themselves existentialists |

| **Depth psychology** | Freud claimed not to have read Nietzsche (probably false — he owned volumes); the will to power, ressentiment, and the unconscious drives have clear parallels; Adler acknowledged the debt directly |
| **20th-c fascism (via misreading)** | Through Elisabeth's distortion; this is a misuse, not a genuine philosophical inheritance — state this explicitly |
