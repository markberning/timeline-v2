## Structure of the book (section names — all confirmed, used as the read's spine)
[Confirmed via Wikipedia "Fear and Trembling" + D. Anthony Storm's commentary
(sorenkierkegaard.org/fear-and-trembling.html) + SparkNotes/CourseHero section guides.]
- ✅ **Epigraph** (Hamann / Tarquinius poppies) → Ch1 epigraph.
- ✅ **Exordium** (a.k.a. Attunement / *Stemning*; a "tuning"/prologue). Contains **four
  different retellings** of Abraham's ride to Moriah. [Confirmed: Storm commentary — "The
  exordium consists of four different and fantastic versions of the story of Abraham's ascent
  to the mount to sacrifice his son."] → Ch2 ¶1–3.
- ✅ **Eulogy on Abraham** (Speech in Praise of Abraham, *Lovtale over Abraham*). The greatness
  scale (against the world / against oneself / against God) and "no one can understand him"
  are the section's substance. [Confirmed: Wikipedia "Eulogy on Abraham"; standard summaries.]
  → Ch2 ¶4–5.
- ✅ **Preliminary Expectoration** (*Foreløbig Expectoration*; lit. a throat-clearing /
  coughing-up). Holds infinite resignation vs faith, the **princess** example, **"by virtue
  of the absurd,"** getting the finite back, and the **knight of faith who looks like a tax
  collector / bourgeois philistine**. [Confirmed: Wikipedia (knight of infinite resignation
  vs knight of faith, "by virtue of the absurd," "an additional movement beyond the finality
  … restoration"); Storm commentary ("they who carry the treasure of faith … externally they
  have a striking resemblance to bourgeois philistinism").] → Ch3.
- ✅ **Problema I** — "Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical?" Abraham vs the
  **tragic hero**. → Ch4.
- ✅ **Problema II** — "Is there an absolute duty to God?" → Ch5.
- ✅ **Problema III** — "Was Abraham ethically defensible in keeping silent …?" Abraham
  **cannot speak**; indirect communication. [Confirmed: Storm commentary — "Abraham remains
  silent — but he cannot speak. Therein lies the distress and anxiety."] → Ch6.

## Genesis 22 (the spine story — told plainly; biblical facts, not Kierkegaard's wording)
- ✅ God commands Abraham to take Isaac to **the land of Moriah** and offer him as a burnt
  offering; Abraham rises early, saddles the donkey, splits the wood, travels **three days**,
  Isaac carries the wood, Abraham carries the fire and the knife; Isaac asks where the lamb
  is, Abraham says God will provide; he builds the altar, binds Isaac, raises the knife; an
  **angel** stops him; a **ram caught in a thicket** is offered instead. [Genesis 22:1–13,
  standard biblical text — public domain narrative facts.] Used in throughline, hook ¶2,
  Ch1 ¶4, Ch6 ¶1. The "three days" point is the book's own emphasis (the suspense the reader's
  foreknowledge erases), foregrounded by Kierkegaard; framed here as the book's reading.
- ✅ Isaac = the long-awaited son of Abraham and Sarah, the child of the promise (descendants
  to outnumber the stars). [Genesis 15:5; 17–18; 21.] Used in hook ¶2, Ch1 ¶4.

## The Hamann epigraph (the ONE quoted line — NOT Kierkegaard's words)
- ✅ Quoted line: **"What Tarquinius Superbus said in the garden by means of the poppies, the
  son understood but the messenger did not."** Attributed `— J.G. Hamann, the epigraph
  Kierkegaard set on the title page of *Fear and Trembling* (1843)`.
  - Source / verification: WebSearch (litencyc + sorenkierkegaard.org commentaries) returned
    this exact wording as the book's epigraph, attributing it to **J.G. Hamann**. The

    poppies to signal his son to kill the leading men of Gabii; the messenger sees only
    flowers cut) is the classical anecdote (Livy I.54; Ovid *Fasti* II) Hamann alludes to.
  - PD status: **Hamann died 1788** → the sentence is centuries out of copyright; the English
    rendering of this single short line is the standard one carried in the book's front matter
    and in PD-era discussions. This is the only quotation in the read and it is **not**
    Kierkegaard's prose, so the era-5 paraphrase-only rule (which governs Kierkegaard's own

## Paraphrase epigraphs (Ch2–6) — each is a faithful PARAPHRASE, NO quote marks, of the
named section's content. None reproduces Kierkegaard's translated wording.
- Ch2 (Eulogy): "No one was as great as Abraham, and no one is able to understand him." —
  paraphrase of the Eulogy's twin claims (Abraham the greatest; none can understand him).
- Ch3 (Prelim. Expectoration): "The knight of faith gives everything up and then receives it
  back whole, on the strength of the absurd." — paraphrase of the two-movements /
  by-virtue-of-the-absurd doctrine.
- Ch4 (Problema I): "Either there is a suspension of the ethical for the single individual,
  or Abraham is lost." — paraphrase of the chapter's either/or (the suspension or Abraham is a
  murderer). The word "lost" is my paraphrase, not a quotation.
- Ch5 (Problema II): "Faith is the paradox that the single individual stands in an absolute
  relation to the absolute." — paraphrase of the book's central formula (the absolute relation
  to the absolute). Stated as paraphrase, no quote marks.
- Ch6 (Problema III): "Abraham cannot speak; that is his agony, for what he could explain
  would no longer be Abraham." — paraphrase of the silence argument (he cannot speak / to
  explain would dissolve the absolute relation).

## Doctrinal claims in the read (all standard scholarship on the book)
- ✅ **Infinite resignation vs faith**; resignation gives up the finite for the infinite and
  expects nothing back; faith gives it up AND expects it back **in this life**, by virtue of
  the absurd. [Confirmed: Wikipedia knight-of-resignation vs knight-of-faith; standard.]
  Ch3 ¶2–5.
- ✅ **The princess example** (a man loves an unattainable princess; resignation keeps the love
  eternal while renouncing her; faith expects to get her after all). [Standard summary of the
  Preliminary Expectoration; SparkNotes/CourseHero.] Ch3 ¶2–4.
- ✅ **The knight of faith looks like a tax collector / bourgeois shopkeeper** who enjoys the
  finite world (the Sunday walk, the dinner) and is indistinguishable from an ordinary
  citizen; the knight of resignation, by contrast, is marked by visible nobility/melancholy.
  [Confirmed: Storm commentary "striking resemblance to bourgeois philistinism"; the
  tax-collector image is from the section.] Ch3 ¶6. ("tax collector" is the standard rendering
  of the figure; used as a label, not a quotation.)
- ✅ **Teleological suspension of the ethical** = the ethical (= the universal, after Hegel) is
  suspended for the single individual for the sake of a higher telos (God); the individual
  stands ABOVE the universal. [Confirmed: Wikipedia; Storm commentary "Abraham … transgressed
  the ethical altogether."] Ch4.
- ✅ **The tragic hero stays within the ethical** — sacrifices a lower duty to a higher duty,
  both within the universal, and is therefore intelligible and admired. Examples named by
  Kierkegaard: **Agamemnon** (sacrifices Iphigenia at Aulis so the becalmed Greek fleet can
  sail for Troy), **Jephthah** (sacrifices his daughter to fulfill a vow to God after victory
  over the Ammonites), **Junius Brutus** (condemns his own sons to death for conspiring
  against the Roman state). [Confirmed: WebSearch returned all three named together as
  Problema I's tragic-hero examples — Agamemnon/Iphigenia/Troy, Jephthah/daughter/vow/
  Ammonites, Brutus/sons/state; Storm commentary confirms Agamemnon in detail + "the tragic
  hero is still within the ethical."] Agamemnon is worked **in full** (the brief's
  instruction); Jephthah and Brutus are named as Kierkegaard's other two tragic heroes. Ch4
  ¶3–5.
- ✅ **Absolute duty to God** (Problema II) — if duty to God just = ethical duty (the Hegelian
  fusion), the phrase is empty and Abraham is condemned; the book asserts a duty to God that
  can override the universal, i.e. **the single individual in an absolute relation to the
  absolute**. [Confirmed: Wikipedia; standard.] Ch5.
- ✅ **Abraham's silence** (Problema III) — he tells no one (not Sarah, not the servants); to
  Isaac's question he says only that God will provide; he is silent because he **cannot speak**
  in a way that would make him understood, since his absolute relation to God cannot be
  translated into the universal terms speech runs on; faith is **indirect communication**.
  [Confirmed: Storm commentary "Abraham remains silent — but he cannot speak"; the
  God-will-provide answer is Genesis 22:8.] Ch6.
- ✅ **The anti-Hegel frame** — the book sets the single individual ABOVE the universal,
  against the Hegelian doctrine that the individual finds his truth by being mediated into the
  shared ethical order (family/society/state, *Sittlichkeit*). [Standard reading; the
  philosophy-data.ts thread `hegel → kierkegaard "reacted: the System is a lie"` corroborates
  the antagonism.] Throughline, brk, Ch4–5. Hegel cross-linked `/philosophy/thinker/hegel`.
