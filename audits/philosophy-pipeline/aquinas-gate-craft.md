# Aquinas thinker read — Gates 2, 3, 4 critique
**Storytelling & Understanding · Comprehensiveness · Clarity**
Critic run: 2026-06-13

---

## Overview

This is the strongest thinker deep-read in the vertical so far. The break block
is superb, the Five Ways chapter is the best single chapter in the whole
philosophy corpus, and the sustained anti-caricature move in Ch5 (angels-on-a-pin
as Protestant propaganda) earns every word. The voice is exactly right —
plain, confident, occasionally dry — and the three-part understanding test
(worked example / steelman / plain restatement) passes almost everywhere.

The gaps are real but local. There is no missing chapter here (unlike Aristotle,
which was missing two). But four things are MUST-FIX before ship: the
essence/existence distinction is load-bearing for the Third Way and the break
block, and gets no worked example; the analogy of being (*analogia entis*) —
a central Aquinas idea, gated in the fact pack, and the hinge between him and
Scotus/Ockham — is entirely absent; natural law's first principle is treated
abstractly and needs one concrete application to land; and the Scotus hinge
in Ch6 is too thin to make the Ockham-nominalism move intelligible. Everything
else is FIX or SHOULD-FIX. No full rewrites required; the existing prose is
worth keeping. The draft is FIX-THEN-SHIP.

## Per-chapter verdicts — Gate 2 (Storytelling & Understanding)

### Break block ("Pagan reason is a danger" → "Pagan reason is faith's own
instrument") — **STRONG**

The three-paragraph break is the best opening in the vertical. The "bomb" metaphor
in the hook (chapter 1 seeds it, the break block lands it), the steelman of BOTH
the theologians who wanted Aristotle quarantined AND the Latin Averroists, and
the "one truth, not two truth-tracks" resolution all earn their place. The
grace/nature sentence is the key passage — quoted from the verified fact pack,
placed in bold where it belongs, and unpacked clearly.

The "chandelier" analogy from the fact pack (simultaneous dependence) does not
appear in the break block, but the block doesn't need it — the "second light"
analogy is more accessible and earns the same point. No structural gap here.

**Three-part test (faith/reason synthesis — the make-or-break idea):**
- Worked example: YES — the two-Summa comparison (ST argues from revelation,
  *contra Gentiles* holds it back) is the lived demonstration of the faith/reason
  map. Clean worked example.
- Strongest version: YES — the "double truth" alarm is steelmanned: "the fear it
  named was real: that reason and faith were drifting into two unconnected worlds."
- Plain restatement: YES — "There are not two truths to ride. There is one."

### Ch1 — "The dumb ox" — **GOOD**

The biography-as-backdrop problem is handled well: the chapter is biography, but
it earns the label because the *point* being taught is the Aristotelian problem
arriving in Paris and the kind of mind needed to absorb it. The mendicant vow
("barefoot begging order") gets a plain-language gloss immediately, which is the
right instinct. The confinement duration is handled with exactly the right
ambiguity ("about a year, possibly longer") per the fact pack. The "dumb ox"
legend is flagged as tradition — twice, with the transmission note in paragraph 4.

The "second Paris term" paragraph is the chapter's intellectual payoff and it
delivers: the reader learns that the Latin Averroist crisis called the heaviest
mind in the room, which sets up Ch2's architecture question perfectly.

**Three-part test (the chapter's teaching: why the Aristotle arrival was a
crisis):**
- Worked example: YES — three named alarming theses (eternal world, shared
  intellect, philosophy running where it leads) concretize the threat.
- Strongest version: YES — the theologians' alarm is called "not stupid" and its
  logic is given before Aquinas's response is introduced.
- Plain restatement: YES — "The dumb ox absorbed the bomb."

**One gap (SHOULD-FIX):** The chapter ends on the second Paris term without
paying off WHY the crisis came to a head in 1268–72 specifically — what the 1270
condemnations were or why they forced the issue. The sentence "the Latin Averroist
crisis, the alarm over how far Aristotle could be pushed, and the Paris
condemnations of 1270" names them but does not explain them. A reader finishing
Ch1 knows Thomas was sent because Paris was "on fire" — but doesn't quite know
what the fire was burning. One clause could close this: the 1270 condemnations
banned specific Aristotelian theses in the arts faculty, naming propositions
about the intellect and the world's eternity. This is a SHOULD-FIX (the fire is
named in the break block and Ch2; Ch1 just drops the thread one sentence early).

### Ch2 — "Two routes to one truth" — **STRONG**

The best architecture chapter in the vertical. The chapter opens by naming the
two wrong readings (rationalist fantasy / fideist retreat), states the actual
claim with precision, and then — crucially — gives the reader the preamble/mystery
distinction (*praeambula fidei* / *mysteria fidei*) in plain language before the
two-Summa worked example closes it. The sequence is perfect: claim → common
misreading → the real distinction → structural proof (two books, two premises).

The Latin Averroist resolution is tight and honest: the "double truth"
caricature is identified as a bishop's caricature, the real Averroes is cleared
(cross-reference to era 2 ch4), and the genuine fear is given its due. No
strawmanning.

**Three-part test (faith/reason architecture — the chapter's one idea):**
- Worked example: YES — the two-Summa structure is the demonstration, not just
  an analogy.
- Strongest version: YES — "not that reason can prove all of Christianity" / "not
  that faith and reason occupy separate walled-off rooms" sets up the strongest
  version clearly before it arrives.
- Plain restatement: YES — "Reason gets you to the door of the faith; it does not
  get you inside. That is the whole point of dividing the work in two."

**One gap (MUST-FIX — Understanding):** The chapter establishes the faith/reason
architecture but never works the **essence/existence distinction** that makes
the Third Way's necessary-being argument coherent. The Third Way (Ch3) claims
that contingent things need a ground because their essence doesn't include their
existence — but that key move is never built. The distinction gets a solid section
in the fact pack (§4: *De Ente et Essentia*, act/potency background, Avicenna
inheritance). The break block and Ch2 both pass over it. As a result, when Ch3
arrives at "something necessarily exists of itself," the reader lacks the
metaphysical vocabulary to see why that follows. Either a short paragraph in
Ch2 or an opening paragraph in Ch4 ("The law and the soul" — which already does
hylomorphism) should introduce essence vs. existence before the Third Way is
invoked. See Comprehensiveness gate M-1 below. **MUST-FIX** (structural gap in
the influence chain; the Third Way is partially unintelligible without it).

### Ch3 — "The Five Ways" — **STRONG**

The best chapter in the draft — possibly the best single chapter in the whole
vertical. The First Way is walked step by step with the exact Dominican Province
translation, the act/potency machinery is unpacked in plain language, and the
anti-domino guardrail paragraph is the pipeline's "make them GET it" requirement
executed at its absolute best. The chandelier analogy (not used in the draft, but
the simultaneous-dependence argument is walked in full) is replaced by the
"hand moves the staff at this moment" illustration, which is cleaner and more
vivid. The "Aquinas held that reason alone cannot prove the world ever had a
beginning" proof-of-interpretation is a genuine teaching moment.

The two self-raised objections (problem of evil; proto-Ockham razor) are the
chapter's signature move — "a mind that arms its own enemies that well, inside
the very article where it argues the opposite, is not running propaganda." That
sentence is the chapter's closer and earns it.

**Three-part test (the First Way — make-or-break idea):**
- Worked example: YES — "hand moves the staff this moment" / chandelier chain /
  "what is holding you up RIGHT NOW" all concretize the simultaneity point.
- Strongest version: YES — the anti-domino guardrail presents the correct
  interpretation at its most demanding before noting how it gets misread.
- Plain restatement: YES — "It is a claim about present dependence, not about
  ancient history."

**One gap (SHOULD-FIX — Understanding):** The **Fourth Way** (from degrees/
gradation) is the weakest of the five in the draft's treatment. The draft gives
it one compact sentence ("Things have more and less of qualities... there must
be something which is to all beings the cause of their being") but never explains
what is doing the philosophical work — the Platonic-participation inheritance
from the fact pack is acknowledged in a parenthetical, but the argument itself
(why does the fact that things come in degrees point to a maximum?) is not walked.
A 15-year-old reading this will nod through the Fourth Way without ever feeling
its pull. This is a SHOULD-FIX: one additional sentence explaining the move
("the graduated presence of a quality in many things points to a source that
has it fully and is the cause of it in the others — the way heat in many things
points to fire as its source") would let the reader feel the argument before
evaluating it.

### Ch4 — "The law and the soul" — **GOOD**

The natural-law four-tier hierarchy is clear, well-ordered, and concretely
illustrated (the speed-limit application of human law; the "interior life that no
human law could ever reach" for divine law). The soul-as-form section does the
hylomorphism work cleanly and the "not a ghost living inside a body" clarification
is vivid.

The chapter's triumph is the resurrection consequence: "bodily resurrection is
not a pious add-on, a sweetener tacked onto the real prize of an immortal soul.
It is a *metaphysical necessity* of his whole anthropology." That move — showing
the systematic consequence of soul-as-form in full — is the three-part test
passed at the highest level.

**Three-part test (natural law):**
- Worked example: PARTIAL — "Good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be
  avoided" is stated but never *applied* to a concrete case. The speed-limit
  illustration handles the human-law tier well, but natural law's first principle
  gets only the abstract formulation. What does "good is to be done" mean in a
  case a 15-year-old would recognize? See MUST-FIX below.
- Strongest version: YES — the universality claim ("the same for every rational
  being, in every culture, regardless of tradition or revelation") is the steelman
  of natural law and it is present.
- Plain restatement: YES — "because it is built into rational nature as such, the
  natural law is *universal*."

**MUST-FIX (Understanding — natural law worked example missing):** The natural
law's first principle ("Good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided")
is stated but never unpacked beyond its literal formulation. What does it mean for
a practical moral question? The fact pack gives the architecture but no worked
example. One sentence connecting it to a concrete case would close this: "From
that single principle Aquinas unpacks the whole of the natural law — that life is
to be preserved, that rational creatures are to live in community, that the truth
is to be told, that children are to be raised — all derived from the basic
inclinations built into what a human being is." Without that, the reader is told
"here is the first principle of all morality" and given only a tautology.
**MUST-FIX.**

**Three-part test (soul-as-form / resurrection):**
- Worked example: YES — "remove the soul and you do not get a body sitting empty;
  you get a corpse, which is a different kind of thing entirely."
- Strongest version: YES — the Platonic contrast is given its full force before
  the Aristotelian move.
- Plain restatement: YES — "A human being is not a soul plus a body; a human
  being is a single thing."

**One gap (SHOULD-FIX — Understanding):** The term *analogia entis* (the analogy
of being) is entirely absent from the draft. This is not a peripheral concept: it
is Aquinas's answer to how any language about God is possible at all (not
univocal, not equivocal, but analogical), and it is the direct precursor to Scotus's
*univocity of being* challenge that closes Ch6. Without the analogy of being in
place, Scotus's challenge ("Aquinas made God too remote — we can't say anything
about him") is undercut: the reader doesn't know what Scotus is rejecting. The
fact pack (§4) has a full section on it. See Comprehensiveness gate M-2 below.
This is a MUST-ADD, not merely SHOULD-FIX, because the Ch6 Scotus move depends
on it.

### Ch5 — "The Summa as a machine" — **STRONG**

This chapter takes on the hardest job in medieval philosophy writing — defending
Scholasticism against five centuries of caricature — and executes it without
a false note. The five-part article structure is walked with precision (the exact
sequence: question → objections → sed contra → respondeo → replies), the
"institutionalization of Abelard's *Sic et Non*" forward-thread is correct and
earns its place, and the angels-on-a-pin section is a masterclass in the
pipeline's anti-caricature requirement.

The angels-on-a-pin section in particular: it does not just debunk the fake
(naming Sclater 1619 and Chillingworth 1637 as the origin, with dates); it
exposes the *mechanism* of the caricature (Protestant polemic stripping a genuine
metaphysical question of its seriousness), and then it *gives the reader the real
question* so they can feel it. "How does a being with no body 'occupy' a location
at all?" is a live question in philosophy of mind. That is the pipeline's
three-part test in action: the real question, its strongest version, and a plain
restatement. Exemplary.

**Three-part test (the Summa's article structure — the chapter's one idea):**
- Worked example: YES — the God-exists article is walked as a live demonstration:
  the two objections (evil; proto-razor) are quoted in full, the sequence is
  named.
- Strongest version: YES — "a model of intellectual honesty that a great many
  later philosophers never matched."
- Plain restatement: YES — "Objection, counter-authority, answer, rebuttal.
  Every single time."

**One gap (SHOULD-FIX):** The chapter treats the *Summa*'s article structure as
Aquinas's invention, mentioning only Abelard's *Sic et Non* as the predecessor.
But the university *disputatio* — the actual live pedagogical event from which
the *Summa* articles descend — is not named. A 15-year-old would not know that
the *Summa* articles are the written transcription of a real academic practice
(the formal disputation, where opponents raised objections in real time). One
sentence would add the human texture: "Behind each article is the reality of
the university disputation — an actual formal debate, opponents raising objections
in the hall, the master answering them; the *Summa* articles are the cleaned-up
written form of that living exchange." This is a SHOULD-FIX; the chapter is
STRONG without it, but the human context adds a dimension that helps the reader
imagine the medieval university.

### Ch6 — "The straw, and after" — **GOOD**

The "straw" quote is handled with exemplary source framing (the transmission
chain is named: Reginald → John del Giudice → Bartholomew of Capua → 1319
canonization, 46 years after the fact). The poisoning rumor is correctly labeled
as "a rumor Dante repeated." The wart (heresy and the death penalty, ST II-II
q.11 a.3) is named, sourced, placed in context, and closed without moralizing —
exactly the pipeline's instruction.

The afterlife section (1277 condemnation → canonization 1323 → Trent → Leo XIII
1879) is the right corrective to the "immediate official philosopher" myth. The
chapter earns its two-part close: the cathedral was real AND it seemed like straw
to the builder.

**Three-part test (the afterlife of Thomism — the chapter's teaching):**
- Worked example: YES — the 1277 condemnation as "thirty-six months after his
  death" gives the timeline a concrete shock.
- Strongest version: YES — the Leo XIII outcome is given its full irony
  (a 19th-century decree, not a 13th-century one).
- Plain restatement: YES — "It was a 19th-century decree, not a 13th-century one,
  and it came after centuries of argument, not by fiat."

**MUST-FIX (Understanding — Scotus/Ockham hinge too thin):** The Scotus paragraph
introduces *univocity of being* in opposition to the analogy of being — but the
reader was never given the analogy of being (it is absent from Ch4; see M-2
below). The sentence "Scotus argued for the *univocity of being* (that 'being'
means exactly the same thing said of God and of a creature — against Aquinas's
analogy, where God-talk only ever stretches creaturely words toward a God who
exceeds them)" is well-written, but it describes a refusal of something the
reader has not seen. Without *analogia entis* in Ch4, "against Aquinas's analogy"
is a black box. The fix is in Ch4 (add the analogy of being), not in Ch6, but
Ch6's Scotus paragraph will remain partially unintelligible until Ch4 carries that
concept. See Comprehensiveness M-2. **MUST-FIX** (dependent on M-2; the sentence
itself is fine once M-2 is added).

**One gap (SHOULD-FIX — Comprehensiveness):** The chapter names Étienne Gilson
and Jacques Maritain as 20th-century Thomists without a one-line gloss on either.
Maritain in particular wrote on art and personalism; Gilson on the history of
medieval philosophy. A 15-year-old needs one phrase of identification. See
Clarity P-1 below.

### MUST-ADD findings

**M-1 (MUST-ADD): Essence/existence distinction is absent.**

The distinction between *essentia* (what a thing is) and *esse* (that it is) is
load-bearing for the Third Way ("something necessarily exists of itself, whose
essence IS existence") and for the Scotus/Ockham contrast in Ch6. Without it,
the Third Way's move from "contingent things" to "something whose essence is
existence" is a gap the reader cannot cross — the distinction between "a thing
whose existence is borrowed" and "a thing whose existence is its own nature" is
precisely the machinery being invoked. The fact pack (§4) has the full section:
*De Ente et Essentia*, Avicenna inheritance, act/potency background, *ipsum esse
subsistens* for God. A short paragraph — three to four sentences — in Ch4
("The law and the soul") before or after the hylomorphism section would close
this. The Third Way in Ch3 can carry a forward-reference or the distinction can
be placed in Ch2 as part of the metaphysical groundwork. **It cannot be left
absent.** Without it, the Third Way teaches the conclusion without the argument.

Draft text that exposes the gap: Ch3 para 5 ("something necessarily exists 'of
itself'; 'this all men speak of as God'") — the reader is given the conclusion
without the metaphysical move that makes it more than an assertion.

**M-2 (MUST-ADD): Analogy of being (*analogia entis*) is absent.**

This is Aquinas's answer to the question: how can we say ANYTHING meaningful about
God, if God infinitely exceeds every concept we have? His answer: terms applied to
God are neither univocal (same meaning as for creatures) nor equivocal (totally
different meaning), but analogical — proportionally related. "Good" said of a
person and of God carries a related-but-not-identical meaning, the way "healthy"
said of a diet and of a person are related rather than identical or unrelated.

This concept is required for two reasons:

1. The *analogia entis* is what makes Ch3's Five Ways coherent in their
   conclusions. When Aquinas says "this everyone understands to be God," he needs
   a theory of how "first mover" can apply to God without being simply equivocal.
   Without the analogy, "and this we call God" is an unexplained label.

2. The Ch6 Scotus paragraph introduces Scotus's *univocity of being* as directly
   opposed to Aquinas's analogy — but the analogy has never been stated. "Against
   Aquinas's analogy, where God-talk only ever stretches creaturely words toward a
   God who exceeds them" describes a position the reader has not seen. The sentence
   is currently half a debate, with only one side present.

A short paragraph in Ch4 (after the natural law section, before or alongside the
soul-as-form discussion) would introduce it: the problem (how do we talk about
God without either overclaiming equality or collapsing into silence?), the solution
(analogical predication: proportional, neither identical nor unrelated), and the
upshot (this is the middle way between Maimonides' radical silence and the Latin
Averroist's Aristotelian literalism). Fact pack §4 has all the content. **This
is the draft's most significant structural gap after M-1.**

### SHOULD-ADD findings

**S-1 (SHOULD-ADD): Fourth Way needs one more sentence.**

The Fourth Way (from degrees of being) is the most philosophically unusual of
the five because its premises lean on Platonic participation in a way the other
four do not. The draft acknowledges this parenthetically ("That fourth argument
leans on Aristotle braided with Platonic participation — the influence line back
to Plato is real") but does not explain WHY degrees of a quality point to a
maximum. The move is: a quality present in degrees in many things requires a
source that has it fully and is the cause of it in the others (the way heat in
many bodies points to fire as source). Without that one sentence the Fourth Way
reads as opaque assertion dressed as argument.

Exact draft text: "Things are more or less good, true, noble, and argues that
gradations point to a maximum that is the cause of the rest." The causation step
is missing: *why* does a maximum cause the others? One sentence: "the way fire
is the cause of heat in things that are merely warm, any quality graduated among
many things points to a maximum that is its cause in the others."

**S-2 (SHOULD-ADD): Aquinas's rejection of Anselm's ontological argument.**

The fact pack (§8 influence chain item 7) flags this explicitly: Aquinas engages
Anselm's proof and rejects it (ST I q.2 a.1 — we do not know God's essence well
enough to see that non-existence is impossible). This is a significant
philosophical datum: the two greatest medieval Christian philosophers disagree on
the most celebrated medieval argument for God. It also prepares the reader for the
wider medieval debate (Scotus redeployed a version of the ontological argument;
Ockham attacked it; the argument's trajectory through the tradition is partly
about this disagreement). A single sentence in Ch3, after the Five Ways are
described, would carry it: "One famous proof Aquinas did *not* endorse was
Anselm's ontological argument — the claim that God, conceived as the greatest
possible being, must exist because non-existence would make him less than greatest.
Aquinas's objection: we don't know God's essence clearly enough to see that it
includes existence." This is a SHOULD-ADD because it sharpens the reader's picture
of what the Five Ways are doing (they are emphatically NOT ontological arguments)
and adds a dimension to the Anselm influence.

**S-3 (SHOULD-ADD): Maimonides as a named influence.**

The fact pack (§8 item 6) establishes Maimonides ("Rabbi Moses") as a direct
influence on Aquinas's negative theology and on his approach to compatibility of
philosophy and revelation. Aquinas cites him respectfully throughout the *Summa*.
The era-2 draft (faith-reason, chapter on Maimonides) has the full story; the
thinker read only needs one sentence acknowledging the cross-tradition influence:
"Among Aquinas's interlocutors was the Jewish philosopher Moses Maimonides —
cited as 'Rabbi Moses' throughout the *Summa* — whose *Guide for the Perplexed*
grappled with the same question (how far can reason go in speaking about God?)
from within a different tradition and pushed the case for silence further than
Aquinas was willing to follow." This completes the influence chain that runs:
Averroes → Aquinas ← Maimonides. Currently Averroes is in the break block but
Maimonides is absent.

**S-4 (SHOULD-ADD): The Gilson/Maritain one-line IDs.**

See Clarity P-1 below. Their role as 20th-century carriers of Thomism is named
but neither man is identified. One phrase each suffices.

## Gate 4 — Clarity (cold-read as a sharp 15-year-old)

### Undefined terms

**CL-1 (MUST-GLOSS): *Scholasticism* / "Scholastics" (break block, Ch5).**

The break block uses "Scholasticism" and "Scholastic theology" without defining
them. The term appears as early as the break block para 1 ("Platonic Augustine
was still the theological default... Then the Arabic transmission delivered the
whole of Aristotle into the universities"). Ch5 uses "Scholasticism" three times
("the 'medieval philosophy equals sterile logic-chopping' picture"; "Scholasticism
at its best"; "Scholastic theology"). A 15-year-old does not know what Scholasticism
is. The era-2 faith-reason read glosses it in chapter 4 (Anselm/Abelard section),
but the thinker read is a standalone document. The word needs a one-sentence
definition on first use in the break block: "the method of doing theology through
systematic rational argument, developed in the medieval universities — organizing
questions, raising objections, and answering them by the logic of the schools
(*scholae*)." **MUST-GLOSS.**

**CL-2 (MUST-GLOSS): "mendicant" (Ch1).**

The draft introduces "mendicants" in Ch1 para 2: "friars who took a vow of poverty
and begged for their living rather than holding land and property the way the old
monastic orders did." This is a good gloss — but the word "mendicant" itself is not
given, so when the term appears elsewhere in philosophy and history texts the
reader has no anchor. Add the word explicitly on first use: "The Dominicans were
*mendicants* — from the Latin *mendicare*, to beg — friars who took a vow of
poverty..." This is minor but the pattern (define the word, not just the concept)
matters for vocabulary acquisition. SHOULD-GLOSS; not structural.

**CL-3 (MUST-GLOSS): "act and potency" (Ch3).**

The act/potency distinction is introduced in the First Way walkthrough: "motion is
nothing else than the reduction of something from potentiality to actuality" and
"a cold cup of coffee is *potentially* hot, and the stove, already *actually* hot,
makes it so." The coffee example is excellent — it concretizes the abstract pair
immediately. But the *terms* "act" and "potency" as technical labels are not
given their own introduction; the reader gets "actually hot" and "potentially hot"
but never "Aristotle calls these *actuality* and *potentiality*, or *act* and
*potency* for short, and the whole machinery of the Five Ways turns on them."
The terms recur in Ch4 (hylomorphism: "existence (esse) is the act of every act")
without re-grounding. One phrase on first use in Ch3 would set them. **MUST-GLOSS**
(the terms are load-bearing in both Ch3 and Ch4 and they are used without formal
introduction).

**CL-4 (NEEDS-GLOSS): "hylomorphic" / "hylomorphic compound" (Ch4).**

The draft introduces hylomorphism via "hylomorphic compound — matter (the body)
organized by form (the soul)." The etymology is not given (unlike in the Aristotle
thinker read, which unpacked *hyle* and *morphe*). The word is intimidating.
A 15-year-old will feel "hylomorphic compound" is a technicality they can skip.
A one-line etymology — "a hylomorphic compound — *hyle* (matter) + *morphe*
(form) in Greek, a thing made of matter organized by form" — would help. SHOULD-
GLOSS; not structural.

**CL-5 (NEEDS-GLOSS): *sed contra* / *respondeo* (Ch5).**

The *Summa* article structure is walked in Ch5, but the Latin terms *sed contra*
and *respondeo* are introduced with translations ("'on the contrary'" and "'I
answer that'") in parentheses — good. However, the reader is not told why these
Latin terms are worth knowing by name (they appear constantly in secondary
literature on Aquinas). A short note: "These Latin tags — *sed contra*, *respondeo*
— are the shorthand scholars use when discussing any article in the *Summa*; you
will see them whenever people quote from it." Minor; SHOULD-GLOSS.

**CL-6 (MUST-GLOSS): "nominalism" (Ch6).**

The term "nominalism" is introduced in Ch6 without a gloss: "Ockham's nominalism
dissolves the shared natures — the universals — that Aquinas's whole metaphysics
is built on." The following clause ("If only individuals exist and 'horse-ness'
is just a name we give a heap of particular horses") gives the content, which is
good. But "nominalism" is itself a term that will recur throughout the philosophy
curriculum and the reader needs the label defined: "nominalism — from the Latin
*nomen*, name — the view that only individual things exist and that general terms
('horse,' 'good,' 'being') are names we apply, not shared essences that exist
in the world." The content gloss is there; just add the explicit label definition.
SHOULD-GLOSS; not structural, but will trip the reader in every subsequent era.

**CL-7 (NEEDS-GLOSS): "voluntarism" (Ch6).**

The Scotus paragraph says he "made the *will*, not the intellect, the primary
faculty" and adds "(voluntarism vs. Aquinas's intellectualism)." The terms are
given but not explained: voluntarism = the will is primary; intellectualism = the
intellect is primary. But WHY does this matter? A 15-year-old needs one sentence:
"For Aquinas, the intellect grasps the good and the will follows it — you will
what you understand to be good. For Scotus, the will can choose freely regardless
of what the intellect presents — love is prior to understanding." SHOULD-GLOSS.

### Silently skipped argument steps

**A-1 (MUST-FIX): The Third Way's essential/contingent distinction is asserted
without the metaphysical machinery.**

Ch3 para 5: "the things around us are contingent — they can fail to exist, they
come and go — and if *everything* were like that, then there is no reason
something rather than nothing should exist at all to ground the whole show; so
there must be something that exists necessarily, of itself." The argument moves
from "contingent things exist" to "something necessarily exists" without the
machinery that Aquinas actually uses. The missing step: if a thing is contingent
(its essence does not include its existence), then at some point it was not — and
if everything is like this, there was a point where nothing existed. But *from
nothing, nothing comes* (ex nihilo nihil fit), so if there were ever nothing, there
would still be nothing. Therefore something exists whose existence is not borrowed
from another — something that exists by its own essence. Without that middle step,
"there must be something" sounds like an assertion, not an argument.

This gap is structural and connects to M-1 above (essence/existence distinction):
the Third Way is the argument where the essence/existence distinction is most
load-bearing. The fix is to add the *ex nihilo* step in Ch3 para 5 AND to supply
the essence/existence framework in Ch2 or Ch4 (see M-1). **MUST-FIX.**

**A-2 (NEEDS-CLARIFICATION): The "double truth" caricature and its target
(break block).**

The break block says "A Paris bishop would brand this the doctrine of 'double
truth' — true in philosophy, false in faith" and notes that "Modern scholars doubt
anyone actually held it so baldly." The 15-year-old will ask: *which bishop?*
(It is Étienne Tempier, 1277 and before — but the break block does not name him.)
And: *why does it matter that scholars doubt anyone held it?* (Because Aquinas is
arguing against a caricature of his opponents, which makes the real target their
institutional drift, not an explicit position.) A brief parenthetical naming
Tempier, or a clause ("as the bishop of Paris, Tempier, later branded it") would
ground the "bishop" reference. SHOULD-CLARIFY.

**A-3 (NEEDS-CLARIFICATION): The "grace does not destroy nature but perfects it"
sentence (break block and Ch2).**

The sentence is quoted, bolded, and unpacked in the break block. But the
*direction* of the analogy is not made explicit for a newcomer: it is Aquinas
arguing AGAINST the position that faith replaces or overrides reason, not just
FOR the position that faith adds to it. A 15-year-old might read "grace perfects
nature" and think it means "faith replaces the natural with something better" —
the exact opposite of what Aquinas means. The second-light analogy in Ch2 ("a
second, higher light added on top of it — not painting over what reason shows")
does close this, but it arrives a chapter later. The break block should add one
clarifying clause: "not replacing reason but completing it — the way a telescope
extends what the eye already sees, rather than covering the eye." SHOULD-CLARIFY.

## Verdict: **FIX-THEN-SHIP**

The draft is the strongest thinker read in the vertical. The break block, the
Five Ways chapter, and the angels-on-a-pin demolition are all exceptional. No
chapters need a full REWRITE; the voice, pacing, and structural bones are right
throughout.

**Four blockers stand between this draft and SHIP:**

1. **The essence/existence distinction is absent (M-1):** Load-bearing for the
   Third Way and for the Ch6 Scotus contrast. Three to four sentences in Ch2 or
   Ch4. Cannot ship without it.

2. **The analogy of being (*analogia entis*) is absent (M-2):** The Ch6 Scotus
   paragraph describes the rejection of a position the reader has never seen.
   One paragraph in Ch4. Cannot ship without it.

3. **The Third Way's *ex nihilo* step is missing (A-1):** The argument from
   contingency arrives at "there must be something necessary" without showing
   why contingent things cannot be self-sufficient. One to two sentences in Ch3
   para 5.

4. **Natural law has no worked example (Ch4 MUST-FIX):** "Good is to be done and
   pursued" is stated but never applied. One sentence drawing out the specific
   inclinations (life, community, truth, raising children) would close it.

Everything else in the SHOULD-FIX list improves a draft that already reads well.
Fix the four blockers, and the draft ships.
