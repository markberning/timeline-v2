## 1. Unmoved mover passage (end of Ch3)

**Claims checked:**
- The regress argument (every motion requires a mover; infinite regress is useless; therefore a first unmoved mover).
- "Pure actuality" — the unmoved mover has no unrealized potential.
- Moves by being *desired* as the final cause, not by pushing.
- "Thought thinking itself" (*noēsis noēseōs*), Metaphysics XII (Lambda).
- Guardrail: not the Christian creator God.
- Aquinas hand-off.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED across all sub-claims.**

Detail:
- The regress argument is faithfully rendered. The unmoved mover argument is in Metaphysics Book XII (Lambda), confirmed at classics.mit.edu and by the Wikipedia "Unmoved Mover" article; the Physics VIII parallel is real and correctly not cited as the sole location.
- "Pure actuality" (no potentiality) is the standard scholarly characterization, confirmed in SEP and Wikipedia.
- The mechanism of motion by desire is well attested. The draft's phrase "moves things the way a goal moves you, or the way something beloved draws you across a room — *by being desired*, not by pushing" accurately captures Metaphysics XII.7's "The object of desire and the object of thought move in this way; they move without being moved" (verified at classics.mit.edu).
- The Greek phrase in the draft is *noesis noeseos*. The fact pack gives the full Greek as *noesis noeseos noesis*; Ross's standard English rendering is "thinking of thinking" or "thought of thought." The draft renders it "thought thinking itself" — a legitimate English gloss, within the range of accepted translations. Not an error.
- The not-the-Christian-God guardrail is fully present and correctly pitched: the draft says the unmoved mover "did not create the world, does not know you exist, answers no prayers, and intervenes in nothing," and explicitly flags Aquinas's re-pointing as Aquinas's move. CONFIRMED.
- The Aquinas hand-off (five ways, Summa) is accurate and correctly attributed.

**No corrections needed.**

## 2. Syllogism passage (Ch6)

**Claims checked:**
- Aristotle *founded* formal logic (the Organon / the syllogistic).
- The "logic" naming caveat (logic as a discipline name is later; the Organon is the collection).
- The worked example: "All men are mortal / Socrates is a man / therefore Socrates is mortal" as a valid categorical syllogism and fair illustration.
- Validity-is-in-the-form point.
- Kant's remark (logic had not had to go a single step backwards since Aristotle).

**Verdict: ONE ISSUE — the Socrates-mortality syllogism is NOT in Aristotle.**

Detail:
- Aristotle founding formal logic via the Prior Analytics / Organon is unambiguously correct. The Organon label is correctly noted as a later editorial collective, not Aristotle's own grouping.
- The "logic" as a discipline name point is addressed in the fact pack but the draft does not make it explicit — the draft says "Aristotle *invented formal logic*" without the Western-tradition caveat the fact pack flags (§9.1: "the Nyāya school in India developed formal logic independently; in the Western tradition Aristotle is unambiguously first"). The draft's scope note is implicit at best. This is a minor framing gap, not a factual error in context (the whole read is implicitly about the Western tradition), and the fact pack's own scope note calls it "SHOULD-FIX if scope note is absent."
- The validity-is-in-the-form point is correctly stated and is a core result of the Prior Analytics.
- Kant's remark is accurately paraphrased; the 1781 date (Critique of Pure Reason, where the remark appears in the Preface) is correct.
- **THE ISSUE:** The "All men are mortal / Socrates is a man / therefore Socrates is mortal" example does **not** appear in Aristotle's Prior Analytics or anywhere in his corpus. In the Prior Analytics, Aristotle uses abstract letter-placeholders (A, B, C), not concrete examples in this form. The earliest verified use of this specific syllogism is John Stuart Mill's *A System of Logic* (1843). The example is a pedagogically valid Barbara-form syllogism and a fair *illustration* of Aristotelian logic — but attributing it as Aristotle's own "textbook example" is inaccurate. The draft's wording ("The textbook example is the cleanest thing in philosophy") frames it as *the* canonical illustration without attributing it to Aristotle himself — so it is not a false attribution in so many words. However the sentence that immediately follows ("Look at what's happening — you don't need to know anything about Socrates or mortality to see that the conclusion *cannot escape*") and the overall presentation risk leaving the reader with the impression Aristotle used this example. **The draft does not explicitly say "Aristotle wrote this example," but it does not say he didn't, and the framing is misleading.** The fact pack (§5f) documents the example as "Barbara (AAA-1 — the 'perfect syllogism')" with a DOCUMENTED tag but does not flag the provenance issue — this is a gap in the pack that passed through to the draft. 

**Correction needed:** Add a brief parenthetical noting that Aristotle himself used letter-variables (A, B, C) in the Prior Analytics, and that the Socrates-mortality illustration, while a valid Barbara syllogism, is a later pedagogical example (Mill, 1843) used to show how the form works. The validity-is-in-the-form point remains fully intact; only the attribution of this specific example to Aristotle needs tightening.

## 3. Six-constitution taxonomy (Ch5)

**Claims checked:**
- Monarchy / aristocracy / polity = three "correct" forms; tyranny / oligarchy / democracy = their corruptions.
- Polity as Aristotle's practical preference.
- Citizenship-exclusion note (slaves, women, manual laborers — Politics III).

**Verdict: CONFIRMED.**

Detail:
- The six-form taxonomy is precisely correct: monarchy (one, common good) → tyranny (one, own interest); aristocracy (few best, common good) → oligarchy (wealthy, own interest); polity (many, common good) → democracy (poor majority, own interest). Verified against Politics III.7 (1279a–b) via Perseus and secondary confirmation.
- The draft's framing of democracy as "a deviant form, the poor majority ruling in their own interest rather than the whole city's" accurately reflects Aristotle's own account — not a modern projection.
- Polity as Aristotle's practical preferred regime for "most real cities" is accurate; he calls it the most achievable stable constitution for cities with a large middle class.
- The citizenship-exclusion note (slaves, women, manual laborers from Politics III) is accurate. Manual laborers (banausoi) are excluded at Politics III.5 on the grounds that their work precludes the leisure needed for virtue; the reference to "Politics III" is correct for the citizenship discussion.

**No corrections needed.**

## 4. The two corrected Rackham women-quotes (Ch5)

**Claims checked — draft wording vs. actual Perseus/Rackham text:**

Draft renders the two passages as:
1. "...has not got the deliberative part at all... without full authority... in an undeveloped form"
2. "the courage of command... that of subordination"

**Verdict: CONFIRMED — exact match with Perseus Rackham text.**

Detail:
- Perseus 1260a11 (Rackham, verified): "the slave has not got the deliberative part at all, and the female has it, but without full authority, while the child has it, but in an undeveloped form." — The draft's condensed quotation using ellipsis ("has not got the deliberative part at all... without full authority... in an undeveloped form") accurately represents the Rackham text. The subject-switching between slave/female/child is clear from context in the prose.
- Perseus 1260a20–23 (Rackham, verified): "the one is the courage of command, and the other that of subordination." — The draft's rendering ("the courage of command... that of subordination") is exact Rackham with accurate ellipsis.
- The fact pack cites 1260a for both passages; the draft correctly attributes to Rackham.

**No corrections needed.**

## 5. "G. E. M. Anscombe" spelling and identity (Ch6)

**Claims checked:**
- Spelling of "Anscombe."
- Described as "the British philosopher."
- Essay "Modern Moral Philosophy," 1958.
- MacIntyre described as "the Scottish-American philosopher."

**Verdict: CONFIRMED on all points.**

Detail:
- Spelling "Anscombe" is correct (full name: Gertrude Elizabeth Margaret Anscombe, 1919–2001).
- "British philosopher" is accurate: Anscombe is consistently described as a British analytic philosopher; she was born in Limerick, Ireland, but her entire academic career was at Oxford and Cambridge, and she is universally described as British.
- "Modern Moral Philosophy," *Philosophy*, vol. 33, no. 124 (1958) — confirmed.
- MacIntyre "Scottish-American": born Glasgow 1929, has worked in the United States since 1970; the "Scottish-American" designation is well attested (Wikipedia, IEP).
