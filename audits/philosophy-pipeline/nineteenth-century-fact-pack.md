## 0. Chapter-arc assessment (§0)

**Proposed 7-chapter arc (era altitude):**

1. **Ch1 — The handover: from system-building to the self.** Setting the break: after
   Kant and Hegel, the next wave asks not "what is the system?" but "how should I live?"
   and "what makes a good society?" The era's two entry ramps: Bentham's utilitarian
   calculus and the Hegelian inheritance that Kierkegaard, Marx, and Nietzsche all
   explicitly react against. Bentham established; Harriet Martineau and political economy
   as context for Mill.

2. **Ch2 — Mill and Harriet Taylor Mill: the utilitarian refinement and the free
   individual.** The chapter's arc: Bentham's crude calculus → Mill's higher/lower
   pleasures → On Liberty's harm principle → *The Subjection of Women* as philosophical
   argument. Harriet Taylor Mill named as genuine intellectual partner and in part
   co-author (the framing gate requires it; On Liberty's dedication is the evidence).

3. **Ch3 — Kierkegaard: the first existentialist.** Either/Or's three stages (aesthetic,
   ethical, religious). Fear and Trembling: Abraham and the teleological suspension of the
   ethical. The Concept of Anxiety: angst as dizziness of freedom. The Concluding
   Unscientific Postscript: subjectivity is truth; what counts as a "leap." The attack on
   Christendom. Pseudonymous authorship explained as method, not evasion.

4. **Ch4 — Marx as philosopher.** Alienated labour from the 1844 Manuscripts. The
   Theses on Feuerbach, especially XI. The critique of Hegel ("standing him on his feet").
   The 1859 Preface: base/superstructure and historical materialism. "Religion is the
   opium of the people" in full context. Commodity fetishism at a glance. The potted-
   summary trap handled head-on: Marx as philosopher, not prophet.

5. **Ch5 — The political philosophers: context, not a detour.** A mid-era connecting
   chapter that places Tocqueville, Mill's political theory, and the socialist tradition
   as the democratic inheritance these four thinkers are talking into — not a catalogue,
   but a bridge that makes the stakes legible for a reader who doesn't already know them.
   (Note: if the comprehensiveness critic finds this redundant with ch2, fold back; if
   it judges the stakes clear from ch2 alone, drop it and keep 6 chapters.)

6. **Ch6 — Nietzsche: the death of God.** Gay Science §108, §125 (the madman), §341
   (eternal recurrence), §343. The will to power (BGE 36 and Genealogy II). Master/slave
   morality + ressentiment (Genealogy I). The Übermensch (Thomas Common: "Superman";
   note the translation trap and use "overman" in house voice with Thomas Common's
   "Superman" acknowledged). Perspectivism. *Twilight of the Idols*: "what does not kill
   me." The posthumous misuse by Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche — named honestly.

7. **Ch7 — The reckoning.** The era as a whole: what it resolved, what it opened. The
   arc from Thales' "what is everything made of" to Nietzsche's "God is dead" framed as
   one long project — Western philosophy's self-interrogation — that now has no fixed
   ground. The 20th century's two heirs (analytics pursuing clarity, continentals pursuing
   existence after Nietzsche) named as the handed-off question, but NOT covered (v1 stops
   here). The final beat: Nietzsche in a Turin square, January 1889, the books finished,
   the mind breaking — framed honestly as the tradition records it, with the horse
   anecdote called out as what it is.

## 2. Position summaries (steelmanned; ⚠️ = potted-summary trap pre-flagged)

### Bentham (backstory, not a full thinker-profile)
Classical utilitarianism: morality judged by consequences; the right action produces
the most happiness (pleasure minus pain) for the greatest number. Radical for its time:
no one's happiness counts more than anyone else's; social reforms (prison system, poor
laws) judged by their effects on aggregate welfare. The utility calculus is meant to be
applied to legislation, not just private choices.
⚠️ **Trap**: "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" as Bentham's phrase is a
potted summary error — the phrase originates with Francis Hutcheson (*Inquiry*, 1725),
appears in Beccaria (1764), and Bentham credits Priestley's 1768 essay (*Essay on the
First Principles of Government*) as his source; he later actually *criticized* the phrase
as incoherent (what if maximizing total happiness requires sacrificing the many?). State
this honestly in a sentence.
⚠️ **Trap 2**: "push-pin is as good as poetry" — from *The Rationale of Reward* (1825),
meaning that if they produce equal pleasure, they're equal *in that measure* — Bentham
isn't saying poetry has no value, but that pleasure is the measure. Mill's *Utilitarianism*
explicitly corrects this with the higher/lower pleasures distinction.

### Mill + Harriet Taylor Mill — the utilitarianism refinement and the free individual
The central philosophical move: **quality vs. quantity of pleasures**. Bentham's felicific
calculus treats pleasure as homogeneous (quantity, intensity, duration). Mill argues
there are *kinds* of pleasure that are intrinsically superior — "higher" pleasures of the
mind and imagination over "lower" bodily pleasures — and that the verdict is made by
those who have experienced both. The worked example (run this in prose): if you had
experienced both the pleasures of intellectual life and the pleasures of mindless
diversion, which would you prefer? Mill's answer: no one who has genuinely experienced
both would choose the lower. The verdict: "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied
than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied" (VERIFIED
§3).

**On Liberty** (1859): the harm principle, stated directly — power over an individual
is legitimate ONLY to prevent harm to others; his own good, physical or moral, is not
sufficient warrant (VERIFIED §3). "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the
individual is sovereign" (VERIFIED §3). The tyranny of the majority as the liberal
danger in democratic societies: the majority can suppress unpopular opinions and
unconventional lives as ruthlessly as any despot. Mill credits Tocqueville for coining
the phrase (credited honestly in the text itself).

**The Subjection of Women** (1869): a philosophical argument, not just advocacy.
The existing system rests on an analogy with force — the strongest prevailed
historically — which has since been dressed in custom and habit as if it were rational.
We cannot know what women's natural capacities are until they have had the same social
conditions as men; the argument from "natural" difference is circular. **Harriet Taylor
Mill's contribution** (the framing gate): Mill's *Autobiography* calls On Liberty "more
directly and literally our joint production than anything else which bears my name" and
states Harriet wrote many of the most important portions; SEP confirms On Liberty's
dedication names her "in part the author." The Subjection of Women drew heavily on her
"Enfranchisement of Women" (1851) which SEP identifies as primarily Harriet's work.
The destroyed-letters problem means the full extent cannot be established; what CAN be
established is named above.

⚠️ **Trap 1**: "Mill invented utilitarianism" — he refined and deepened it; it
originates with Bentham and, before him, Hume's and Hutcheson's consequentialist strands.
⚠️ **Trap 2**: Mill's *Utilitarianism* is sometimes read as capitulating to Kantianism
(the justice chapter especially) — this is a live scholarly debate; avoid stating
"Mill ultimately abandoned consequentialism" (contested).
⚠️ **Trap 3**: "the harm principle is simple" — it generates hard cases immediately
(what counts as harm to others? does offense count? does self-harm?); these are
deliberate and Mill discusses them.

### Kierkegaard — the first existentialist (with a big footnote)

⚠️ **Trap**: "the first existentialist" is a retrospective label — Kierkegaard never
called himself this, and the word "existentialism" comes later (Sartre). Use the label
as the inherited description it is, with one honest sentence about its anachronism.

**The three stages** (better: "three spheres"): the *aesthetic* — life organized
around experience, sensation, the interesting; the aesthetic person manages boredom by
keeping all options open and never committing; ends in despair (Either/Or I). The
*ethical* — life organized around duty, commitment, social roles; Judge William's
letters in Either/Or II advocate this as the genuine life (marriage as the ethical's
example: sustaining love through choice rather than feeling). The *religious* — a
relationship with God that cannot be mediated by ethical categories; what Abraham
does in Fear and Trembling cannot be explained to Isaac's mother.

**Fear and Trembling** (Johannes de silentio, 1843): Abraham's near-sacrifice of
Isaac (Genesis 22) as the test case. The ethical says "thou shalt not kill"; Abraham
does it anyway. Kierkegaard's analysis: Abraham is not a murderer because there is a
"teleological suspension of the ethical" — the absolute duty to God supersedes the
universal ethical. The *knight of faith* cannot explain himself to the ethical
community; faith is irreducibly first-person, non-transferable. The pseudonym matters:
Johannes de silentio narrates *from outside* the religious stage — he admires Abraham
but cannot understand him; Kierkegaard does not claim to have made the leap himself.

**The leap**: Kierkegaard uses the word "leap" (*Spring* in Danish) repeatedly,
especially in *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846), for the movement from ethical
to religious life. **"Leap of faith" as a PHRASE does not appear in Kierkegaard's
own writings** — it is later English shorthand, first associated with his early
interpreters. The concept IS his; the slogan is not. State this distinction plainly.
(His closer phrase is "qualitative leap" — a leap across incommensurable categories,
not just a big step along the same path.)

**Subjectivity is truth** / "truth is subjectivity": the *Concluding Unscientific
Postscript* (1846), under the pseudonym Johannes Climacus. The claim: for matters of
existence — how to live, what to commit to — "objective" impersonal knowledge is not
only unavailable but the wrong category. Faith is holding fast to objective uncertainty
with infinite passion. This is NOT relativism ("any belief is as good as any other"); it
is a claim about the *kind* of engagement required for existential truth vs. scientific
truth. **PARAPHRASE-ONLY** (no PD English of the Postscript verified for this pack).

**The Concept of Anxiety** (*Begrebet Angest*, 1844; Vigilius Haufniensis): anxiety
(*Angst*) as the dizziness of freedom — not fear of a specific object (that is fear)
but the vertigo of infinite possibility, of being free to choose and bearing full
responsibility for that freedom. Anxiety precedes sin but is not itself sinful; it is
the condition of the being that knows it is free. **PARAPHRASE-ONLY** (no PD English
of the Concept of Anxiety verified for this pack).

**The attack on Christendom** (1854–55): Kierkegaard's final campaign, explicitly
signed in his own name — the most direct writing he ever did. Target: the Danish state
church and the European comfortable Christianity he calls "Christendom," a culture that
calls itself Christian while avoiding the actual demands of New Testament faith. The
pamphlets (*Øjeblikket*, 10 issues) are polemical, satirical, and deliberately readable.
He died mid-campaign.

**Pseudonymous authorship**: Kierkegaard's method. Direct communication can transmit
information; existential truth must be encountered by each reader themselves, or not at
all. The pseudonyms allow him to present a position from within without endorsing it
(Anti-Climacus speaks from a higher religious register than Kierkegaard claims for
himself; Johannes de silentio speaks from outside the faith he describes). He revealed
the pseudonymous authorship was his in *The Point of View for My Work as an Author*
(1859, posthumous) — do not suppress this.

⚠️ **Trap**: "Kierkegaard was an irrationalist who thought faith meant believing
contradictions" — more careful formulation: faith involves a *paradox* (the eternal
enters the temporal; God becomes a particular man) that cannot be resolved by reason;
this is not the same as saying contradiction is good. His position is that reason has
limits and the religious stage exceeds them, not that logic doesn't apply.

### Marx as philosopher — the four-part frame

**Alienation (1844 Manuscripts)**: the concept of species-being (*Gattungswesen*) — Marx's
adaptation of Feuerbach's term. Human beings, unlike other animals, can make their
life-activity itself the object of their will and consciousness; we are, in principle,
conscious self-creators. Capitalism's specific damage: the worker is alienated from the
product (it belongs to someone else), from the act of production (it is compelled, not
chosen), from other workers (competition replaces solidarity), and from species-being
itself (the capacity for creative, conscious, self-directed labour is stunted into a
mere means of survival). **PARAPHRASE-ONLY** (the standard 1844 Manuscripts translations
are in copyright; the verified content in §3 is Capital/Preface/Theses only).

**Critique of Hegel**: Hegel's *Phenomenology of Spirit* and *Philosophy of Right*
treat history as the development of "Geist" (Mind/Spirit) actualizing itself — ideas
are primary. Marx inverts this: material conditions — how people produce what they need
to survive — determine their consciousness, their legal systems, their philosophy.
"It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social
existence that determines their consciousness" (VERIFIED §3, 1859 Preface). In the
1873 Afterword to *Capital* Vol. 1, Marx writes that Hegel's dialectic is "standing on
its head" and "must be turned right side up again, if you would discover the rational
kernel within the mystical shell" (VERIFIED §3, Moore/Aveling).
⚠️ **"Standing Hegel on his head"**: this exact phrase is often attributed to Marx,
but his actual formulation is "turned right side up" (or "inverted") — not literally
"on his head" (though the German *vom Kopf auf die Füße* = "from head to feet" also
appears in the same vicinity). State this precisely.

**Historical materialism and base/superstructure** (1859 Preface): "The totality of
[the] relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, the real
foundation, on which arises a legal and political superstructure and to which correspond
definite forms of social consciousness" (VERIFIED §3). The model: the economic
*base* (the relations and forces of production) conditions the ideological and legal
*superstructure* (law, politics, religion, philosophy). "Conditions" ≠ "rigidly
determines" — this is a genuine scholarly debate; state the stronger claim and note the
qualifier.

**"Religion is the opium of the people"** (1844 Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of
Right — the **Introduction**, written 1843/published 1844): in FULL context (VERIFIED §3):
*"Religious distress is at the same time the expression of real distress and also the
protest against real distress. Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart
of a heartless world, just as it is the spirit of spiritless conditions. It is the opium
of the people."* — This is not contempt for religion; it is a diagnosis. Marx is saying
religion is both a response to real suffering AND an ideological tool that channels
suffering into acceptance rather than change. The fuller sentence makes this plain: "the
heart of a heartless world" is sympathetic, not sneering.
⚠️ **Trap 1**: rendering the phrase as "opiate of the masses" — the German *Opium
des Volkes* = "opium of the people"; "masses" is a different word and a different register.
⚠️ **Trap 2**: the decontextualized reading — treating this as simple contempt for
religion misses the "sigh of the oppressed creature" clause entirely.

**Theses on Feuerbach, Thesis XI** (1845; Engels' edited version, 1888): "The
philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it"
(VERIFIED §3, Marxists.org, W. Lough trans.). **Note the version distinction**: this is
Engels' lightly edited 1888 publication; Marx's original manuscript (first published 1924)
differs slightly — the original reads roughly "The philosophers have only interpreted
the world in various ways; the point, however, is to change it" (the word "however" is
present, "only" may vary slightly). Ship with "as Engels published it in 1888" — the
difference is minor but accurate. The significance of XI: the whole of Western philosophy
diagnosed as commentary; the task now is transformation. This thesis is often carved on
Marx's tombstone — literally (it IS on his grave at Highgate Cemetery, DOCUMENTED).

**Commodity fetishism** (*Capital* Vol. 1, Chapter 1, §4, Moore/Aveling 1887): "A
commodity appears, at first sight, a very trivial thing, and easily understood. Its
analysis shows that it is, in reality, a very queer thing, abounding in metaphysical
subtleties and theological niceties" (VERIFIED §3). The central idea (PARAPHRASE for the
mechanism, since the full passage is long): when products are produced as commodities
for exchange, the social relations between producers appear as material relations between
things — the relationship between people is mystified as a relationship between objects.
Marx calls this "fetishism" by analogy with religious anthropology (attributing powers
to objects that actually come from human relations). The table-turning metaphor: the
table, as a commodity, "stands on its head, and evolves out of its wooden brain grotesque
ideas, far more wonderful than 'table-turning' ever was" (VERIFIED §3).

⚠️ **The big trap — Marx as PROPHET of 20th-century state communism**: the 1844
Manuscripts were unknown until 1932; the communist states invoked a curated Marx (via
Engels, Kautsky, Plekhanov, Lenin). The philosopher's texts and the 20th-century
political movements of his name are different things; this era's job is the former.
State this clearly and move on — do NOT litigate 20th-century history here.

### Nietzsche — the death of God and beyond

**"God is dead"**: Gay Science §108 (VERIFIED §3 — content confirmed from multiple
cross-referenced sources): "God is dead; but given the way of men, there may still be
caves for thousands of years in which his shadow will be shown. — And we — we still
have to vanquish his shadow, too." And §125, The Madman (VERIFIED §3 — see full text
below): the declaration is made by a madman who runs into the marketplace at noon with
a lantern. The onlookers mock him — they have already stopped believing. His response:
"we have killed him." The declaration is not atheism as doctrine (Nietzsche takes for
granted that the Enlightenment has done that work already) — it is a **diagnosis of
cultural catastrophe**: the entire moral order of the West was grounded in the Christian
God; now that ground has been removed, and most people haven't noticed yet. §343 is
the response: this is the "greatest recent event" (VERIFIED §3 from secondary sources —
see §3 for exact wording), and it calls for a total revaluation of values, not despair
— but only by those who can bear it.
⚠️ **The central framing trap**: "God is dead" as a cheerful boast of atheism is wrong.
The Madman's question — "How shall we comfort ourselves, the murderers of all murderers?"
— is horror, not celebration. The task ahead is almost impossible: find new values after
the death of the source. Frame this correctly in the prose.

**The will to power**: NOT a doctrine of political domination or strength-as-violence.
A more fundamental drive: life as self-overcoming, expansion, the discharge of strength.
In *Beyond Good and Evil* §13 (Zimmern): "A living body's cardinal instinct is above all
to discharge its strength — self-preservation is only one of the indirect and most
frequent results thereof" (PARAPHRASE-ONLY for exact wording — the fetched text
confirmed the content but returned partial text; see §10 for the note). In BGE §36 (the
world seen from within = will to power — VERIFIED §3). Corrects Schopenhauer's "will to
live" and Darwinist "survival" — the primary drive is not mere persistence but
self-overcoming and enhancement.
⚠️ **Trap**: the will to power as "desire to dominate others" — this is the Elisabethan
reading, not Nietzsche's. His examples often emphasize the will to create, the artist's
will, overcoming resistance *in oneself*. State the correct interpretation and note the
history of misreading.

**Master/slave morality**: *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887), First Essay.
Master morality: the aristocratic type, naming *good* (= noble, powerful, vital) and
*bad* (= common, low, weak) as first-person valuations — no resentment, just affirmation.
Slave morality: arises in reaction to the powerful. The slaves cannot win by force, so
they win by *revaluation* — naming the master's values "evil" and their own weakness
"good." The mechanism is *ressentiment* (Nietzsche uses the French word deliberately):
bottled-up hostility that cannot discharge itself as action, so it becomes creative — it
generates values (the "slave revolt in morals"). The genius of slave morality: it *won* —
Christianity and democratic egalitarianism are its children, Nietzsche argues. Steelman:
this is NOT saying the powerful are morally better; Nietzsche's point is genealogical and
descriptive — this is how our current moral vocabulary got its shape, starting from
ressentiment and reactive valuation. He wants us to see the origins and ask whether we
endorse them freely or inherited them blindly.
⚠️ **Trap**: "Nietzsche endorsed the masters and despised the weak" — his admiration is
for *life-affirming* creation of values, which can come from anywhere; his target is
*reactive* morality that secretly hates life. These are different claims.

**Eternal recurrence**: Gay Science §341 (VERIFIED §3 — the Thomas Common rendering
confirmed from multiple sources). The thought: what if a demon told you that you will
live this life, exactly as it is, over and over again, infinitely? Would you despair or
affirm it? Nietzsche's framing: this is the "heaviest burden" — not a cosmological
claim (he may not have believed it literally) but a test of one's relationship to life.
The person who can answer "yes, again, and again" has achieved what Nietzsche calls
*amor fati* (love of fate). This connects to the Übermensch: the overman is the person
who can make this affirmation.

**The Übermensch** (Thomas Common translates "Superman"; Kaufmann's "overman" is better
and is the modern preference but Kaufmann is in copyright — name both and use
"overman" in house voice). Zarathustra's teaching: "I teach you the Superman!" — the
*next* human type, not a specific race or person. The Übermensch is whoever creates
new values after the death of God, whoever can bear the heaviest burden and say yes.
**The Aryan-superman reading is Elisabeth's distortion, not Nietzsche's concept.**

**Perspectivism**: the claim that all knowledge is knowledge from a particular
standpoint; there is no "view from nowhere." In BGE §22 (Zimmern): what we call "laws
of nature" are interpretations, not texts. A posthumous fragment (1886–87) puts it most
sharply: "Against positivism — 'There are only facts' — I would say: no, facts are just
what there aren't, there are only interpretations." **Note the status**: this is from the
unpublished *Nachlass* (notebooks), NOT a published text — cite it as such. The
published formulation is BGE §22.

**Twilight of the Idols, "Maxims and Missiles" §8** (Ludovici 1911, VERIFIED §3):
"That which does not kill me, makes me stronger." — This is REAL and PD-VERIFIED.
Full aphorism: "From the military school of life. — That which does not kill me, makes
me stronger." The surrounding "military school of life" tag is load-bearing — the maxim
is a compressed training principle, not a cosmic reassurance.

## 5. "Why this is a break" — era-level

**What came before:** Kant's critical philosophy set the terms — reason has limits, the
thing-in-itself is unreachable, but the moral law and mathematical knowledge are
synthetic a priori. Hegel then made history itself the engine of reason's
self-realization: the Absolute unfolds through time, contradiction is productive, and
the Prussian state (for better or worse) is where Spirit has arrived so far.

**What the nineteenth century breaks from:**

- **From system-first to life-first (Kierkegaard)**: Hegel's system has a place for
  everything — except the particular, suffering human being who must decide how to live
  *right now*. Kierkegaard's existential break: the system cannot help Abraham; the
  individual stands before God alone.

- **From contemplation to transformation (Marx)**: philosophy has interpreted the
  world. Thesis XI says: stop. The Hegelian inversion — materialism turning idealism
  right side up — makes the philosopher responsible for what the world *becomes*, not
  just what it *is*.

- **From convention to creation (Nietzsche)**: the death of God is not the end of
  values but the beginning of the hardest question: if values are not given from outside,
  what do we do? The answer is not despair (Schopenhauer) or calm observation (the
  scientists): it is self-overcoming, the creation of new values, the amor fati of
  eternal recurrence.

The **concrete before/after**: a reader before this era has Aquinas's two roads (reason
and revelation, both pointing to one truth) and Kant's moral law carved into every
rational agent. A reader after this era has none of that ground — not because it was
argued away but because the *conditions* that sustained it (Christianity as the
organizing frame of European culture) have dissolved. The challenge: what do you build
on in its place?

**The "Why this is a break" block must name:**
1. The Hegelian inheritance each figure reacts against (Kierkegaard's individual vs.
   system; Marx's materialism vs. Hegel's idealism; Nietzsche's "the will to power" vs.
   Schopenhauer's "will to live" and vs. the Enlightenment's optimism about reason).
2. The concrete death-of-God as cultural event, not argument — it is the collapse of a
   whole world-ordering.
3. The era as the *last* in the v1 arc: this is the question the arc has been building
   toward, and it is handed to the reader without a satisfying answer. That's the point.

## 8. "First to" claims (§8)

| Claim | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| "Bentham invented utilitarianism" | **OVERSIMPLIFIED** | The utilitarian tradition precedes Bentham (Hutcheson, Hume, Priestley, Beccaria); Bentham systematized and gave it its name and method. |
| "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" — Bentham's phrase | **WRONG** | Phrase originates with Hutcheson (1725); appears in Beccaria (1764); Bentham credits Priestley (1768) as his source and later CRITICIZED the phrase. Bentham gets credit for the *systematic program*, not the slogan. |
| "Kierkegaard coined 'leap of faith'" | **WRONG** | The phrase is later English shorthand; Kierkegaard used "leap" (*Spring*) but not the phrase "leap of faith." His concept = qualitative leap; the English idiom is post-Kierkegaard. |
| "Marx invented historical materialism" | **OVERSIMPLIFIED** | The concept has roots in Hegel, Feuerbach, the classical political economists. Marx refined and systematized it. Engels' role in shaping the published Marx also matters. |
| "Nietzsche was the first existentialist" | **CONTESTED; USUALLY KIERKEGAARD** | Kierkegaard is typically credited as the first existentialist in the retrospective academic consensus; Nietzsche is the second major figure. Neither called themselves existentialists. |
| "Nietzsche proclaimed God is dead as a triumphant atheist" | **WRONG (framing)** | The madman proclaims it as catastrophe and asks "how shall we console ourselves?" — not triumph. §343 adds a "new dawn" for philosophers, but §125 is horror first. |
| "Tyranny of the majority" — Mill's phrase | **MOSTLY WRONG** | The phrase was coined by Tocqueville (*Democracy in America*, 1835); Mill adopted it and made it famous in English. Mill's text actually credits the concern's antecedents. |
| "The Will to Power" — Nietzsche's book | **⚠️ TRAP** | *The Will to Power* as a book is Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche's posthumous assembly (1901/1906) of his notebooks. It is NOT a book Nietzsche wrote. The concept appears in his published works (*BGE*, *Genealogy*, *Zarathustra*); the "book" is the compilation. State this distinction. |

## 9. APOCRYPHA BLACKLIST

The single biggest blacklist in the v1 corpus: this era produces more fake quotes than
any other. Do not use any of the following:

| Phrase | What's wrong | Correct version |
|---|---|---|
| "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit." — attributed to Aristotle | **FAKE** (era-1 blacklist carried forward) | Will Durant paraphrasing Aristotle in *The Story of Philosophy* (1926); not Aristotle's words |
| "God is dead" — as triumph/boast | **WRONG FRAMING** | Horror and challenge (Gay Science §125); the madman is not celebrating |
| "The Will to Power" (book) by Nietzsche | **THE BOOK IS APOCRYPHAL** | Not a Nietzsche book; an Elisabeth compilation from notebooks. The concept is real; the book is posthumous editorial construction. Never cite it as a work Nietzsche wrote. |
| "That which is done out of love is always beyond good and evil." | **GARBLED** | Real aphorism, garbled wording. Correct Zimmern: "What is done out of love always takes place beyond good and evil." (BGE §153). |
| "Eternal return means living forever" or "literal cosmological claim" | **WRONG** | Nietzsche presented it as a thought experiment / test; whether he believed it literally is contested. Ship as a test of one's relationship to life, not a physics claim. |
| Nietzsche the proto-Nazi / antisemite | **WRONG and HISTORICALLY DOCUMENTED AS WRONG** | Nietzsche explicitly despised antisemitism (BGE §251; private letters); the Nazi reading was Elisabeth's distortion. |
| "Leap of faith" as Kierkegaard's own phrase | **WRONG** | He used "leap" (*Spring*) but not the phrase "leap of faith." |
| "Philosophers have only interpreted the world" (without the "change it") | **INCOMPLETE** | The full Thesis XI = "interpreted... the point is to change it." Neither half works alone. |
| "Opium of the masses" (for Marx) | **WRONG WORD** | *Des Volkes* = "of the people"; "masses" is a different word (*der Massen*). Use "opium of the people." |
| "Religion is the opium of the people" as Marx contemning religion | **FRAMING ERROR** | The full sentence includes "the heart of a heartless world" — compassionate diagnosis, not contempt. Always quote with context. |
| "To understand everything is to forgive everything" — attributed to various including Kierkegaard or Nietzsche | **NOT TRACED TO EITHER** | The phrase is associated with various French writers; do not attribute to either. |
| Duns Scotus buried alive | **APOCRYPHAL** (carried from era-2 blacklist) | Centuries-later legend; no contemporary source. |
| Turin horse story as established fact | **APOCRYPHAL** | Thirdhand hearsay, single source, told after Nietzsche's death; scholars call it apocryphal. Use: Nietzsche collapsed in Turin on January 3, 1889. The rest is tradition. |
| "That which is not forbidden is permitted" — Kant | **NOT KANT** | Often misattributed; not a verified Kant quote. |
| "Bending the arc of the moral universe" — as 19th-century | Not relevant to this era but a common cross-contamination from Parker; flag if it appears. |

## 10. Soft spots / items for the fact-checker

1. **Gay Science §343 exact Thomas Common wording** — content VERIFIED via secondary
   sources; exact PD wording needs author-stage fetch of the full Gutenberg #52124 text.
   Flag as VERIFY at author stage.

2. **BGE §36 full text** (Zimmern) — the fetched HTML excerpt cut off; author should
   pull the complete §36. The "will to power" claim in §36 is confirmed; exact wording
   needs verification.

3. **The extent of Elisabeth's editorial distortion** — the consensus is that she
   distorted selectively; recent scholarship (notably Robert Holub) argues her primary
   motive was protecting Nietzsche's reputation rather than deliberate
   falsification, while still agreeing the results were distorting. The narrative should
   say "distorted" without claiming malicious intent as established fact — the motivation
   is contested, the effect is not.

4. **The Swenson/Lowrie Kierkegaard translations' copyright status** — this pack treats
   them as in copyright; a copyright specialist or library check could confirm. If a PD
   version is found, the Kierkegaard chapter can carry quotes.

5. **Harriet Taylor Mill's primary authorship of *The Subjection of Women***: The SEP
   says the work "draws heavily" on her "Enfranchisement of Women" (1851) and was
   developed jointly; it does NOT attribute her as primary author of *Subjection*
   outright (Mill drafted it 1861, revised 1869). Ship as: "developed from ideas and
   drafts Harriet had been shaping since at least the 'Enfranchisement of Women'
   (1851)." Do not overclaim sole authorship for her; do not erase her contribution.

6. **2026 news: On Liberty now officially has two authors** — a March 2026 finding is
   noted in search results (Daily Nous headline "On Liberty Now Officially Has Two
   Authors"). The fact-checker should verify what this refers to — whether a
   publisher or archival authority has formally recognized dual authorship. If confirmed,
   this is a clean "framing gate satisfied" moment that the narrative should name.

7. **Marx's 1844 Manuscripts**: written 1844 but NOT PUBLISHED until 1932. This is
   load-bearing for the narrative: when we say Marx "developed" the concept of alienation
   in 1844, we mean *privately*; it had no public influence until the 20th century.
   The influence chain runs through the *published* Marx (Manifesto, Capital, 1859
   Preface), not the Manuscripts. The Manuscripts are important for understanding his
   philosophy; state the publication gap honestly.

8. **Nietzsche's illness**: the 1889 collapse has been attributed to syphilis (the
   historical-popular account), to CADASIL (cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy),
   to a hereditary degenerative disease, and to other causes. The true cause is unknown;
   the syphilis story is unconfirmed. State that the cause is medically uncertain.

9. **"Übermensch" translation in Thomas Common**: Common translates it "Superman" (1909).
   This was before the comic-book character. The term is now often rendered "overman" to
   avoid the confusion. The narrative should acknowledge both translations and use
   "overman" consistently in house voice, noting the translation history.

10. **Perspectivism exact locus**: "There are only interpretations" is from Nietzsche's
    *Nachlass* (posthumous notebooks), not a published work. The published perspectivism
    is in BGE §22 (interpretation of "natural laws"). Distinguish the notebook fragment
    from published claims when the narrative uses it.

## Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche / posthumous misuse — PINNED

Per the framing gate requirement: the following are confirmed and must appear named
in the era 5 narrative, not as footnotes:

1. Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche (1846–1935) was Nietzsche's sister and took control of
   his archive after his collapse and eventual death.
2. She assembled *The Will to Power* from his notebooks (1901/1906) — this is NOT a
   book Nietzsche wrote.
3. She shaped the published record to align with her own antisemitic nationalist views,
   which were the opposite of Nietzsche's own stated positions.
4. The Nazi appropriation of Nietzsche ran through her; Hitler attended her funeral.
5. Nietzsche himself despised antisemitism and German nationalism: documented in BGE
   §251, in his private letters (break with his sister over her marriage to Förster),
   and in *The Antichrist*.
6. The Übermensch is not an Aryan type; it is an existential category.

## "God is dead" in context — PINNED

Per the framing gate requirement:

1. The declaration appears three times in Gay Science: §108 (shadow), §125 (the madman),
   §343 (greatest recent event).
2. §125 is NOT a celebration — the madman asks "how shall we console ourselves, the
   murderers of all murderers?" The horror is the point.
3. The diagnosis is cultural, not cosmological: the Enlightenment has made the Christian
   God's role in grounding values untenable; Nietzsche is asking what we build on now.
4. §343 is the constructive turn: "new dawn" for free spirits — but only for those who
   can bear it, and the task is immense.
5. The framing trap to refuse: presenting this as atheism as a doctrine, as Nietzsche's
   victory lap. It is instead the announcement of a crisis with unknown resolution.
