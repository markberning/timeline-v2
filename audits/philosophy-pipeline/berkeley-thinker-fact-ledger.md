## The works, as Berkeley titled them
- ***A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*** (1710) — the systematic
  statement, in numbered sections. Cited "*Principles* §N."
- ***Three Dialogues Between Hylas and Philonous, in Opposition to Sceptics and
  Atheists*** (1713) — the popular restatement as a debate. **Hylas** (from Greek
  *hyle*, matter) defends material substance; **Philonous** (Greek "lover of mind")
  is Berkeley's mouthpiece. The subtitle states the apologetic aim in Berkeley's own
  words: the book is set *against sceptics and atheists*.

## The core doctrine — VERBATIM quotes (cite by section)

### *esse est percipi* — Principles §3 (the prompt's required anchor)
The surrounding English, verbatim:
> "I think an intuitive knowledge may be obtained of this by any one that shall attend
> to WHAT IS MEANT BY THE TERM EXIST, when applied to sensible things. The table I
> write on I say exists, that is, I see and feel it; and if I were out of my study I
> should say it existed—meaning thereby that if I was in my study I might perceive it,
> or that some other spirit actually does perceive it. … For as to what is said of the
> absolute existence of unthinking things without any relation to their being perceived,
> that seems perfectly unintelligible. **Their ESSE is PERCIPI, nor is it possible they
> should have any existence out of the minds or thinking things which perceive them.**"
> — *Principles* §3
- NOTE on the Latin: Berkeley's own Latin tag in §3 is "*Their ESSE is PERCIPI*" (i.e.
  *esse est percipi* in the standard citation form, "to be is to be perceived"). The
  prose around it is the verbatim English above. Treat the dictionary tag *esse est
  percipi* as the conventional shorthand; the on-page sentence is the §3 line.

### The apple = a bundle of sensible ideas — Principles §1 (verbatim)
> "Thus, for example a certain colour, taste, smell, figure and consistence having been
> observed to go together, are accounted one distinct thing, signified by the name
> APPLE. Other collections of ideas constitute a stone, a tree, a book, and the like
> sensible things." — *Principles* §1

### Mind / spirit — Principles §2 (verbatim)
> "This perceiving, active being is what I call MIND, SPIRIT, SOUL, or MYSELF. By which
> words I do not denote any one of my ideas, but a thing entirely distinct from them,
> WHEREIN THEY EXIST … for the existence of an idea consists in being perceived."
> — *Principles* §2

### We never perceive anything but ideas — Principles §4 (verbatim)
> "For, what are the fore-mentioned objects but the things we perceive by sense? and
> what do we PERCEIVE BESIDES OUR OWN IDEAS OR SENSATIONS? and is it not plainly
> repugnant that any one of these, or any combination of them, should exist
> unperceived?" — *Principles* §4

### An idea can be like nothing but an idea — Principles §8 (verbatim)
> "I ANSWER, an idea can be like nothing but an idea; a colour or figure can be like
> nothing but another colour or figure." — *Principles* §8
- This is the hinge of the anti-matter argument: the supposed "copies" thesis (our
  ideas resemble unperceived material things) is incoherent, because resemblance only
  holds between ideas.

### No substance but spirit — Principles §7 (verbatim)
> "From what has been said it follows there is NOT ANY OTHER SUBSTANCE THAN SPIRIT, or
> that which perceives. … for an idea to exist in an unperceiving thing is a manifest
> contradiction, for TO HAVE AN IDEA IS ALL ONE AS TO PERCEIVE." — *Principles* §7

### Matter as the source of the error / abstraction — Principles §5 (verbatim)
> "If we thoroughly examine this tenet it will, perhaps, be found at bottom to depend
> on the doctrine of ABSTRACT IDEAS. For can there be a nicer strain of abstraction than
> to distinguish the existence of sensible objects from their being perceived, so as to
> conceive them existing unperceived?" — *Principles* §5

### Primary vs secondary qualities collapsed — Principles §9 (verbatim)
> "Some there are who make a DISTINCTION betwixt PRIMARY and SECONDARY qualities. By the
> former they mean extension, figure, motion, rest, solidity or impenetrability, and
> number; by the latter they denote all other sensible qualities, as colours, sounds,
> tastes, and so forth. … But it is evident from what we have already shown, that
> extension, figure, and motion are ONLY IDEAS EXISTING IN THE MIND, and that an idea
> can be like nothing but another idea … Hence, it is plain that the very notion of what
> is called MATTER or CORPOREAL SUBSTANCE, involves a contradiction in it."
> — *Principles* §9
- The target named here is **Locke's** distinction (primary qualities resemble the
  thing; secondary qualities don't). Berkeley argues the two cannot be pried apart, so
  if secondary qualities are mind-dependent (which the Lockeans grant), so are primary.
  [The "Locke" attribution is the standard reading; §9 names the position, not Locke by
  name — keep prose accurate: "the view Locke held," not "Berkeley quotes Locke."]

### The trees-in-a-park puzzle (Berkeley's own version of the "tree in the forest") — Principles §23 (verbatim)
> "But, say you, surely there is nothing easier than for me to imagine trees, for
> instance, in a park, or books existing in a closet, and nobody by to perceive them. I
> answer, you may so, there is no difficulty in it; but what is all this, I beseech you,
> more than framing in your mind certain ideas which you call BOOKS and TREES, and the
> same time omitting to frame the idea of any one that may perceive them? BUT DO NOT YOU
> YOURSELF PERCEIVE OR THINK OF THEM ALL THE WHILE? … IT IS NECESSARY THAT YOU CONCEIVE
> THEM EXISTING UNCONCEIVED OR UNTHOUGHT OF, WHICH IS A MANIFEST REPUGNANCY."
> — *Principles* §23
- This is the **master argument** (so named by later scholars; do NOT call it that on
  the page as if Berkeley named it). Use it where the prose handles "unperceived
  existence."

### The world keeps existing — God / other spirits — Principles §48 (verbatim)
> "though we hold indeed the objects of sense to be nothing else but ideas which cannot
> exist unperceived; yet we may not hence conclude they have no existence except only
> while they are perceived by US, since THERE MAY BE SOME OTHER SPIRIT THAT PERCEIVES
> THEM THOUGH WE DO NOT. … It does not therefore follow from the foregoing principles
> that bodies are annihilated and created every moment, or exist not at all during the
> intervals between our perception of them." — *Principles* §48

### God as the will that produces the ideas of sense — Principles §29–30 (verbatim)
> "But, whatever power I may have over MY OWN thoughts, I find the ideas actually
> perceived by Sense have not a like dependence on my will. … There is THEREFORE SOME
> OTHER WILL OR SPIRIT that PRODUCES THEM." — *Principles* §29
> "The ideas of Sense are more strong, lively, and DISTINCT than those of the
> imagination; they have likewise a steadiness, order, and coherence … the admirable
> connexion whereof sufficiently testifies the wisdom and benevolence of its Author. Now
> THE SET RULES OR ESTABLISHED METHODS WHEREIN THE MIND WE DEPEND ON EXCITES IN US THE
> IDEAS OF SENSE, ARE CALLED THE LAWS OF NATURE." — *Principles* §30
- The argument that distinguishes Berkeley from solipsism: the ideas of sense are NOT
  up to me (I can't choose not to see the room in daylight), they come in a steady law-
  governed order I didn't author, so they are produced by another, greater mind — God.

### Real things vs chimeras — God is the "Author of nature" — Principles §33 (verbatim)
> "The ideas imprinted on the Senses by the Author of nature are called REAL THINGS; and
> those excited in the imagination being less regular, vivid, and constant, are more
> properly termed IDEAS, or IMAGES OF THINGS, which they copy and represent. … the sun
> that I see by day is the real sun, and that which I imagine by night is the idea of the
> former." — *Principles* §33 (sun line is §36; both verified)
> "And in this sense the sun that I see by day is the real sun, and that which I imagine
> by night is the idea of the former." — *Principles* §36

### This is NOT the claim that the world is unreal — Principles §34–35 (verbatim)
> "by the principles premised we are not deprived of any one thing in nature. Whatever we
> see, feel, hear, or anywise conceive or understand remains as secure as ever, and is as
> real as ever. There is a RERUM NATURA, and the distinction between realities and
> chimeras retains its full force." — *Principles* §34
> "I do not argue against the existence of any one thing that we can apprehend either by
> sense or reflexion. That the things I see with my eyes and touch with my hands do
> exist, really exist, I make not the least question. The only thing whose existence we
> deny IS THAT WHICH PHILOSOPHERS CALL MATTER or corporeal substance. … The Atheist
> indeed will want the colour of an empty name to support his impiety." — *Principles* §35
- §35 is load-bearing TWICE: (1) the tree/world is as real as ever, only mental; (2) the
  apologetic motive in Berkeley's own words — denying matter is meant to knock the prop
  out from under atheism.

## *Three Dialogues* — VERBATIM quotes (cite by speaker)
### Philonous quitting metaphysics for common sense — Dialogue 1 (verbatim)
> "I have quitted several of the sublime notions I had got in their schools for vulgar
> opinions. And I give it you on my word; since this revolt from metaphysical notions to
> the plain dictates of nature and common sense, I find my understanding strangely
> enlightened." — Philonous, *Three Dialogues*, First Dialogue
- Berkeley's self-image: not a denier of the world but a returner to common sense, the
  plain man who says the table is just what he sees and feels.

### God necessarily inferred — Dialogue 2/3 (verbatim)
> "Farther, from my own being, and from the dependency I find in myself and my ideas, I
> do, by an act of reason, necessarily infer the existence of a God, and of all created
> things in the mind of God." — Philonous, *Three Dialogues*, Third Dialogue

## The "if a tree falls in a forest" attribution — 🟡 LEGEND-FRAME-IT
- The popular riddle "If a tree falls in a forest and no one is around to hear it, does
  it make a sound?" is **NOT Berkeley's line.** No extant Berkeley text poses it. The
  modern phrasing traces to *Physics* (Mann & Twiss, 1910); a near version appeared in
  *The Chautauquan*, June 1883. Berkeley's *own* version of the puzzle is the trees-in-
  a-park passage, *Principles* §23 (above).
- Source: Wikipedia, "If a tree falls in a forest…" (origin section). The page is the
  standard reference; the 1910/1883 datings are well-attested there.
- ON-PAGE RULE: if the falling-tree line is used, frame it AS a later popularization
  Berkeley never wrote, then give the real §23 passage. Never put it in his mouth.

## "Why this is a break" block content (steelmanned BEFORE / AFTER)
- **beforeLabel** (steelman, the view Berkeley broke from): "There is a material world
  out there, behind our perceptions." The natural, near-universal view (Locke's, the
  scientists', common sense): perceptions are caused by, and are copies of, mind-
  independent material objects; matter is the stuff that stays when no one looks.
- **afterLabel**: "There is no matter at all; to be is to be perceived." Only minds and
  their ideas exist. An ordinary object just IS its bundle of sensible ideas; "matter"
  underneath is an empty, self-contradictory abstraction.
- The concrete move (gated): run Locke's own rule — every idea traces to an experience —
  on the idea of *matter itself*. We only ever perceive ideas/sensations (§4); an idea
  can resemble only an idea (§8); so "unperceived material substrate" answers to no
  possible experience and does no explanatory work. Drop it. What's left is minds, ideas,
  and the greatest Mind holding the world steady (§§29–30, §48).
- Steelman the loser fairly (framing gate, Berkeley-specific): the materialist isn't a
  fool. Matter explains the order, persistence, and shared-ness of experience. Berkeley
  must replace ALL of that work, and his replacement is God, not nothing. Present the
  materialist case at full strength before the §4/§8 objection.

## Apocryphal / potted-summary traps the fact-checker must guard
1. "If a tree falls in a forest" = NOT Berkeley (above). 🟡
2. **"Berkeley denied the world is real" / "Berkeley thought nothing exists"** — the
   #1 caricature, and FALSE by §34–35 in his own words. He affirms the tree, the table,
   the sun are exactly as real as ever; he denies only *matter* (the unperceived
   substrate). The framing gate flags any prose that lets the caricature stand
   unanswered. The genre's besetting sin (strawmanning Berkeley) is named in the
   pipeline doc itself.
3. **"Subjective idealism = the world is just in MY head" (solipsism)** — FALSE. §29–30,
   §48: the ideas of sense are NOT up to me, they come law-governed from another mind
   (God); other spirits perceive too. Berkeley is an idealist, not a solipsist.
4. **"Idealism means having ideals"** — the single most misleading term in the corpus
   (pipeline clarity gate). Define it on first use: idealism = the view that reality is
   fundamentally mental (made of minds and ideas), nothing to do with high principles.
5. **Dr. Johnson's "I refute it thus" (kicking the stone)** — Boswell reports Samuel
   Johnson kicking a stone to "refute" Berkeley. 🟡 LEGEND-FRAME-IT: it is a genuine
   Boswell anecdote (1763), but it is NOT a refutation — Berkeley never denied the stone
   or the felt resistance; the kick is exactly a bundle of sensible ideas. Use it only as
   a famous *mis*understanding, framed as such, never as a hit on Berkeley. [Optional
   color; if used, attribute to Boswell's *Life of Johnson*, fact-check at fact-check.]
6. Do not call §23 "the master argument" or §1's apple "the bundle theory" as if Berkeley
   coined those labels — they are later scholars' names. State the argument; don't
   credit Berkeley with the terminology.
