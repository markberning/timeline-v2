## Ordered gloss-gap list — MUST-FIX on top

### MUST-FIX

**MF-1 — Ch 1: Bentham introduced without a one-liner who-he-is.**
"A blunt English reformer named Jeremy Bentham (1748 to 1832) was systematizing pleasure." The dates are there but there is no one-line who-he-is — reformer in what sense? of what institutions? A newcomer following the era-4 Kant-and-Hegel arc has no frame for Bentham. Every thinker in earlier eras got "this is who they were before we get to the ideas." Bentham gets his dates and his idea in the same breath.
**Fix:** "a blunt English legal reformer named Jeremy Bentham (1748 to 1832) — a man who wanted to replace custom and scripture with pure calculation in the design of laws and prisons — was systematizing pleasure."

---

**MF-2 — Ch 1 / Ch 2: "the greatest happiness principle" / "greatest happiness of the greatest number" — the name is used, the doctrine is not labeled.**
Utilitarianism is correctly glossed at first use in Ch 1: "the view that the right action is the one that produces the most happiness, counting pleasure as a plus and pain as a minus, for the greatest number of people." That is solid. But the phrase "greatest happiness principle" (the standard name for this doctrine, used in philosophy courses and by Mill himself in *Utilitarianism*) never appears. When a reader later encounters it in secondary sources or in Mill's own text, they will not recognize it as the thing they already learned. This is the landmine term the gate spec flags explicitly.
**Fix:** After the gloss in Ch 1, add: "— a principle Mill would later formalize under the name 'the greatest happiness principle.'" OR label it in Ch 2 where Mill develops it: "what Bentham had called the greatest happiness principle, the core of utilitarian moral reasoning."

---

**MF-3 — Ch 2: "political economy" used without a gloss.**
"Doing political economy as a boy the way other children do chores." A sharp 15-year-old in 2026 will hear "political economy" as a historical-sounding phrase they cannot place. It is not defined anywhere in the draft.
**Fix:** one parenthetical: "doing political economy (the study of how wealth is produced and distributed — what we now call economics) as a boy."

---

**MF-4 — Ch 4: species-being NEVER named.**
Marx's concept of *Gattungswesen* (species-being) — the idea that what makes humans distinctive is their capacity for conscious, self-directed creative labor, the very thing alienation destroys — is the philosophical foundation of the whole alienation argument. The draft explains the IDEA fully ("human beings are conscious self-creators; we can make our own life-activity, our work, the deliberate object of our will and imagination") but never gives it its name. The gate spec explicitly lists "species-being" as a landmine term to confirm glossed at first use.
**Fix:** In the alienation passage, after "conscious self-creators," add: "— what Marx calls our species-being, our nature as the one kind of animal that works consciously and imaginatively rather than by instinct."

---

**MF-5 — Ch 4: proletariat and bourgeoisie NEVER named.**
The class analysis is the scaffolding under the whole chapter — the factory owner vs. the worker, who owns the means of production vs. who sells their labor — but the words "proletariat" (the working class, people who own only their labor) and "bourgeoisie" (the owning/capitalist class) never appear. The gate spec lists both explicitly as landmine terms. A reader who later picks up any Marx text, or any discussion of Marx, will immediately hit these words and have no anchor.
**Fix:** In the alienation worked example, after "A man spends twelve hours a day fitting one bolt...": "The product he makes is not his; it belongs to the owner — who Marx calls the bourgeoisie, the class that owns the factories and tools — and is sold past him. That worker belongs to what Marx calls the proletariat: the class that owns nothing but its own labor and must sell it to survive."

---

**MF-6 — Ch 4: Engels introduced without a who-he-is.**
"Leaned heavily on a curated, simplified Marx assembled by his collaborator Engels and later political movements." Friedrich Engels appears here as a proper name with no introduction — no dates, no who-he-is. He reappears at the end of the chapter as the man who published the *Theses on Feuerbach*. A newcomer has no idea who Engels is.
**Fix:** At first use: "his collaborator Friedrich Engels — a German industrialist's son who co-wrote the *Communist Manifesto* with Marx in 1848 and spent decades editing and publishing his notes."

---

**MF-7 — Ch 5: ressentiment gloss has a critical gap.**
The gloss is present and better than most: "a bottled hostility that cannot vent itself in action and so turns inward and becomes *creative*, generating values instead of blows." The problem is that the draft then immediately quotes the *Genealogy* using the English word "resentment" in the quote ("The revolt of the slaves in morals begins in the very principle of resentment becoming creative..."), and the explanation for WHY Nietzsche uses the French word "on purpose" is never given. A newcomer reading this will think "ressentiment" is just a fancy French spelling of the same thing. The distinction matters: Nietzsche uses the French to signal that this is a precise technical concept, not ordinary grudge-holding — it is specifically the psychology of those who cannot act, who sublimate their impotence into a creative moral inversion.
**Fix:** After the gloss, add: "He borrows the French word deliberately, to mark this as a technical term — not ordinary resentment, which is a passing feeling, but a chronic psychological state where blocked aggression turns into the engine of value-creation."

---

**MF-8 — Ch 5: nihilism NEVER named or defined.**
Nihilism — the view that life has no inherent meaning, value, or purpose — is the philosophical crisis the death of God produces. It is the answer Nietzsche is most urgently trying to avoid when he proposes the will to power, eternal recurrence, and the overman. Without the word "nihilism," a newcomer cannot understand what the stakes of Nietzsche's project are. The draft gets as far as "the whole structure of values hangs in midair with nothing under it" — that IS nihilism — but never names it. The gate spec lists "nihilism" as a landmine term to confirm glossed at first use.
**Fix:** In the "death of God" passage, after "the whole structure of values hangs in midair with nothing under it": "— the condition philosophers call nihilism, the collapse of any ground for saying that anything matters or has value."

---

**MF-9 — Ch 5 / Ch 6: "existentialists" used without prior definition.**
Ch 6 says: "Another line, the existentialists and their heirs, picked up exactly Nietzsche's and Kierkegaard's question, how to live, how to make meaning, in a universe that no longer hands it to you." "Existentialists" appears here as a label the reader has never been given. Existentialism — the philosophical movement centered on individual existence, freedom, choice, and the making of meaning in the absence of a given essence — is never defined anywhere in the draft. The gate spec lists "existentialism" as a landmine term.
**Fix:** Either define it in Ch 5 when Nietzsche's project is described — "Nietzsche's question became the founding question of the existentialist movement (the view that existence precedes essence — that humans are thrown into the world without a given nature or purpose and must make meaning for themselves)" — OR gloss it inline in Ch 6 at the word's first appearance.

---

**MF-10 — Ch 5: perspectivism NEVER mentioned.**
Perspectivism — Nietzsche's claim that there are no facts, only interpretations, and that every view of the world is a view from somewhere — is one of his five or six most load-bearing ideas and is named in the gate spec as a landmine term to confirm glossed at first use. It is the philosophical background of the "death of God" claim: the reason the West's moral order loses its claim to objective truth once its divine backing is gone is precisely because, on Nietzsche's account, all supposed objective truths are perspectival. The draft walks around the concept without naming it.
**Fix:** After the will-to-power passage, add a short paragraph: "Underneath all of it is what he calls perspectivism: the claim that there is no single, God's-eye view of the world, no set of facts that are just true from nowhere. Every interpretation of the world is made from a position, shaped by the needs and drives of the creature making it. This is not relativism — Nietzsche did not think all perspectives are equally good — but it does mean that any claim to absolute, context-free truth should make you suspicious."

### SHOULD-FIX

**SF-1 — Era hook / break block: "Schopenhauer" named without a full gloss.**
The break block says: "He had started as a disciple of Schopenhauer, the gloomy heir of Kant who taught that a blind 'will to live' drives everything." This is functional — the reader who has done era 4 (Kant and the Germans) should know Schopenhauer. But if any reader missed or half-remembered that chapter, "gloomy heir of Kant" is thin. SHOULD-FIX rather than MUST-FIX because the idea ("will to live") is explained and Nietzsche's correction of it is the point.

---

**SF-2 — Ch 3: "Lutheran" used without a gloss.**
"Kierkegaard grew up in Copenhagen under a severe, guilt-haunted Lutheran father." Lutheran (a follower of Martin Luther's Protestant reform of Christianity, which rejected Catholic authority and emphasized personal faith and scripture) is not defined. A newcomer with no religious background will not know what makes a "Lutheran father" different from any other kind of Christian father, or why guilt and severity are specifically associated with it. Not MUST-FIX because the word is biographical color, not load-bearing for the philosophical argument.
**Fix:** "a severe, guilt-haunted father in the Lutheran tradition — the branch of Protestant Christianity most saturated with the idea of sin and grace."

---

**SF-3 — Ch 4: "the Communist Manifesto" cited without a gloss.**
"It is carved on Marx's grave at Highgate Cemetery in London, alongside the closing cry of the *Communist Manifesto*." The *Manifesto* (1848, co-written with Engels) is Marx's most famous published work — a political pamphlet calling for workers to unite and overthrow capitalist rule — but it is cited here as if the reader already knows it. A newcomer who has been told to "leave the twentieth century at the door" will not know what the *Manifesto* is.
**Fix:** At the first reference: "the *Communist Manifesto* — the 1848 pamphlet Marx wrote with Engels calling on workers to unite and overthrow the system that exploited them, the most read political document of the next century."

---

**SF-4 — Ch 5: "the Genealogy" cited without a full title.**
"He puts it directly in the *Genealogy*." A newcomer will not know what "the Genealogy" is — the full title is *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887). The draft mentions "the genealogy of our morals" as a concept earlier in the same paragraph, which helps, but the book title is still dropped as a shorthand without the full name ever appearing.
**Fix:** On first mention, give the full title: "*On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887) — the book where he traces the historical origins of our moral vocabulary."

---

**SF-5 — Ch 5: "free spirits" — used without a gloss.**
"A possible new dawn for what he calls free spirits, people who could treat the collapse of the old values as a clearing rather than only a grave." "Free spirits" is Nietzsche's term for the rare type who can survive the death of God with their intellectual integrity intact and potentially create new values. The draft's immediate parenthetical ("people who could treat the collapse of the old values as a clearing") is the right instinct, but a newcomer may not grasp that this is a technical term Nietzsche uses across several books, not just a general compliment.
**Fix:** Minimal: flag it as his term: "what he calls free spirits — his term for the rare type of mind that can bear the death of God and treat the wreckage as possibility."

---

**SF-6 — Ch 5: "amor fati" — Latin phrase used without translation at point of use.**
"The person who could hear the demon and say *yes, again, and again* has achieved what Nietzsche calls amor fati, the love of fate." The translation immediately follows — "the love of fate" — so this is almost correct. But the Latin appears first and the translation second, which may make a newcomer stall for a beat before the gloss arrives. Small sequencing issue.
**Fix:** Flip the order: "what Nietzsche calls the love of fate (*amor fati* in Latin)."

---

**SF-7 — Ch 4: "commodity" — used as the technical term before the phrase "commodity fetishism" introduces the philosophy.**
"Second, commodity fetishism, the one piece of his mature economics that is pure philosophy. In *Capital* Marx notices something odd about a thing for sale." The word "commodity" does a lot of work: in Marx it does not just mean "a product" but specifically a product produced for exchange and sale rather than for direct use. The distinction (use-value vs. exchange-value) is what makes the fetishism point land — the fetishism is precisely the confusion of a social relationship (exchange-value) with a natural property of the object. The draft skips this step.
**Fix:** At the top of the commodity-fetishism paragraph: "A commodity — Marx's word for any good produced not to use but to sell — appears at first sight..."

---

**SF-8 — Break block: "Nietzsche... had started as a disciple of Schopenhauer" — the specific Schopenhauer link (will to live → will to power correction) is explained in the break block but not repeated in Ch 5.**
Ch 5 says "the will to power is not a lust to dominate other people. It is Nietzsche's correction of his old teacher Schopenhauer, who had said the basic drive in all life is a blind will to survive." This is repeated from the break block — good. However, the break block uses "his old teacher Schopenhauer" and Ch 5 also uses "his old teacher Schopenhauer," but Schopenhauer was not literally Nietzsche's classroom teacher; he read Schopenhauer as a student and was profoundly influenced by him. This is not a clarity gap for a newcomer, but it is a small imprecision worth flagging. Not MUST-FIX.

## Argument-step audit — skipped steps

The draft is notably strong at walking arguments. No chapter fails on mysticism gaps. Three places where a step is compressed enough to flag:

**AS-1 — Ch 2: The Mill breakdown → higher pleasures connection is compressed.**
The narrative says Mill had a breakdown when he asked himself if achieving all his reforms would make him happy, and the answer was no. Then it jumps directly to Mill's distinction between higher and lower pleasures. A newcomer might miss the logical link: the breakdown revealed that Bentham's calculus could not account for the *quality* of happiness, only the quantity — and that is precisely why Mill needed the higher/lower distinction. The connection is implicit, not stated.
**Fix (SHOULD-FIX):** One bridging sentence: "The breakdown told him something Bentham's system had no room for: that what kind of happiness matters, not just how much of it you can stack up."

**AS-2 — Ch 4: The base → superstructure model is explained, but the claim that ruling-class IDEAS are the dominant ideas is skipped.**
Marx's argument in *The German Ideology* ("The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas") is the key step between "the base shapes the superstructure" and the critique of religion and philosophy. The draft explains the structure but not the mechanism: WHY does the base produce ideas that defend it? The answer Marx gives is that the class that controls the means of production also controls the means of intellectual production. Without this step, the claim that ideas "grow out of, and tend to defend, the way that society makes its living" feels asserted rather than argued.
**Fix (SHOULD-FIX):** Add one sentence: "And since the class that owns the factories also controls the schools and the printing presses, its preferred story about the world — that property rights are natural, that the current arrangement is just — tends to be the story everyone learns."

**AS-3 — Ch 5: The connection between "the death of God" and "nihilism" (even without the word) is implied but not stated as a logical consequence.**
The draft says: "Pull that God out, as European culture had effectively done, and the whole structure of values hangs in midair with nothing under it." This is the consequence. But the next sentence moves to "that is the catastrophe" rather than spelling out why the loss of God specifically takes the moral structure with it. A newcomer might ask: why can't you keep the moral system without the theology? This is the crux of Nietzsche's argument — that Western morality is not free-standing but depends on the metaphysical framework it grew inside.
**Fix (SHOULD-FIX):** One sentence: "And the morality cannot simply detach itself and survive on its own; it grew inside the theology, assumed it, borrowed its authority from it — so when the theology goes, the morality has no ground left to stand on either."

## Summary assessment

This is the cleanest draft of the five sections gate 4 has reviewed. The argument-walk chapters — especially the teleological suspension of the ethical and the death of God — are exemplary: every step stated, every term defined, every steelman in place. The treatment of the death of God in Ch 5 is the best in the draft by some distance.

The MUST-FIX list concentrates in two areas:

1. **Terms the spec explicitly required that are simply absent:** "greatest happiness principle" (the standard name for utilitarianism's core), "species-being" (the philosophical foundation under alienation), "proletariat/bourgeoisie" (the class terms that run through all of Marx), "nihilism" (the crisis Nietzsche is answering), "perspectivism" (the epistemological backstory of the death of God), "existentialism" (used as a known label in Ch 6 without definition).

2. **Partial glosses with a gap:** ressentiment is glossed but the French-vs-English distinction is not explained.

None of the MUST-FIX items require rewriting argument passages. MF-1 (Bentham), MF-2 (greatest happiness principle), MF-3 (political economy), MF-6 (Engels), MF-7 (ressentiment gap), MF-8 (nihilism) are one-sentence additions. MF-4 (species-being) and MF-5 (proletariat/bourgeoisie) require two sentences each in the alienation passage. MF-9 (existentialism) needs one sentence either in Ch 5 or Ch 6. MF-10 (perspectivism) is the only item that needs a short paragraph — it is a genuine absence, not a labeling gap.

No chapter needs structural rewrite on clarity grounds.

**Overall gate verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (PASS-WITH-FIXES). 10 MUST-FIX items. No structural rewrites required.**
