### Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion: composition and withholding
- Main draft written 1750–52 (some sources say 1751–52 during Edinburgh residence).
- Revised c. 1757 and c. 1761.
- Final alterations 1776, shortly before death.
- Hume showed MS to friends who dissuaded publication; also feared religious forces

- Published posthumously 1779 (without author's or publisher's name initially); Hume's
  nephew arranged publication.

## 2. KEY PASSAGES

All passages below verified at davidhume.org (the canonical scholarly text, keyed to
Selby-Bigge/Nidditch [SBN] locus) or Project Gutenberg.

### 2.1 Copy Principle — impressions vs. ideas (Treatise 1.1.1)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/t/1/1/1:

**Impressions:**
> "Those perceptions, which enter with most force and violence, we may name impressions:
> and under this name I comprehend all our sensations, passions and emotions, as they make
> their first appearance in the soul."

**Ideas:**
> "The faint images of these in thinking and reasoning; such as, for instance, are all the
> perceptions excited by the present discourse."

**The Copy Principle stated:**
> "That all our simple ideas in their first appearance are deriv'd from simple impressions,
> which are correspondent to them, and which they exactly represent."

✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/t/1/1/1; also Gutenberg #4705)

**Missing shade of blue** (Treatise 1.1.1) — acknowledged exception to the copy principle:
> "A person who has enjoyed his sight for thirty years, and has become perfectly well
> acquainted with colours of all kinds, excepting one particular shade of blue...
> whether 'tis possible for him, from his own imagination, to supply this deficiency,
> and raise up to himself the idea of that particular shade, tho' it had never been
> conveyed to him by his senses?"

Hume concedes most would say yes — he calls it "so particular and singular, that 'tis
scarce worth our observing, and is not able to overturn our general maxim."
✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/t/1/1/1; Gutenberg #4705 confirmed in era-3)

### 2.2 Hume's Fork — Relations of Ideas vs. Matters of Fact (Enquiry §4)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/e/4:

> "All the objects of human reason or enquiry may naturally be divided into two kinds, to
> wit, Relations of Ideas, and Matters of Fact."

- **Relations of Ideas** (geometry, algebra, arithmetic): knowable through reason alone,
  certain even if no circles or triangles exist in nature.
- **Matters of Fact**: "The contrary of every matter of fact is still possible; because
  it can never imply a contradiction."
✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/e/4)

### 2.3 Billiard balls — causation as constant conjunction (Enquiry §4)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/e/4 (era-3 verbatim; deepened here):

> "Motion in the second Billiard-ball is a quite distinct event from motion in the first;
> nor is there any thing in the one to suggest the smallest hint of the other."

✅ VERIFIED (era-3 verified; davidhume.org/texts/e/4 also confirmed here)

### 2.4 The induction problem — why we expect the future to resemble the past (Enquiry §4)
Hume's core formulation at davidhume.org/texts/e/4:

> "all our experimental conclusions proceed upon the supposition that the future will be
> conformable to the past."

And the circularity problem:
> "It is impossible, therefore, that any arguments from experience can prove this
> resemblance of the past to the future; since all these arguments are founded on the
> supposition of that resemblance."

This is the induction problem: experience cannot justify experience without going in a
circle. ✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/e/4; also phrasing confirmed via Stanford/Web
search secondary corroboration)

### 2.5 Custom or Habit — the sceptical solution (Enquiry §5)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/e/5:

> "This principle is **Custom** or **Habit**. For wherever the repetition of any particular
> act or operation produces a propensity to renew the same act or operation, without being
> impelled by any reasoning or process of the understanding; we always say, that this
> propensity is the effect of *Custom*."

> "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life. It is that principle alone, which
> renders our experience useful to us, and makes us expect, for the future, a similar
> train of events with those which have appeared in the past."

After the constant conjunction passage:
> "After the constant conjunction of two objects, heat and flame, for instance, weight
> and solidity, we are determined by custom alone to expect the one from the appearance
> of the other."

✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/e/5)

### 2.6 Bundle self (Treatise 1.4.6)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/6:

> "they are nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed
> each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement"

> "The mind is a kind of theatre, where several perceptions successively make their
> appearance; pass, re-pass, glide away, and mingle in an infinite variety of postures
> and situations."

Argument: "I never can catch myself at any time without a perception" (T 1.4.6.3) —
no stable impression of a permanent self, therefore no such idea.
✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/6; era-3 also VERIFIED)

### 2.7 Is/ought gap — full passage (Treatise 3.1.1)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/t/3/1/1:

> "In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark'd,
> that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes
> the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I
> am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and
> *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought
> not*. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as
> this *ought*, or *ought not*, expresses some new relation or affirmation, 'tis necessary
> that it shou'd be observ'd and explain'd; and at the same time that a reason should be
> given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction
> from others, which are entirely different from it."

(SBN locus: T 3.1.1.27; paragraph reference IEP gives as Treatise 3.1.1.26 — minor
paragraph-count variance between editions, same passage.)
✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/t/3/1/1)

**What this means (for the author, not to be voiced as Hume's words):** Hume does not
say you CAN'T derive ought from is — he says no one has explained how, and that the
transition requires explicit justification. The logical gap (Hume's guillotine) is the
standard reading; some scholars dispute it as a mere methodological observation. The
thinker page can use "Hume's guillotine" as an explanatory label but NOT as Hume's
own term — he never used it.

### 2.8 Reason is the slave of the passions (Treatise 2.3.3)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/t/2/3/3:

> "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to
> any other office than to serve and obey them."

(SBN: T 2.3.3.4/415 per SEP citation)

**Surrounding argument (PARAPHRASE — verified from davidhume.org/texts/t/2/3/3 and SEP):**
- Reason alone produces no impulse toward action.
- Any apparent conflict between reason and passion is mislabeled — what opposes a
  passion is another passion, not reason.
- The impulse arises from passion; reason only "directs it" by tracing causal connections.
  ("the impulse arises not from reason, but is only directed by it.")
- Passions are "original existences" — they contain no representative quality and cannot
  contradict truth. Only when a passion rests on a false belief about causes or effects
  can reason "correct" it — but reason is correcting the false judgment, not the passion.
- **This is NOT nihilism about ethics.** Hume's point: desire supplies the ends; reason
  tells you how to get them. Combined with his moral sentimentalism (sympathy underlies
  moral approval), this is a substantive ethical theory. Desires, not reason, do the
  motivational work.
✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/t/2/3/3; SEP "David Hume")

### 2.9 Miracles — key maxim (Enquiry §10)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/e/10:

**Definition of miracle:**
> "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable
> experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature
> of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined."
(E 10.12)

**The maxim:**
> "That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of
> such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it
> endeavours to establish; and even in that case there is a mutual destruction of
> arguments, and the superior only gives us an assurance suitable to that degree of
> force, which remains, after deducting the inferior."
(E 10.13)

✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/e/10; also confirmed via secondary search of exact wording)

**Weighing testimony:**
> "there is proof against proof, of which the strongest must prevail, but still with a
> diminution of its force, in proportion to that of its antagonist"
(E 10.11)

✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/e/10)

### 2.10 Mitigated scepticism vs. Pyrrhonism (Enquiry §12)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/e/12:

**The Pyrrhonian extreme (rejected):** Produces paralyzing doubt; no "durable good"; nature
overwhelms such principles, leaving practitioners identical to other philosophers. (E 12.23)

**Mitigated scepticism (endorsed):**
> "a more mitigated scepticism or academical philosophy, which may be both durable and
> useful"
(E 12.24)

This inspires intellectual modesty by "revealing the strange infirmities of human
understanding" and limits inquiry "to such subjects as are best adapted to the narrow
capacity of human understanding." (E 12.25)

✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/e/12)

### 2.11 Commit it to the flames (Enquiry §12)
Verified (era-3 + secondarily here):

> "If we take in our hand any volume; of divinity or school metaphysics, for instance;
> let us ask, Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No.
> Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No.
> Commit it then to the flames: For it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."
(E 12.34)

✅ VERIFIED (era-3; davidhume.org/texts/e/12 section confirmed; exact wording also confirmed
via web search of secondary sources quoting the passage)

### 2.12 Problem of evil via Philo (Dialogues Part X)
Verified at davidhume.org/texts/d/10:

**The Epicurus/Philo questions:**
> "Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not
> willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?"
(D 10.25)

**Philo's core argument:**
> Whatever God wills is executed: "but neither man nor any other animal is happy;
> therefore he does not will their happiness." Divine wisdom never errs in choosing means:
> "but the course of Nature tends not to human or animal felicity: therefore it is not
> established for that purpose."
(D 10.24)

Nature serves "only preservation of individuals and propagation of the species" with
"no care or concern for the happiness of the members." (D 10.26)

Even granting compatibility between suffering and divine attributes is insufficient —
one must "prove these pure, unmixt, and uncontrollable attributes from the present mixt
and confused phenomena," which Philo calls impossible. (D 10.35)

✅ VERIFIED (davidhume.org/texts/d/10)

**Note on Philo's Part XII "concession":** In Dialogues Part XII, Philo appears to
concede that "a purpose, an intention, a design strikes everywhere the most careless,
the most stupid thinker." This is widely debated — most scholars read it as ironic
concession or rhetorical cover, not a genuine change of position. The thinker page
should NOT present this as Hume endorsing design; present it as the Dialogues' deliberate
ambiguity. ⚠️ NEEDS-CHECK (coordinator: confirm the ironic-concession reading is the
dominant scholarly view before authoring)

## 3. IDEAS AS DOCUMENTED POSITIONS

### 3.1 Copy principle and impressions/ideas
All ideas are copies of prior impressions (simple ideas from simple impressions, complex
from complex). Only exception: missing shade of blue — acknowledged by Hume as a
counterexample but "so particular and singular" he sets it aside. Source: Treatise 1.1.1.
The copy principle is the foundation of Hume's empiricism: if a term cannot be traced to
any impression, it has no determinate meaning. This is his deflationary move against
rationalist concepts like "substance," "necessary connection," and "the self."

### 3.2 Causation: no necessary connection in the objects
Hume's argument (Enquiry §4, Treatise 1.3): We observe that A is followed by B (constant
conjunction), never the necessary connection between them. The idea of "necessary
connection" cannot be traced to any external impression. The only impression it can be
traced to is the internal feeling of mental expectation formed by habit. Therefore
"necessary connection" names a feature of our minds, not the world.

### 3.3 Induction: cannot be rationally justified
The assumption that the future will resemble the past ("all our experimental conclusions
proceed upon the supposition that the future will be conformable to the past," Enquiry
§4) cannot be proved by reason (that would assume what's in question) or by experience
(that would be circular). Hume's answer: we rely on custom/habit — psychologically
unavoidable, not rationally compelled.

### 3.4 The self: no impression, no idea
Hume's bundle theory (Treatise 1.4.6): introspection yields only a succession of
perceptions, never an abiding impression of a self. Personal identity is therefore a
fiction produced by the imagination linking successive perceptions. He admits in the
Appendix to the Treatise that he cannot reconcile this with his own theory — the
"bundle problem" remains unresolved.

### 3.5 Is/ought gap (moral epistemology)
See §2.7 above. Hume is not saying morality is impossible — he has a substantial moral
theory based on sentiment and sympathy. He is saying that descriptive facts cannot
logically entail normative conclusions without an explicit account of the bridge.
Source: Treatise 3.1.1.

### 3.6 Moral sentimentalism
Hume's positive moral theory (Enquiry Concerning Principles of Morals; Treatise Bk III):
moral judgments are not products of reason but of sentiment (feeling of approval or
disapproval). Sympathy — the capacity to share others' feelings — is the psychological
mechanism underlying moral approval of social utility. Reason tells us what produces
what; sentiment tells us whether to approve or condemn it.

### 3.7 Miracles
An argument from experience: the uniform experience of natural law always outweighs
testimony, because to trust the testimony we must suppose nature failed more often than
the testimony failed — a greater violation of probability. Source: Enquiry §10.

### 3.8 Natural religion / design argument
The Dialogues' Philo systematically dismantles Cleanthes's design argument (the world
looks like a watch, therefore it has a watchmaker). Philo's moves: (1) an imperfect
world suggests an imperfect or finite designer at most; (2) the problem of evil shows
the evidence is inconsistent with infinite benevolence and omnipotence; (3) even granting
some remote analogy between the world and a human artifact, we cannot infer a being with
specifically Christian attributes.

### 3.9 Mitigated scepticism (positive epistemology)
Hume is not a total skeptic. He accepts Custom and Habit as psychologically necessary
guides that cannot be defeated by argument. What he rejects is (1) speculative
metaphysics beyond experience and (2) dogmatic confidence in the conclusions of pure
reason about matters beyond the senses. He endorses restricting inquiry to "common life"
and to subjects "best adapted to the narrow capacity of human understanding." Source:
Enquiry §12.

## 4. POTTED-SUMMARY TRAPS

### Trap 1: "Hume was an atheist" ❌ DO NOT USE AS FACT
⚠️ This is the #1 potted-summary trap. Hume NEVER called himself an atheist.
He described his position as "total scepticism" or "mitigated scepticism" with respect
to theological claims. He consistently professed ignorance rather than denial. His
public positions were always carefully hedged (for prudential reasons as much as
intellectual). Even on his deathbed (Boswell visit, July 7 1776), when Boswell asked
about a future state, Hume said it was "a most unreasonable fancy that we should exist
for ever" and that he had "never entertained any belief in religion since he began to
read Locke and Clarke" — but he did not say "I am an atheist."
Sources: DOCUMENTED (Boswell private notebooks, whereofonecanspeak.com account; IEP;
SEP). The IEP describes Hume as "skeptical toward religion but not definitively atheist."
**Author instruction:** Do not say "atheist." Say: Hume was skeptical of all religious
claims, accepted mitigated scepticism, and faced death with equanimity and without any
religious consolation — which scandalized observers more than any explicit denial would
have. See §6 for the deathbed scene.

### Trap 2: "Hume's guillotine" as Hume's own term
He never used this phrase. It is a 20th-century label. Use as explanatory gloss only,
never as Hume's terminology.

### Trap 3: Attributing the "dogmatic slumber" phrase to Hume
This is KANT'S line about himself. Exact verified quote (Prolegomena, 1783, 4:260 per
scholarly standard citation):
> "I freely confess: it was the objection of David Hume that first, many years ago,
> interrupted my dogmatic slumber."
(Alternate verified form from era-3: "the suggestion of David Hume was the very thing,
which many years ago first interrupted my dogmatic slumber, and gave my investigations
in the field of speculative philosophy quite a new direction." — minor translation
variance; both from the Prolegomena Preface.)
✅ VERIFIED (era-3; web search secondary confirmation 2026-06-13)
**Author instruction:** Must be clearly framed as Kant saying this about himself in
response to Hume, NOT as Hume's self-description.

### Trap 4: The *Treatise* as ignored forever
Hume called it a failure in "My Own Life" ("fell dead-born from the press") — but this
was his own retrospective verdict on its initial reception, written in 1776. The Treatise
was NOT ignored by history; it is now considered his masterwork. The story is: it
disappointed on first publication, Hume himself considered the *Enquiries* his preferred
versions, but subsequent philosophy has consistently returned to the *Treatise* as the
deeper and more original text.

### Trap 5: Confusing the two Enquiries
*An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) ≠ *An Enquiry Concerning the
Principles of Morals* (1751). The first is the epistemology book (induction, causation,
miracles); the second is the ethics book (sympathy, utility, virtue). Hume considered
the SECOND his best work ("incomparably the best"). Easy to conflate.

## 5. HONEST FRAMING — RACIST FOOTNOTE

### The essay and the footnote
- "Of National Characters" was first published in 1748 in *Essays, Moral and Political*.
  The 1748 version did NOT contain the racist footnote. ✅ VERIFIED
  (branemrys.blogspot.com analysis: footnote "added — for reasons unknown to us — to the
  1753 edition"; secondary search also confirmed "footnote added 1753 edition, not
  present in 1748")
- The footnote was added to the 1753 edition. ✅ VERIFIED (branemrys.blogspot.com;
  multiple secondary sources consistent on 1753 date)

### 1753 footnote wording ✅ VERIFIED (branemrys.blogspot.com; secondary search)
> "I am apt to suspect the negroes, and in general all the other species of men (for
> there are four or five different kinds) to be naturally inferior to the whites."

The footnote continued with claims about civilized nations, ingenious manufactures, and
European slaves, ending:
> "In JAMAICA, indeed, they talk of one negroe as a man of parts and learning; but 'tis
> likely he is admired for very slender accomplishments, like a parrot, who speaks a few
> words plainly."

(Note: "Francis Williams" — the Jamaican man referred to — is named in some later
scholarship; the footnote itself does not name him.)

### 1777 revised footnote wording ✅ VERIFIED (branemrys.blogspot.com; secondary search)
> "I am apt to suspect the negroes to be naturally inferior to the whites. There scarcely
> ever was a civilized nation of that complexion, nor even any individual eminent either
> in action or speculation."

### What changed between 1753 and 1777 ✅ VERIFIED
The revision **NARROWED** the claim — it did NOT sharpen or expand it:
- REMOVED: "and in general all the other species of men (for there are four or five
  different kinds)" — the claim about all non-white races was dropped
- CHANGED: "There **never** was a civilized nation" → "There **scarcely ever** was" —
  the absolute was weakened to a qualified claim
- CHANGED: "'tis likely he is admired for **very** slender accomplishments" → "it is
  likely he is admired for slender accomplishments" — minor softening of register
- The specific claim about Black people remained; the generalization to all other races
  was removed.

**Scholarly debate on the revision:** Immerwahr (1992) reads the 1777 revision as a
response to James Beattie's published attacks; Garrett (2000) questions this reading.
The motivation for the revision remains disputed. Neither the reason for adding (1753)
nor the reason for the partial revision (1777) is definitively established.
Sources: jmphil.org/article/id/2544/ (Hume's Cultural Bigotry); branemrys.blogspot.com;
secondary search citing Immerwahr and Garrett.

**Author instruction on framing:**
- State the footnote was added in 1753, not in the 1748 original.
- Quote the 1753 wording accurately.
- Say the 1777 revision narrowed the scope (removed the generalization to all non-white
  races) and weakened the absolute "never" to "scarcely ever" — but retained the specific
  racist claim about Black people.
- Do NOT say the revision "sharpened" it (the r2 fact-checker caught this error in the
  era-3 pipeline; it is wrong).
- Do NOT frame the revision as a retraction — it wasn't.

## 6. DEATHBED SCENE (1776)

### Context
- Hume diagnosed with intestinal condition mid-1775; health declined through 1776.
- He wrote "My Own Life" in April 1776, months before death. DOCUMENTED.
- Died 25 August 1776. VERIFIED.

### Boswell's visit (7 July 1776)
James Boswell visited Hume on 7 July 1776 — six weeks before Hume's death.
Boswell's account (private notebooks; later published):
- Found Hume "lean, ghastly, and quite of an earthy appearance" but "seemed to be placid
  and even cheerful."
- Hume told Boswell he had "never entertained any belief in religion since he began to
  read Locke and Clarke."
- On immortality: it was "a most unreasonable fancy that we should exist for ever."
- On annihilation: he was no more perturbed by the idea of ceasing to exist than by the
  idea that he had not existed before he was born.
- Boswell documented his own distress afterward: "I left him with impressions which
  disturbed me for some time."
- Boswell tried to call on Hume twice more but was turned away.
✅ DOCUMENTED (whereofonecanspeak.com/2013/04/06/james-boswell-on-the-death-of-david-hume/;
Aeon/Dennis Rasmussen "He died as he lived"; secondary search)

**Boswell's emotional state:** The account reveals Boswell was shaken — he had hoped
to witness a deathbed recantation. Instead he found what he described as genuine
equanimity. The visit "disturbed" him. ✅ DOCUMENTED

**Samuel Johnson:** Johnson was also disturbed by Hume's composure. Boswell urged Johnson
to engage with Hume directly; Johnson reportedly refused. PARAPHRASE-ONLY — specifics
of Johnson's reaction as cited in general biographies; no verbatim primary source
confirmed independently here. ⚠️ NEEDS-CHECK (coordinator: verify Johnson's specific
reaction before including — risk of legendary embellishment)

### Adam Smith's letter to William Strahan (November 1776)
Verified at en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Life_of_David_Hume,_Esq./Letter_from_Adam_Smith:

Smith described Hume's final months:
> "telling jokes, playing cards, and conversing cheerfully with his friends"
(paraphrase of the letter's description — exact phrasing from davidhume.org/texts/mol/
secondary sources; Wikisource text confirms the letter's substance)

The famous closing:
> "I have always considered him, both in his lifetime and since his death, as approaching
> as nearly to the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man, as perhaps the nature of
> human frailty will permit."
✅ VERIFIED (en.wikisource.org; econlib.org)

Also verified — Hume's witty Charon dialogue (from Smith's letter): when told to inform
Colonel Edmondstone of his recovery, Hume replied:
> "I am dying as fast as my enemies, if I have any, could wish, and as easily and
> cheerfully as my best friends could desire."
✅ VERIFIED (Wikisource; econlib summary)

**The controversy:** Smith's eulogy drew enormous public abuse — Smith reportedly said
it brought him "10 times more abuse than the very violent attack I had made upon the
whole commercial system of Great Britain." ✅ DOCUMENTED (Aeon / Dennis Rasmussen
account; widely cited in scholarship)

## 8. LINEAGE

### What Hume inherits

**From Locke:**
1. **Anti-innatism:** Locke's *Essay* Book I demolishes innate ideas; Hume accepts and
   radicalizes — if there are no innate ideas, all content must come from impressions.
   ✅ DOCUMENTED (standard philosophical genealogy; SEP "Innateness History"; IEP "Locke
   Epistemology")
2. **Simple/complex ideas:** Locke's distinction (passively acquired simple ideas, actively
   constructed complex ideas) maps roughly onto Hume's impressions vs. ideas, though Hume's
   framework is more systematic and the copy principle sharper. ✅ DOCUMENTED
3. **Limits of knowledge:** Locke argued human understanding has definite limits; Hume took
   those limits further (we can't even know causal necessity in the world). ✅ DOCUMENTED

**From Berkeley:**
1. **Skeptical method applied to matter:** Berkeley showed that positing external matter
   (beyond ideas) is unnecessary and unknowable. Hume applies the same skeptical move to
   the self — just as Berkeley removed external material substance, Hume removes mental
   substance. The key aphorism: "if you subtract God from Berkeley, you get Hume."
   ✅ PARAPHRASE-ONLY (philosophypages.com; philosophydungeon.weebly.com — standard
   pedagogical formulation, not a dated primary-source quote)
2. Hume reportedly called Berkeley's arguments "the best lessons of skepticism in
   philosophy." PARAPHRASE-ONLY — verify exact wording before quoting.

**From Newton:**
Hume explicitly framed the *Treatise* as applying Newton's experimental method to "moral
subjects" — hence the subtitle. This is not a vague ambition; Hume wanted to be the
Newton of the human mind: discover the laws governing association of ideas and moral
sentiment the way Newton discovered the laws of motion. ✅ VERIFIED (Treatise subtitle
verified; Newton as explicit model is documented in Hume scholarship — SEP, IEP)

### What Hume generates

**→ Kant (the dogmatic slumber reaction, 1783)**
Kant's specific problem: Hume's skepticism about causation threatened Newtonian physics.
If "necessary connection" is just a habit of mind, there is no rational foundation for
the causal laws Newton described. Kant's entire *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) is, in
large part, an attempt to answer this challenge by showing how synthetic a priori
knowledge (knowledge that goes beyond mere definitions but is knowable prior to experience)
is possible. Kant's Prolegomena (1783, 4:260): "I freely confess: it was the objection of
David Hume that first, many years ago, interrupted my dogmatic slumber."
✅ VERIFIED (era-3; confirmed 2026-06-13 via web search)

**→ Utilitarianism (Bentham and Mill)**
Two specific channels:
1. Hume's account of the social utility of the "artificial virtues" (justice, fidelity)
   influenced Bentham's conception of utility. DOCUMENTED (SEP "Utilitarianism History")
2. Hume's account of the role of sentiment in moral judgment and commitment to norms
   influenced Mill's account of the internal sanctions of morality. DOCUMENTED (SEP)
Note: Hume himself is not a utilitarian — he does not derive an "ought to maximize utility"
from his sentimentalism (he is careful about exactly that gap). His pseudo-utilitarianism
is empirical, not prescriptive in the Bentham-Mill sense. ✅ DOCUMENTED (SEP; PARAPHRASE
distinction)

**→ Logical positivism (A. J. Ayer)**
Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) explicitly acknowledges Hume's "commit it to
the flames" passage (Enquiry §12.34) as a direct ancestor of the verification principle:
"this is but a rhetorical version of our own thesis that a sentence which does not express
either a formally true proposition or an empirical hypothesis is devoid of literal
significance." ✅ VERIFIED (Ayer quotation confirmed via secondary source: kant-touch-this
.blogspot.com; angelfire.com/md2/timewarp/ayer.html — both consistent on Ayer's exact
acknowledgment)

**→ Philosophy of science (induction problem)**
Hume's problem of induction is unresolved and foundational. Karl Popper's falsificationism
is a direct response (replace induction with conjecture + refutation). Bertrand Russell
made Hume's problem central to 20th-century epistemology. PARAPHRASE-ONLY (standard
intellectual history; not independently verified here with primary sources — no competing
claim, safe to use as framing).
