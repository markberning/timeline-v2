# Fact pack — PLATO'S *SYMPOSIUM* (Philosophy WORK read, kind = WORK)

**Scope:** One work, in full — Plato's *Symposium* (Greek *Symposion*, "the drinking party"),
a single dialogue read cover to cover: the frame, the seven speeches on Eros (love), and the
Alcibiades coda. This is a deep WORK-level read below the PLATO thinker-pack
(`plato-fact-pack.md`), whose Ch6 ("Platonic love") summarized Diotima's ladder at thinker
altitude. This pack does NOT copy-paste that summary — it walks the WHOLE dialogue as one
structure (all seven speakers, not just Socrates/Diotima) and must NOT contradict the
thinker-pack. Where the two overlap (the ladder wording, the "beauty absolute" line, Diotima's
fictionality), the wording here is re-fetched and re-verified and is the authoritative version
for the WORK read.

**Built:** 2026-06-13, per `audits/philosophy-content-pipeline.md`. Every quotation below was
fetched word-for-word from a public-domain source TODAY (not recalled) and matched

**Status tags (same scheme as the thinker / school / *Republic*-work packs):**
- `VERIFIED` — exact wording confirmed against the linked PD source, translation + locus named.
- `PARAPHRASE-ONLY` — idea is real, no verbatim PD English verified here; ship as paraphrase, never in quote marks.
- `DOCUMENTED` — fact with a close, named source.
- `PRECISION-NOTE` — a place where the common phrasing is loose and the author must be exact.
- `FRAMING-TRAP` — a common potted-summary error; flagged with the correction.

**THE PLATO FIDELITY RULE (carried from the thinker- and *Republic*-packs — sharpened here):**
Plato never speaks in his own voice. Every word is spoken by a character, and in the *Symposium*
the layering is DEEPER than in most dialogues: the narrator is **Apollodorus**, who is reciting
what **Aristodemus** (an eyewitness) told him, years after the party. So a speech is "what
Apollodorus says Aristodemus said that X said." Socrates' own contribution is deeper still: he
reports the teaching of **Diotima**, a (likely fictional) priestess. The author must keep the
nesting straight — write "Socrates argues," "Plato has Socrates report Diotima saying," "in
Aristophanes' speech" — and must NEVER flatten Diotima's or Aristophanes' words into "Plato
believed." The two views the dialogue ELEVATES (Diotima's ascent to Beauty itself) and the one
it stages as comic invention (Aristophanes' soulmate myth) are different things; see
FRAMING-TRAP 1.

## 1. The argument-arc — the whole dialogue, speech by speech

This is the spine the author walks. The *Symposium* is a series of seven encomia (speeches of
praise) on **Eros**, deliberately escalating: the early speeches are partial or playful, each
corrected by the next, until Socrates/Diotima reframes the whole question (love is not a god to
be praised but a *desire* directed at something it lacks), and the ladder of love carries the
argument to its summit — Beauty itself. Then Alcibiades crashes the high philosophy back down to
earth with a drunk, devastatingly personal speech about Socrates the man. The structure is itself
the argument: each lower account of love is *taken up and surpassed*, mirroring the ascent
Diotima describes.

### Frame — Apollodorus recounts the party years later
**Apollodorus**, walking to Athens from Phalerum, is asked by a companion (Glaucon) to retell the
famous speeches at Agathon's party. He wasn't there; he heard it from **Aristodemus**, "a little
fellow who never wore any shoes," a devoted follower of Socrates who WAS there, and he checked
some of it with Socrates himself. So the whole dialogue reaches us at TWO removes, long after the
fact — Agathon has long since left Athens, the party was "in our boyhood." This distancing frame
matters: the *Symposium* is a memory of a memory of an evening that already feels lost. (Verified
text §2, Q-A1/Q-A2.)

### The setup — drink lightly, send away the flute-girl, speak in praise of Love
At Agathon's victory feast the guests, hung over, agree to drink moderately, dismiss the
flute-girl, and — at Eryximachus' suggestion (relaying Phaedrus) — each deliver a speech praising
**Eros**, a god Phaedrus complains has gone strangely unhonored by poets. They speak in order
around the couches.

### Speech 1 — PHAEDRUS: Love is the eldest of the gods, and the inspirer of virtue
Phaedrus argues Eros is **"the eldest of the gods"** (Q-B): no poet names his parents, so he must
be primordial (Hesiod: after Chaos came Earth "And Love"). And because a lover would be most
ashamed to act basely before the beloved, Love is the great spur to virtue and courage — the
proof is **Alcestis**, who alone was willing to die in her husband's place (Q-C), and Achilles,
who died to avenge Patroclus. Love makes ordinary people dare heroic things.

### Speech 2 — PAUSANIAS: two Loves, Common and Heavenly
Pausanias corrects Phaedrus: praising "Love" flat is too crude, because **there are two
Aphrodites and so two Loves** (Q-D). The elder, motherless, born of Uranus alone, is **Heavenly /
Uranian** Love (*Aphrodite Ourania*) — a love of mind and virtue, oriented to the soul. The
younger, daughter of Zeus and Dione, is **Common** Love (*Aphrodite Pandemos*) — bodily,
indiscriminate, the love of "the body rather than the soul." Only the Heavenly love is to be
praised. (This is the speech where the dialogue's pederastic cultural frame — older lover, younger
beloved — is most explicit; the author should contextualize it as the Athenian social setting, not
endorse it.) `PRECISION-NOTE`

### Speech 3 — ERYXIMACHUS the physician: Love runs through ALL of nature
Eryximachus, a doctor, takes Pausanias' "two Loves" and universalizes them: the double love is not
just an affair of the human soul but is **"to be found in the bodies of all animals and in
productions of the earth, and I may say in all that is"** (Q-E). His craft, medicine, is "the
knowledge of the loves and desires of the body" — health is the right harmony of opposites
(hot/cold, wet/dry), which is the body's good love overcoming its bad. Music, astronomy, weather,
religion are all governed by the same principle of attuning opposites. Love is a cosmic force of
harmony. (His speech is the most "scientific" and is gently comic — the physician sees his own
discipline everywhere.) `DOCUMENTED`

### Speech 4 — ARISTOPHANES: the great myth of the split humans (the "soulmate" origin)
The comic poet's speech is the dialogue's most famous set-piece. Original human nature was
different: **the sexes were three** — male, female, and a third, the **androgynous** "man-woman"
(now lost, the word surviving only "as a term of reproach"). Each primeval human was **round, with
four hands and four feet, two faces on one round neck, four ears** — child of sun (the all-male),
earth (all-female), or moon (the man-woman) — and so powerful they "made an attack upon the gods."
**Zeus, to humble them, cut them in two** "like a sorb-apple which is halved for pickling, or as
you might divide an egg with a hair" (Q-F), having Apollo turn the face around and tie off the
skin at the navel. Ever since, each half **"is but the indenture of a man, and he is always
looking for his other half"** (Q-G): love is the longing of the bisected creature for its missing
half, the drive "to make one of two, and heal the state of man." Hephaestus, offered to weld a
pair permanently into one, would find no lover who'd refuse. So **"the desire and pursuit of the
whole is called love"** (Q-G). This is the origin of the modern "soulmates" idea — and it is COMIC
INVENTION inside the dialogue, not the doctrine the dialogue endorses (FRAMING-TRAP 1).

### Speech 5 — AGATHON: Love is the youngest, fairest, most virtuous god (the florid encomium)
The host, a tragic poet, gives the showiest speech — Socrates will tease that it is more about
*beautiful words* than about love. Against Phaedrus, Agathon declares Love **not the oldest but
"the youngest of the gods,"** ever-youthful, tender (he walks only "in the hearts and souls… which
are of all things the softest"), beautiful, supple, and the possessor of every virtue —
**"the fairest and best himself, and the cause of what is fairest and best in others"** (Q-H):
just, temperate, courageous, wise, the source of all art and the peace of the gods. It is rhetoric
at full sail, beautiful and empty — and it sets up the puncture.

### Speech 6 — SOCRATES (reporting DIOTIMA): love is desire for what one lacks, a daimon, and the LADDER
Socrates begins by gently dismantling Agathon with a few questions: **Love is always love OF
something; one desires only what one LACKS; so if Love is love of beauty and goodness, Love
himself cannot possess beauty and goodness — he must lack them** (Q-I). Love is therefore NOT a
beautiful god at all. Then Socrates says he learned the truth from **Diotima of Mantinea**:
- **Love is a great *daimon* (spirit), intermediate between mortal and divine** — "neither mortal
  nor immortal, but in a mean between the two," who "interprets between gods and men… the mediator
  who spans the chasm which divides them" (Q-J). He is the child of **Plenty (Poros)** and
  **Poverty (Penia)**, conceived at Aphrodite's birth-feast — so always needy yet always
  resourceful, neither wise nor ignorant but a *lover of wisdom* (a philosopher). Love is not the
  beloved beautiful object; love is the *yearning* for it.
- **Love is desire to possess the good FOREVER** — and so, at bottom, a desire for **immortality**;
  mortal creatures pursue it by "birth in beauty," whether of the body (children) or the soul
  (virtue, poems, laws, lasting fame).
- **The ladder / ascent of love (210a–211d)** — the dialogue's summit. Rightly guided, a person
  begins by loving **one beautiful body**, then realizes the beauty in all bodies is one and the
  same and so loves **all beautiful bodies**; rises to value **beauty of souls** above bodies; then
  to the beauty of **institutions and laws**; then to the beauty of **the sciences/knowledge**; and
  at last, "drawing towards and contemplating the vast sea of beauty," **suddenly perceives "a
  nature of wondrous beauty"** — **"beauty absolute, separate, simple, and everlasting"** (Q-K),
  the Form of Beauty itself, of which all beautiful things partake. To live "in the contemplation
  of beauty absolute" is the best human life; one who reaches it "brings forth… not images of
  beauty, but realities," true virtue, and becomes "the friend of God" and immortal. THIS is the
  view the *Symposium* elevates — physical love as the FIRST RUNG of a ladder whose top is the
  eternal Form, not its suppression (FRAMING-TRAP 2).

### Speech 7 — ALCIBIADES: the drunk crashes in and praises SOCRATES instead of Love
The doors burst open: **Alcibiades**, gorgeous, garlanded, very drunk, propped up by a flute-girl.
Told the rule is to praise Love, he refuses and instead praises **Socrates** — and in doing so
delivers the most personal portrait of Socrates in all of Plato:
- **The Silenus image:** Socrates is like the carved figures of **Silenus** sold in shops, ugly on
  the outside but **"made to open in the middle, and have images of gods inside them"** (Q-L); and
  like **Marsyas the satyr**, who enchanted with the flute, except Socrates enchants "with words
  only." Open him up, Alcibiades says, and "what temperance there is residing within… divine and
  golden images." The form is the philosophical point of the whole dialogue made flesh: surface
  ugliness, hidden beauty — the body is not where the beauty is.
- **The failed seduction:** Alcibiades, sure Socrates was "seriously enamoured of my beauty,"
  contrived to be alone with him, then lay all night under one cloak with him to win his
  teaching with his body — and Socrates, utterly unmoved, treated him with "natural temperance and
  self-restraint." Alcibiades **"arose as from the couch of a father or an elder brother"** (Q-M):
  nothing happened. The most beautiful young man in Athens could not seduce Socrates — the living
  proof that Socrates has *climbed past* bodily love.
- **Socrates' endurance at Potidaea:** on campaign Socrates out-endured everyone — went without
  food, "marched better than the other soldiers… with his bare feet on the ice," and could
  out-drink any man yet was never seen drunk (Q-N). Physical self-mastery as the outward sign of
  the inward ascent.

The dramatic irony is the dialogue's final move: Alcibiades, the beautiful body who tried to *buy*
wisdom with sex, embodies the failure of Common love; Socrates, who refused him, embodies the
Heavenly love that has already started up Diotima's ladder. The man who praised Socrates instead
of Love has, without knowing it, given the truest speech about Love of all — by describing a man
who lives it.

### Speech 4 — Aristophanes

"He spoke and cut men in two, like a sorb-apple which is halved for pickling, or as you might

**Q-G · The "other half" and love as pursuit of the whole · 191d / 192e–193a:**
"Each of us when separated, having one side only, like a flat fish, is but the indenture of a man,
and he is always looking for his other half."
…and the definition:
"And the reason is that human nature was originally one and we were a whole, and the desire and

`VERIFIED`.
**PRECISION-NOTE / FRAMING-TRAP (the headline term-issue of this work):** The modern word
**"soulmate"** does NOT appear in Jowett (or in Plato) — it is a much later coinage. Jowett's
images are **"the indenture of a man"** (a tally/tally-stick half — note "we shall be like
tallies" appears a few lines later) and **"his other half."** Quote those; never put "soulmate"
inside quotation marks or attribute it to Plato/Jowett. The "find your other half" idea IS
Aristophanes' — but it is COMIC, and not the dialogue's own doctrine of love (FRAMING-TRAP 1).
Note also Jowett's frank "lovers of women / female attachments / a section of the male follow the
male" passage: Aristophanes' myth explains opposite-sex AND same-sex attraction symmetrically — the
author should not silently heterosexualize it, but should present it in the dialogue's own terms
(the pederastic-Athenian frame) without anachronism.

### Speech 5 — Agathon

**Q-H · Love the youngest and fairest god · 195a / 197c:**
"Many things were said by Phaedrus about Love in which I agree with him; but I cannot agree that he
is older than Iapetus and Kronos:—not so; I maintain him to be the youngest of the gods, and
youthful ever."
…and the encomium's refrain:
"I say of Love that he is the fairest and best in himself, and the cause of what is fairest and
best in all other things."
`VERIFIED`. *(Agathon directly contradicts Phaedrus' "eldest" — the two speeches are designed to
clash, and Socrates will side with neither: Love is no god at all. The tenderness conceit — Love
"walks not upon the earth… but in the hearts and souls… which are of all things the softest" — is
also `VERIFIED`.)*

### Speech 6 — Socrates / Diotima

**Q-I · Love desires what it LACKS (the puncture of Agathon) · 200a–e:**
"Then he and every one who desires, desires that which he has not already, and which is future and
not present, and which he has not, and is not, and of which he is in want;—these are the sort of
things which love and desire seek?"
`VERIFIED`. *(The pivot of the whole dialogue: one desires only what one lacks, so Love, being love
OF the beautiful and good, cannot itself possess them — Agathon's beautiful-god encomium collapses.
"he who desires something is in want of something" is the key prior premise, also `VERIFIED`.)*

**Q-J · Love is a daimon, intermediate between mortal and divine · 202d–e:**
"He is a great spirit (daimon), and like all spirits he is intermediate between the divine and the
mortal… He interprets… between gods and men, conveying and taking across to the gods the prayers
and sacrifices of men, and to men the commands and replies of the gods; he is the mediator who
spans the chasm which divides them, and therefore in him all is bound together… For God mingles not
with man; but through Love all the intercourse and converse of God with man, whether awake or
asleep, is carried on."
`VERIFIED`. *(Jowett writes "(daimon)" parenthetically — so the Greek term IS in the dialogue text
here, uniquely; elsewhere he uses "spirit." The Poros/Penia parentage — "the god Poros or Plenty…
Penia or Poverty… plotted to have a child by him… and conceived Love" — is also `VERIFIED`, as is
"the lover of wisdom is in a mean between the wise and the ignorant. And of this too the cause is…")*

**Q-K · The ladder of love — the ascent and Beauty itself · 210a–211b:**
"For he who would proceed aright in this matter should begin in youth to visit beautiful forms; and
first, if he be guided by his instructor aright, to love one such form only—out of that he should
create fair thoughts; and soon he will of himself perceive that the beauty of one form is akin to
the beauty of another… he will become a lover of all beautiful forms; in the next stage he will
consider that the beauty of the mind is more honourable than the beauty of the outward form… until
he is compelled to contemplate and see the beauty of institutions and laws… and after laws and
institutions he will go on to the sciences… until… he will suddenly perceive a nature of wondrous
beauty… but beauty absolute, separate, simple, and everlasting, which without diminution and
without increase, or any change, is imparted to the ever-growing and perishing beauties of all
other things."
`VERIFIED`.
**PRECISION-NOTE:** This is the SAME passage VERIFIED in `plato-fact-pack.md` §3 (Ch6) — wording
matches character-for-character; no conflict. The rungs in Jowett's own words, lowest to highest:
**one beautiful form/body → all beautiful forms/bodies → beauty of the mind/soul → beauty of
institutions and laws → the sciences → "beauty absolute" (Beauty itself).** The closing image —
"using these as steps only, and from one going on to two, and from two to all fair forms… until…

## 3. FRAMING-TRAPS — the places this work is most often misread

**TRAP 1 — "Plato believed in soulmates / 'you complete me' is Platonic."** This is the single
most common *Symposium* misreading, and it is BACKWARDS. The "we were once whole, split in two,
and love is the search for our missing other half" myth (Q-F, Q-G) is **Aristophanes'** speech —
and Aristophanes is the COMEDY POET in the room, telling a deliberately funny just-so story
(round eight-limbed people rolling like tumblers, Zeus halving them like pickled apples). It is
comic invention *inside* the dialogue, not Plato's doctrine of love. The view the dialogue
ELEVATES is **Diotima's** (Q-K): love is not the search for a missing person but the ascent of
desire from one beautiful body up a ladder to Beauty itself. Diotima even pointedly corrects the
Aristophanes line — "they are seeking neither for the half… but for the good" (text line 2282).
The author must keep these straight: Aristophanes = the charming wrong answer the dialogue stages
and surpasses; Diotima = the answer it endorses. And the word **"soulmate"** is a later coinage,
never in the text (PRECISION-NOTE at Q-G). `FRAMING-TRAP`

**TRAP 2 — "'Platonic love' means chaste, non-sexual affection."** A LATER distortion (the modern
folk-sense, likely via Ficino's Renaissance circle — the exact term-history is unverified and
should not be asserted as fact; flagged in plato-fact-pack §influence). Diotima's ladder does NOT
suppress physical love — it **begins** with it: the FIRST rung is loving "one such form only," a
single beautiful body, and uses that erotic pull as the engine for the whole ascent (Q-K). Eros is
the *driving force*, not the thing renounced. "Platonic love = love with the body removed" inverts
the text, which makes bodily attraction the indispensable first step. State the ladder accurately
and flag the modern phrase as a distortion. `FRAMING-TRAP`

**TRAP 3 — "Diotima was a real historical priestess Plato is quoting."** She appears ONLY in the
*Symposium*; there is no external evidence she existed; scholarly consensus leans toward Platonic
invention (matches plato-fact-pack). Don't assert her reality. Write "the priestess of Mantinea to
whom, Plato has Socrates say, he owed the teaching." Note the curiosity worth surfacing: Plato puts
his deepest account of love in the mouth of a WOMAN — at a men's drinking party — which is itself a
striking authorial choice. `PRECISION-NOTE`

**TRAP 4 — Socrates vs. Plato (and the nesting).** The doctrines of the climactic speech (Forms,
Beauty itself, love as ascent to the eternal) are **Plato's own**, in a MIDDLE dialogue, dramatized
through a Socrates who reports them from Diotima. Do not attribute the ladder to "the historical
Socrates." And mind the frame: every speech reaches us as "Apollodorus says Aristodemus said X
said…" — the author should not present any speech as a verbatim transcript. Write "Plato has
Aristophanes tell," "Socrates reports Diotima saying." `PRECISION-NOTE`

**TRAP 5 — flattening the pederastic frame either way.** The dialogue's social setting is the
Athenian institution of the older male lover (*erastēs*) and younger male beloved (*erōmenos*) —
explicit in Pausanias' and Alcibiades' speeches. Don't anachronistically read it as "gay rights"
nor silently scrub it into heterosexual romance. Present it as the historical frame the speeches
assume, and let Diotima's ascent (which moves *past* any particular body) be the dialogue's own
transcendence of it. `PRECISION-NOTE`

## 4. "Why this is a break" block material (work-level)

**BEFORE — what "love" was on the table.** In Greek culture Eros was a god to be praised and a
disruptive bodily passion to be managed; the symposium's own early speeches give the standard
takes — Love the ancient god (Phaedrus), Love split into noble vs. vulgar (Pausanias), Love as a
cosmic harmony the physician sees everywhere (Eryximachus), Love as the comic ache for a lost
other half (Aristophanes), Love as a beautiful young god draped in fine words (Agathon). Each is a
praise of Eros as an *object* — a god, a force, a feeling.

**THE MOVE — what the *Symposium* does that the encomia before it could not.** Socrates, through
Diotima, refuses the whole genre. Love is **not a god and not beautiful** — because you only
desire what you LACK, and Love is love *of* the beautiful, so Love must be needy, in-between,
a *daimon* (Q-I, Q-J). That single reframing converts love from a thing-to-praise into a
*directional desire* — and once it's a desire pointed at beauty, it can be **trained to climb**:
from one body, to all bodies, to souls, to laws, to knowledge, to **Beauty itself** (Q-K). Eros
becomes the engine of philosophy — the very pull toward the eternal Forms. No prior speech had
turned erotic desire into an epistemology and a metaphysics; the *Symposium* makes wanting-the-
beautiful the same motion as wanting-to-know-the-truth. And then it grounds the abstraction in a
man: Alcibiades' speech (Q-L–N) shows the ascent embodied — Socrates, ugly Silenus outside, golden
within, who out-endures and out-resists everyone because he has already started up the ladder and
left bodily love below.

**The concrete line to state in the block:** the *Symposium* is the first work to argue that erotic
desire and the desire to know the truth are **the same drive** — that love, rightly schooled, is
the soul's ladder from a single beautiful body up to Beauty itself, the eternal Form — and that the
proof of it is a snub-nosed philosopher whom the most beautiful man in Athens could not seduce.

next; the dialogue *is* an ascent.

**(d) CANDIDATE SIGNATURE DIAGRAM — Diotima's ladder as a vertical ascent (recommended):**
A vertical climb, lowest rung at the bottom, top rung at the apex:

```
            ▲  BEAUTY ITSELF  (the Form — "beauty absolute, separate, simple, everlasting")
            │  knowledge / the sciences
            │  beauty of institutions & laws
            │  beauty of souls (the mind over the body)
            │  all beautiful bodies
            │  ONE beautiful body            ← the first rung: physical eros, the engine
            ▼  (start here, in youth)
```
This is the natural signature image — it captures the work's single biggest idea (love as ascent)
AND corrects the "Platonic love = chaste" trap by SHOWING the bottom rung is a physical body. Six
rungs in Jowett's own terms (body → bodies → souls → laws → sciences → Beauty itself). NOTE: label
it "ladder" (the conventional name) but the rung-words are Jowett's "steps" (soft-spot §5.8). A
parallel two-column structure (e.g. Common vs Heavenly love, or Aristophanes' myth vs Diotima's
ladder) is a weaker fit — the work's spine is an ASCENT, so the vertical ladder is the right shape.

## 8. One-paragraph summary of the arc + verification cautions

**The arc.** The *Symposium* (Greek *Symposion*, "drinking party," composed c. 385 BCE, set c. 416
BCE) reaches us at two removes — Apollodorus retelling what an eyewitness told him about the night
Agathon celebrated his first tragic victory, when the guests agreed to skip heavy drinking and each
praise Eros instead. Seven speeches climb in sophistication: **Phaedrus** (Love is the eldest god,
the spur to virtue — Alcestis dying for her husband); **Pausanias** (two Loves, a vulgar Common and
a noble Heavenly); **Eryximachus** the physician (Love as a harmony running through "all that is");
**Aristophanes** (the comic myth — we were once whole, Zeus split us, and love is the search for our
other half); and **Agathon** (Love as the youngest, fairest, most virtuous god — gorgeous, empty
rhetoric). Then **Socrates** refuses the whole genre: through the priestess **Diotima** he argues
Love is no god and not beautiful, because you desire only what you LACK — Love is a needy *daimon*
between mortal and divine, a desire to possess the good and the beautiful forever, and so trainable
into an ascent: the **ladder of love**, from one beautiful body up through all bodies, souls, laws,
and knowledge to **Beauty itself**. Finally **Alcibiades** crashes in drunk and praises Socrates
instead of Love — the Silenus ugly outside and golden within, who out-endured everyone at war and
whom the most beautiful man in Athens could not seduce: the ladder made flesh, bodily love refused
in favor of the higher kind.

**Verification cautions.** Every quotation in §2/§6 was fetched today from Jowett (Gutenberg #1600,
dialogue-text half) and matched word-for-word — all are `VERIFIED`. The cautions are: (1) the
Aristophanes "other half" myth is the COMIC speech, not the dialogue's doctrine — and "soulmate" is
a modern coinage never in the text, so never quote it as Plato (FRAMING-TRAP 1, §5/Q-G); (2)
"Platonic love" as chaste affection INVERTS the ladder, whose first rung is a physical body
(FRAMING-TRAP 2); (3) Diotima is likely a Platonic invention — don't assert her reality (TRAP 3);
(4) the immortality/"birth in beauty" stretch (206b–209e), Pausanias' custom passage, and
Eryximachus' music/astronomy extensions are recorded as PARAPHRASE — fetch before quoting (§5.1–5.3);
(5) Greek terms are PARAPHRASE-ONLY in Jowett EXCEPT "(daimon)," which Jowett prints at 202e (§5.6);
(6) Jowett says "steps," not "ladder," and "heavenly/common," not "Uranian/Pandemos" — use the
conventional labels but quote Jowett's words (§5.8, Q-D); (7) the two dates must stay distinct
