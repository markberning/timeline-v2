## What works (keep)

- **The two-thread framing in the throughline + hook is excellent and load-bearing.** Telling the reader up front (a) that the "missing other half" idea is Aristophanes' joke the dialogue climbs *past*, and (b) that everything reaches us at two removes — these are exactly the two traps a newcomer falls into, named before they can. This is the same caliber of inoculation the *Republic* read does with "the Cave is not about reality being an illusion."
- **The building-sequence is genuinely staged, not listed.** Each chapter intro re-grips the contest ("each speaker trying to outdo the last," "the host speaks last before Socrates," "the most personal... portrait of Socrates anywhere in Plato"). The reader feels the speeches climbing.
- **The Socrates-dismantles-Agathon hinge (Ch 5) is landed cleanly** — "Now the trap closes" walks the lack-argument step by step, and the payoff ("love is the needy thing reaching for them") is earned, not asserted.
- **The Alcibiades coda (Ch 7) is the strongest chapter.** The Silenus image, the failed seduction, and the barefoot-on-ice endurance are each tied back to the ladder ("the ladder made flesh," "came to *buy* wisdom with his body," "the man who couldn't be seduced is the man who has started up Diotima's ladder"). The throughline pays off in a person, exactly as promised.
- **The pederastic frame (Ch 2 final block) is handled plainly and without anachronism** — names it as the historical setting, refuses both scrubbing-into-modern-romance and reading-as-modern-sexuality-statement, and points forward to the ascent moving *past* any particular body. This is the hardest comprehension/voice tightrope in the piece and it's walked well.
- **"Platonic love" correction (Ch 6 final block) is sharp** and matches the thinker read's framing.

## Findings

### 1. POLISH — Ch 6, the ladder does not get the rung-by-rung scaffold the brief demands; it is delivered as two dense paragraphs

**Location:** Ch 6, blocks 5 and 6 (the "ladder of love" / "Form of Beauty" blocks).

**Problem:** This is the climax the whole book builds to, and the brief specifically flags it: "the Diotima ladder... must be scaffolded rung by rung." The *thinker* read (`plato.ts` Ch 6) does exactly this — it labels each rung ("**Rung one: one beautiful body.**", "**Rung two: all beautiful bodies.**", etc.), so the reader physically counts the climb. The WORK read instead folds all five-plus rungs into running prose inside two paragraphs ("the first rung is a single beautiful *body*... Then comes the first insight... Next he rises... Then up again... then to the beauty of 'the sciences'... And at the very top..."). It is *correct and readable*, but the rungs blur together; a zero-knowledge reader cannot easily answer "how many steps, and what was step 3?" after one pass. The set-piece the brief most wants landed is the one delivered flattest relative to the rest of the chapter.

**Concrete rewrite:** Adopt the thinker read's own device for the WORK read's climax — break the ascent into explicitly counted rungs, either as bolded rung labels or as a tighter "first... then... then... then... and at the top" with each rung getting its own beat and visible numbering. Minimally, recast block 5 so each rung is its own short sentence with the rung named: a single body → the beauty common to all bodies → beautiful souls → beautiful laws and institutions → the beauty of knowledge → Beauty itself. The point is the reader should *feel the steps under their feet*, which is the whole metaphor. (This is the single highest-value change in the read.)

### 2. POLISH — `daimon` is well-defined, but the reader is never told plainly what "the Forms / Beauty itself" *is* as a system until it's already in use

**Location:** Ch 6, block 6 — "This is the **Form of Beauty**, in Plato's larger system — the single, perfect, changeless original that every beautiful thing in the world borrows from and only ever copies."

**Problem:** The definition is good but arrives *at the top of the ladder*, the moment the reader most needs to already have it to feel the payoff. The `brk` block defines "theory of Forms" earlier (good), but a reader who reads chapters straight through (not the break block) meets "Beauty itself" first inside Diotima's climb. The phrase "beauty itself" and "Beauty as such" appear in Ch 6 block 6 before the inline definition lands at the end of the same sentence — workable, but tight for a zero-knowledge reader.

**Concrete rewrite:** No structural change needed, but consider a one-clause forward-glossed touch the first time "Beauty itself" is used as the destination (early in block 5: "...and at last catch sight of Beauty itself — not any beautiful thing but the single changeless original behind all of them, which the chapter will reach in a moment"). Low priority; the existing definition does land within the same sentence it's needed. Flagging because it's the one spot a newcomer could momentarily float.

### 3. POLISH — Eros is glossed once in the hook but the gloss is split across two words; confirm it reads as a definition, not an aside

**Location:** Hook block 1 — "each give a speech in praise of **Eros** — love, the Greek god and the Greek word for desire."

**Problem:** Minor, and an em-dash is being used for an inline definition here, which `WRITING-RULES.md` ("Use commas and parentheses, not em-dashes") says to avoid. The rule is a house standard the bar reads mostly honor with parens for definitions. The Eros gloss is the clearest definition-by-em-dash instance.

**Concrete rewrite:** "...each give a speech in praise of **Eros** (love — the Greek god, and the Greek word for desire)." Or recast with a comma/paren. See finding 7 for the corpus-wide em-dash note; this is the one that is specifically a *definition* via em-dash.

---

### 4. POLISH — "symposium" / "symposion" is defined twice in close succession (hook redundancy)

**Location:** Hook block 1 opens with the modern-vs-Greek "symposium" contrast; the hero `cap` and `alt` also gloss "a drinking party (Greek *symposion*)."

**Problem:** Not a reader-facing defect (hero caption and hook are read in different contexts), but within the hook itself the drinking-party scene-set ("the tables were cleared, the wine was mixed with water, a flute-girl played") slightly overlaps the same beat that recurs in Ch 1 block 4 ("they will drink lightly, send the flute-girl away"). The recurrence in Ch 1 is *functional* (it's the plot of the evening), so this is fine — flagging only to confirm it's intentional, not accidental double-explanation. **No change required.**

---

### 5. POLISH — Two speakers (Eryximachus, Agathon) get tighter, more summary treatment than the others; verify this is deliberate pacing, not a dip

**Location:** Ch 3 blocks 1–2 (Eryximachus); Ch 4 (Agathon, 3 short blocks).

**Problem:** The brief asks to "flag flat/listy speech-summaries." Eryximachus and Agathon are the two speeches the dialogue itself treats as lesser (the doctor who finds his own profession is the secret of the universe; the rhetorician who praises pretty words), and the read *says so* and uses the brevity as characterization ("quietly the most comic," "a beautiful speech, and a hollow one"). So the compression is earned and voiced, not a flatness — this is the read making a deliberate choice that the middle speeches are the runway, not the climb. **This works; no change.** Noting it so the reconcile pass doesn't "fix" it by padding — the unevenness is correct.

---

### 6. POLISH — Aristophanes' myth (Ch 3 blocks 3–5) is the second-biggest set-piece and lands well, but the "even-handed about same-sex/opposite-sex attraction" point is delivered in a parenthetical at the very end

**Location:** Ch 3 block 5, final parenthetical — "(His myth is also even-handed in a way later retellings often quietly drop: it explains attraction between the sexes and within them in the same breath...)"

**Problem:** This is a genuinely interesting and clarifying point (the myth covers all attraction, not just the heterosexual version pop culture remembers), buried in a closing parenthetical longer than ~15 words — which `WRITING-RULES.md` says should become its own sentence. It's load-bearing content (it ties back to the Ch 2 pederastic-frame handling), not a true aside.

**Concrete rewrite:** Promote it out of the parenthetical into a plain sentence or two: "His myth is also even-handed in a way later retellings quietly drop. A halved creature might be hunting a half of either sex, so the same story explains attraction between the sexes and within them in one breath. The dialogue presents this plainly, in its own Athenian terms." Removes the over-long parenthetical and gives the point the weight it deserves.

---

### 7. POLISH — Em-dash density: the read uses em-dashes heavily for asides and appositives, against the house rule

**Location:** Corpus-wide in the draft; e.g. Ch 1 block 2 ("got from someone else — a man named Aristodemus... — a Socrates-follower who actually *was* there — and"), Ch 5 block 2 ("must be, of all things, neither beautiful nor good"), throughline, and many more.

**Problem:** `WRITING-RULES.md`: "Use commas and parentheses, not em-dashes." The bar reads (`republic.ts`, `plato.ts`) also use em-dashes fairly freely, so the philosophy corpus appears to have *de facto* relaxed this for dramatic appositive dashes — but the rule is on the books and the voice memory's spirit is "plain, confident narration." The Symposium draft's em-dash use is comparable to the bar, not worse, so this is **consistency-with-house-rule, not a regression**. Two genuinely stacked instances are worth softening for readability (the triple-dash in Ch 1 block 2 makes the reader hold three nested clauses): consider commas/parens there.

**Concrete rewrite (Ch 1 block 2 only, the worst stack):** "Everything he is about to recite he got from someone else. A man named Aristodemus (a-ris-TOD-em-us), Jowett's 'little fellow, who never wore any shoes,' a Socrates-follower who actually *was* there, told it to him — and Apollodorus checked a few details with Socrates afterward." Lower priority elsewhere; match the bar's level if not formally reducing.

### 8. PASS-LEVEL NOTE — Voice is clean of the banned habits from `feedback_philosophy_voice_plain`

Checked against the five banned patterns:
1. **Second-person imperatives / commanding the reader** — none. The closest are "Two things to fix before the party starts" and "One thing to name plainly," which are *narrator* framing, not commands *to the reader* ("Read that again," "feel the pull"). Clean.
2. **Meta-narration / throat-clearing** ("here's the thing," "the part people miss") — none. "Here is the first thing to keep straight" (Ch 1 block 2) is borderline but functions as a genuine signpost for a structural fact (the two-removes frame), not a tease. Acceptable, matches the bar's "Keep it in view."
3. **Self-labeled asides** ("a small honesty note") — none. The "[VERIFY]" tags are pipeline artifacts, stripped at render, not reader-facing.
4. **Condescension / over-explaining the obvious** — none. The two-dates point (composed 385 / set 416) is explained because it's genuinely non-obvious, not BC-arithmetic hand-holding.
5. **Vague gestures** ("you watched it happen this morning") — none.

Voice is at bar level. No BLOCKER on the voice axis.
