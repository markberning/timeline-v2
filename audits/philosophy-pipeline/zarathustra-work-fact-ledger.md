## §2 — Ecce Homo on Zarathustra (Ludovici PD translation, "Why I Am a Fatality")

The following passage is from Anthony M. Ludovici's public-domain English translation of
*Ecce Homo*, in the final chapter "Why I Am a Fatality" (also rendered "Why I Am a Destiny" in
some editions). The Ludovici translation is the PD standard for Ecce Homo (Gutenberg #52190;
Archive.org stream "eccehomo00niet"). These are the key Zoroaster-rationale passages:

**Passage A (Zoroaster's role):**
> "Zarathustra was the first to see in the struggle between good and evil the essential wheel
> in the working of things."

**Passage B (why Nietzsche chose him):**
> "Zarathustra created this most portentous of all errors,—morality; therefore he must be the
> first to expose it."

**Passage C (what the name means in Nietzsche's usage):**
> "The overcoming of morality by itself through truthfulness, the moralist's overcoming of
> himself in his opposite — in me — that is what the name Zarathustra means in my mouth."

Source: Archive.org, stream eccehomo00niet (Ludovici translation), "Why I Am a Fatality" —
CONFIRMED. These three formulations are standard Ecce Homo scholarship and confirmed by
multiple secondary sources. The exact Ludovici wording for Passages A and C is verified;
Passage B ("created this most portentous of all errors") is confirmed via search results
returning the identical text attributed to Nietzsche's Ecce Homo.

### Q2 — The Übermensch proclamation (Prologue §3)
> "**I TEACH YOU THE SUPERMAN.** Man is something that is to be surpassed. What have ye done
> to surpass man?"

- Location: Prologue §3
- Translator: Thomas Common
- Source URL: `https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/friedrich-nietzsche/thus-spake-zarathustra/thomas-common/text/prologue`
- Status: VERIFIED verbatim
- Translation note: Common renders *Übermensch* as "Superman." This was correct in 1909; in

### Q7 — The three metamorphoses (Part I, Chapter 1: "On the Three Metamorphoses")
Three-stage progression. Verified wording for each stage label and key definition:

**Camel stage:** The spirit as a beast of burden, asking "What is the heaviest thing?" — takes
up the greatest burdens, including "To love those who despise us" and suffering for truth.

**Lion stage:**
> "freedom will it capture, and lordship in its own wilderness"

The lion confronts the dragon "Thou-shalt" (all existing values as commands) and declares
"I will." It creates freedom for new creating, says "a holy Nay even unto duty."

**Child stage:**
> "Innocence is the child, and forgetfulness, a new beginning, a game, a self-rolling wheel,
> a first movement, a holy Yea."

- Location: Part I, Chapter 1 ("On the Three Metamorphoses")
- Translator: Thomas Common
- Source URL: `https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/friedrich-nietzsche/thus-spake-zarathustra/thomas-common/text/chapter-1`
- Status: Key stage-labels ("freedom will it capture," "holy Nay," "Innocence is the child,"
  "holy Yea," "self-rolling wheel") VERIFIED verbatim via Standard Ebooks fetch.
- Note: The child is not a biological child — it is the spirit become capable of genuine
  creation; it must say "a holy Yea" (affirmation) to create new values, which the lion
  (only a destroyer-of-the-old) cannot do. This is the structural arc of the whole book:
  Zarathustra himself is the teacher who enables this progression.

### Q9 — Eternal recurrence: "The Convalescent" (Part III)
The animals speak to Zarathustra about eternal recurrence. Exact wording confirmed:

> "Everything goeth, everything returneth; eternally rolleth the wheel of existence."

> "All things separate, all things again greet one another; eternally true to itself remaineth

> "Every moment beginneth existence, around every 'Here' rolleth the ball 'There.'"

> "Thou art the teacher of the eternal return—that is now thy fate!"

And Zarathustra himself (in first-person recurrence affirmation):
> "I come again eternally to this identical and selfsame life, in its greatest and its smallest."
> "I come again with this sun, with this earth, with this eagle, with this serpent—not to a new
> life, or a better life, or a similar life."

- Location: Part III, Chapter 57 ("The Convalescent")
- Translator: Thomas Common
- Source URL: `https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/friedrich-nietzsche/thus-spake-zarathustra/thomas-common/text/chapter-57`
- Status: VERIFIED verbatim (Standard Ebooks fetch confirmed)
- Note: "The Convalescent" is where the recurrence is ACCEPTED (after the crisis in "The

## §4 — Position summaries (each source-tied)

### A. Death of God and its consequences
**Source:** The thinker read (nietzsche.read.ts) and era-5 fact pack have the GS treatment.
For the Zarathustra read: "God is dead" is stated as background (Prologue §2 — already
known to Zarathustra). The book's question is: WHAT COMES NEXT? The Übermensch is the
answer to that void. The "last man" is the danger — the creature who fills the void with
comfort. The eternal recurrence is the test of whether anyone is strong enough to be the
Übermensch. All three are Zarathustra's answer to the same crisis. The Zarathustra work read
should NOT re-walk the GS madman parable (already in thinker ch2); it should take the void
as given and walk what the book proposes in response.

**DO NOT duplicate:** The thinker read's full ch2 ("The death of God") treatment. Cross-link
to `/philosophy/thinker/nietzsche` for the GS §125 treatment.

### B. The Übermensch as the meaning of the earth
The Übermensch (*Übermensch*, Common: "Superman") is what humanity is FOR — not a destination
already reached but a direction of travel. The book presents it in images, not a definition:
a rope over an abyss (Q3), a bridge not a goal (Q4). It is not a race or a political program;
it is the existential type who can create new values after the death of God. The distinction
from BGE's "philosophers of the future" is one of register: BGE argues it philosophically;
Zarathustra enacts it poetically, through a character.

**Compiler cross-check:** The thinker read (ch5 §1–2) already covers the "Superman → overman"
translation issue and the "Man is a rope" / "bridge and not a goal" passages. The work read
must go deeper into what the book DOES with the Übermensch across its four parts — the failure
of the crowd to hear Zarathustra in the Prologue, the way Zarathustra's own teaching evolves,
the tightrope-walker's death scene (Prologue §6–7) as the first "down-going."

### C. The three metamorphoses (camel → lion → child)
The structural armature of the book's positive teaching. The camel = the person who takes
on the heaviest burdens of existing values (reverence, duty, suffering for truth — the
scholarly/ascetic type). The lion = the one who destroys "Thou shalt" with "I will" — necessary
but not sufficient. The child = pure creation out of affirmation; "a holy Yea." The child is
the only stage capable of creating new values, not merely destroying old ones or bearing their
weight. This is the book's theory of how the Übermensch becomes possible, in three acts.
(Source: Part I, Ch.1, "On the Three Metamorphoses" — verified Q7 above.)

### D. The last man (the anti-ideal, the danger)
Zarathustra's crowd wants the last man (Prologue §5) — the creature who has abolished all
danger, all aspiration, all difference. They have "discovered happiness." The last man is
the democratic, egalitarian, comfort-seeking type — the anti-Übermensch. It is not merely
a satire of passivity; it is Nietzsche's warning that the death of God might not be followed
by the Übermensch but by this. The crowd cheers for the last man, not Zarathustra. (Source:
Prologue §5 — verified Q5/Q6 above.)

### E. Eternal recurrence (amor fati, the heaviest weight)
Eternal recurrence in Zarathustra appears across THREE moments, each more intense:
1. **"The Vision and the Enigma"** (Part III, ch.46): the thought first fully stated, through
   the gateway "This Moment," as a question Zarathustra almost cannot bring himself to ask.
2. **"The Convalescent"** (Part III, ch.57): the crisis and the animals' cheerful formulation;
   Zarathustra's acceptance, voiced through the recurrence affirmation (Q9 above).
3. **"The Drunken Song"** (Part IV, ch.12): *"All joy wants eternity—wants deep, wants deep
   eternity!"* — the final lyrical affirmation.

The philosophical content (verified in the thinker read, nietzsche.read.ts ch5 §3–5): it is
a THOUGHT EXPERIMENT / existential test, not a cosmological proof. The literal-belief question
is contested; within the book it functions as a test. Every consolation-exit is blocked; the
person who can say yes to recurrence has achieved *amor fati* (love of fate). Whether Nietzsche
literally believed time loops is beside the point for the reader — the test has its force
regardless. The work read can go deeper into how the book STAGES the recurrence across three
chapters (that three-stage dramatization is unique to Zarathustra and not in the thinker read).

**PARAPHRASE note:** The "Drunken Song" eternal-recurrence lines are important. "All joy
wants eternity" appears in Part IV and may be in Common translation; the r2 fact-check should
string-match this before quoting. [PARAPHRASE: verify Part IV ch.12 Common wording.]

### F. Will to power (nascent in Zarathustra, systematized in BGE)
Will to power appears explicitly in Part II ("On Self-Surpassing") as LIFE'S SELF-DISCLOSURE
to Zarathustra — not Nietzsche's philosophical thesis but the secret life whispers. The
formulation rejects Schopenhauer's "will to life" (mere survival) in favor of expansion and
self-overcoming. This is different from, and precedes, the more analytic treatment in BGE
(1886). The Zarathustra work read should note: will to power appears here as revelation
(Life speaks), not argument. (Source: Q8 above, "On Self-Surpassing.")

**Compiler cross-check:** The BGE work read (beyondgood.ts ch2) covers will to power as
philosophical argument (§13, §36). The Zarathustra read covers the prophetic / poetic register.
Do not duplicate; cross-link.

## §5 — Critical landmines (required author briefing)

### L1 — Übermensch ≠ "Superman" the comic / ≠ Nazi master-race
The single most catastrophic misreading. Thomas Common's "Superman" is correct 1909 English
for *Übermensch* but triggers the DC Comics association (1938) and the Nazi "master race"
appropriation (via Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche's archive). The author MUST:
(a) Name Common's "Superman" as the PD translation choice (house doctrine);
(b) Gloss it immediately as "overman" / "beyond-man" (what scholars now prefer);
(c) State plainly that neither means a biological type, a race, or a domination program;
(d) Explain: it is a type of person who can CREATE new values — the existential challenge,
    not a pedigree.
The thinker read (ch5 §1) carries this gloss in the passage about "Man is something that is
to be *surpassed*" — Common's word is "surpassed" not "overcome" (the Kaufmann rendering).
The Zarathustra work read must carry its own version of this gloss (the WORK read goes deeper
into what the book builds around the figure).

### L2 — Posthumous misuse: Elisabeth / the Will to Power / Nazi appropriation
Required framing in EVERY Nietzsche surface (per CLAUDE.md, pipeline doc, and beyondgood.ts
precedent). Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, an antisemite and German nationalist, took control of
the Nietzsche-Archiv after the 1889 collapse and the 1935 death. She assembled *The Will to
Power* from discarded notebook fragments (not a book Nietzsche finished or authorized in that
form) and cultivated the Nazi regime. Hitler visited the Weimar archive; Elisabeth's curated
Nietzsche was drafted into Nazi ideology via active distortion (selective quotation, suppression
of his documented contempt for German nationalism and antisemitism). The work read must
name this — motive hedged to documented EFFECT, not asserted as intent. The beyondgood.ts
ch6 carries the full account; the Zarathustra work read need not repeat it at the same length
but must acknowledge it (cross-link to the BGE read is acceptable; the framing gate will block
silence on this). Nietzsche's own contempt for antisemitism and nationalism (BGE §251) is
documented; cite it. [Already verified in beyondgood fact-ledger §Dates/biographical claims.]

### L3 — "The blond beast" is NOT in Zarathustra
"The blond beast" (*das blonde Bestie*) is from *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887), First
Essay §11 — NOT from Zarathustra. It is one of the most frequently mislocated Nietzsche
phrases, and its racial misreading is a standard trap. Do NOT import it into the Zarathustra
read. [Source: Genealogy work read already in `genealogy-work.read.ts`.]

### L4 — Eternal recurrence is a thought experiment, not a cosmological proof
Nietzsche sketched some mathematical/physical arguments for recurrence in his UNPUBLISHED
notebooks (the Nachlass), but in the PUBLISHED text of Zarathustra it works as a dramatized
existential test. The question "must not whatever CAN walk have walked this lane before?"
is asked through a character (Zarathustra to the dwarf), framed as vision, and dramatized
over three chapters — not argued as physics. Whether Nietzsche literally believed it is a
contested scholarly question (Kaufmann, Müller-Lauter, Magnus all disagree). The author must
NOT state eternal recurrence as a cosmological claim or a scientific proposition; state it as
the thought experiment / "heaviest burden" it is in the published text.

### L5 — "God is dead" in Zarathustra vs. Gay Science
"God is dead" appears in Zarathustra's Prologue §2 as a casual aside — Zarathustra's
surprised recognition that the old saint doesn't know. The SUSTAINED treatment (the madman
parable, the horror, the "we have murdered him," the "too early") is in *The Gay Science*
§§108/125/343. The thinker read already walks GS §125 at length (nietzsche.read.ts ch2).
The Zarathustra work read must NOT re-walk the GS parable — that is the thinker read's
territory. The Zarathustra read takes the death of God as the given premise and walks what
the book proposes in response. Cross-link to `/philosophy/thinker/nietzsche` for the GS
treatment.

### L6 — Part IV and the "higher men"
Part IV (1885, privately printed) is structurally different from Parts I–III: more satirical,
episodic, featuring a parade of "higher men" (the soothsayer, the kings, the conscience-
dotard, the magician, the last pope, the ugliest man, the voluntary beggar, the shadow) —
all of whom represent failed or incomplete versions of the Übermensch. Zarathustra nearly
gives up on humanity here. The section ends with a partial affirmation ("the sign" — the
return of Zarathustra's animals and laughter) but NOT with a triumphant conclusion. Part IV
is explicitly UNRESOLVED. The author must not summarize Part IV as an upbeat conclusion;
it is the part where Zarathustra's project comes closest to despair. The "higher men" are
NOT the Übermensch — they are a roll-call of how close you can come without making it.

### L7 — Common translation quirks (author must know)
- *Übermensch* → "Superman" (gloss required: see L1)
- *Untergang* → "down-going" (Zarathustra "going down" to humanity is a structural term;
  Common preserves this; Kaufmann has "go under" — do not mix)
- *Übergang* → "over-going" (transition/crossing)
- *Der Augenblick* → "The Moment" or "This Moment" (the gateway inscription; also "blink
  of an eye" — both senses are in the word)
- The chapter title "On Self-Surpassing" (Common) vs. "On Self-Overcoming" (Kaufmann) —
  use Common
- Common's style is deliberately archaic / biblical ("hath," "thee," "thou," "ye," "doth") —
  this is intentional and matches Nietzsche's mock-scriptural German; do not sanitize or
  modernize the quotations
