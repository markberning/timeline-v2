### Hook + Throughline

**Verdict: STRONG**

The hook earns its length. The stove-room image is vivid without being ornate; the
sceptical project is stated plainly ("doubt everything") before the complications
arrive. The legend-vs.-document distinction in paragraph 2 is model framing — it
names the trap and steps around it. Paragraph 3 gives the reader the "why this
matters now" (the old certainties cracking under Copernicus and Galileo) without a
lecture. Paragraph 4 plants the *through-line* — four words, the most famous in
philosophy; he never finished the building — and the chapter promises to pay off the
gap between those two facts. That is exactly the right promise for a thinker read.

The throughline text is good: "the stone may not be big enough to hold the house" is
the right tension. No MUST-FIX here.

### Break block (`brk`)

**Verdict: STRONG**

All three requirements of a break block hit:

- **Before (steelmanned):** The Scholastic inheritance is presented without condescension —
  "a coherent and defensible picture" — and the Jesuit education is named concretely
  (Aquinas, the faith-and-reason chapter). The pinhead-debate potted-summary is
  pre-empted in the first sentence: "the textbook caricature of 'the Scholastics' as
  dusty fools counting angels on pinheads is exactly backwards." Good.
- **The concrete new move:** "Method before content" is crisply defined; universal
  methodic doubt explained with its on/off distinction (not skepticism — tool); and
  the modern move — "the subject becomes the starting point" — is stated as a positive
  claim with forward-pointing consequences.
- **The catch (foreshadowing):** The final sentence names the loop (once you shut
  yourself inside your own mind, you need to get back out) and names the circle to
  come. This is structural setup, not a spoiler.

The Socrates demolition-tool comparison is excellent; the Hegel "father of modern
philosophy" flagged as convention is exactly right.

No MUST-FIX. One SHOULD-FIX: the plain-language restatement of **what methodic
doubt is** (the off-switch part, not just the on-switch) is clear in the body but
slightly buried by qualifications. The "method has an on-switch and an off-switch"
formulation could be front-loaded.

### Chapter 1 — "The man who doubted everything"

**Verdict: GOOD**

This is the biographical chapter, and the pipeline doc flags biography-with-ideas-as-captions
as a failure mode. Ch 1 is almost entirely biography — which is correct at this position
in the structure (the ideas have their own three chapters coming), but it has to earn
its keep by **motivating** the ideas, not just narrating the life.

**What works:**
- The La Flèche dissatisfaction is earned: "he came out of the best education on the
  continent convinced that none of it rested on anything firm." That is the idea in
  biography's clothing.
- The stove-room vs. three-dreams distinction is handled exactly right — two sources,
  two different distances, one named. The Baillet 1691 framing is precise. This is the
  kind of thing that looks easy and costs gated prose when skipped.
- The Galileo/Le Monde suppression paragraph is the strongest in the chapter: "the man
  who told everyone to doubt all authority knew exactly which authority could not safely
  be doubted out loud." Earned wit, substantive point.
- Death by 5 a.m. lessons is well-set up from the opening, pays off neatly.

**What needs work (MUST vs SHOULD):**

SHOULD-FIX: The Isaac Beeckman meet is thin. The draft says he "woke something up in
him" — which is fine — but the fact pack (§1) gives more: Beeckman redirected him
specifically toward *mathematical natural philosophy*. One more clause here would
explain WHY analytic geometry becomes the template for certainty — the connection
from Beeckman to the *Géométrie* to the dream-of-geometry that drives the *Meditations*
is the biographic spine of this chapter, and it's slightly underbuilt.

SHOULD-FIX: The draft says Descartes served in "at least two armies." The fact pack
says "at least three" (Maurice of Nassau, Maximilian of Bavaria, and possibly Bucquoy).
This is not a fact-checker item (close enough, and "possibly" on the third), but "two"
slightly understates the pack's own documentation.

No REWRITE needed. The chapter passes gate 2 for biography chapters: it motivates the
ideas, it frames the legends correctly, the warts (Le Monde suppressed, daughter, the
actual arithmetic of his death) are present. What it does not do — and is not supposed
to do in chapter 1 — is make the reader understand the philosophy. That is chapters
2–5's job, and the setup is solid.

### Chapter 2 — "The demolition"

**Verdict: STRONG**

This is the make-or-break chapter for a Descartes read, and the draft gets it right on
the things that matter. The three-level demolition (senses → dreams → evil genius) is
run **move by move**, with the reader invited explicitly to feel each one. The key
structural choice — "to feel why the cogito lands like a thunderclap in the next chapter,
you have to first feel the ground actually give way under you" — is correct and earned.

**Worked example:** The chapter runs the demolition as a first-person procedure, not
a lecture about what Descartes did. "Sit for a moment in the wreckage Descartes has
deliberately made" at the close is the right move — it holds the reader in the bottom
before the turn.

**Strongest version:** The dream argument is given its full bite: "I have dreamt, with
total conviction, that I was sitting by a fire when in fact I was lying undressed in
bed. And there is no certain mark, no test I can run right now, that proves I am awake."
That is the steelman, not a weakened version.

**Plain restatement:** The evil genius paragraph has the plain version implicit in the
structure but does not give a one-sentence summary the way chapter 3's "plain-language
version" does explicitly. This is a SHOULD-FIX, not a must: the chapter ends strongly
("clearing the site") but a brief "in short: three moves, and the floor is completely
gone" would help the reader know they've received the full argument.

**Two things gotten right about the demon:** The draft does the right thing by naming
both corrections explicitly — "Descartes does not believe in the evil genius" and "the
demon is an old idea pushed to its limit." The Ockham ancestry of the demon is
surprising and true.

No MUST-FIX.

### Chapter 3 — "The one thing left" (cogito + wax)

**Verdict: STRONG**

This is the most technically demanding chapter and is handled with precision throughout.

**Worked example:** The cogito argument is run one move at a time: the demon trying to
deceive requires a me to deceive; doubt is thinking; thinking proves a thinker. Not
dressed up, not summarized — walked. Textbook pass.

**The slogan-locus distinction:** The three-formulation paragraph is the centerpiece of
the chapter and of the entire read. It is worth calling out because it is both correct
and unusual for popular philosophy writing. The *Discourse* (French, narrative "I
observed"), the *Meditations* (Latin, "ego sum ego existo," no "therefore"), and the
*Principles* (Latin axiom, "ego cogito ergo sum") are each accurately identified with
their publication dates, languages, and register. The Veitch editorial gloss (the
uppercase COGITO ERGO SUM as his own addition, not Descartes' 1637 text) is named. This
is the gate's "load-bearing hallucination trap" and the draft catches it cleanly.

**What the cogito proves vs. doesn't:** This section ("the harder and more interesting
question") is excellent — "the certainty is real but it is thin" is exactly the right
formulation, and the list of what it does NOT prove (body, world, persistence over time)
is specific and complete. The reader finishes this section knowing the difference
between "I have a floor" and "I have a building."

**Wax argument:** The wax is introduced well (picked up from the break block's "quiet
hinge" setup) and its two jobs are clearly separated: (1) knowledge of material things
is intellectual not sensory; (2) first hint of dualism. Good structural sequencing.

SHOULD-FIX: The plain-language restatement is explicitly labeled in paragraph 2 ("The
plain-language version") — this is correct and good. But after the three-formulation
paragraph (the most technical paragraph in the chapter), there is no brief
"so to summarize what you just read." A reader who got slightly lost in the Discourse/
Meditations/Principles breakdown would benefit from a one-sentence recap of why it
matters before the "what does it prove?" turn.

No MUST-FIX.

### Chapter 4 — "Mind and body"

**Verdict: STRONG**

**Strongest version before objections:** The chapter opens with the precise *res
cogitans / res extensa* distinction, then does something the pipeline requires and most
retellings skip: it asks the reader to **feel how strange substance dualism is** before
anyone objects to it. "Right now you are reading these words — an event in your mind,
made of no matter, located nowhere you could point to — while your eyes track across
the page, an event in your body." That is a worked example of the dualist thesis, and
it lands before any objection is raised.

**Steelmanning:** The potted-summary demolition ("Descartes invented mind-body dualism")
is handled well — the Plato, Augustine, Scholastic inheritances are all named before the
"what IS new" turn. The two precise new moves (two complete substances; body as machine)
are stated separately and clearly. The crisp re-frame at the close — "he didn't invent
the idea that mind and body are different things; he invented the problem of how they
connect" — is the pack's own formulation and it is excellent.

**Pineal gland:** The chapter gives the pineal gland the steelman it deserves (logic of
indivisible mind → unpaired structure), before noting the absurdity only appears post hoc.
This is exactly right.

**Animal automata:** Present and framed correctly — "the cost of the system," not a gotcha.

SHOULD-FIX: The transition from "two complete substances" to "body as machine" is clean,
but the full consequence — that Descartes is both inheriting and BREAKING from Aristotle
in a specific technical way (hylomorphism: form + matter as aspects of one substance) —
is stated in the fact pack (§5d) but not in the draft. "In Aristotle and the Scholastics,
soul and body are form and matter — two *aspects* of one substance" appears in the
chapter, but the term **hylomorphism** (the Aristotelian doctrine) is not used. This is
a clarity question (see gate 4 below) as much as a comprehensiveness one.

No MUST-FIX for gate 2.

### Chapter 5 — "God, the circle, and the princess"

**Verdict: GOOD (one MUST-FIX)**

**What works:**
- The two God proofs are walked correctly: trademark argument (causal proof, Meditation
  III) and ontological argument (Meditation V) are each given their structure, their
  Anselm ancestry is named, and their role in the system is stated: "God is the bridge
  from the lonely cogito back out to a knowable world."
- The Cartesian circle is explained clearly: clear-and-distinct ideas require God to
  guarantee them, but God is proved by clear-and-distinct ideas. The circular structure
  is visible on first read.
- Elisabeth's interaction objection is given the full weight the pipeline requires. The
  chapter names her *pesanteur* catch (his own physics refutes his analogy) and delivers
  the verdict: "He never gave one — not in the correspondence, not anywhere — and the
  honest verdict... is that there is no good answer inside his system." No softening.
- The final paragraph on Elisabeth correctly refuses to footnote her: "She is not a
  sidebar to Descartes." The dedication of the *Passions* is named.

**MUST-FIX:**
The chapter does not give a **plain-language restatement** of either God proof. The
trademark argument is explained step by step, which is good, but the reader should be
able to say it back in one sentence: "I have an idea of infinite perfection that I —
a finite, flawed thing — couldn't have invented; so something actually infinite and
perfect must have put it there." The ontological argument needs the same. Currently both
proofs are explained in the procedural mode but the "here is the dinner-table version"
is missing. Gate 2 requires the plain restatement for every major idea.

SHOULD-FIX: The interaction problem is the chapter's second major idea, but it does not
have a worked example the way chapters 2–3 do. The draft gives Elisabeth's argument in
paraphrase (correctly, since the letters are not PD), but a brief concrete scene —
"you decide to lift your hand; the decision is a thought, unextended; but your hand is
a piece of matter. According to Descartes' own physics, matter only moves when something
pushes it — by contact, by force. How does a non-spatial, non-extended thought push
anything?" — would make the problem *feel* like a problem rather than an abstract
objection. Paraphrase is fine; what is missing is concreteness.

SHOULD-FIX (small): The chapter opens with "Descartes is now in a corner of his own
making." This is strong. But the word **"clear and distinct ideas"** as a term of art
is introduced here without a gloss — the reader has not been told what "clear" and
"distinct" mean precisely (present to an attentive mind; precisely bounded to what is
clear). It is a technical term used as if it were plain English.

### Chapter 6 — "The afterlife of a wound"

**Verdict: GOOD**

The chapter does its structural job: each of the three rationalist responses to the
interaction problem is assigned its own paragraph with a named solution. Spinoza
(dissolution), Leibniz (pre-established harmony), Malebranche (occasionalism) are all
identified correctly and in enough detail that the reader understands *how* each is
a response to the problem, not just that it is.

The empiricist reaction — Locke on innate ideas, Hume on the substantial self — is
handled with good compression. "The era that opened with Descartes certain a single
mind could doubt its way to bedrock closed with Hume showing, politely and
devastatingly, that the same mind, followed honestly, cannot prove the sun will rise
tomorrow" is excellent.

**MUST-FIX:**
Malebranche's name is introduced here **with no "who he was"** gloss. He is the first
new thinker introduced in the afterlife chapter, and the reader has not met him. A
one-liner is needed: something like "(Nicolas Malebranche, a French Oratorian priest
and philosopher, 1638–1715)." This is also a gate-4 clarity issue — see below.

SHOULD-FIX: The final paragraph ("measure the man the way he would have wanted to be
measured") is strong but very long — five compound sentences of warts-then-legacy.
It could be tightened: the honest-verdict list is doing double duty as both a recap
and a closing statement, and the resulting paragraph reads slightly winded before the
last sentence, which is genuinely good.

SHOULD-FIX: The Kant forward-pointer at the very end ("era 4 — Kant, trying to referee
the fight Descartes started") is correct and useful, but it is attached to the
penultimate thought in parentheses, where it needs to feel like an end note. Consider
moving it to a clean final sentence that transitions out of Descartes explicitly:
"The next chapter, Kant's, is the referee of the fight Descartes started." Brief.

### B. Influence chain — silent missing links check

The pipeline requires no silent missing links. Check each direction:

**THIN — MUST-ADD: The Augustine parallel is entirely absent from the draft.**
The fact pack (§5b, §8a) documents a structural parallel between Augustine's *si fallor
sum* ("if I am deceived, I am" — *De Civitate Dei* XI.26) and the cogito. The pack
specifically says: "ship as a structural parallel that scholars note, not as documented
influence." The era-2 article ("Faith meets reason") covered Augustine at length; many
readers of this thinker read will have come from there. Without at least one sentence
acknowledging the parallel — "scholars have noticed that Augustine's *si fallor sum*
anticipates the cogito in form; Descartes may not have read Augustine directly for this,
but the intellectual inheritance runs through the medieval schools they both inhabited"
— the influence chain has a silent gap. The reader of the era-2 article will notice the
omission. MUST-ADD (one sentence in the break block or at Ch 3's opening).

**THIN — SHOULD-CONSIDER: Avicenna's Flying Man.**
The fact pack (§8a) documents that Avicenna's "Flying Man" thought experiment
(a person suspended in air with no sensory input; they still know they exist) is a
structural anticipation of the cogito, sourced to the era-2 fact pack. At thinker
altitude, where the influence chain is explicit, a brief mention of this parallel would
complete the inheritance picture: Descartes was not thinking in a vacuum. The era-2
article covered Avicenna; the thinker read should close the loop. One clause in Ch 3
or the break block ("Avicenna had posed a structurally similar question...") would
suffice. SHOULD-CONSIDER.

**COVERED: Scholastic-Aristotelian inheritance** (break block, Ch 4). Good.

**COVERED: Galilean mechanics / Le Monde** (Ch 1). Good.

**THIN — SHOULD-CONSIDER: The *Objections and Replies* as a format.**
The fact pack (§2, Meditations entry) calls this "a remarkable thing in itself:
Descartes published the strongest attacks on his own book bound together with the book."
The draft (Ch 5) names Mersenne and Arnauld and the Objections and Replies, but does not
call out how unusual the format was — which is a storytelling beat as much as a
comprehensiveness one (the Plato comparison in the fact pack: *Parmenides*, Aquinas's
"best arguments against God before answering them"). SHOULD-CONSIDER.
