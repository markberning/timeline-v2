## THE SOCRATES FIDELITY RULE (this thinker's #1 issue — the Socratic Problem)

**Socrates wrote nothing.** Every word attributed to him comes from others. There are three
incompatible Socrateses in the ancient record, and the tension between them is not a footnote —
it is the defining intellectual fact about this figure.

### The three source traditions

**1. Aristophanes' Socrates** — *The Clouds* (first produced at the Lenaia or City Dionysia,
423 BC; the version that survives is a *revision* prepared c. 420–417 BC for a second
production that was never staged — see note below). Aristophanes' Socrates runs a "Thinkery"
(*phrontistērion*), teaches young men rhetorical tricks, charges fees, studies natural philosophy
(the heavens and the earth), and treats the gods with contempt. He is a sophist-buffoon: hair
uncombed, barefoot, eyes rolling. This portrait is closer to Anaxagoras or the sophists than to
any version Plato or Xenophon give. Note the revision: the surviving text is the unperformed
second draft; the original 423 BC script is lost. What matters for the pipeline: Plato's Socrates
explicitly addresses this portrait at his trial (*Apology* 18a–19c) — the oldest charges against
him are those of Aristophanes, and he says they are false. The Clouds is dated firmly (won third
prize at the 423 BC City Dionysia), so it is a genuinely *contemporary* source — produced
when Socrates was in his forties — which makes it important even as caricature.

**2. Xenophon's Socrates** — four works: *Memorabilia*, *Symposium*, *Apology*, *Oeconomicus*
(all written after Socrates' death, c. 390s–370s BC). Xenophon (c. 430–354 BC) knew Socrates,
but scholars note he was absent from Athens for much of the relevant period. His Socrates is a
practical, down-to-earth moral advisor — conventional in religious observance, interested in
household management, physical fitness, military virtue. Not the philosophical explorer of
Plato. The distinction matters: Plato's Socrates makes claims about Forms and the immortal
soul; Xenophon's Socrates sensibly advises young men on how to live. They are not the same
person. **Xenophon is the source for the characterization of Xanthippe in the Symposium
(Xenophon's, not Plato's) — see §5.**

**3. Plato's Socrates** — the most philosophically rich and most influential portrait. Plato
(c. 429–347 BC) knew Socrates; in the *Phaedo* he even writes himself out of the death scene
("Plato was ill"). Scholars divide Plato's dialogues into three temporal tiers:
- *Early* (aporetic) dialogues — *Apology*, *Euthyphro*, *Crito*, *Charmides*, *Laches*,
  *Ion*, *Hippias Minor* et al.: pure cross-examination, no positive doctrine, widely thought
  closest to the historical Socrates.
- *Middle* dialogues — *Meno*, *Phaedo*, *Symposium*, *Republic*, *Phaedrus*: the positive
  machinery (Forms, immortal soul, recollection, philosopher-king) appears; scholarly consensus
  treats this "Socrates" as Plato's own mouthpiece.
- *Late* dialogues — *Parmenides*, *Theaetetus*, *Sophist*, *Philebus*: the Forms come under
  attack; the voice of "Socrates" shifts or recedes.

**The pipeline rule:** When this pack says "Socrates argued X," it means *Plato's early-dialogue
Socrates argued X* — and the author MUST maintain that seam. For middle-dialogue material (Forms,
immortality, recollection), the author must write "Plato, through the voice of Socrates" or
"the Plato of the *Republic*" — not a flat "Socrates said." The Plato thinker read handles the
middle-dialogue material fully; this Socrates read focuses on what the early dialogues and the
Apology give us that is closest to the historical figure.

**Scholarly consensus on the Socratic problem (2026 state):** The problem is recognized as
"seemingly impossible to clarify" (IEP, SEP). Most scholars work with the early-dialogue
Plato as the best evidence for Socrates' own views, treat Xenophon as a corrective check,
and treat Aristophanes as contemporary satirical caricature (important for reconstructing
how Socrates appeared to Athenian popular opinion — which is exactly what the *Apology*
addresses). No scholar claims to have fully solved the problem.

## 2. The trial (399 BC) — documented, key surfaces

**The charges (formal):**
1. Asebeia (impiety): "failing to acknowledge the gods of the city" and "introducing new deities." The second charge refers to Socrates' *daimonion* — his personal divine sign (see §4).
2. Corrupting the youth.

**Accusers:** Meletus (primary accuser, the one who brought the formal charge), Anytus (powerful politician, democratic leader), Lycon. Source: Plato, *Apology*; confirmed Wikipedia, Trial of Socrates article.

**Jury size:** ~501 Athenian citizens (a standard Athenian jury; sources vary slightly on exact count).

**Vote count on guilt/innocence:**
- Guilty: 280
- Not guilty: 221
Source: Wikipedia, Trial of Socrates (draws from Apology and ancient commentators). Note: Plato's *Apology* (36a) does not give the exact guilty/innocent split, but does say "had thirty votes gone over to the other side, I should have been acquitted" — which implies the split was approximately 280–221 from a 501-person jury. `VERIFIED` (Apology 36a); the exact numbers are `LATE-TRADITION` from ancient commentary.

**Penalty phase — Socrates' counter-proposal:**
Socrates initially proposes *free meals in the Prytaneum* (the state dining hall reserved for honored benefactors and Olympic victors), saying this is the reward he deserves. Source: Plato, *Apology* 36d–37a — Jowett translation (classics.mit.edu): Socrates says he deserves "maintenance in the Prytaneum, O men of Athens, a reward which he deserves far more than the citizen who has won the prize at Olympia." He later proposes a fine of 100 drachmae, raised (at friends' urging) to 30 minae. `VERIFIED` (Jowett, *Apology* 36d–37a).

**Vote on death penalty:** After Socrates' provocative counter-proposal, the jury voted MORE heavily for death than for guilt — the death vote was larger than the guilty vote. This is noted by commentators; the *Apology* implies it. The exact death-penalty vote numbers are not given in Plato's text.

**The thirty-day delay:** A sacred ship had sailed to Delos before the trial; Athenian law forbade executions while it was away. Socrates spent approximately 30 days in prison. This is why the *Crito* (set during this period) and the *Phaedo* (the day of execution) are set after the trial. Source: Plato, *Phaedo* 58a–b.

### Political context — load-bearing for the trial

The trial happened against the background of:
1. **The Peloponnesian War's catastrophic end** (Athens surrendered to Sparta in 404 BC, losing its empire and democracy temporarily).
2. **The Thirty Tyrants** (404–403 BC): a brutal 13-month oligarchic regime backed by Sparta. Their leader **Critias** was a former associate of Socrates — Socrates had known him. Critias and Charicles, two Thirty leaders, had even tried (unsuccessfully) to order Socrates to stop speaking to men under thirty. Critias died fighting the democratic restoration.
3. **Alcibiades**: another famous Socrates associate. He had commanded (and catastrophically wrecked) the Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BC), defected to Sparta, then switched sides again. He was dead by 404 BC.
4. **The Amnesty of 403 BC**: when democracy was restored, a general amnesty was proclaimed against prosecuting people for acts during the civil war. The trial of Socrates in 399 BC formally avoided citing Critias and Alcibiades (it could not — the amnesty forbade it), but many Athenians connected Socrates to the men who had wrecked the city. This is the **real subtext** of the "corruption of youth" charge. Source: SEP "Socrates," Wikipedia "Trial of Socrates."

**Author note:** The anachronism trap is acute here. Do NOT read the trial as Athens versus free speech. Read it as a democracy that had just barely survived oligarchic terror, trying a man associated (however unfairly) with two of its worst traitors. The formal charges are impiety and corruption; the unofficial background is political and personal. Plato's *Apology* presents Socrates' own account, which emphasizes the philosophical charges and the Delphic oracle mission — but the author must keep both layers visible.

  (`VERIFIED` — classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html and confirmed at lexundria.com/plat_apol/38/j)

### Q2 — The Delphic oracle / Socratic ignorance — REAL passage vs APOCRYPHAL version

**THE APOCRYPHAL QUOTE: "I know that I know nothing."**
This bare formulation IS NOT in Plato's text. It is a paraphrase that hardened into a quotation over centuries of secondary transmission. Wikipedia's article "I know that I know nothing" (2026) states plainly: "the paraphrased saying, though widely attributed to Plato's Socrates in both ancient and modern times, actually occurs nowhere in Plato's works in precisely the form 'I know I know nothing.'" `APOCRYPHAL` as a standalone quotation. The author MUST NOT place this in quotation marks attributed to Socrates. If it appears in text, it must be flagged: "the famous (and paraphrased) 'I know that I know nothing' is a later simplification of what Plato's text actually says."

**WHAT PLATO'S APOLOGY ACTUALLY SAYS — the Delphic oracle narrative (21a–23b):**
The account runs as follows. Socrates' friend Chaerephon went to the oracle at Delphi and asked whether anyone was wiser than Socrates; the oracle said no. Socrates, puzzled (he believed he had no wisdom), went to interview men reputed to be wise — politicians, poets, craftsmen — and found in each case that they believed they knew things they did not in fact know. He concluded:

**Key passage at 21d (Jowett):**
"I am better off than he is — for he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows. I neither know nor think that I know."
(`VERIFIED` — classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html, Jowett translation)

**Key passage at 21e/22a (Jowett — the summary statement):**
"I know that I have no wisdom, small or great."
(`VERIFIED` — classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html, Jowett translation)

**Key passage at 23a–b (Jowett — the conclusion):**
Socrates concludes that "real wisdom is the property of God, and this oracle is his way of telling us that the wisdom of men is worth little or nothing... He is the wisest, who, like Socrates, knows that his wisdom is in truth worth nothing."
(`VERIFIED` — classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html)

**Scholarly nuance the author must preserve:** Socratic ignorance is NOT the same as knowing nothing. The *Apology* also shows Socrates claiming to know things (29b–c: he knows that acting unjustly and disobeying his divine superior is base and evil). The IEP notes: "Socrates does not claim here that he knows nothing. He claims that he is aware of his ignorance and that whatever it is that he does know is worthless." The position is: he does not have *human wisdom* (the kind that gives confident answers to big questions about virtue), while others wrongly think they do. This distinction is the whole point — strip it from the aphorism and you get the caricature.

- **SourceUrl:** https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html

### Q4 — The daimonion (divine sign)
- **Source:** Plato, *Apology* 31c–d
- **Jowett (exact):** "This sign I have had ever since I was a child. The sign is a voice which comes to me and always forbids me to do something which I am going to do, but never commands me to do anything."
  (`VERIFIED` — classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html)
- **Context and significance:** The *daimonion* (divine something, inner sign) is the basis for one prong of the impiety charge — "introducing new deities." Socrates is explicit that it *only forbids, never commands*. It forbade him from entering politics (he says this saved his life — 31d–e). The sign is NOT a voice of God giving positive instructions; it is more like a veto, an inner warning signal. Scholars debate whether this is supernatural, psychological, or literary. The author should present it as Socrates himself presents it: a personal divine sign he takes seriously, not a general theological claim.
- **Note:** The *daimonion* also appears at *Apology* 40a–c, where Socrates notes that the sign did NOT warn him against anything on the day of the trial — which he reads as evidence that death may be a good thing.
- **SourceUrl:** https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html

## 4. The ideas — documented positions, with sources

Each idea below is tied to a named dialogue and Stephanus section. Where the idea appears in the
early dialogues (and is thus closer to the historical Socrates), this is noted. Where it appears
only in middle or late dialogues, it is flagged as *Plato's Socrates* per the fidelity rule.

### 4A. The elenchus (Socratic method)
**Source:** Plato, early dialogues throughout — *Euthyphro*, *Charmides*, *Laches*, *Meno* (early
portion), *Republic* Book I; also described at *Apology* 23b.

**What it is (documented):** Socrates asks his interlocutor for a definition of a virtue term
("What is piety?" *Euthyphro*; "What is courage?" *Laches*; "What is justice?" *Republic* I).
The interlocutor offers a definition. Socrates draws out further commitments through questioning.
He then shows that these commitments are logically inconsistent with the original definition. The
interlocutor must abandon or revise the definition. The process leaves both parties in *aporia*
(puzzlement). The method is called *elenchos* (Greek: cross-examination, refutation).

**The "only strips, never builds" reading:** This is the standard scholarly reading of the early
dialogues (IEP; SEP; the *Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on the Socratic elenchus).
The early-dialogue Socrates produces no positive doctrine; he demolishes bad answers. The IEP
presents two competing interpretations: (a) **non-constructivist** — the elenchus shows only
logical inconsistency between the interlocutor's premises, it cannot tell us which premise to
drop, so it generates no positive knowledge; (b) **constructivist** (Vlastos's view) — Socrates'
own consistent moral beliefs survive cross-examination, so the method tacitly builds a positive
ethics. The pipeline reads: the **non-constructivist reading** is how the early dialogues present
it. The *Apology* Socrates claims no knowledge of virtue — he is only wiser because he knows
he does not know. Plato's middle dialogues introduce the positive machinery (Forms, recollection),
but that is *Plato's* development, not what the early Socrates claims. The author should present
the method as "strips confident answers rather than building new ones" — that is both accurate
and the more striking story.

**The priority of the definitional question:** Aristotle (in the *Metaphysics* 987b1, the
"amicus Plato" passage area) credits Socrates with two innovations: (1) inductive arguments
and (2) universal definitions. The philosophical move is: before you can know whether a
particular act is courageous, you must know what courage IS (the *ti esti* question —
"what is it?"). You cannot advance to cases before you have the universal. This "priority of
the definitional question" is documented as a Socratic contribution, not merely a Platonic
one, by Aristotle. `PARAPHRASE-ONLY` for the author (Aristotle's Metaphysics passage requires
a verified PD text the pack has not yet assembled — flag if the author needs the exact Aristotle
wording).

### 4B. Socratic ignorance
**Source:** Plato, *Apology* (passim, 21a–23b especially); *Meno* 80d (Meno calls the elenchus
a "torpedo fish" sting — making him numb and confused); *Theaetetus* 149a–151c (the midwife
metaphor, where Socrates says he himself is barren of ideas but helps others give birth to theirs).

**Documented position:** Socrates claims to have no special wisdom about the big questions
(virtue, beauty, justice, piety). Others — politicians, poets, craftsmen — think they know
these things but do not. He knows he does not know, which makes him a "little bit wiser."
The position is *NOT* global skepticism. It is specifically about the definitions of the
virtues and of the Good. The *Apology* shows Socrates making positive claims: he *knows* it
is base and evil to disobey his divine superior (*Apology* 29b). The midwifery metaphor in
the *Theaetetus* (Plato's Socrates says he helps others give birth to their own ideas, while
himself remaining barren) gives a self-portrait consistent with this: his role is to test others'
ideas, not to produce his own. `DOCUMENTED` via Plato, *Apology* and *Theaetetus*.

### 4C. "Virtue is knowledge" / No one does wrong willingly
**Source:** Plato, *Protagoras* 352b–360e; *Meno* 87c–89a; *Hippias Minor* (the disturbing
dialogue where Socrates argues the intentional liar is better than the unintentional one, which
is the reductio of the doctrine); *Gorgias* 460b–e; the *Apology* at 25e–26a (where Socrates
argues that if he corrupted the young, he did so unwillingly — for no one does harm willingly,
since harm comes back on you).

**Documented position:**
1. Every virtue is a form of knowledge: courage = knowledge of what is genuinely to be feared;
   piety = knowledge of what the gods truly require; justice = knowledge of what is truly good.
   This is *Plato's Socrates* in multiple dialogues — treated by scholars as genuinely Socratic
   (appears in early aporetic dialogues, not just the middle doctrine-heavy ones).
2. No one does wrong willingly (the Socratic paradox): if a person truly knows what is good,
   they will do it. Wrongdoing is always a result of ignorance — mistaking what is genuinely
   harmful for what is good. This leads to the conclusion that nobody is voluntarily wicked.
3. This doctrine is genuinely paradoxical and was treated as such in antiquity. Aristotle
   objects to it explicitly (*NE* VII.2–3): he thinks it ignores *akrasia* (weakness of will,
   acting against one's better judgment). The *Protagoras* argument is the fullest treatment.
   `DOCUMENTED` via Plato, multiple dialogues; `PARAPHRASE-ONLY` for exact *Protagoras* wording
   unless the author needs specific passages.

**NOTE for author:** Do NOT flatten this into "virtue is knowledge = be smart and you'll be good."
The full version is: the virtues are not separate traits but forms of a single knowledge (the
unity of virtue); and that knowledge, if genuine, necessitates virtuous action, because no one
chooses harm-to-themselves knowingly. The counterintuitive implication: all wrongdoers are
ignorant, not wicked. This is the part that bothered Aristotle and still bothers readers.

### 4D. Care of the soul (psyche)
**Source:** Plato, *Apology* 29d–30b (the most direct statement); also *Crito* 47c–e; *Phaedo*
(the whole dialogue is a meditation on the soul's preparation for death).

**Documented position (Jowett, *Apology* 29e–30a):**
"I do nothing but go about persuading you all, old and young alike, not to take thought for your
persons or your properties, but first and chiefly to care about the greatest improvement of the
soul."
(`VERIFIED` — classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html; also confirmed via Fowler at Perseus 30a)

Socrates' whole mission is framed as attention to the soul (*psyche*): the soul is the core of
a person, the seat of virtue and wisdom, more important than the body or wealth or reputation.
Neglecting it (as most Athenians do, according to Socrates) is the gravest error. The *Crito*
(47e, Jowett) states: "the life which is destroyed by doing injustice and acting wrongly has no
value" — only a well-ordered soul has genuine value.

This is the Socratic predecessor to Plato's tripartite soul (in the *Republic*) and the
immortal soul of the *Phaedo* — those are Plato's philosophical elaborations; the care-of-soul
doctrine in the early dialogues is closer to the historical Socrates. `DOCUMENTED` via Plato,
*Apology* 29d–30b.

### 4E. The "what is X?" questions (priority of definition)
As noted in 4A — Socrates opens every major ethical inquiry by demanding a definition of the
term at issue. Why does this matter philosophically? His implicit claim: without knowing what
justice IS (the universal, not a list of examples), we cannot tell whether any particular act
is just. Examples do not answer the question; they presuppose its answer. This is one of the
genuinely Socratic innovations Aristotle credits him with, independent of the Platonic machinery.
`DOCUMENTED` (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 987b1; attested through secondary sources in this pack —
author should flag if they need the exact Aristotle wording).

## 5. Anecdotes — documented vs. legend

### DOCUMENTED (via Plato — primary, close to events)

**Standing motionless at Potidaea** (`DOCUMENTED` — Plato, *Symposium* 220c–d)
Alcibiades describes how Socrates once stood in one place from dawn through noon into the
following morning, absorbed in a problem, while soldiers came to watch him in wonder. When
dawn came he offered a prayer to the sun and walked away. Exact Jowett wording: "how on one
occasion he had stood for an entire day and night absorbed in reflection amid the wonder of
the spectators" (*Symposium*, Jowett). The alternative Jowett phrasing: "on one occasion he
had stood for an entire day and night absorbed in reflection."
(`VERIFIED` — Gutenberg Symposium, via faculty.sgc.edu/rkelley/SYMPOSIUM.pdf Jowett text)

**Bravery at Delium** (`DOCUMENTED` — Plato, *Symposium* 221a–b; *Laches* 181b)
Alcibiades (Plato's *Symposium* 221b) describes how at Delium, after the Athenian defeat,
Socrates retreated "stalking about like a pelican, rolling his eyes" — keeping cool when others
fled. In *Laches* (181b), General Laches says that if others had conducted themselves at Delium
as Socrates had, "the honour of our country would have been upheld."

**Chaerephon and the Delphic oracle** (`DOCUMENTED` — Plato, *Apology* 21a–23b)
Chaerephon asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates; the oracle said
no one was. Socrates took this as a puzzle to solve, not a compliment. The *Apology* says
Chaerephon is now dead, but that his brother is present to confirm this. This makes it not just
Socrates' word — it has a named corroborating witness. The oracle story is fundamental to the
Apology's narrative.

**The counter-penalty: free meals** (`DOCUMENTED` — Plato, *Apology* 36d)
After the guilty verdict, Socrates famously proposed that his penalty be free maintenance in
the Prytaneum — the honor given to Olympic victors. This was taken by the jury as provocative
and contributed to the heavier death vote. His friends convinced him to offer a fine of 30
minae, but the damage was done.

**The *daimonion* as evidence in the trial** (`DOCUMENTED` — Plato, *Apology* 31c)
The divine sign is invoked by Socrates himself in the Apology as the explanation for why he
stayed out of politics (it would have gotten him killed earlier) and why he seems to be
"meddling" in others' affairs while neglecting public service.

### DOCUMENTED (via Xenophon — secondary but genuine, contemporaneous with events)

**Xanthippe's temperament (Xenophon's Symposium, not Plato's)**
Xenophon's *Symposium* (2.10) has Socrates explain that he married Xanthippe because he knew
if he could get along with her, he could get along with anyone: "Since I wish to deal and
associate with people, I thought that if I could endure her, I could easily associate with all
other people." This is NOT the shrewish-wife caricature — it is Socrates explaining a
philosophical rationale for his choice. Xenophon presents him as pragmatic, not tormented.
`DOCUMENTED` — Xenophon, *Symposium* 2.10. (Note: this is Xenophon's *Symposium*, a distinct
work from Plato's *Symposium*, set at a different dinner party.)

**Lamprocles' behavior toward Xanthippe (Xenophon, Memorabilia II.2)**
Xenophon shows Socrates reproving his son Lamprocles for being rude to Xanthippe, defending
her by saying she was more patient with Lamprocles than with anyone else. Suggests Xanthippe
was not purely a caricature in Xenophon either.

### LATE-LEGEND (DL) — via Diogenes Laertius, c. AD 225–250

All of the following come from or are amplified by Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent
Philosophers*, Book II, Chapter 5 (writing roughly 625 years after Socrates' death). Laertius
compiled from many earlier sources of varying reliability; he often gives multiple conflicting
versions without comment. These anecdotes are culturally important and are the source of much
popular Socrates lore, but NONE of them should be stated flat. Frame as "Diogenes Laertius
records" or "according to the much-later tradition" or "six centuries later, Diogenes Laertius
tells us."

- **"Whichever you do you will repent it" (marriage advice):** When asked whether to marry or
  not, Socrates said "Whichever you do you will repent it." DL, II.5. `LATE-LEGEND (DL)`
  (Note: The popular "By all means, marry; if you get a good wife you will be happy, if you get
  a bad one you will become a philosopher" has NO ancient source at all — it is an `APOCRYPHAL`
  modern invention, circulating from the 18th–19th century onward. Do NOT attribute it to Socrates.)

- **Xanthippe pouring water on his head:** When Xanthippe scolded him and then doused him with
  water, Socrates reportedly said "Did I not say that Xanthippe's thunder would end in rain?"
  DL, II.5. `LATE-LEGEND (DL)`

- **Father's profession as sculptor:** DL describes Sophroniscus as a "sculptor" (glypheus) and
  even says Socrates worked as a sculptor himself in his youth, supposedly carving the Three
  Graces near the Acropolis. This is `LATE-LEGEND (DL)` — no contemporary source confirms it.
  (Plato and Socrates-era sources suggest stonemason/stone-worker more broadly.)

**On DL's birth year (469/468 BC):** DL places Socrates' birth "in the fourth year of the 77th
Olympiad" which works out to 469/468 BC — consistent with the scholarly consensus, though DL is
not the primary basis for it.

## 6. Honest framing — anachronism warnings

### The democracy that killed him

The standard framing — Athens killed Socrates because it feared free thought — is an
anachronism. The more honest framing:

Athens in 399 BC was a democracy *that had just barely survived an oligarchic coup* backed by
two men closely associated with Socrates (Critias died fighting the democratic restoration;
Alcibiades' catastrophes were recent). The amnesty of 403 meant the jury could not formally
cite this — but the subtext was present. Socrates was 70; the youngest jurors had grown up
watching Alcibiades and Critias use the rhetoric of philosophical elite over demos to justify
political betrayal. The impiety charge was also real — Athenian religion was civic religion,
not private faith. "Introducing new deities" was not a theological dispute; it was a challenge
to civic life. A reading of the trial that makes it purely about free thought versus repression
misses all this context. The author should hold both: the charges were politically motivated in
context; and Socrates' ideas genuinely challenged Athenian civic norms.

### The "father of Western philosophy" claim

This is a convention, not a documentable first. Pre-Socratics existed; the Sophists existed.
The specific thing that makes Socrates a pivot-point: he turned philosophy from cosmology
(what is the world made of?) to ethics (how should I live?). This is Cicero's summary
(*Tusculan Disputations* V.4): Socrates "called philosophy down from the heavens, established
her in the cities of men, introduced her even into their homes, and compelled her to consider
life and morals." `LATE-TRADITION` (Cicero writing ~350 years after Socrates) but widely
accepted as capturing the real shift. The author should use it as a framing device, not as a
verbatim quote attributed to Socrates.

### "Philosophy" as anachronism

Socrates did not use the word "philosopher" (lover of wisdom) in the way later usage implies.
He examined his own ignorance and that of others; he did not write treatises or found a school.
The *Academy* belongs to Plato. The tidy "Socrates → Plato → Aristotle = philosophy" sequence
is a retrospective construct. The author should acknowledge that the category itself is partly
a later imposition — while keeping the story moving.

## 8. Lineage — answered / answered by

### Who Socrates answered (intellectual predecessors and adversaries)

**The natural philosophers (pre-Socratics):**
Socrates explicitly turned *away* from natural philosophy (cosmology, physics). In Plato's
*Phaedo* 96a–99d, Socrates describes his youthful enthusiasm for Anaxagoras (who said Mind
governs everything) and his crushing disappointment when Anaxagoras explained everything
mechanically, not teleologically. This is the documented pivot: Socrates moved from nature
to ethics. `DOCUMENTED` — Plato, *Phaedo* 96a–99d.

**The Sophists (Protagoras, Gorgias, Thrasymachus, Hippias):**
The Sophists were itinerant professional teachers who charged fees to teach rhetoric and
political argument. In Plato's dialogues they are Socrates' primary intellectual adversaries.
The *Protagoras* (Protagoras), *Gorgias* (Gorgias), *Republic* Book I (Thrasymachus), *Hippias
Major* and *Minor* (Hippias) are all direct engagements. Protagoras' famous claim "Man is the
measure of all things" is the contrast to Socratic universalism. Socrates rejects fee-taking,
argues that virtue cannot be taught (or at least that the Sophists aren't teaching it), and
challenges their claim to knowledge. `DOCUMENTED` — multiple early Platonic dialogues.
(Note: The framing gate requires the Sophists be steelmanned — they were not charlatans but
serious thinkers with real positions. See the *greeks-fact-pack.md* for their treatment at
era altitude.)

### Who Socrates taught (influence chain — consistent with greeks-fact-pack.md)

| Thinker | School founded | Lineage note |
|---|---|---|
| **Plato** (c. 429–347 BC) | Academy | The primary literary heir; the Platonic system (Forms, immortality, philosopher-king) is largely Plato's, not the historical Socrates' — the fidelity rule |
| **Xenophon** (c. 430–354 BC) | Historian/general; no school | Recorded Socrates; presented the practical moralist version |
| **Antisthenes** (c. 445–365 BC) | Founded the **Cynics** | Taught Diogenes of Sinope; Cynicism → through Crates → **Zeno of Citium** and the Stoics. The data spine edge (socrates → zeno via antisthenes) is correct and consistent with the greeks-fact-pack |

**Note for the author:** The pipeline's previous thinker read (Plato) covers the Plato branch exhaustively. This thinker read should make the *branching* visible — that the same teacher produced both Plato (toward Forms, immortality, philosopher-kings) and Antisthenes (toward asceticism, Cynicism, virtue-through-simplicity) and Aristippus (toward pleasure as the good), which are three deeply incompatible directions. The spread shows both how rich Socrates' influence was and how little he nailed down.

**The Antisthenes → Zeno spine:** Antisthenes → Diogenes of Sinope → Crates of Thebes → Zeno of Citium (founder of Stoicism). This chain is the path from Socratic ethics to Stoic ethics; the greeks-fact-pack.md names it and this pack must be consistent.

## 9. Key-passage block — ABSENT with story

Per the pipeline specification, every thinker gets a "Key passage" block OR an explicit
`absent: true` with the story told. This is Socrates' case.

**ABSENT — Socrates wrote nothing.**

The substitute sources (per the pipeline spec, the absent block "still links the best
substitute source"):

1. The *Apology* is the closest thing to a first-person Socratic document — it is Plato's
   record of Socrates' own defense speech. It is not a written work by Socrates, but it is
   the canonical account of Socrates speaking in his own voice about his own mission, in a
   specific historical context. The "unexamined life" sentence, the gadfly, the Delphic oracle
   narrative, and the care of the soul are all here.
   **SourceUrl:** https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html

2. The *Phaedo* gives the death scene and Socrates' last hours (Plato not present, reported
   by Phaedo).
   **SourceUrl:** https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/phaedo.html

3. The *Crito* gives the argument against escape (the personified Laws of Athens).
   **SourceUrl:** https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/crito.html

**The story of the silence:** Three incompatible Socrateses (Plato's philosopher, Xenophon's
practical moralist, Aristophanes' sophist-buffoon) exist because Socrates left no text. He
was 46 when Aristophanes portrayed him as a fraudulent teacher of rhetoric; he spent his
life claiming to know nothing while making everyone around him feel like fools; he was
executed at 70 for impiety and corrupting the youth; and everything we call "Socratic
philosophy" comes from people who were either mocking him, admiring him, or — in Plato's
case — possibly using his name for their own ideas. The silence is not just a biographical
fact; it is the central philosophical problem this thinker read must make the reader feel.
The author should open on the silence, not paper over it.
