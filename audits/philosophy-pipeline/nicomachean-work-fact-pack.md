The *Republic* fidelity rule was about who-speaks (Plato never speaks in his own voice). The

young or for those who live by passion, because the point is to *act* well, not just to know
(I.3) — the reader should feel that this is practical philosophy aimed at becoming good, not
a theory to admire.

## 1. The argument-arc — the whole treatise, Book by Book

This is the spine the author walks. The NE is one argument with a single question driving it:
**what is the human good (the best life), and how do we live it?** Book I names the good
(eudaimonia) and argues for it (the function argument); Books II–V analyze the **virtues of
character** (moral virtue, the mean, responsibility, the particular virtues, justice); Book VI
turns to the **virtues of the intellect** (especially *phronesis* and *sophia*); Book VII
handles the **failures** (*akrasia*) and pleasure; Books VIII–IX give the long treatment of
**friendship**; Book X returns to **pleasure** and delivers the culminating verdict — the
**contemplative life** is the highest happiness — then hands off to the *Politics*. The famous
set-pieces (function argument, the mean, *akrasia*, the three friendships, contemplation) are
MOVES inside this single structure, not detachable highlights.

### Book I — the good, eudaimonia, and the function argument
The treatise opens with its master claim: **every action and pursuit aims at some good**, so
"the good" is "that at which all things aim." Goods form a hierarchy of means and ends; there
must be some **highest good** sought for its own sake (or we'd desire endlessly to no point).
Everyone names that highest good **eudaimonia** — but they disagree wildly about what it *is*
(pleasure, honor, wealth, virtue). Aristotle rejects pleasure (the life of a grazing animal),
honor (it depends on others, not on us), and wealth (merely a means). Then the decisive move,
the **function (ergon) argument** (I.7): a thing's good lies in performing its characteristic
function well — a flautist's in playing well, an eye's in seeing. The distinctively HUMAN
function is **activity of soul in accordance with reason** (plants merely grow, animals merely
sense; only humans act rationally). Therefore the human good is **activity of soul in
accordance with virtue** — and it must be over a **complete life** ("one swallow does not make
a summer"). Eudaimonia needs **external goods** too (friends, some wealth, health, decent
fortune) as conditions, and is **not secure against catastrophe** — a hard, un-cozy point.
Aristotle closes Book I by splitting virtue into two kinds — **of character** (moral) and **of
intellect** — setting the agenda for Books II–VI.

### Book II — virtue of character: habit, the state, and the doctrine of the mean
**Moral virtue is acquired by habit, not by nature or teaching.** Nothing natural can be
trained against its nature (a stone won't learn to fall up); but virtue we get by *practicing*
it — **"we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing
brave acts."** The very word for character-virtue, *ēthikē*, is a "slight variation" from
*ethos* (habit). Virtue is therefore not a feeling and not a capacity but a **state of
character** (*hexis*) — a stable, trained disposition. And the kind of state it is: a **mean**
(*mesotēs*). Every moral virtue is a **mean between two vices, one of excess and one of
deficiency** — **courage** between **cowardice** (deficiency of confidence / excess of fear)
and **rashness** (excess of confidence); **temperance** between self-indulgence and (the rare)
insensibility. The headline definition (II.6): virtue is **"a state of character concerned
with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational
principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it."**
Two cautions Aristotle builds in himself: the mean is **relative to us** (not an arithmetic
midpoint — the right amount differs by person and situation), and **some actions admit no
mean** at all — spite, shamelessness, envy, adultery, theft, murder are bad *as such*: "It is
not possible, then, ever to be right with regard to them; one must always be wrong."

### Book III — voluntariness, choice, responsibility; then courage and temperance
Before cataloguing virtues, Aristotle settles **when we are responsible**. An action is
**voluntary** when "the moving principle is in the agent himself" who knows the particulars;
**involuntary** when done **under compulsion** (external force) or **through ignorance** of the
particulars (and regretted). **Choice** (*prohairesis*) is narrower than the voluntary: it is
voluntary action preceded by **deliberation** — and we **"deliberate not about ends but about
means"** (the doctor doesn't deliberate *whether* to heal but *how*). The payoff is the
foundation of moral responsibility and praise/blame: **"virtue also is in our own power, and
so too vice. For where it is in our power to act it is also in our power not to act."** Then
the first two particular virtues in detail: **courage** (*andreia*) — a mean about fear and
confidence, "more with the things that inspire fear," and most properly shown in **facing a
noble death in war**; and **temperance** (*sōphrosynē*) — a mean concerned with **bodily
pleasures** (touch and taste), not all pleasures.

### Book IV — the particular virtues (incl. the great-souled man)
The catalogue of character-virtues continues, each a mean:
- **Liberality / generosity** (*eleutheriotēs*) — the mean about **giving and taking wealth**
  (small/moderate scale), between **prodigality** (excess giving) and **meanness** (deficient
  giving, excess taking).
- **Magnificence** (*megaloprepeia*) — liberality on a **grand scale**: "a fitting expenditure
  involving largeness of scale" (public works, religious offerings) done in good taste, between
  vulgarity/tastelessness and niggardliness.
- **Magnanimity / "greatness of soul"** (*megalopsychia*) — the **"great-souled man"** (Ross:
  "the proud man") who **thinks himself worthy of great things and IS worthy** of them; his
  concern is chiefly **honour**, the greatest external good. The famous physical portrait: **"A
  slow step is thought proper to the proud man, a deep voice, and a level utterance."** He
  helps others but is ashamed to be helped; he would rather own "beautiful and profitless
  things" than useful ones. **This reads oddly to moderns** — present it in Aristotle's terms,
  do not sand it (FRAMING-TRAP §3).
- **Proper ambition** (the right attitude to small honours), **good temper** (*praotēs*, the
  mean about anger — "angry at the right things and with the right people… when he ought, and
  as long as he ought"), and the social virtues — **friendliness**, **truthfulness** (the mean
  between boastfulness and self-deprecation/*eirōneia*), **wit** (between buffoonery and
  boorishness). Shame is discussed as a quasi-virtue (a feeling proper to the young, not a true
  *hexis*).

### Book V — justice
A whole Book on **justice** (*dikaiosynē*), the most complex virtue. Aristotle splits the word:
- **Universal / general justice** = **complete virtue in relation to others** — lawfulness, the
  whole of virtue exercised toward one's neighbour. In this sense "**in justice is every virtue
  comprehended**." (He quotes the proverb that justice is the best of the virtues, "neither
  evening nor morning star so wonderful.")
- **Particular justice** = **fairness**, a specific virtue about distribution and exchange,
  which itself splits in two:
  - **Distributive justice** — dividing **honours, money, and other shareable goods** among
    citizens; it follows **geometric proportion** (according to merit/desert), so "the just…
    is a species of the proportionate."
  - **Rectificatory / corrective justice** — restoring the balance in **transactions** (both
    voluntary, like sales, and involuntary, like theft/assault); it follows **arithmetic
    proportion** (the judge restores the equal, treating the parties as equals and just
    cancelling gain against loss).
- Justice is therefore an **intermediate** and **the equal**: "the unjust is unequal, the just
  is equal." Aristotle also treats **reciprocity** in exchange (and the role of money as a
  measure), **political justice**, and closes with **equity** (*epieikeia*) — a *correction* of
  law where the law, being universal, fails the particular case: "the equitable… is better than
  one kind of justice" (better than the rigid letter of law), "yet it is just."

### Book VI — the virtues of the intellect (phronesis vs. sophia)
Book II–V handled character; Book VI handles the **rational part of the soul** and its
excellences. The mean is determined "by a rational principle" — Book VI is where Aristotle
finally says *which* one. He names **five states by which the soul reaches truth**: **art**
(*technē*, making), **scientific knowledge** (*epistēmē*, demonstration of the necessary),
**practical wisdom** (*phronesis*), **philosophic wisdom** (*sophia*), and **intuitive reason**
(*nous*, grasp of first principles). The two that matter most:
- **Practical wisdom** (*phronesis* / prudence): **"a true and reasoned state of capacity to
  act with regard to the things that are good or bad for man."** It is about **action**, about
  **deliberating well** on human goods, about **particulars** as well as universals. It cannot
  be reduced to a formula — it is the calibrating excellence that hits the mean in real
  situations. **Pericles** is the example: men like him "can see what is good for themselves
  and what is good for men in general." Crucially, **you cannot be good without phronesis, and
  you cannot have phronesis without moral virtue** — the two are interlocked.
- **Philosophic / theoretical wisdom** (*sophia*): knowledge of the **highest, eternal,
  necessary things** — *nous* (grasp of first principles) **combined with** *epistēmē*
  (demonstration), "as it were its proper completion." It is the **most finished** form of
  knowledge, "wisdom… of the highest objects." This is the seed of Book X: *sophia*, not
  *phronesis*, crowns the contemplative life.

### Book VII — akrasia (weakness of will) and pleasure
Two topics. First, the long, famous treatment of **incontinence** (*akrasia*) — **acting
against one's own better judgment**. The **incontinent** person **"knowing that what he does is
bad, does it as a result of passion,"** while the **continent** person knows his appetites are
bad and **"refuses on account of his rational principle to follow them."** This sets up the
**Socratic puzzle**: Socrates (cf. socrates / plato packs) denied *akrasia* is even possible —
**"no one… when he judges, acts against what he judges best,"** because (for Socrates) to know
the good is to do it; apparent weakness is really ignorance. Aristotle says this **"plainly
contradicts the observed facts"** — people manifestly do act against what they know — and
resolves it by distinguishing **kinds of knowing**: the incontinent person *has* the knowledge
but is **"not exercising it"** (like someone asleep, drunk, or mad), and **appetite** drags
him toward the particular object while his universal knowledge ("this kind of thing is bad") is
not active in the moment. So Socrates is **partly right** (full active knowledge is not
dragged about) and partly wrong (we can have knowledge we fail to use). Second, **pleasure**:
against those who call all pleasure bad, Aristotle argues **pleasure is in a sense a good** and
some pleasures are choiceworthy — though bodily pleasures pursued to excess are not. (Book X
returns to pleasure for the full account.)

### Books VIII–IX — friendship (philia)
The single longest treatment in the NE — **two whole Books** — a measure of how central
friendship is to the good life. Book VIII opens by calling friendship **"a virtue or implies
virtue, and is besides most necessary with a view to living"**: **"without friends no one
would choose to live, though he had all other goods."** The structural core is the **three
kinds of friendship**, keyed to the **three lovable things — the good, the pleasant, the
useful**:
- **Friendship of utility** — loving the other for the **benefit** they bring (business
  partners, allies); dissolves when the usefulness ends.
- **Friendship of pleasure** — loving the other for the **pleasure** they give (often the
  young, ruled by passion); dissolves when the pleasure fades.
- **Perfect / complete friendship** — **"the friendship of men who are good, and alike in
  virtue"** — loving the other **for who they are**, wishing them well for their own sake. It
  alone is **lasting** ("goodness is an enduring thing"), rare, and slow to form. Utility and
  pleasure friendships are **"easily dissolved."**
Book IX develops the philosophy of it: the **friend as "another self"** ("his friend is another
self"); friendship as an extension of the **good person's right relation to himself** (proper
self-love — the good man "should be a lover of self," assigning himself the noblest acts, not
the selfish hoarder's self-love); whether the happy/self-sufficient person **needs** friends
(yes — the happy man wants to "contemplate worthy actions… of a good man who is his friend");
and a practical limit — you **cannot have many** true friends, since deep friendship "can only
be felt towards a few people."

### Book X — pleasure, and the contemplative life as the highest happiness
A two-part climax. First, the **full account of pleasure**: pleasure is not the good itself
(against Eudoxus) nor an evil (against Speusippus) — it **"completes the activity"** as a
"supervening end," like the bloom on youth. Good pleasures complete good activities; the best
pleasure completes the best activity. Which lands the whole treatise on its **culminating
claim** (X.7–8): **perfect happiness is contemplative activity** (*theōria*). Why: it is the
activity of the **best thing in us** (*nous*, reason); its objects are the **highest and most
knowable** (the eternal); it is the **most continuous** activity we can sustain; the **most
self-sufficient** (the wise person can contemplate alone); and it alone seems **"loved for its
own sake; for nothing arises from it apart from the contemplating."** Such a life is **higher
than the merely human** — Aristotle's most startling note: **"such a life would be too high for
man; for it is not in so far as he is man that he will live so, but in so far as something
divine is present in him,"** and so we must, **"so far as we can, make ourselves immortal"**
(*athanatizein*) and live by the best thing in us. **"Happiness, therefore, must be some form
of contemplation."** The **moral-virtue life** (practical, political, exercising *phronesis*
and the virtues of character) is happy too — but **in a secondary degree**. Then the **hand-off
to the *Politics***: knowing the good is not enough; we must become good, and that requires
**laws and good upbringing**, so the inquiry must turn to **constitutions** to "complete… our
philosophy of human nature." The NE ends mid-stride, opening the door to the *Politics*.

**Q-B · Eudaimonia named as the agreed-upon name (but disputed in content) · I.4, 1095a:**

excellently across a complete life, not a mood. Note that Ross's own gloss in Q-B ("living well
and doing well") carries the right sense. In the prose: gloss eudaimonia as **flourishing /
living well** on first use, quote Ross's "happiness," and explicitly warn the reader off the
feeling-reading. (Matches aristotle-fact-pack §4 EUDAIMONIA FRAMING TRAP; SEP: eudaimonia "is
not a feeling.") `FRAMING-TRAP`

**Q-I · The doctrine of the mean — THE definition · II.6, 1106b36–1107a2:**
"Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean

*phronesis*, not a rule, calibrates it (links to Book VI); (3) it is **"concerned with

of *some* virtues, not a maxim covering all conduct. `FRAMING-TRAP`

### Book III

**Q-L · We deliberate about means, not ends · III.3, 1112b11:**
"We deliberate not about ends but about means. For a doctor does not deliberate whether he

## 3. FRAMING-TRAPS — the places this work is most often misread

**TRAP 1 — "Eudaimonia just means happiness / feeling happy."** The anachronism trap, and the
single most important framing call in the whole read (Q-B). Ross translates it "happiness," but
*eudaimonia* is **flourishing** — an **objective, whole-life** assessment of excellent rational
activity, not a subjective mood. A person can *feel* cheerful and not be eudaimōn (if they live
badly), and the eudaimōn life is **not guaranteed to feel pleasant moment-to-moment** and is
**vulnerable to catastrophe** (great misfortune can wreck it — "one swallow does not make a
summer," Q-D, is about the *whole life*). Always gloss as "flourishing / living well" on first
use; quote Ross's "happiness" with the warning attached. `FRAMING-TRAP` (carries
aristotle-fact-pack §4 / §9.3).

**TRAP 2 — "The golden mean = moderation in all things."** Aristotle refutes this himself
(Q-J). The mean is **not** a maxim covering all conduct, and **not** a mathematical midpoint:
(a) it is **relative to us** and **set by *phronesis***, not a rule (Q-I); (b) the two extremes
are **not equally bad** in every case (cowardice is worse and commoner than rashness; the mean
is "nearer" to one extreme for different virtues); (c) **some actions have NO mean** — spite,
adultery, theft, murder are wrong *as such*, "one must always be wrong" (Q-J). "Moderation in
all things" is the potted-summary distortion; the real doctrine is a theory of *some* virtues,
calibrated by practical wisdom. `FRAMING-TRAP` (carries aristotle-fact-pack §9.2).

**TRAP 3 — "The great-souled man is an arrogant snob / Aristotle endorsed vanity."** The
*megalopsychos* (Ross: "the proud man," Q-P) reads oddly and even off-puttingly to moderns —
slow gait, deep voice, refusing to receive favours, owning beautiful useless things, "despising
justly." But for Aristotle it is a **virtue**: the man who correctly judges himself worthy of
great things and *is* worthy. The vice would be **vanity** (claiming more than you're worth) or
**pusillanimity / undue humility** (claiming less). **Present it in Aristotle's terms** — it
reflects an aristocratic Greek ideal of self-sufficiency and earned honour, and it can read as
elitist; **don't sand it into a modern "healthy self-esteem," and don't mock it either.** State
what Aristotle says (the bearing, the concern with honour, the self-assessment) and let the
reader sit with how alien it is. `FRAMING-TRAP`

**TRAP 4 — "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit."** This
is the **most-quoted fake in philosophy** and it attaches to THIS work. It is **NOT Aristotle**
— it is **Will Durant**, *The Story of Philosophy* (1926), paraphrasing NE II.1. Full debunk in
§4; this is load-bearing and must appear (as a debunk) in any draft that touches the habit
argument. The real Aristotle text is Q-E/Q-F. `APOCRYPHAL` (carries aristotle-fact-pack §3).

**TRAP 5 — "The contemplative-life conclusion contradicts the rest of the Ethics."** A real
scholarly tension, present it honestly, don't pretend it away: Books I–IX build a rich,
engaged, practical picture of the good life (virtues of character, justice, friendship, the
active citizen exercising *phronesis*) — and then Book X seems to crown a **solitary,
god-like, theoretical** life above all of it, calling the moral-virtue life happy only "in a
secondary degree" (Q-AK). Scholars dispute whether Book X is **inclusive** (contemplation is
the *best part* of a full virtuous life) or **dominant/exclusive** (contemplation is the one
thing happiness really is). The author should **present both Aristotle's claim AND the tension**
— note that contemplation needs the moral virtues and friends to be livable, and that Aristotle
himself never says drop the virtues. Do not resolve it falsely in either direction.
`FRAMING-TRAP` / `PRECISION-NOTE`.

**TRAP 6 — "Nicomachean" names a doctrine or theme.** No. It is a **family name** — almost
certainly Aristotle's **son Nicomachus** (dedicatee/editor), possibly his **father** (also
Nicomachus). The title says nothing about contents. Gloss it once so the reader isn't left
wondering. `PRECISION-NOTE`.

**Bonus — Aristotle vs. Socrates on akrasia.** Book VII (Q-AB) is Aristotle directly correcting
the Socratic intellectualism ("no one does wrong willingly / to know the good is to do it,"
cross-ref socrates / plato packs). Frame it as a genuine philosophical advance: Aristotle takes
the **observed fact** of weakness of will seriously where Socrates explained it away, and
dissolves the puzzle by distinguishing *having* knowledge from *actively using* it. Don't flatten
it to "Aristotle disagreed with Socrates" — show what the disagreement *was*. `PRECISION-NOTE`.

## 4. THE APOCRYPHAL-QUOTE ALERT — the Durant fake (load-bearing, re-stated for the WORK read)

### The fake quote
> "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit."

**NOT Aristotle.** It appears nowhere in the *Nicomachean Ethics* or anywhere in his corpus.

### The real source
**Will Durant**, *The Story of Philosophy* (Simon & Schuster, 1926), **paraphrasing** NE II.1.
Durant's passage reads: *"Excellence is an art won by training and habituation… we are what we
repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit."* Durant is **narrating his own
reading** of Aristotle; the famous two sentences are **Durant's**, not a translation. Over
decades the phrase detached and was re-attributed to Aristotle.
`DOCUMENTED` — Snopes, Check Your Fact, umb.edu/quoteunquote; corroborated by Richard Kraut
(Northwestern): "Those aren't Aristotle's words translated into English. But it is an accurate
and succinct way of conveying what Aristotle says in a more complicated way."

### What Aristotle ACTUALLY says (NE II.1, Ross, VERIFIED — Q-E/Q-F above)
> "moral virtue comes about as a result of habit, whence also its name (ethike) is one that is
> formed by a slight variation from the word ethos (habit)."
> "we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave
> acts."

**How to frame it:** Give Durant credit (it's a fine summary), but show the reader the *real*
text — it is more qualified and more interesting than the bumper sticker, and the Durant line
must never appear in this read except explicitly as a debunk. `APOCRYPHAL`.

## 5. "Why this is a break" block material (work-level)

**BEFORE — the question on the table.** Two big ethical traditions hung over Aristotle. **Plato**
(and the Socrates of the dialogues) said virtue is **knowledge** — know the Good (the Form,
grasped by reason, off in the intelligible realm) and you will *be* good; to do wrong is just to
be ignorant, so **no one does wrong willingly** (the intellectualism Aristotle attacks at VII,
Q-AB). And ordinary Greek ethics gave lists of admirable traits (courage, justice, piety) without
a unifying account of **why** they're good or **how** you get them. So the open questions were:
*what actually makes a life go well?* and *if knowing the good isn't enough, how do people become
good — and why do they so often fail (weakness of will)?*

**THE MOVE — what the *Nicomachean Ethics* does that nothing before it had.** Aristotle builds the
first **systematic, this-worldly, empirical** ethics, and welds four moves into one structure:
(1) he **defines the good functionally** — the human good is *activity of soul in accordance with
virtue, over a complete life* (the function argument, Q-C), grounding ethics in **human nature**
rather than a transcendent Form; (2) he separates **how you become good** (by **habit and
practice** — "we become just by doing just acts," Q-F) from **what you know**, breaking with
Plato's virtue-is-knowledge — so moral education is **training**, not lecturing, and **children
and the vicious aren't ready** for ethics because the point is to *act*; (3) he gives virtue a
**precise shape** — a trained disposition (*hexis*) to hit the **mean** relative to us, set by
**practical wisdom** (Q-I), neither a rule nor a feeling; (4) he takes **failure seriously** —
*akrasia*, weakness of will, which Socrates denied was even possible, Aristotle dissolves by
distinguishing knowing-that from using-knowledge (Q-AB). And he crowns it with a startling
verdict: the highest happiness is **contemplation** (Q-AK), the activity of the divine spark in
us. The break is that ethics becomes a **practical science of becoming good** — grounded in
function and habit, calibrated by judgment, honest about weakness — rather than a knowledge of a
Form that would automatically make you good.

**The concrete line to state in the block:** before Aristotle, to be good was to *know* the good
(Plato); after Aristotle, to be good is to *do* good — repeatedly, until it becomes who you are —
under the guidance of practical wisdom. Ethics stops being a branch of metaphysics and becomes a
training manual for character.

## 9. One-paragraph arc summary + verification cautions

**The arc.** Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* (Ross translation; lecture notes from his Lyceum
years, the 340s–320s BCE; named for a family member, probably his son Nicomachus) asks one
question across ten Books — *what is the human good, and how do we live it?* Book I answers that
the good is **eudaimonia** (flourishing, not a feeling) and argues for it by the **function
argument**: the human good is "activity of soul in accordance with virtue" over a complete life
("one swallow does not make a summer"). Books II–V analyze the **virtues of character**: virtue
is acquired by **habit** ("we become just by doing just acts"), is a trained **state of
character** that hits the **mean** between excess and deficiency (courage between cowardice and
rashness) — though the mean is relative to us, set by practical wisdom, and **some acts have no
mean at all**. Book III grounds **responsibility** (the voluntary, choice, "virtue is in our own
power"); Book IV catalogues the particular virtues including the alien-to-moderns **great-souled
man**; Book V dissects **justice** (universal vs. particular; distributive vs. rectificatory;
equity). Book VI turns to the **intellectual virtues**, splitting **practical wisdom**
(*phronesis*, about action and human goods) from **philosophic wisdom** (*sophia*, about the
highest things). Book VII tackles **weakness of will** (*akrasia*) — correcting Socrates, who
denied it was possible — and pleasure. Books VIII–IX give the long account of **friendship** —
the three kinds (utility, pleasure, and the rare, lasting virtue-friendship), the friend as
"another self." Book X delivers the verdict: pleasure "completes the activity," and the highest
happiness is **contemplation** (*theoria*), the activity of the divine spark in us — we should
"make ourselves immortal" — with the moral-virtue life happy "in a secondary degree." It ends by
handing off to the **Politics**: to actually become good we need laws and upbringing, so ethics
opens onto the study of the city.

translation (MIT Internet Classics Archive, one page per Book) and matched word-for-word — all
