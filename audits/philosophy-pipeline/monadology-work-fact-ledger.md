## §1 — BIBLIOGRAPHIC FACTS (fully verified)

### Composition
- Written in **French** in **1714**, during Leibniz's last stay in **Vienna**
  (he was there from 1712 until September 1714 in the service of the Holy Roman
  Emperor Charles VI). — ✅ CONFIRMED (Wikipedia Monadology; Britannica)
- The text is **90 numbered sections** (sometimes called "paragraphs" or
  "articles"). — ✅ CONFIRMED (Wikipedia: "ninety paragraphs"; every edition)
- Leibniz wrote it in French, not German or Latin. The later standard editions
  are translations. — ✅ CONFIRMED

### The title is NOT Leibniz's
- **Leibniz gave the text no title.** The manuscript circulated untitled.
  — ✅ CONFIRMED: "Leibniz himself gave no title to the manuscript" (Wikipedia
  Monadology; scholars universally agree).
- The title was coined **by Heinrich Köhler (1685–1737)** when he published the
  first German translation in **1720** as *Lehrsätze über die Monadologie*
  (rough English: "Propositions on the Monadology"). — ✅ CONFIRMED (Wikipedia
  Monadology; EBSCO Research Starters on Monadology).
- When the **original French text** was finally published in **1840** (in Johann
  Eduard Erdmann's anthology of Leibniz's works), Erdmann adopted Köhler's title
  *La Monadologie*. Subsequent editors (Jacques, Janet, Gerhardt) retained it,
  dropping the earlier subtitle *Principia philosophiae* ("Principles of
  Philosophy"). — ✅ CONFIRMED (Wikipedia Monadology; EBSCO).
- **Author brief note:** The work is universally known as *The Monadology* and
  the author SHOULD use that title, but the read must note — once — that Leibniz
  himself left it untitled, and that the name came from Köhler's 1720 German
  edition. This is a real and interesting fact, not pedantry.

### Publication history — posthumous
- Leibniz **died 14 November 1716**, two years after writing the text (gout,
  Hanover). — ✅ CONFIRMED (Britannica; MacTutor)
- **First published:** German translation by Heinrich Köhler, **1720**
  (*Lehrsätze über die Monadologie*). — ✅ CONFIRMED
- **Latin edition:** 1721, in the *Acta Eruditorum* supplement, under the title
  *Principia Philosophiae*. — ✅ CONFIRMED (Wikipedia; EBSCO)
- **Original French text:** not published until **1840**, in Erdmann's edition.
  — ✅ CONFIRMED
- An English translation is not among the early publications; Robert Latta's
  1898 Oxford edition was among the first important English translations.
  — ✅ CONFIRMED (Archive.org metadata; Wikipedia "Robert Latta (philosopher)")

### The Köhler translation — an important nuance
Köhler's 1720 German text was made from **an earlier draft** he may have obtained
from Leibniz personally in the summer of 1714, NOT from the final manuscript. The
German and Latin translations thus predate the French original by 120 years and
were made from a different textual witness. Three manuscripts of the final French
text survive (one autograph with corrections, two further copies). Editors noted
this manuscript complexity when the French was finally published in 1840.
— ✅ CONFIRMED (Wikipedia Monadology; EBSCO Research Starters)

## §2 — RECIPIENT / OCCASION

### Nicolas Remond — the primary recipient
- The *Monadology* was written for **Nicolas-François Remond** (Rémond de
  Montmort; royal librarian and philosopher in Paris), who had written to Leibniz
  asking for a condensed exposition of his philosophy after reading the
  *Théodicée*. — ✅ CONFIRMED (Wikipedia; HandWiki; EBSCO). This is the
  documented primary addressee.

### Prince Eugene of Savoy — a real but nuanced connection
- Leibniz's companion piece, the ***Principes de la nature et de la grâce fondés
  en raison*** (*Principles of Nature and Grace*), was **explicitly written for
  Prince Eugene of Savoy (1663–1736)**, the military commander and statesman,
  and was sent to Remond on 26 August 1714 — with a note that it had been
  composed for Prince Eugene. — ✅ CONFIRMED (Wikipedia Monadology; Springer
  Principles of Nature and Grace chapter).
- **The *Monadology* itself:** Some sources say it too was written for or at
  Eugene's suggestion via Remond's mediation; others assign it to Remond alone.
  The most careful account (Wikipedia, HandWiki) treats the *Principles of Nature
  and Grace* as definitely Eugene's, and the *Monadology* as definitely Remond's —
  two parallel condensations written simultaneously. The anecdote that Eugene
  "kept it like a jewel in a case" (Robert Latta's introduction, 1898) refers to
  whichever of the two texts Eugene received — most likely the *Principles of
  Nature and Grace*. — ⚠️ AUTHOR CAUTION: State the Prince Eugene connection
  as the *Principles of Nature and Grace* (documented); say the *Monadology* was
  written "in the same summer" for Remond's circle in Paris; do NOT assert that
  the *Monadology* was dedicated to or specifically written for Eugene — that is
  the *Principles*' story, and conflating them is the common error.

### The companion piece — *Principles of Nature and Grace* (1714)
- The *Principes de la nature et de la grâce fondés en raison* and the
  *Monadologie* were **written simultaneously, Vienna, summer 1714**, as two
  distinct condensations of the same philosophical system. — ✅ CONFIRMED
- The *Principles of Nature and Grace* is **more accessible** — an "essentially
  popular" exposition of the natural world and metaphysics, noted for its
  optimistic tone. The *Monadology* is more technical and compact. — ✅ CONFIRMED
  (Springer chapter; Wikipedia)
- Scholars sometimes describe the *Principles* as "a preliminary study out of
  which the *Monadology* was elaborated," though the exact sequence is not fully
  settled. — ✅ NOTE: framing for the author; treat them as parallel, not
  one-deriving-from-the-other without qualification.
- The *Principles of Nature and Grace* was **published in French in the
  Netherlands shortly after Leibniz's death** (1714 for Eugene, printed c.1718).
  — ✅ CONFIRMED (Wikipedia)

## §3 — ARGUMENT WALK (section-by-section structure, for the author)

The 90 sections fall into identifiable blocks. The author should narrate the
argument STEP BY STEP (the `kind: work` shape). Below is the verified skeleton:

**Block 1 — §§1–6: What a monad is**
- §1: The monad is a **simple substance** that goes to make up compounds; "simple"
  = without parts. — ✅ CONFIRMED (Latta)
- §2: There MUST be simple substances, because compounds are nothing but
  collections of simples; if there were no simples there would be nothing at all.
  — ✅ CONFIRMED (Latta)
- §3: Simple substances have no extension, no shape, no divisibility. — ✅ CONFIRMED
- §5: Monads are created (begin by creation, end by annihilation — they cannot
  begin or end by natural means, only "all at once"). — ✅ CONFIRMED

**Block 2 — §§7–18: Windowlessness and internal change**
- §7: **Monads have no windows.** Change must come from within. — ✅ CONFIRMED
  (full section text verified below)
- §10–13: Every monad is different from every other (no two monads are identical
  — this is the identity of indiscernibles in embryo). — ✅ CONFIRMED
- §14–18: All monads have **perception** (representing multiplicity in a unity)
  and **appetition** (the drive from one perception to the next). §14 draws the
  key distinction: perception vs. **apperception** (self-conscious awareness of
  one's perception). — ✅ CONFIRMED

**Block 3 — §§19–30: Degrees of monads**
- §19: Lower monads (bare/simple) have perception but no memory or reason.
  §24–25: Animals have something like memory (through the association of images)
  but not reason. §29–30: Only **spirits** (rational souls) rise to reason and
  self-awareness; they are capable of **reflective knowledge** and thus of
  **science, morality, and knowing God**. — ✅ CONFIRMED

**Block 4 — §§31–37: Two great principles**
- §31: Principle of **Contradiction** — we judge false what involves a
  contradiction; true what is opposed to the false. Governs **truths of reason**
  (necessary truths, true in all possible worlds). — ✅ CONFIRMED
- §32: Principle of **Sufficient Reason** — there can be no fact, no true
  statement, without a sufficient reason why it is so and not otherwise. Governs
  **truths of fact** (contingent truths, true in this world but not necessary).
  — ✅ CONFIRMED
- §33–35: The distinction between **truths of reason** (necessary; their opposite
  is impossible; analysis can always find the contradiction in the negative) and
  **truths of fact** (contingent; their opposite is possible; analysis goes on to
  infinity — only God can finish it). — ✅ CONFIRMED
- §36–37: Every sufficient reason for contingent truths must ultimately rest on a
  **necessary being**; the chain cannot go on forever. — ✅ CONFIRMED

**Block 5 — §§38–48: God as the necessary monad**
- §38: "Thus the final reason of things must be in a necessary substance, in which
  the variety of particular changes exists only eminently, as in its source; and
  this substance we call God." — ✅ CONFIRMED (Latta; consistent across multiple
  sourced searches)
- §39: There is only one God; He is sufficient reason for everything.
- §40: God must be unlimited, containing as much reality as possible (the source of
  all possible essences). — ✅ CONFIRMED
- §45: God alone has the privilege that His existence follows from His essence
  (the **ontological argument**, used here without ceremony). — ✅ CONFIRMED

**Block 6 — §§49–60: The universe as a harmony of mirrors**
- §53–55: The **best of all possible worlds** argument. Among infinitely many
  possible universes in God's understanding, God chooses the best one.
  — ✅ CONFIRMED (see §3 of this ledger below)
- §56: Each monad expresses all the others — the "perpetual living mirror."
- §57: The town-from-many-sides image — one universe seen from infinitely many
  viewpoints, each a monad. — ✅ CONFIRMED
- §58: The best world = maximum variety with maximum order; this is the content
  of "best," not that every event is good. — ✅ CONFIRMED

**Block 7 — §§61–81: The body, pre-established harmony**
- §62–63: Every monad has an "organic body" of lower monads surrounding it; the
  soul (dominant monad) and the body (lower monads) do not causally interact.
  — ✅ CONFIRMED
- §§78–80: **Pre-established harmony** stated explicitly. Soul follows its own
  laws (final causes); body follows its own laws (efficient causes); they agree
  because God established the harmony from the start. — ✅ CONFIRMED

**Block 8 — §§82–90: Spirits, the City of God**
- §§82–86: Rational spirits (human and higher) are like "little gods" made in
  God's image (§83); they form the **City of God** (§86) — a moral community under
  divine governance. — ✅ CONFIRMED
- §87–90: The harmony of the **realm of nature** (efficient causes, physics) and
  the **realm of grace** (final causes, spirits, morality) — Leibniz's final
  synthesis. §90 closes on a single quoted sentence: the two realms bring it
  about that things lead to grace by natural paths. — ✅ CONFIRMED

## §5 — KEY POSITION SUMMARIES (source-tied)

Each position below is tied to the section(s) that state it. All paraphrase
(except where a quotation appears above) is drawn from the verified Latta text
and from SEP / IEP entries.

**1. Monads as simple, indivisible, immaterial substances**  
Monads are the ultimate constituents of reality. They are simple (no parts, §1–2),
have no extension or shape (§3), cannot be divided, and cannot be naturally created
or destroyed (§5 — only God creates and annihilates monads). They are not material
in the ordinary sense: matter, as we know it, is composed of monads, and is itself
a "well-founded phenomenon" — real but dependent on these ultimate simples.
— Source: §§1–6 (Latta); SEP "Leibniz's Metaphysics."

**2. Windowlessness — no external causation**  
No monad receives any causal input from any other created thing (§7). Change in a
monad comes entirely from within — from its own **internal principle** and its own
**appetition** (drive from one perceptual state to the next). This means monads
do not literally interact: they don't push, pull, or communicate. The appearance
of causal interaction between, say, a billiard ball striking another is real at
the level of phenomena but has no ultimate metaphysical grounding in one monad
changing another.  
— Source: §§7–15 (Latta); SEP "Leibniz's Philosophy of Mind."

**3. Each monad mirrors the whole universe from its viewpoint**  
Though monads have no windows, each one **represents** (perceives) the whole
universe (§56) — just as a town seen from the east looks utterly different from
the same town seen from the west (§57). God "programs" each monad at creation to
perceive the universe from its own particular viewpoint, and each viewpoint is
complete: nothing in the universe is truly hidden from any monad — only clear or
confused (§49). The difference between a rock (confused, dim perceptions) and a
spirit (clear, rational apperceptions) is one of degree of clarity, not of kind.
— Source: §§49–57 (Latta); SEP "Leibniz's Metaphysics."

**4. Pre-established harmony**  
Soul and body do not interact causally. They run in perfect parallel because God
synchronized them at creation — like two clocks made so accurately that they keep
the same time forever without ever touching (this is Leibniz's own clock image,
from his 1696 *New System* clarification; paraphrased, not quoted, since no PD
English of the 1696 text is available). §§78–79 state it for the Monadology.
This dissolves the mind-body problem Descartes left open: instead of explaining
how two incompatible substances could interact, Leibniz denies they interact at
all.  
— Source: §§78–80 (Latta); IEP "Leibniz: Metaphysics."

**5. Principle of contradiction**  
Necessary truths — those true in all possible worlds, including mathematical and
logical truths — are governed by the principle of contradiction. A necessary truth
is one whose denial is self-contradictory (§31). Their analysis, in principle,
always terminates.  
— Source: §31 (Latta).

**6. Principle of sufficient reason**  
Contingent truths — those true in this world but not in all possible worlds — are
governed by the principle of sufficient reason: nothing is so without a reason why
it is so and not otherwise (§32). The analysis of a contingent truth in principle
goes on forever (only God can complete it). This principle is what forces Leibniz
to posit a necessary being (God) as the ultimate reason for the contingent world's
existence (§§36–38).  
— Source: §§32–38 (Latta).

**7. The principle of the best — best of all possible worlds**  
God surveys infinitely many possible universes (§53) and, being perfectly wise,
good, and powerful, chooses the one that balances the greatest variety with the
greatest order/simplicity of laws — the best possible package (§54–55, §58). This
is Leibniz's theodicy: not that every event in this world is good, but that the
WHOLE of this world is better than any other possible whole. Evil (metaphysical
gap/imperfection; physical suffering; moral sin) is the unavoidable cost of a
world with free rational creatures and law-governed nature — a smaller evil in
service of a greater whole.  
— Source: §§53–55, §58 (Latta); *Theodicy* (1710), paraphrased (Huggard not PD).

**8. Degrees of perception and apperception**  
All monads have **perception** (representing multiplicity in a unity, §14). But
most monads have only confused, unconscious perception — "petites perceptions"
(little perceptions). Higher monads (souls of animals) add **memory** and
something like experience. Only **spirits** (rational souls, §29–30) have
**apperception**: conscious, reflective awareness of their own perceptions. This
lets them know themselves, reason, and know God. §14 explicitly marks this as
what Descartes missed: Descartes said animals with no conscious thought were mere
machines, but Leibniz says there is a full gradient of perception below the
threshold of consciousness — and even a machine, at the right scale, would have
low-level monadic perceptions.  
— Source: §§14–30 (Latta); SEP "Leibniz's Philosophy of Mind."

**9. God as the necessary monad**  
The sufficient reason for the contingent world must ultimately rest in a necessary
being — one that exists by its own essence, that needs no external reason for its
existence. That being is **God**, the unique monad whose existence is necessary
(§§36–45). God is not a monad like the others: God is the source of all possible
essences, the unlimited monad, outside time and space. Created monads are
"fulguration" (flashes, §47 in some editions) — something like God making monads
as ongoing expressions of his own essence.  
— Source: §§36–48 (Latta); SEP "Leibniz's Metaphysics."

## §6 — THE POTTED-SUMMARY / CARICATURE TRAP (mandatory flag)

### "Best of all possible worlds" — what the caricature does

**The caricature (Voltaire's *Candide*, 1759):** Dr Pangloss, a Leibnizian tutor,
insists "all is for the best in this best of all possible worlds" no matter the
horror — through earthquakes, inquisitions, shipwrecks. Voltaire wrote *Candide*
partly in response to the 1755 **Lisbon earthquake** (which killed tens of
thousands, shook European confidence in a benevolent divine order, and made
Leibniz's sunny theodicy seem callous). *Candide* was banned immediately by civil
and religious authorities yet became a bestseller. Pangloss = a caricature of
Leibniz. — ✅ CONFIRMED (Britannica "Candide"; Conversation "Guide to the
Classics"; Sparknotes "Pangloss")

**What Leibniz actually argues (steelmanned):** "Best of all possible worlds" is
a **comparative, holistic** claim about total possible histories, not an
event-by-event endorsement of suffering. The argument: God faces an infinite menu
of possible worlds (complete histories, all internally consistent); being perfectly
good, wise, and powerful, God chooses the one with the **greatest balance of
variety and order** (§58 — "as great variety as possible, along with the greatest
possible order"). Suffering and evil are not denied; they are the unavoidable cost
of having free rational creatures and law-governed nature. A world without any
suffering would require either no freedom or no stable laws — and the loss of
those would cost more total good than the suffering does. Leibniz distinguishes:
**metaphysical evil** (imperfection, the gap between creature and God),
**physical evil** (suffering), and **moral evil** (sin) — all three exist in the
best world because they are the price of its goodness.

**Why the caricature is a caricature:** Pangloss argues moral complacency — "do
nothing, all is already fine." Leibniz's theodicy is about **explanation**
(why is there evil in a world made by a good God?) not **prescription** (don't
try to reduce suffering). The read must frame Pangloss as the caricature, state
Leibniz's actual position fairly, then note that the caricature has been culturally
dominant since 1759. — ✅ CONFIRMED as the scholarly consensus framing
(Britannica; Wikipedia "Best of all possible worlds"; scholarship via "Is Pangloss
Leibniz?")

## §7 — THE LEIBNIZ–NEWTON CALCULUS DISPUTE — NOTE FOR THIS WORK READ

The calculus dispute is **fully documented in the thinker ledger** (section H)
and need NOT be repeated in the work read. Its only relevance to the *Monadology*
is biographical/contextual: by 1714 (when the *Monadology* was written), the
dispute was already bitter — the Royal Society's rigged *Commercium Epistolicum*
had appeared in 1712, and Leibniz's reputation in England was already damaged.
The *Monadology* was written partly as an act of philosophical legacy-building by
a man who knew he was out of favor and running out of time. This context is worth
a sentence, but the work read should not re-litigate the calculus war — the
thinker page carries that story.

## §9 — UNVERIFIABLE / FLAGGED ITEMS

1. **Exact sequence: *Principles of Nature and Grace* vs *Monadology* — which
   came first?** Some sources say the *Principles* is a "preliminary study" from
   which the *Monadology* was elaborated; others treat them as parallel. The
   most careful scholarly position is that they were written simultaneously and
   the order is not definitively settled. — ⚠️ AUTHOR: say "written in the same
   summer" and leave sequence open.

2. **"Kept it like a jewel in a case" (Prince Eugene).** This anecdote appears
   in Robert Latta's 1898 introduction. It is plausible and reported by Latta, but
   it is not independently corroborated by a primary source in these searches.
   — ⚠️ LEGEND-FRAME-IT: use if cited as Latta's account, not as bare fact.

3. **Whether Leibniz was in Vienna for the whole composition.** The standard
   account says the *Monadology* was completed before he left Vienna in September
   1714; the Latta 1898 introduction and Wikipedia agree. — ✅ CONFIRMED for
   practical purposes; exact composition timeline within 1714 is not settled.

4. **The phrase "best of all possible worlds" in the *Monadology* itself.**
   Leibniz uses the argument (§§53–55) but not necessarily this exact English
   phrasing in the Latta translation. The phrase is most strongly associated with
   the *Theodicy* (1710). The work read should use §55 (quoted above, Q9) for the
   verbatim claim, and paraphrase the *Theodicy* framing rather than quoting it
   (Huggard 1951 is NOT PD). — ⚠️ CONFIRMED caution from thinker ledger; restated.

5. **Leibniz's own Monadology section references.** Leibniz wrote cross-references
   into the manuscript (footnote numbers pointing to the *Théodicée*). These are
   preserved in some editions but dropped in others. Latta's Wikisource text does
   not reproduce them in the main text. — NOTE for completeness; not a factual
   error risk.
