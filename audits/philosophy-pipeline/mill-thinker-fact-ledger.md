## QUOTES (all PD, Mill in English — no translator)

### Q1 — *On Liberty* (1859), harm principle, Ch. I (Introductory)
> "The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised
> community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or
> moral, is not a sufficient warrant."
- Source: Project Gutenberg, *On Liberty*, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-h/34901-h.htm — Ch. I.
- Verified verbatim (first sentence; the read also paraphrases the "his own good… not a sufficient warrant" continuation, which is verbatim above).

### Q2 — *On Liberty*, Ch. I — individual sovereignty
> "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign."
- Source: same, Ch. I. Verified verbatim.

### Q3 — *On Liberty*, Ch. I — the only freedom that deserves the name
> "The only freedom which deserves the name, is that of pursuing our own good in our own way, so
> long as we do not attempt to deprive others of theirs."
- Source: same, Ch. I. Verified verbatim.

### Q4 — *Utilitarianism* (1861/1863), Ch. II — Greatest Happiness Principle
> "The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness
> Principle, holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as
> they tend to produce the reverse of happiness."
- Source: Project Gutenberg, *Utilitarianism*, https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11224/pg11224.txt — Ch. II. Verified verbatim.

### Q5 — *Utilitarianism*, Ch. II — "doctrine worthy only of swine"
> "a doctrine worthy only of swine, to whom the followers of Epicurus were, at a very early period,
> contemptuously likened"
- Source: same, Ch. II. Verified verbatim. (This is Mill reporting the OBJECTION, not endorsing it.)

### Q6 — *Utilitarianism*, Ch. II — Socrates dissatisfied
> "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates
> dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. And if the fool, or the pig, is of a different opinion, it is
> because they only know their own side of the question."
- Source: same, Ch. II. Verified verbatim. NOTE: it is "**is** of a different opinion" (singular),
  not the commonly-misquoted "are". The read uses "is".

### Q7 — *Utilitarianism*, Ch. II — competent judges / higher pleasure test
> "Of two pleasures, if there be one to which all or almost all who have experience of both give a
> decided preference, irrespective of any feeling of moral obligation to prefer it, that is the more
> desirable pleasure."
- Source: same, Ch. II. Verified verbatim.

### Q8 — *Autobiography* (1873), Ch. V — the question that broke him
> "Suppose that all your objects in life were realized; that all the changes in institutions and
> opinions which you are looking forward to, could be completely effected at this very instant:
> would this be a great joy and happiness to you?" And an irrepressible self-consciousness
> distinctly answered, "No!"
- Source: Wikisource, *Autobiography* Ch. V, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Autobiography_(Mill)/Chapter_V ; cross-checked utilitarianism.com/millauto/five.html. Verified verbatim.

### Q9 — *Autobiography*, Ch. V — the collapse
> "the whole foundation on which my life was constructed fell down"
- Source: same Ch. V. Verified verbatim. (Read also paraphrases "I seemed to have nothing left to
  live for," which is verbatim in Ch. V.)

### Q10 — *Autobiography*, Ch. V — Wordsworth
> "a medicine for my state of mind"
> "the very culture of the feelings, which I was in quest of"
- Source: same Ch. V. Both verified verbatim. Wordsworth's poems expressed "states of feeling, and
  of thought coloured by feeling, under the excitement of beauty" (verbatim, used as paraphrase in read).

### Q11 — *Autobiography* — tribute to Harriet Taylor on *On Liberty*
> "The 'Liberty' was more directly and literally our joint production than anything else which bears
> my name, for there was not a sentence of it that was not several times gone through by us
> together, turned over in many ways, and carefully weeded of any faults, either in thought or
> expression, that we detected in it."
- Source: *Autobiography* Ch. VII; widely reproduced (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Harriet
  Taylor Mill," https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/harriet-mill/ ). Verified verbatim.

### Q12 — *The Subjection of Women* (1869), Ch. I — the thesis
> "That the principle which regulates the existing social relations between the two sexes—the legal
> subordination of one sex to the other—is wrong in itself, and now one of the chief hindrances to
> human improvement; and that it ought to be replaced by a principle of perfect equality, admitting
> no power or privilege on the one side, nor disability on the other."
- Source: Project Gutenberg, *The Subjection of Women*, https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/27083/pg27083.txt — opening of Ch. I. Verified verbatim.
- NOTE: original uses em-dashes; this appears ONLY inside the quoted epigraph text (allowed by brief).

### Q13 — *The Subjection of Women*, Ch. I — wife as bond-servant
> "the wife is the actual bond-servant of her husband: no less so, as far as legal obligation goes,
> than slaves commonly so called."
- Source: same. Verified verbatim. Used as the worked slavery-analogy passage.

### Q14 — *The Subjection of Women*, Ch. I — solitary relic
> "a single relic of an old world"
- Source: same (full phrase: "a solitary breach of what has become their fundamental law; a single
  relic of an old world of thought and practice"). Verified verbatim.

### Q15 — *Autobiography* — the MP amendment
> "perhaps the only really important public service I performed in the capacity of a Member of
> Parliament"
- Source: *Autobiography* Ch. VII, quoted widely (History of Parliament,
  https://victoriancommons.wordpress.com/2017/05/19/ ). Mill's own phrasing of his women's-suffrage
  amendment. Verified verbatim (Mill's words about the 1867 amendment).

## BIOGRAPHY / DATES

- Born **20 May 1806**, Pentonville, London. Died **7 May 1873**, Avignon, France. (Wikipedia,
  John Stuart Mill; SEP.) — read uses 7 May 1873.
- Father **James Mill** (1773–1836), Scottish historian/economist, close associate of Jeremy
  Bentham. Designed JSM's education.
- Forced education: **began Greek at age 3** (Aesop, then Herodotus, Xenophon, Plato in the
  original); **Latin at about 8**; logic, political economy (Ricardo) in his early teens. He served
  as tutor to his younger siblings. (Autobiography Ch. I–II; SEP.) — read states Greek at 3, Latin
  ~8, Plato as a boy.
- As a teenager Mill read **Bentham** (Dumont's French redaction *Traité de législation*) and
  described it as giving his life an object, a creed, a religion — the conversion to utilitarianism.
  (Autobiography Ch. III.)
- **Mental crisis: autumn 1826, age 20.** (Autobiography Ch. V, "A Crisis in My Mental History.")
- Recovery via poetry, esp. **Wordsworth** (read 1828), and the broader "cultivation of the
  feelings." (Autobiography Ch. V.)
- Met **Harriet Taylor (née Hardy, 1807–1858)** in **1830**; she was married to John Taylor. Long
  intimate intellectual partnership. John Taylor died **1849**; JSM and Harriet married **1851**.
  Harriet died of respiratory disease at **Avignon, 1858**; Mill bought a house near her grave and
  spent much of his remaining life there.
- Harriet's own published essay: **"The Enfranchisement of Women" (1851)**, *Westminster Review*
  (long attributed to Mill, now credited to her). (SEP, Harriet Taylor Mill.) — read names it and
  credits her.
- **Career:** worked for the **East India Company** (Examiner's Office) from 1823 until the Company
  was wound up in 1858. NOT an academic. (SEP.) — read notes the day job.
- **Works/dates:** *A System of Logic* (1843); *Principles of Political Economy* (1848); **On
  Liberty (1859)**, published the year after Harriet's death and dedicated to her; **Utilitarianism**
  (serialized in *Fraser's Magazine* 1861, published as a book 1863); *Considerations on
  Representative Government* (1861); **The Subjection of Women** (written 1861, published 1869);
  *Autobiography* (published posthumously 1873).
- **Member of Parliament for Westminster, 1865–1868.** On **20 May 1867** he moved an amendment to
  the Second Reform Bill to replace "man" with "person," which would have enfranchised women on the
  same property terms as men. Defeated; the division was **196 (or 194) to 73 (or 75)** — sources
  vary slightly on the exact tally, so the read says "73 members" voted for it and notes it was
  defeated, without over-precising the losing count. First such vote in Parliament.
  (History of Parliament; Victorian Commons.) — read states 73 in favour, defeated.
- Mill presented the **first mass women's suffrage petition** to the Commons on **7 June 1866**
  (brought by Emily Davies and Elizabeth Garrett; ~1,500 signatures). — read mentions the petition.

## IDEAS / FRAMING (landmine-aware)

- **Utilitarianism** defined plainly: rightness measured by tendency to promote happiness
  (pleasure and the absence of pain). Distinguished from the "ends justify any means" cartoon — the
  read keeps Mill's own guardrails (higher pleasures, the harm principle, individual liberty,
  rights) central, per brief.
- **Higher vs lower pleasures**: Mill's amendment to Bentham — pleasures differ in **quality**, not
  just quantity; the test is the verdict of those who have experienced both (competent judges). This
  is the answer to the "doctrine worthy of swine" charge. Bentham had held all pleasures
  commensurable ("quantity of pleasure being equal, push-pin is as good as poetry" — Bentham's
  push-pin line is genuine, from *The Rationale of Reward*; the read attributes the push-pin
  doctrine to Bentham as the view Mill broke from). Verified: Bentham did write that push-pin/poetry
  equivalence (Rationale of Reward, Bk III).
- **Harm principle / On Liberty**: liberty of the individual limited only by harm to others;
  self-regarding conduct is sovereign. Worked on a real-type case in the read (an adult's private
  self-harming choice vs. an action that injures another). Credited as shaped WITH Harriet Taylor
  (Q11), per brief — genuine partner, not footnote.
- **The Subjection of Women**: equality of the sexes; legal subordination as a relic; the marriage-
  as-bondage analogy (Q13). Mill the practicing legislator (the 1867 amendment) ties idea to act.
- **Representative government**: Mill defended representative democracy as the best form while
  worrying about the "tyranny of the majority" (a phrase he uses in *On Liberty*, drawn partly from
  Tocqueville, whom Mill reviewed — verified Mill uses "tyranny of the majority"). Read uses it.

## BREAK BLOCK
- FROM: **Bentham's** hedonic calculus — pleasures differ only in quantity, all commensurable,
  push-pin equals poetry; the individual is a unit in an aggregate sum.
- TO: **Mill's** revised utilitarianism — qualitatively higher pleasures, an inviolable sphere of
  individual liberty (the harm principle), and something functioning like rights — a utilitarianism
  with a conscience about the individual.
