## 1. Third Critique section (Ch6) — *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790)

**Claims checked:**
- Publication year 1790 — CONFIRMED. Universally documented.
- The beautiful as "purposiveness without purpose" — CONFIRMED. This is Kant's own phrase (*Zweckmäßigkeit ohne Zweck*), verified against the SEP entry on Kant's Aesthetics and Routledge Encyclopedia. The draft's prose description ("looks as if it were designed to please you... yet it serves no actual function") is an accurate, un-overstated gloss.
- The sublime: night sky / storm example — CONFIRMED with minor flag. The draft names "a vast mountain, or a violent storm at sea, or under a night sky of 'worlds upon worlds'" as examples. The storm-at-sea and mountain are canonical Kantian examples of the dynamic and mathematical sublime. The "night sky / worlds upon worlds" language the draft uses is drawn directly from the *Critique of Practical Reason* Conclusion (verified Abbott text, §3.D of fact pack), not directly from the Third Critique sublime section — but using it here is defensible and editorially consistent (the draft explicitly cross-references the tombstone line). NOT an error; editorial choice confirmed as intentional.
- Teleological judgment as a "regulative / as-if" principle — CONFIRMED. Both the fact pack and independent sources (Wiley EJP 2019; ResearchGate; Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews) confirm that Kant's teleological judgment is explicitly a regulative, "as if" principle, not a constitutive claim about actual design. The draft's wording ("a **regulative** principle — a working rule the mind adopts and reasons *as if* it were true, without claiming it *is* true") is accurate and precisely scoped.
- Third Critique bridges nature (1st Critique) and freedom (2nd) — CONFIRMED. This is a standard summary of the work's function, confirmed by SEP, Routledge, and Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews. The draft's "incalculable gulf" language is Kant's own phrase (used by Kant in the Third Critique introduction); the draft handles this correctly.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED across all four sub-claims.** No overstating detected.

## 2. Postulates of practical reason (Ch5)

**Claims checked:**
- Three postulates: freedom, immortality, God — CONFIRMED. The standard scholarly listing (SEP, IEP, Cambridge Core, Gutenberg text of the *Critique of Practical Reason*) is exactly: (1) freedom, (2) immortality of the soul, (3) existence of God. The draft lists them in this order and explains each. Correct.
- "Abolish knowledge to make room for belief/faith" makes room for these — CONFIRMED. The connection is exactly Kant's own framing in the B-preface. The fact pack verified the Abbott wording of the "abolish knowledge" quote; the draft uses it correctly. The postulates are what the cleared room is for.
- Explanation of each postulate — CONFIRMED accurate and not overstated:
  - Freedom: "ought implies can" — morality requires the agent be free. Correct.
  - Immortality: perfection of virtue requires endless progress, which requires the soul to persist. Correct.
  - God: virtue must deserve happiness; nature doesn't guarantee this; a power adequate to align them must be postulated. Correct.
- Kant "did not prove God exists or the soul survives" — CONFIRMED. The draft is explicit that these are postulates, not proofs, and that "no such proof is possible." This accurately reflects Kant's own carefully limited claim.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED. Three postulates correctly named and correctly explained. The "believe vs. know" framing is accurate.**

## 3. *What Is Enlightenment?* / *Sapere aude* (1784)

**Claims checked:**

### 3a. Opening quotation wording
The draft quotes: *"Enlightenment is the human being's emergence from his self-incurred minority. Minority is inability to make use of one's own understanding without direction from another."*

**CONFIRMED** as matching the marxists.org text (live-fetched 2026-06-13). The wording is accurate.

### 3b. Translator credit — WRONG (minor, non-display)
The fact pack (§3.F) attributes the marxists.org translation to "Mary C. Smith." The marxists.org page credits the translation to **Mary J. Gregor** (Cambridge University Press, 1996, *Practical Philosophy* volume). These are different people; the fact pack has the wrong first name. The draft itself does not name the translator in display text — it says only "in a standard translation" — so **this error is confined to the fact pack and does not appear in the reader-facing draft**. No correction needed in the draft. The fact pack should note this discrepancy if it is revised.

### 3c. *Sapere aude* translated as "dare to know!" — MINOR FLAG
The draft translates *Sapere aude* as **"dare to know!"** The marxists.org/Gregor translation renders the phrase with the gloss **"[dare to be wise]"** and then "Have courage to make use of your own understanding!" The Wikipedia *Sapere aude* article notes the primary literal meaning is "dare to know," with "dare to be wise" as a looser alternative. Horace scholars typically gloss it as "dare to be wise." The fact pack (§3.F, second paragraph on Horace) is aware of this: it notes Kant uses it as the "motto of enlightenment" and gives "dare to be wise" as Horace's own meaning. The draft uses "dare to know!" — this is not wrong (it is the literal Latin), but it diverges from the Gregor/marxists.org rendering the draft implicitly relies on. This is a nuance, not a factual error. The draft does not over-claim; it just picks the more literal gloss. No correction required; worth noting.

### 3d. Horace attribution — CONFIRMED
The attribution to Horace (*Epistles* I.2.40) is correct and is standard scholarly knowledge. Verified via Wikipedia *Sapere aude* article (Horace, *First Book of Letters*, line 40: *"Dimidium facti, qui coepit, habet; sapere aude, incipe"*). Kant did not coin the phrase; the draft correctly says he "lifts" it from Horace. CONFIRMED.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED on substance. One translator name error (fact pack only, not display text). "Dare to know" vs "dare to be wise" is a translation-choice nuance, not a factual error.**

## 4. Image caption — "Portrait of Kant, c.1790."

**The inline caption reads:** "Portrait of Kant, c.1790 — painted around the close of his great decade, by which time he had become the fixed point every later German philosopher pushed off from. No photographs of Kant exist; every likeness is a painted portrait."

**The hero caption reads:** "Portrait of Immanuel Kant, c.1790. A painting, made in Kant's mid-sixties, the decade he was finishing the three Critiques. No photographs of Kant exist — he died decades before the camera; every likeness is a painted portrait."

**Claim: "c.1790" is a fair description.**

CONFIRMED. The Wikimedia Commons file (`Immanuel_Kant_portrait_c1790.jpg`) is filed as "anonymous painter, circa 1790." The World History Encyclopedia entry for this image also uses "c. 1790." The "c." qualifier is correctly present in both captions. The fact pack (§10) flags the painter as "unknown (possibly Elisabeth von Stägemann, Anton Graff school)" with no confirmed attribution — the captions wisely omit any artist attribution, which is appropriate. Neither caption over-claims.

**One minor check:** "Kant's mid-sixties" — Kant was born 1724; c.1790 puts him at approximately 65–66. "Mid-sixties" is accurate.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED. Fair and accurate. The anonymous-painter status is correctly handled by omitting attribution.**
