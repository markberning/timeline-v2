## CRITICAL ATTRIBUTION LANDMINE (handled)
- **"perfect equality" is NOT in the Enfranchisement essay.** That phrasing is from
  J.S. Mill's later *The Subjection of Women* (1869) ("the principle which regulates
  the existing social relations between the two sexes… ought to be replaced by a
  principle of perfect equality"). The draft NEVER attributes "perfect equality" to
  Taylor's 1851 essay. Her equality language is quoted as-is ("In all things, the
  presumption ought to be on the side of equality"; the "genuine friendship which
  only exists between equals" line). — VERIFIED ABSENT from her essay.
- Her essay vs. Mill's *Subjection*: the read keeps these straight throughout. The
  *Subjection* (1869) is Mill's, published after her death; the *Enfranchisement*
  (1851) is hers. Her essay is MORE radical on (a) the vote and (b) women in paid
  work than Mill's later book. — standard scholarly view.

## A. Biography (CONFIRMED — SEP, Britannica, Wikipedia)

  gives "October 1807"; 8 Oct 1807 is the standard date, Walworth/London). Date given
  as "8 October 1807" — [VERIFY day-precision]: SEP only states "October 1807";
  Wikipedia/most refs give 8 October 1807. Draft uses "October 1807" to be safe.
- First marriage to **John Taylor** (a wholesale druggist / pharmaceuticals
  wholesaler), **14 March 1826**, at eighteen. — CONFIRMED (SEP).
- **Three children** with Taylor: **Herbert** (1827), **Algernon** ("Haji", 1830),
  **Helen** ("Lily", 1831). — CONFIRMED (SEP). Helen later edited Mill's works and was
  herself a suffrage activist.
- Met **John Stuart Mill in 1830**, introduced through the radical Unitarian minister
  **William Johnson Fox** (W.J. Fox), leader of her South Place / Unitarian
  congregation, around whose *Monthly Repository* circle the radicals gathered. They
  fell in love quickly. — CONFIRMED (SEP).
- The long partnership during her first marriage: documented intimate intellectual
  partnership; from **1833** Harriet and John Taylor largely lived apart, Taylor
  "remarkably tolerant" of the relationship (SEP). The arrangement scandalized
  Victorian society. Presented as documented, with the propriety question flagged as
  what contemporaries made of it. — CONFIRMED (SEP).
- **John Taylor died 1849** (rectal cancer, SEP). Harriet married **John Stuart Mill
  in April/spring 1851**. — CONFIRMED (SEP).
- Died **3 November 1858, Avignon, France**, of **respiratory failure** (lung
  congestion; SEP notes tuberculosis), while travelling south for her health. Mill
  bought a house near the cemetery at Saint-Véran and was himself buried beside her
  on his death in 1873. — CONFIRMED (SEP; the house + later burial is standard).

## B. Her work / ideas (CONFIRMED)
- **The Enfranchisement of Women**, *Westminster Review*, **July 1851**, published
  anonymously. Now scholarly consensus = **HER essay** (sole/primary author). Mill
  credited it to her in his *Autobiography* and, reprinting it in his *Dissertations
  and Discussions*, attached a headnote naming his wife as author. Modern editors (Jo
  Ellen Jacobs, *The Complete Works of Harriet Taylor Mill*, 1998; Ann P. & John M.
  Robson, *Sexual Equality*) treat it as hers. — CONFIRMED.
- It was prompted by the **1850 Worcester, Massachusetts National Woman's Rights
  Convention** (the first national US women's rights convention); Taylor quotes and
  endorses its resolutions. — CONFIRMED (the essay is explicitly a response to it).
- An **earlier essay on marriage and divorce (c. 1831–1832)**, exchanged privately
  with Mill (the two wrote paired essays on the subject), unpublished in her lifetime:
  she argued marriage as it stood subordinated women, favored access to divorce, and
  pressed women's economic independence. — CONFIRMED (SEP; the paired marriage essays
  are well documented).
- Credited contribution to Mill's **Principles of Political Economy** (1848),
  especially the chapter **"On the Probable Futurity of the Labouring Classes"** —
  Mill said in the *Autobiography* this chapter was largely due to her. — CONFIRMED
  (standard; Mill's own statement).

## C. The attribution debate (presented FAIRLY — neither inflated nor erased)
- **What Mill himself said** (quoted, verbatim, as HIS claims):
  - *On Liberty* dedication (1859): she was "the inspirer, and in part the author, of
    all that is best in my writings"; "Like all that I have written for many years, it
    belongs as much to her as to me." — VERIFIED VERBATIM (Gutenberg #34901).
  - *Autobiography*: *On Liberty* was "more directly and literally our joint
    production than anything else which bears my name, for there was not a sentence of
    it that was not several times gone through by us together…" — VERIFIED VERBATIM
    (widely reproduced; matches the Toronto/CW text).
  - *Autobiography*: "The whole mode of thinking of which the book was the expression,
    was emphatically hers." — VERIFIED VERBATIM.
- **The scholarly spread** (named, fair):
  - SEP characterizes the documentary evidence as **"equivocal"**: Mill's claims range
    from the "joint production" language to leaving her out of his formal bibliography
    of his writings; stylometric work suggests her hand in several passages but stays
    speculative. — CONFIRMED (SEP, quoted phrase "equivocal").
  - **Skeptical pole**: many biographers long held Mill exaggerated her role out of
    love and grief (the older 20th-c. consensus, e.g. as represented in earlier
    treatments). Presented as the deflationary reading. — standard.
  - **Recovery pole**: **Friedrich Hayek**, *John Stuart Mill and Harriet Taylor:
    Their Correspondence and Subsequent Marriage* (1951), reconstructed the
    relationship from the letters and took her intellectual partnership seriously;
    **Jo Ellen Jacobs** (editor of her *Complete Works*) argues she has been
    systematically underestimated. — CONFIRMED (Hayek 1951; Jacobs 1998).
  - The read's verdict: the *Enfranchisement* is the firm ground (clearly hers, and
    can be judged on its own); the joint-authorship of *On Liberty* etc. is the
    genuinely contested ground, where Mill's own generous testimony and the gaps in
    the record both stand. Neither inflated nor erased. — matches brief + SEP.

## D. Verified QUOTES — The Enfranchisement of Women (1851), HER essay, PD
Source: archive.org `enfranchisemento00mill` (1868 reprint of the 1851 essay) +
Gutenberg #73404. Period American spelling ("labors", "coëqual", "favor") preserved
exactly as printed in the reprint quoted.

1. **The question itself**: "This question is, the enfranchisement of women; their
   admission, in law, and in fact, to equality in all rights, political, civil and
   social, with the male citizens of the community." — VERIFIED.
2. **The convention resolution on suffrage** (she transcribes + endorses): "_Resolved_,
   That women are entitled to the right of suffrage, and to be considered eligible to
   office…" — VERIFIED. (Draft attributes this as the resolution she quotes and backs,
   not as her own sentence.)
3. **Her own claim on the franchise**: "That women have as good a claim as men have, in
   point of personal right, to the suffrage, or to a place in the jury-box, it would be
   difficult for any one to deny." — VERIFIED.
4. **Universal suffrage that excludes half**: "For with what truth or rationality could
   the suffrage be termed universal, while half the human species remain excluded from
   it?" — VERIFIED.
5. **Partnership in industry** (the demand): "Partnership in the labors and gains, risks
   and remunerations, of productive industry." — VERIFIED. (Spelling note: British
   editions print "labours"; the reprint quoted prints "labors". Quoted as printed.)
6. **Servant to partner**: "the joint income of the two would be the same as before,
   while the woman would be raised from the position of a servant to that of a partner."
   — VERIFIED.
7. **Tyranny to shut out half the competitors**: "But so long as competition is the
   general law of human life, it is tyranny to shut out one half of the competitors. All
   who have attained the age of self-government have an equal claim to be permitted to
   sell whatever kind of useful labor they are capable of, for the price which it will
   bring." — VERIFIED.
8. **The "proper sphere" rejected**: "We deny the right of any portion of the species to
   decide for another portion, or any individual for another individual, what is and
   what is not their 'proper sphere.' The proper sphere for all human beings is the
   largest and highest which they are able to attain to. What this is, cannot be
   ascertained without complete liberty of choice." — VERIFIED VERBATIM (archive.org OCR
   lines 502–507).
9. **Wives only for want of a career**: "Numbers of women are wives and mothers only
   because there is no other career open to them, no other occupation for their feelings
   or their activities." — VERIFIED.
10. **A mere appendage**: "why each woman should be a mere appendage to a man, allowed to
    have no interests of her own… the only reason which can be given is, that men like
    it." — VERIFIED. (Ellipsis joins printed clauses; quoted only as the bracketed
    phrases, not stitched into one false sentence.)
11. **Presumption on the side of equality**: "In all things, the presumption ought to be
    on the side of equality." — VERIFIED.
12. **Friendship of equals**: "The highest order of durable and happy attachments would
    be a hundred times more frequent than they are, if the affection which the two sexes
    sought from one another were that genuine friendship which only exists between equals
    in privileges as in faculties." — VERIFIED.
13. **Careers open to all by trial**: "Let every occupation be open to all, without favor
    or discouragement to any, and employments will fall into the hands of those men or
    women who are found by experience to be most capable of worthily exercising them…
    Each individual will prove his or her capacities, in the only way in which capacities
    can be proved, by trial." — VERIFIED VERBATIM (archive.org OCR lines 519–527).
14. **Prohibition manufactures the incapacity**: "to interfere beforehand by an arbitrary
    limit, and declare that whatever be the genius, talent, energy, or force of mind, of
    an individual of a certain sex or class, those faculties shall not be exerted… is
    not only an injustice to the individual, and a detriment to society, which loses what
    it can ill spare, but is also the most effectual mode of providing that, in the sex
    or class so fettered, the qualities which are not permitted to be exercised shall not
    exist." — VERIFIED VERBATIM (archive.org OCR lines 528–537).
15. **Custom, the formidable obstacle**: "The chief of these obstacles is that most
    formidable one — custom." — VERIFIED (sense; the OCR prints it as "that most
    formidable one—custom"; draft renders without em-dash where in narration, keeps it
    inside the quote).
16. **Subjection rests on physical force**: "There is no difficulty in understanding why
    the subjection of women has been a custom. No other explanation is needed than
    physical force." — VERIFIED.
17. **If the preference were natural, no need to enforce it**: "for, if the preference be
    natural, there can be no necessity for enforcing it by law." — VERIFIED.
