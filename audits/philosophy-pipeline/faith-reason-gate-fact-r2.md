### Tag 2 — Ch 4 (Maimonides): The Maimonidean controversy as a 13th-century internal Jewish dispute over Maimonides' rationalism

**R2 text (lines 509–513):**
> "Maimonides was disputed within Judaism for a generation after his death, the
> 'Maimonidean controversy,' a fight over whether his rationalism was compatible
> with traditional practice that was as sharp as anything in the Latin schools."

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

**Sources:** Wikipedia — Maimonidean controversy; SEP entry on Maimonides.

The dispute was real, prolonged, and genuinely bitter. The acute phase was the
1230s (Provence), but the full arc runs late 12th into early 14th century. The
opposing camps issued reciprocal bans; in 1232 French rabbis denounced
Maimonides' philosophical works to the Dominican Inquisition, which burned them.
The "as sharp as anything in the Latin schools" comparison is defensible — the
Maimonidean controversy involved community bans and book-burning that matched the
intensity of the Sens and 1277 proceedings.

**One precision note:** the r2 text says "a generation after his death" (he died
1204); the acute dispute is the 1230s, which is two to three decades — the
characterization "a generation" is loose but not wrong at era altitude.

**No change required.**

### Tag 3 — Ch 5 (Bernard of Clairvaux): his affective-mystical theology and his specific charge that Abelard's dialectical treatment of the Trinity bred heresy

**R2 text (lines 681–692):**
> "Bernard held that theology is not a debating exercise to be staged in front of
> a crowd of students, that the mystery of God exceeds what the dialectical method
> can hold, and that Abelard's public syllogizing of doctrines like the Trinity
> was exactly the kind of categorical mistake that breeds heresy. Behind that
> negative judgment sat a positive theology of his own: God is reached through

> Bernard spent his life writing..."

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

**Sources:** SEP — Bernard of Clairvaux; Wikipedia — Bernard of Clairvaux;

All four components check out:

  theology, centered in *De diligendo Deo* (On Loving God) and his 86 *Sermons on

- *Charge about the Trinity*: confirmed as his flagship indictment. Bernard drew

  treatment of the Trinity verged on Arianism/Sabellianism.
- *Drove the case at Sens 1141*: confirmed; Bernard organized the council and
  presented the charges.
- *Dialectic as pride / mystery exceeds method*: confirmed as Bernard's stated
  position, not a modern gloss.

### Tag 5 — Ch 6 (1277 articles): the condemnation includes the eternity of the world and celestial determinism drawn from Aristotle directly; Boethius of Dacia named as target alongside Siger of Brabant

**R2 text (lines 828–832):**
> "understand what was actually being condemned: not stray provocations but
> teachings out of Aristotle himself, the eternity of the world above all (that
> the universe never had a beginning, the same thesis al-Ghazālī had branded
> unbelief two centuries earlier, Chapter 3) and the determining grip of the
> heavens on events below... And understand what was actually being condemned...
> men like Siger of Brabant and Boethius of Dacia..."

**Sub-claim 5a — eternity of the world: ✅ CONFIRMED**

Proposition 87 of the 1277 condemnation explicitly condemns the doctrine that the
world is eternal; the content is drawn from Aristotle's *Physics* and *De Caelo*.

**Sub-claim 5b — celestial determinism: ✅ CONFIRMED**

Multiple articles condemn astrological determinism and the doctrine that celestial
bodies govern terrestrial events, content traceable to Aristotle and his Arabic
commentators.

**Sub-claim 5c — "Boethius of Dacia named as target alongside Siger of Brabant": ❌ WRONG — MUST-FIX**

**Sources:** Wikipedia — Condemnations of 1277; SEP — Condemnations at Paris;
R. Hissette, *Enquête sur les 219 articles condamnés à Paris le 7 mars 1277*.

The condemnation document names **no individuals**. Bishop Tempier's preamble
referred only to "certain scholars at the arts faculty." Siger of Brabant and
Boethius of Dacia appear in marginal rubrics added to some manuscripts, and
modern historians identify them as the principal intended targets (about 79 of
219 propositions have been traced to known works), but neither was named in the
original document.

**Fix:** The r2 text currently reads "men like Siger of Brabant and Boethius of
Dacia" — the introductory "men like" already softens this somewhat. However, the
surrounding context ("the targets, by tradition...") from the pack's own framing
handles this correctly (pack §2: "named by tradition, not in the document"). The
r2 text dropped that hedge. Replace:

> "the targets, by tradition, were arts-faculty masters... men like Siger of
> Brabant and Boethius of Dacia"

with:

> "the principal targets, identified by later historians though named in no
> surviving document, were arts-faculty masters such as Siger of Brabant and
> Boethius of Dacia"

Or restore the r1 pack's phrasing ("named by tradition, not in the document").

### Tag 6 — Ch 7 (Scotus/1277): the 1277 condemnation's aftermath opened space for Scotus's more cautious, will-centered metaphysics

**R2 text (lines 853–856):**
> "Part of what opened the road for him was the 1277 condemnation itself: with
> Aristotle's confident reach under official suspicion, there was room for a more
> cautious, will-centered metaphysics, and Scotus, a Franciscan, was the one who
> built it."

**Verdict: 🟡 NEEDS-REFRAME — SHOULD-FIX**

**Sources:** Wikipedia — Condemnations of 1210–1277; SEP — Duns Scotus.

The causal link between 1277 and Scotus is made by historians and is defensible.
However, the word "cautious" is misleading in the context of "will-centered
metaphysics." Scotus's voluntarism — stressing God's radical freedom and the
primacy of will — was not a more restrained position than Aquinas's; it was in
some ways bolder. What the condemnations made dangerous was Aristotelian
necessitarianism (the thesis that things must be as they are, that the world is
eternal, etc.), not bold claims about God's will. Scotus moved away from
necessity, not toward timidity.

Additionally, Franciscan voluntarism predated 1277 (Bonaventure), so the
condemnations accelerated a pre-existing tendency.

**Fix:** Change "a more cautious, will-centered metaphysics" to "a more
will-centered, less necessitarian metaphysics." The word "cautious" carries the
wrong connotation; "less necessitarian" or "voluntarist" is accurate.

Suggested r2 replacement:
> "with Aristotle's confident reach under official suspicion, there was room for
> a will-centered, less necessitarian metaphysics, and Scotus, a Franciscan, was
> the one who built it."

### Tag 7 — Ch 7 (Scotus univocity): Scotus's univocity of being and Aquinas's contrasting doctrine of analogical predication; and that univocity is what lets Ockham apply uniform logical tools to God

**R2 text (lines 862–871):**
> "he argued that the word 'being' means *the same thing* whether you say it of
> God or of a creature, the doctrine called the univocity of being, that we can
> reason about God and creatures using exactly the same logical concepts. That
> sounds technical, and it is a loaded gun, because Aquinas had denied exactly
> this: for Aquinas you could only speak of God by *analogy*, stretching creaturely
> words toward a God who exceeds them, so God always stayed partly out of logic's
> reach. Scotus pulls God back inside the same logical net as everything else,
> which is precisely what lets the next man run the same ruthless logic on God and
> the world without special pleading."

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED (with one precision note on Ockham)**

**Sources:** SEP — Duns Scotus (§3 Univocity); SEP — Analogy in Medieval Philosophy;
SEP — William of Ockham.

- Scotus's univocity of being: confirmed as his central metaphysical innovation,
  exactly as described.
- Aquinas's analogical predication: confirmed; Aquinas held that terms applied to
  God and creatures are neither univocal (exactly the same) nor equivocal (entirely
  different) but analogical.
- The Scotus → Ockham link: the directional claim is confirmed by SEP. One
  precision note: Ockham did not simply adopt Scotus's univocity — he used the
  univocity framework while dissolving Scotus's realism about common natures (for
  Ockham there are no natures, only individuals, so the univocity operates at the
  concept level). But this internal complication is below era altitude, and the
  r2 text's claim ("what lets the next man run the same ruthless logic") is
  directionally accurate.

**No change required.**

### Tag 8 — Ch 7 (via moderna): Buridan as a leading via moderna logician, and Luther's training under nominalist masters at Erfurt

**R2 text (lines 968–971):**
> "Ockham's nominalism did not vanish with him. It spread through the universities
> as the *via moderna*, 'the modern way,' carried by logicians like Jean Buridan,
> and it became the air a whole generation of students breathed, including the
> young friar Martin Luther, who trained under nominalist masters at Erfurt before
> he broke the Western Church in two."

**Sub-claim 8a — Buridan as a leading via moderna logician: ❌ WRONG framing — MUST-FIX**

**Sources:** SEP — Jean Buridan; Wikipedia — Jean Buridan; Wikipedia — Via moderna.

Jean Buridan (c. 1301 – c. 1360) was **a near-exact contemporary of Ockham**
(c. 1287–1347), not a generation after him. Ockham was roughly 14 years older;
they entirely overlap. The r2 text says the via moderna was "carried by logicians
like Jean Buridan" as if Buridan came after Ockham and transmitted his legacy —
but Buridan was actually a contemporary, working in Paris while Ockham was in
Oxford and Avignon. Buridan's via moderna role is real (he was the leading Parisian
nominalist), but framing him as a transmitter of Ockham's nominalism implies a
generational succession that is false.

**Fix:** Change to frame Buridan as a contemporary carrier, not a transmitter.
Suggested replacement:
> "It spread through the universities as the *via moderna*, 'the modern way,'
> with logicians like Jean Buridan — Ockham's Parisian contemporary — leading the
> nominalist current at the heart of the university world,"

Or, if the sentence needs to name only one figure and the succession problem is
hard to solve in one clause, drop Buridan's name and write:
> "It spread through the universities as the *via moderna*, 'the modern way,'
> carried forward by the nominalist current at the university's heart,"

then add Luther. The pack's §2 close notes Ockham hands to era 3 via the via
moderna; the key fact for the reader is that the current reached Luther, not who
carried it.

**Sub-claim 8b — Luther at Erfurt under nominalist masters: ✅ CONFIRMED**

**Sources:** SEP — Luther; Wikipedia — Martin Luther (education); Wikipedia —
Erfurt University.

Luther's Erfurt professors Jodocus Trutfetter and Bartholomaeus Arnoldi of Usingen
were confirmed via moderna followers; Erfurt was a leading center of the nominalist
"Ockham renaissance." Luther later spoke of their influence ambivalently ("hoggish
theologians"), but the formative role of nominalist training is documented and not
in dispute among scholars.

### Augustine privation-of-evil worked paragraph (Ch 1, lines 116–143)

**New material:** The expanded Manichaean-rival → privation move, including the
"dent, not the car" dinner-table line.

**Check against Augustine's actual position:**

The r2 account accurately reflects Augustine's anti-Manichaean privation theory
of evil. Augustine develops evil-as-privation-of-good throughout the *Confessions*
Book VII (where he describes reading the Platonists and finding the solution) and
in the *Enchiridion* chapters 10–14. The Manichaean account (evil as a dark
co-equal substance) is accurately named as the rival view Augustine had held.
The r2 formulation — "evil is not a *thing* at all. It is a lack, a privation,
the way darkness is not a substance pumped into a room but only the absence of
light, and a hole in your sock is not made of anything" — is a fair and accurate
paraphrase of the position. The "dent, not the car" line is the author's own
illustration, not attributed to Augustine. ✅

**One small check:** The r2 text says Augustine "had spent years inside a third
answer, the one the Manichaeans sold" — the *Confessions* records he was a
Manichaean auditor for "nine years" (Book IV). The r2's "years" is accurate.

**The Augustine privation paragraphs: ✅ CONFIRMED — no issues.**

### al-Ghazālī → Avicenna occasionalism paragraph (Ch 3, lines 400–411)

**New material (B2 addition):** The paragraph showing al-Ghazālī's occasionalism
frays Avicenna's own Necessary Existent argument.

**R2 text:** "Avicenna had reasoned his way to God along a chain of causes: every
borrowed existence points up to a lender, and the chain must end in the Necessary
Existent. But that whole argument leans on causes *necessitating* their effects,
on the links in the chain being real and binding. Pull the necessity out of cause
and effect, as al-Ghazālī just did, and you have not only embarrassed the
physicists, you have loosened the bolts on Avicenna's own road to God."

**Check:** Is this philosophically accurate? Does al-Ghazālī's occasionalism
actually undercut Avicenna's chain-of-causes argument for the Necessary Existent?

Yes. Avicenna's argument from contingency to the Necessary Existent relies on a
chain of causally necessary connections: contingent things exist because they
receive existence from something else, that something must also receive it or
ground it, and the chain terminates in a being whose essence is existence (the
Necessary Existent). If al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is correct — that no causal
link between created things is necessary, that God creates the burning alongside
the fire as a matter of habit not necessity — then the causal chain that Avicenna
builds his proof on is not binding, and the argument from necessity is undercut.
This is a recognized point in the secondary literature on the relationship between
al-Ghazālī and Avicenna.

**The al-Ghazālī → Avicenna occasionalism paragraphs: ✅ CONFIRMED — philosophically accurate.**
