## 1. Vitals — birth, family, formation, conversion, death

**Birth:** 13 November 354 (exact date known — he records it), in **Thagaste**
(modern Souk Ahras, Algeria), a small Roman city in the province of Numidia,
Roman North Africa. `VERIFIED` via SEP "Augustine of Hippo" (fetched 2026-06-13).

**Family:**
- Mother: **Monica** (c. 331–387), a devout Christian — the *Confessions* paints her
  as the book's second protagonist, her prayers the rain that eventually soaks the
  reluctant son. She appears at his side at Ostia (the "Ostia vision" in *Confessions*
  IX), where they share a mystical experience just before her death.
- Father: **Patricius**, a small landowner, pagan (baptized only on his deathbed
  c. 371). Not a figure of drama — Augustine gives him few lines.

**Education:**
- Schooled in Thagaste and Madauros (grammar and basic rhetoric), then at
  **Carthage** (North Africa's metropolis, across the Mediterranean from modern
  Tunisia) for advanced rhetoric, arriving c. 370–371.
- In Carthage, around 372–373, he read **Cicero's *Hortensius*** (a now-lost
  exhortation to philosophy) — *the* intellectual spark: "it changed my affections,
  and turned my prayers to Thyself, O Lord" (*Confessions* III.4, Pusey — PARAPHRASE
  of a VERIFIED passage, the exact wording of that line not independently re-checked
  here; the fact of Cicero as the spark is DOCUMENTED in the Confessions and
  unremarkable in the literature).

**The concubine and Adeodatus:**
- Around age 17–18, in Carthage, Augustine took a **concubine** (a socially
  recognized but non-marriage union). He lived with her for approximately **14–15
  years** in "a monogamous union" (SEP's wording — DOCUMENTED). She is **never named
  in any ancient source**, including the *Confessions* itself; she is unnamed in the
  SEP, in Wikipedia, and in all standard modern scholarship. Augustine writes of
  "the woman I was wont to share my bed" and of his grief when she was sent away, but
  never names her. Do NOT supply a name; the silence is the fact.
- She bore him his son **Adeodatus** (Latin: "given by God") — born c. 372–373.
  Adeodatus was baptized alongside Augustine in Milan at Easter 387 and died not long
  after, around 389–390, aged approximately 17–18. Augustine wrote a philosophical
  dialogue, *De Magistro* (*On the Teacher*, 389), dedicated to and featuring
  Adeodatus — a remarkable paternal document.
- When Augustine became engaged to a respectable heiress (arranged by Monica,
  c. 385), the concubine was **sent away** to North Africa. Augustine describes his
  grief: "my heart, which clave to her, was racked, and wounded, and bleeding." He then
  took a second, temporary concubine while waiting for the fiancée to come of age —
  an episode that complicates any sanitizing narrative. He then abandoned the engagement
  altogether at his conversion. `DOCUMENTED` via *Confessions* VI.15.

**Manichaeism (nine years, c. 373–382):**
- After reading Cicero's *Hortensius*, Augustine turned to the **Manichees** — a
  dualist religion teaching that the universe is the battleground of two eternal
  co-equal powers, the Good (Light) and Evil (Darkness). He was a Manichee
  **hearer** (*auditor*) — the lower of two tiers — for approximately **nine years**
  (SEP says "adherence to Manicheism lasted for nine years"; Wikipedia says "about
  nine years," c. 373–382). He never reached the higher rank of "elect."
- **Why it attracted him:** the Manichee answer to the problem of evil. If evil is a
  co-eternal substance, a dark power at war with God, the origins of evil cease to be
  God's problem. The young Augustine, troubled by the evil he saw in himself and the
  world, found this answer satisfying — temporarily.
- **Why he left:** the Manichee teacher Faustus, whom Augustine had hoped would
  answer his growing doubts, turned out to be eloquent but intellectually hollow.
  Augustine's disillusionment with Faustus (narrated in *Confessions* V) was the
  Manichee chapter's end. `DOCUMENTED`.

**From Carthage to Rome to Milan:**
- 383: Augustine moved to **Rome**, partly to escape his mother's surveillance,
  partly for career reasons.
- 384: Appointed professor of rhetoric in **Milan** (the imperial court was then at
  Milan, not Rome — a high-profile post).
- In Milan he came under the influence of **Ambrose** (Bishop of Milan, c. 339–397),
  who taught him the allegorical method of scriptural interpretation — which resolved
  his intellectual objection that scripture was too crude and literal to be taken
  seriously. Augustine attended Ambrose's sermons but initially for rhetorical study,
  not spiritual conversion. `DOCUMENTED` via *Confessions* V–VI; confirmed SEP.

**Neoplatonism and the Platonist books:**
- c. 386, shortly before his conversion, a Milanese friend (identified in the
  secondary literature as **Marius Victorinus's** circle, i.e., thinkers influenced
  by the Christian Neoplatonist Marius Victorinus) gave Augustine "certain books of
  the Platonists, translated from Greek into Latin" (*Confessions* VII.9 — VERIFIED
  below, §3). These were almost certainly works of **Plotinus** (the *Enneads*) and
  possibly **Porphyry** — Neoplatonist texts that taught him: (a) God as incorporeal,
  not a body; (b) the soul as immaterial; (c) evil as privation, not substance.
  Without Plotinus, Augustine could not have broken free of the Manichee worldview.

**The garden conversion, summer 386:**
- In a garden in Milan, Augustine heard what he took to be a child's voice
  chanting **"Take up and read; take up and read"** (*tolle lege, tolle lege*). He
  opened Paul's epistle to the Romans at random and read Romans 13:13–14 ("not in
  rioting and drunkenness...but put on the Lord Jesus Christ"). He describes
  instant conversion (Pusey): "No further would I read; nor needed I: for instantly at
  the end of this sentence, by a light as it were of serenity infused into my heart, all
  the darkness of doubt vanished away." `VERIFIED` (Confessions VIII.12 — see §3 for
  full passage; the "security"/"gloom" form is Pilkington, corrected here to Pusey). **Frame as literary reconstruction**: this account was written
  c. 397, eleven years after the event, by a master rhetorician with literary models
  (Anthony's call in Athanasius's *Life*). As the era-2 pack correctly notes:
  "As Augustine tells it" is the honest frame.

**Baptism and post-conversion:**
- Baptized at **Easter 387** (the night of 24–25 April) by **Ambrose** in Milan.
  Adeodatus baptized at the same time. `DOCUMENTED`.
- His mother Monica died at Ostia (en route back to Africa) in 387.
- Returned to North Africa 388. Founded a small ascetic community at Thagaste.
- **Ordained priest** at Hippo Regius (modern Annaba, Algeria) in **391** —
  apparently against his will (SEP), by popular demand when he came to Hippo looking
  for a site for a monastery.
- **Consecrated bishop of Hippo** c. **396** (SEP: "About five years later [after
  391] he succeeded the local bishop"). Some sources say 395; the SEP prefers c. 396.
  Note: the era-2 pack says "bishop 395" following a common convention; SEP says
  c. 396. Use "c. 395/396" or "around 395–396" to cover both.

**Death:** 28 August **430**, in Hippo, while the city was under **Vandal siege** —
the Vandals (a Germanic people) had crossed from Spain into North Africa and were
working their way along the coast. Hippo fell shortly after. Augustine died of
illness, not violence, in his city's final hours. `DOCUMENTED` via conventional
sources, confirmed SEP. No legend problem on the death itself.

## 2. Major works — dates, form, what they argue

**1. *Confessions* (c. 397–400)**
- Form: not a modern autobiography — thirteen books of **prayer addressed directly
  to God**, excavating Augustine's own memory. The first nine books narrate his life
  to his mother's death (387); books X–XIII turn to philosophical and theological
  reflection on memory, time, and creation.
- What's new in literature: the **first sustained inward autobiography** — the
  reader is inside the consciousness of a person who doesn't know what he's looking
  for. Nothing in ancient literature is like it. Greek and Roman autobiography
  (Caesar, Josephus) records deeds; the *Confessions* records desire and its
  misdirection.
- Load-bearing ideas: the restless heart; the inward turn; time as distension of
  the soul (Book XI); the pear theft as study of motiveless sin; the garden
  conversion.
- The key passage block for the thinker read is here — see §3 for verified wording.

**2. *On the Trinity* (*De Trinitate*, begun 399, completed c. 419–426)**
- Twenty-two books; Augustine's most systematic work.
- First half: the scriptural case for the Trinity (the Christian doctrine that one
  God is three persons: Father, Son, Holy Spirit). Second half: the "psychological
  analogy" — the Trinity is mirrored in the human mind's own triadic structure
  (memory, understanding, will). This is his deepest Neoplatonist synthesis: the soul
  is an image of God, and by examining the soul's structure we approach the Trinity's
  structure.

**3. *City of God* (*De Civitate Dei*, begun **412**, completed **426**)**
- Occasion: the **sack of Rome by the Visigoths in 410**. Pagans blamed Christianity
  (Rome had abandoned its old gods and now the gods had abandoned Rome); Augustine
  spent twenty-two books answering that charge.
- The two-cities doctrine (Book XIV.28, VERIFIED §3): history is the story of
  **two cities formed by two loves** — the Earthly City (love of self, to the
  contempt of God) and the Heavenly City (love of God, to the contempt of self).
  This is NOT church versus state — both cities are intermixed throughout earthly
  history; the Church contains bad members; the Empire can contain good people.
  The drama resolves only at the Last Judgment. This vision of history as a moral
  drama of love became the single most influential philosophy of history in the
  Latin West.

**4. *On Free Choice of the Will* (*De Libero Arbitrio*, begun **388**, completed
   c. **395**)**
- Written partly before, partly after his conversion; the pre-conversion books show
  him working through the problem of evil against the Manichees. His answer: evil
  originates in the **free will** of rational creatures, who can turn from the higher
  good (God) toward the lower good. This will become complicated later (see §4:
  Pelagian controversy).

**5. Anti-Pelagian writings (c. 412–430)**
- Key texts: *On the Spirit and the Letter* (412); *On Nature and Grace* (413–415);
  *On Grace and Free Will* (426–427); *On the Predestination of the Saints* (428–429);
  *On the Gift of Perseverance* (428–429). The last works, unfinished at his death:
  *Against Julian, an Unfinished Work* (*Contra Iulianum opus imperfectum*).
- These represent Augustine's hardest line: human free will is **enslaved** by
  original sin, unable to choose the good without divine grace; grace is freely
  given to some (the predestined elect) and withheld from others, with no injustice
  because all deserve damnation. This is DARKER than the *De Libero Arbitrio*
  position and Augustine knew it — he retroactively qualified his early work.

**6. Other significant works:**
- *On the Teacher* (*De Magistro*, 389) — philosophical dialogue with Adeodatus on
  language and inner illumination.
- *On Christian Doctrine* (*De Doctrina Christiana*, begun 396/7, completed 426/7) —
  his theory of biblical interpretation and rhetoric.
- *Retractions* (*Retractationes*, c. 426–427) — Augustine reviews his own works in
  chronological order, correcting what he later thought wrong. A rare act of
  systematic self-criticism in ancient literature.
- *True Religion* (*De Vera Religione*, 389–391) — contains the famous "noli foras
  ire" passage (§4 below).

### D. *Confessions* XI.14 — time

> "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that
> asketh, I know not."

- Translation: E. B. Pusey (1838).
- sourceUrl: Pusey via CCEL/Gutenberg #3296 (Book XI ch. XIV).
- Status: `VERIFIED` — Pusey wording confirmed in `confessions-work-fact-pack.md` §2-M.
- Note on wording (**corrected 2026-06-13**): the genuine Pusey form is "If no one
  **asks me**, I know: if I wish to explain it to **one that asketh**, I know not." The
  prior wording here ("If no one **ask of me**... **to him who asks**") was the
  **Pilkington/New-Advent** text mislabeled as Pusey. Use the Pusey form above.
- Context: Augustine is setting up his long philosophical treatment of time in Book
  XI. His answer (the "distension of the soul"): the past exists in the mind as
  memory; the future exists as expectation; only the present is real, but it is
  infinitely thin. Time is a stretching (*distentio*) of the mind/soul across past
  and future — a psychological, not cosmological, account of time.

- "A pear tree there was near our vineyard, laden with fruit, tempting neither for
  colour nor taste."
- "For I stole that, of which I had enough, and much better. Nor cared I to enjoy

- "It was foul, and I loved it; I loved to perish, I loved mine own fault, not that

- Translation: E. B. Pusey (1838).

## 4. Ideas as documented positions (not caricature)

### A. The inward turn — "noli foras ire"

The phrase is from *De Vera Religione* (c. 389–391, §39.72 — the standard citation):
*"Noli foras ire, in te ipse redi; in interiore homine habitat veritas."*
Translation: "Do not go abroad; return within yourself; in the inward man
dwells truth."

- Source of Latin: *De Vera Religione* §39.72 — standard scholarly reference,
  consistent across the literature. This is NOT from the *Confessions*; it is a
  slightly earlier philosophical work.
- Status: `PARAPHRASE-ONLY for English` — no verified PD English translation of this
  passage is available in this pack. The Latin is confirmed via the scholarly consensus
  (SEP, Wikipedia, standard Augustine scholarship). The standard English rendering
  above is the scholarly paraphrase; ship in reported speech, not in quote marks.
- Significance: this is the philosophical core of Augustine's epistemology. Truth is
  not found by looking outward at the material world (as the Manichees did) but by
  turning inward — the soul is the place where one finds God, because the soul is made
  in God's image. This connects the Platonic inwardness (Plotinus: to know the One,
  turn inward) with Christian theology. It is the era's founding gesture of
  interiority and arguably the origin of the Western philosophical tradition of
  self-reflection.

### B. Evil as privation (*privatio boni*) — the era's load-bearing worked idea

**The documented position** (against the Manichee dualism he held for nine years):
Evil is not a substance, not a rival power, not a thing God made. It is the
**absence or privation of good** — the way blindness is not a substance added to
an eye but sight missing from it; the way darkness is not stuff pumped into a room
but light absent from it.

The argument runs:
1. Everything that exists was made by God.
2. Everything God made is good (Genesis 1: "God saw that it was good").
3. Therefore everything that exists is good in some degree.
4. Evil is not a thing in its own right; it is what you get when something good is
   corrupted or lacking.
5. To ask "where did evil come from?" is like asking "where did darkness come from?" —
   it is not a something that comes from somewhere; it is a privation.

Verified anchor: *Confessions* VII.12 (Pusey): "That evil, then, which I sought
whence it was, is not any substance; for were it a substance, it would be good."
`VERIFIED` above (§3.F).

The fuller worked argument appears in *Enchiridion* (Handbook on Faith, Hope, and
Love, ch. 10–14), a late work (~421). Key passage: "And in the universe, even that
which is called evil, when it is regulated and put in its proper place, only enhances
our admiration of the good; for we enjoy and value the good more when we compare it
with the evil." `PARAPHRASE-ONLY` — the Enchiridion translation by J. B. Shaw
(1955) is the standard but not PD-era.

**Steelman the objection:** Is a torturer's cruelty really just an *absence* of
good? Augustine's answer: yes — the capacity for love and justice that should be
in the will is absent/corrupted; the cruelty is the privation of right love made
active. It is not a fully satisfying answer to everyone; it has been argued over
ever since. But it was the philosophically available move against Manichee dualism,
and it IS his documented position.

### C. Original sin and the enslaved will

Augustine's mature position (developed against Pelagius, post-412):
- Adam's sin was not merely a bad example; it introduced a **corruption into human
  nature itself**, transmitted biologically through generation (he uses the
  metaphor of a diseased root spreading to the branches).
- After the Fall, the human will is **not free** to choose the good without divine
  grace — it is *liberated* by grace, not inherently free. This is the position
  the *De Libero Arbitrio* (early work) does NOT fully hold; the later Augustine
  explicitly walked back the early work's more generous view.
- This means the human race is, without exception, a "mass of perdition" (*massa
  perditionis* — a phrase Augustine uses; `PARAPHRASE-ONLY` for this exact Latin
  in English context, but the concept is his documented teaching confirmed throughout
  the anti-Pelagian corpus).

### D. Grace, predestination, and the Pelagian controversy

**What Pelagius actually argued (steelman, documented):**
- Pelagius (a British monk, active c. 390–418) held that God cannot command what
  is impossible, and therefore humans must have the ability to obey — i.e., human
  nature, not corrupted by Adam's sin, retains **free will and the capacity not to
  sin**.
- He acknowledged divine grace as a gift that assists the will, but he insisted
  that grace is given in proportion to merit (human effort comes first, divine
  reward follows), and that individual humans can, in principle, achieve sinlessness
  through their own will.
- His follower Julian of Eclanum pushed the position further: original sin does not
  exist as Augustine claims; Augustine's doctrine makes God the author of injustice
  (punishing infants for Adam's sin). This was not a stupid position — it forced
  Augustine to some of his least comfortable formulations.
- Pelagianism was condemned at the **Council of Carthage (418)** and subsequently
  by Pope Zosimus. The controversy involved complex church politics as well as
  theology.

**Augustine's position:**
- Predestination: God **predestines** the elect to salvation before the world began
  (based on God's foreknowledge and sovereign will, not on merited response). The
  rest are justly damned for original sin. This is his late position; he did not
  soften it.
- **Honest framing:** Augustine's predestination doctrine proved the most divisive
  legacy of his theology. The "Semipelagian" controversy (fifth-century monks in
  southern Gaul who argued that humans can at least *begin* to seek God without
  prevenient grace) was resolved at the Second Council of Orange (529), roughly 100
  years after Augustine's death, which settled broadly in his favor.

### E. The coercion of the Donatists — "compel them to come in"

**What happened:** The **Donatists** were a rigorist Christian sect in North Africa
who held that sacraments administered by traditors (clergy who had handed over
scriptures under Diocletian's persecution) were invalid. They formed a separate,
parallel North African church. Augustine spent much of his episcopal career opposing
them.

**The theological innovation with long consequences:** Augustine eventually
justified using imperial coercion against the Donatists, citing Luke 14:23
("compel them to come in" — the great banquet parable). He wrote to the Donatist
bishop Vincentius: "You are of opinion that no one should be compelled to follow
righteousness; and yet you read that the householder said to his servants,
'Whomsoever ye shall find, compel them to come in.'" `DOCUMENTED` via the Donatism
Wikipedia article (fetched 2026-06-13), citing Augustine's letter to Vincentius.

**Honest framing (required by the pipeline's "posthumous-misuse honesty" gate):**
Augustine moved from opposing to supporting state coercion in religion, using a
gospel parable as justification. This argument — that forcing people into the Church
is a form of Christian love, like a father correcting his children — became the
theological foundation later cited to justify medieval inquisitions and forced
conversion. Augustine did not personally oversee torture or execution; his argument
was for exile and fines, not death. But the theological logic he provided was
subsequently extended. Name this honestly; it is documented and important.

### F. The two cities — documented position

Verified in §3.H above. The two-cities doctrine resists two popular
misreadings: (1) it is NOT church = good vs. state = bad; (2) it is NOT a
blueprint for theocracy. The two cities are intermingled in history; individual
members of the institutional Church may belong to the Earthly City; individual
pagans may, by God's grace, belong to the Heavenly City. The division is of
**loves** (orientation of desire), not of institutions.

### G. Time as distension of the soul — *Confessions* XI

Documented position (PARAPHRASE from verified passage context, §3.D):
- Augustine asks: if the past is gone and the future doesn't yet exist, where does
  time exist at all? Answer: in the **mind**. The past exists as **memory**; the
  future exists as **expectation**; the present is the razor-thin "now" of
  **attention**. Time is not an external container; it is the mind's
  *distentio* — its stretching out across the no-longer and the not-yet.
- The **worked example**: try to measure a sound while it is still sounding.
  You cannot measure the past (gone) or the future (not yet here); you must hold
  the sound in memory while it passes, comparing what-you-heard to what-you're-
  hearing. The measurement happens in the mind, not in the sound. Time is that
  mental stretching.
- This is the first philosophical account of time as **psychological** rather than
  cosmological — preceding Kant's view of time as a form of inner intuition by
  thirteen centuries, though it is not the same argument.

### H. The "si fallor, sum" argument — proto-cogito

Documented in §3.J above. The argument appears in *City of God* XI.26: even
if I am being deceived, it takes an existing "I" to be deceived — existence is
secured by the very act of doubting it. This is structurally similar to Descartes'
*cogito ergo sum* (1641) but different in purpose: Augustine is not building an
epistemological foundation for knowledge; he is defending the soul's immateriality.
The **Descartes parallel** should be noted, precisely, as a parallel, not as direct
influence (there is no evidence Descartes was responding to this passage,
and he claimed independent discovery).

## 5. Honest framing — the warts axis

**The abandoned concubine:** Augustine sent away the woman he had lived with for
14–15 years to arrange a marriage his mother had organized — then took a second
temporary concubine while waiting for the fiancée to come of age, then abandoned
the fiancée too at his conversion. He describes grief at losing the first woman but
gives her no name and no agency. She disappears from the text. This is not a footnote
to suppress; it is part of the story's honesty about who he was before the
conversion.

**The hardening on grace and predestination:** The early Augustine (writing *De
Libero Arbitrio* before 395) is more generous about human free will than the later
Augustine (writing the anti-Pelagian works after 412). He was not consistent; he
knew it; he wrote the *Retractationes* partly to acknowledge the shift. The later
positions — that most of humanity is a mass of perdition, predestined to damnation
through no fault they could have avoided — are genuinely disturbing to many
readers, and should be named as his documented position, not softened.

**The coercion of the Donatists:** Covered in §4.E. The "compel them to come in"
argument is documented and has a long, dark afterlife. Name it.

**His framing of women:** The *Confessions* gives Monica a heroic role but treats
the concubine as an object of desire with no interiority. Augustine's theological
anthropology assigns women a full rational soul (made in God's image equally) but
also assigns them a subordinate role in the social order. The *Confessions* is an
honest document of its author's era.

## 6. Lineage — inherited and answered-by

### A. Inherited

- **Cicero's *Hortensius*** (now lost): the intellectual spark (c. 372–373). No
  other ancient author except Scripture is cited as a personal turning point in the
  same way. `DOCUMENTED`.
- **Manichaean thought** (9 years): a formative negative influence — he built his
  privation theory precisely AGAINST the Manichee dualism he had once held. You
  can't understand the privation theory without knowing what it was designed to
  refute.
- **Plotinus / Porphyry (Neoplatonism):** transmitted via Marius Victorinus's Latin
  translations; Augustine explicitly credits "certain books of the Platonists"
  (VERIFIED §3.F) as teaching him incorporeal truth and giving him the privation
  theory. The era-1 pack (§11, r2-verified) establishes the Plotinus → Augustine
  edge as confirmed.
- **Ambrose of Milan:** gave him the allegorical method of scripture, freeing him
  from his objection that the Bible was crude. `DOCUMENTED`.
- **Paul's letters** (especially Romans and Galatians): the tolle lege passage
  (Romans 13:13–14, VERIFIED §3.C) and Paul's theology of grace and the law became
  Augustine's permanent framework.

### B. Answered-by / led to

- **Medieval Latin theology as a whole:** Augustine is the single most-cited
  authority in the medieval Latin Church after scripture itself. Anselm,
  Peter Lombard, Aquinas, Bonaventure — all work within an Augustinian horizon.
  His influence is so pervasive that the era-2 read does not spell it out as a
  single edge because it is every edge.
- **Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109):** Anselm's *crede ut intelligas* ("I believe
  in order to understand") is explicitly Augustine's formula from *De Libero
  Arbitrio* and the *Confessions* — faith seeking understanding. The era-2 read
  correctly frames Anselm as systematizing Augustine's approach.
- **Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274):** heavily indebted to Augustine on grace,
  predestination, and the two-cities idea, though he resolves the faith/reason
  tension more optimistically. Aquinas is in some sense the correction of Augustine's
  epistemological pessimism about human reason.
- **The Reformation:**
  - **Martin Luther (1483–1546):** an **Augustinian friar** (he joined the
    Augustinian Hermits order in 1505 — the order that read and venerated
    Augustine). His doctrine of salvation by grace through faith alone (*sola gratia,
    sola fide*) is directly indebted to Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings. Luther
    read the late Augustine, the Augustine of enslaved will and predestination, and
    used him against the medieval Church's emphasis on meritorious works. `DOCUMENTED`
    — confirmed via SEP "Augustine of Hippo" (Luther as Augustinian friar).
  - **John Calvin (1509–1564):** his doctrine of **double predestination** (God
    predestines both the elect and the damned) is the most consistent logical
    development of Augustine's late position. Calvin cited Augustine extensively. The
    era-2 read's closing reference to Descartes' demon having "medieval grandparents"
    applies equally to Calvinist predestinarianism having Augustinian grandparents.
    `DOCUMENTED` via general scholarship; the Calvin-Augustine link is uncontested.
- **René Descartes (1596–1650):** the **"si fallor, sum"** parallel (§3.J, §4.H).
  Note precisely: the parallel is structural and real, but direct influence is
  unconfirmed. Descartes was aware of Augustine (Mersenne pointed out the parallel
  to him; Descartes acknowledged the resemblance while claiming independent
  discovery). Frame as: "the argument Descartes would reach by a different road
  twelve centuries later" — NOT as "Descartes got this from Augustine."
- **The data-spine edge in the app:** plotinus → augustine (confirmed era-1 §11);
  augustine → anselm (PARAPHRASE framing, era-2 pack); augustine → luther / calvin
  (documented above).

## 8. "Why this is a break" — material for the block

Augustine is not a "break" from the era-2 standpoint (he IS the era's opening
figure), so this section serves the thinker read's own break block: what is new
about Augustine compared to the pagan philosophers he inherited?

**BEFORE (his Neoplatonist and Manichee inheritance):**
- Plotinus: turn inward, ascend to the One — the journey is intellectual; the One
  is reached through philosophical contemplation, permanently, if you have the
  discipline.
- Manichaean: evil is a co-equal power; salvation is releasing the light-particles
  within you from the dark material prison.

**AFTER (Augustine's move):**
- The Neoplatonist inward turn now leads not to philosophical absorption in the
  impersonal One but to encounter with a **personal God** who is already there,
  waiting, in the inner room — "You were within, and I without" (VERIFIED §3.G).
- The will, not just the intellect, is the problem. The Neoplatonists showed him
  the destination; they could not make him go. That failure points to the
  doctrine of grace: knowing the good is not enough; the will must be turned by
  something outside itself.
- **The concrete move:** he imports Neoplatonist metaphysics (privation, inwardness,
  the soul as image of God) and repoints it at the drama of a **particular will** —
  his own — that cannot save itself. The abstract becomes biographical; the
  philosophical becomes the confessional. That is new.

## 9. Apocrypha / potted-summary traps — specific to Augustine

1. **"Augustine invented the concept of original sin"** — he did not invent it;
   he developed and systematized a doctrine with earlier roots (Paul's Romans 5,
   Irenaeus, Tertullian). What he did was make the *transmission* of original sin
   a heritable, biological corruption — that particular framing is his. Use
   "developed" or "gave its definitive Western form to."

2. **"Give me chastity and continency, only not yet" as comic self-knowledge** —
   it is often quoted as though Augustine is winking at the reader; in context
   it is self-laceration, not wit. The full sentence makes clear he is diagnosing
   a divided will he is ashamed of, not chuckling at it.

3. **"Augustine hated the body / was a sex-obsessed killjoy"** — the man who
   wrote the *Confessions*'s frank analysis of desire was the diagnostician of
   the divided will, not its enemy. His theology does NOT say the body is evil
   (that would be the Manichee position he rejected); it says the rightly ordered
   will directs bodily desire appropriately. A hostile caricature.

4. **"The City of God is about the Church vs. the Roman Empire"** — wrong; the
   two cities are two orientations of love, intermixed in history, not two
   institutions. See §4.F.

5. **Fake Augustine quotes — a large cluster:**
   - "The truth is like a lion; let it loose and it will defend itself" — no
     source in Augustine; modern origin.
   - "He who sings prays twice" (*Qui cantat bis orat*) — not found in Augustine
     in this form; the nearest authentic text is a comment on singing Psalms in
     his *Commentary on Psalm 73*, not this slogan.
   - "The Church is a whore but she is my mother" — no Augustine source; 20th-century
     provenance (attributed to various modern theologians).
   - "Our heart is restless until it rests in You" — real (VERIFIED §3.A), but
     often quoted as "hearts ARE restless" (plural) rather than Pusey's
     "heart is restless" (singular). Small difference; use Pusey.
   - "Love God and do as you please" — a garbled version of Homily 7 on 1 John
     (VERIFIED §3.I); the real passage is "Love, and do what you will" and has
     communal and corrective context that the popular version strips.

6. **"Augustine was the most important Christian thinker of the first millennium"**
   — defensible at era altitude but frame carefully; the Eastern tradition (Basil,
   the Gregorys, John Chrysostom) contests the "most important" superlative, and
   the Eastern Church has never privileged Augustine the way the Latin West has.
   Scope to: "the single most influential thinker in the Latin Christian tradition
   before the modern age."

   covers the range safely.
