## Paraphrase-only (NEVER quoted) — flagged
- **The four grounds for free speech** are summarized in the read in Mill's own structure
  (true / portion-of-truth / dead-dogma / lost-meaning); the chapter's verbatim
  "recapitulation" sentences are confirmed in ch.2 but the read paraphrases the four-fold
  list and quotes only the load-bearing phrases ("collision of adverse opinions," "dead
  dogma, not a living truth," "He who knows only his own side…").
- **The self-/other-regarding distinction** is Mill's framework; "self-regarding" and
  "other-regarding" are the standard scholarly labels for it and are used as descriptors,
  not put in quotation marks as Mill's words.
- **The drunken soldier / broken-duty hard case** (ch.4) is paraphrased from Mill's
  discussion that self-harm becomes society's business only when it breaks a specific duty
  to assignable others; not quoted.
- **The "sell himself as a slave" / cannot be free not to be free passage** (ch.5, lines
  ~4253–4265 of the ed.: "he abdicates his liberty; he foregoes any future use of it…",
  "He therefore defeats, in his own case, the very purpose…", "He is no longer free") is
  PARAPHRASED in the read, NOT quoted. The paraphrase tracks Mill's actual wording
  (abdicates liberty / defeats the purpose / no longer free) but uses no quotation marks.
- **Free trade rests on different grounds from liberty; sale of poisons regulable for
  third-party harm** (ch.5) — paraphrased from the confirmed ch.5 passages on Free Trade
  and the sale of poisons (labels/registers); not quoted.

## Position-summary discipline (potted-summary / landmine traps)
- **Harm principle is NOT "anything goes."** Read states it precisely: the ONLY ground for
  coercion is preventing harm to others; a person's own good is never sufficient. Limits
  are worked (incitement, broken duties, third-party harm, the bridge, poisons).
- **Mill's utilitarianism is NOT the ends-justify-any-means cartoon** (brief landmine).
  The read plants "utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man
  as a progressive being" and frames liberty as itself the long-run utility case, pointing
  to /philosophy/work/utilitarianism for the full defense (higher pleasures, etc. — NOT
  restated here).
- **Harriet Taylor credited as a genuine partner** (brief landmine), from Mill's own
  dedication ("in part the author," "belongs as much to her as to me"), not as a footnote.
- **The imperial / "backward states" caveat (ch.1)** is named honestly as the book's most
  dated and most criticized passage and explicitly NOT endorsed; the read flags that later
  readers rightly refuse to follow Mill there. This is the honest-about-what-he-said-and-
  didn't move, not airbrushing.
- **"Tyranny of the majority"** stated precisely: Mill's worry is social/customary coercion
  (opinion, disapproval) that can be worse than legal coercion because it penetrates daily
  life — not a claim that democracy is bad.
- **Reception/afterlife** (ch.5 close) stated at the level the sources support: the harm
  principle became the standard frame for limits-of-law debates (consenting adults,
  obscenity, drugs, suicide, speech); critics pressed the elasticity of "harm" and the
  imperial caveat. No specific later legislation is asserted as caused by the book; framed
  as "the standard frame," which is accurate and uncontroversial.
