## Descartes's *Discourse on the Method* (WORK read)
Critic: Sonnet (storytelling + understanding gate). Date: 2026-06-17.

---

## Overall assessment

The draft is clean by the standards of most philosophy writing and often very good.
The hook, the "break" block, and Chapters 2 and 5 are the high-water marks. The main
systemic weakness is the GET-IT axis: Chapters 1 and 6 lean on biography and framing
without running a full three-part understanding pass; Chapter 4's God proof is too
compressed to land. Voice blockers are few and manageable; **no em-dashes appear in
narration at all** (every em-dash in the file is an epigraph attribution line —
"— Descartes…" — which the spec explicitly exempts). That is a model clean draft on
that axis.

## Voice-blocker sweep

Working through blockers (a)–(g) across every paragraph.

**(a) Reader-commands / second-person imperatives**

> "Consider the rules running on an ordinary tangled question…" (Ch 2, block 5)

"Consider" is on the banned list. The passage is doing genuine work (it IS a worked
example), but the opener frames it as an instruction to the reader. **MUST-FIX.**

Fix: "The rules running on an ordinary tangled question, say whether some
long-held belief about the world is actually true, work like this."

> "A reader will notice that these are recognizably the questions still asked of
> machines that talk, three and a half centuries early." (Ch 5, block 4)

"A reader will notice" is a variant of "notice that…" — it tells the reader what to
perceive rather than stating the observation directly. REVISE-LEVEL.

Fix: "These are recognizably the questions still asked of machines that talk, three
and a half centuries early."

**(b) Meta-narration / throat-clearing**

> "It should be said plainly that this is a sketch." (Ch 4, block 5)

"It should be said plainly" is throat-clearing — an announcement of what is about
to be said rather than saying it. **MUST-FIX.**

Fix: "The *Discourse* gives the gist in a few pages; the full version, with the
objections of other philosophers printed alongside Descartes's replies, is the work
of the *Meditations*."
(The offending label clause "It should be said plainly that this is a sketch" is
redundant once the sentence already says "a few pages.")

> "A word on that famous Latin." (Ch 4, block 3)

Scene-setting announcement. Not the worst example — it is a single short label
before a genuine clarification — but it follows the pattern of prescriptive framing.
SHOULD-FIX.

Fix: Start the block at the content: "Descartes wrote the *Discourse* in French, and
the sentence reads *je pense, donc je suis*, 'I think, therefore I am.'" The subject
itself signals the shift; no label is needed.

**(c) Self-labeled asides**

> "the plain-language version, the one you could say at dinner" — not present. ✓

No self-labeled aside constructions found beyond the throat-clearing already flagged.

**(d) Condescension / glossing what a smart reader knows**

> "not a stove. The old image of Descartes meditating inside an oven is a
> mistranslation" (Ch 2, block 1)

Factually useful, not condescending. Fine.

> "the way a 'furnace room' is a room and not a furnace" (Ch 2, block 1)

This is an explanatory parenthetical to cement the stove/room distinction — the
analogy is slightly over-extended. The *poêle* clarification already landed; the
"furnace room" gloss re-explains the same point in a slightly more finger-wagging
tone. SHOULD-FIX (tighten, not remove).

**(e) Vague gestures referencing nothing concrete**

No vague-gesture failures found. Whenever a "profound" or "revolutionary" framing
might appear, the draft names what changed concretely. ✓

**(f) Em-dashes in narration**

Zero, as documented above. ✓

**(g) Self-reference to the artifact**

> "A note on a phrase often attached to this passage" (Ch 6, block 4)

"This passage" refers to the passage just quoted, not to the read/narrative as an
artifact — no violation.

No "this read," "this narrative," "this page," or "the road to it starts here"
constructions found. ✓

## Per-chapter grades and findings

---

### Hook — STRONG

The four-paragraph hook is well-built. It opens with the concrete bibliographic fact
(anonymous, French, three-essay preface), earns the stakes in paragraph 2 (full
title = the whole ambition; French as argument), explains the strange autobiography
form in paragraph 3, and lands the cogito in paragraph 4 with the French original
and the word "first principle." Each paragraph does a distinct job. The hook is not
front-loading the argument; it is building genuine curiosity. The decision to hold
the *cogito* until paragraph 4, rather than leading with it, is correct.

No voice-blocker violations.

**Verdict: STRONG.**

---

### "The Break" block — STRONG

Before/after labels are exact and non-vague. The three paragraphs earn the break:
paragraph 1 steelmans the tradition (Latin/scholar/authority/syllogism as "coherent
and genuinely impressive"), paragraph 2 names every axis of the break (language,
audience, authority, order of inquiry), paragraph 3 gives the concrete move (Part IV
structure summarized). The final sentence — "That relocation, from inherited authority
to first-person certainty, is the reason this short French preface is usually marked
as the start of modern philosophy" — is a strong plain restatement.

**Verdict: STRONG.**

---

### Chapter 1 — GOOD (with one SHOULD-FIX)

**Hook:** Opens on the joke/premise of the opening line — this is the right move.
Parsing the joke and what it implies (the problem is use, not equipment) is done
cleanly and the analysis is genuinely sharp.

**Stakes:** Clear. The chapter frames the autobiography as doing philosophical work
(a man who argues against authority can't use authority to sell his method) — that
is an insight worth having.

**Pacing:** Fine. Five blocks that move from joke → inference → form → education →
travel. No padding.

**Voice:** Good. The parenthetical on "good sense" = reason is handled cleanly; the
"medieval tangle of streets" analogy is held for Ch 2. The closing line ("a student
who has lost faith in everything he inherited and is ready to start over") is a clean
transition.

**GET-IT test:**
- Worked example: The ACTUAL argument of Part I is "everyone thinks they reason well,
  therefore the problem must be procedure, not equipment." The draft unpacks this well
  — this counts as the worked example, and it's in the first two blocks.
- Strongest version: Descartes's argument for the method's necessity (if only
  mathematics yields certainty, extract its procedure) gets one sentence at the end
  of block 4. It could be one sentence sharper — "if the certainty belongs to the
  method, not the subject, then the same method aimed at anything else should yield
  the same certainty" — but the gist is there.
- Plain restatement: The closing sentence of block 5 functions as one.

GET-IT: GOOD. Not a worked example in the full three-part sense, but this chapter's
"idea" is the framing observation about reason's equal distribution, not a formal
argument, and the draft unpacks it as fully as it needs to.

**SHOULD-FIX:** The biography/reception risk is real here — this chapter has the
most biographical content of the six. The philosophical payload (the argument that
procedure matters more than faculty) is present but runs thinner than the
scholastic-curriculum survey in block 4. Consider one extra sentence tightening
the implication in block 4: "If certainty comes from mathematics' *procedure* rather
than its *subject*, the same four-step discipline aimed at anything else should
produce the same certainty." That is the hinge the method in Ch 2 hangs on and it is
only implied, not stated, here.

**Verdict: GOOD.**

### Chapter 2 — STRONG

**Hook:** Opens on the famous setting (the stove-heated room) and immediately corrects
the mistranslation. This is exactly the right move — the correction is interesting,
earns trust, and sets the tone.

**Stakes:** Clear. The question "what procedure could rebuild all of knowledge from
scratch?" is the biggest possible stakes after Part I's windup.

**Pacing:** The five blocks move well: setting → architecture analogy → the four
rules quoted in full → source in mathematics → worked example. The architecture
analogy in block 2 is the best single passage in the draft — it concretizes the
abstract project perfectly.

**Voice:** Dry and accurate. "The popular story of three revelatory dreams that night
comes from a much later biography…it is not in the *Discourse* at all" — flagging the
legend without belaboring it. The phrase "a reader could actually carry in their head"
(block 5 closer) is a small bit of reader-management but barely rises to a violation.

**GET-IT test:**
- Worked example: Block 5 runs the four rules on "whether some long-held belief about
  the world is actually true." This is a genuine worked example and it is the best
  passage in the chapter — it shows exactly how the method works in practice. ONE
  MUST-FIX: the block opener is "Consider the rules running…" which triggers blocker
  (a). Fix per above.
- Strongest version: Block 4 ("The four rules sound thin until their source is in
  view") does the steelman job cleanly. The case that mathematical certainty comes
  from procedure not subject matter is the load-bearing claim and the draft makes it
  explicit.
- Plain restatement: "The method is mathematical rigor, lifted out of mathematics and
  aimed at everything." That's a dinner-table line. ✓

**Verdict: STRONG** (after fixing the "Consider" opener in block 5).

### Chapter 3 — GOOD

**Hook:** Block 1 names the problem ("a man cannot suspend all his beliefs and still
get through a Tuesday") crisply.

**Stakes:** Clear. This chapter's stakes are about why methodic doubt doesn't
paralyze life.

**Pacing:** Four blocks (intro + one per maxim). The third maxim gets extra space,
correctly — it is the deepest and the bridge to Part IV.

**Voice:** Clean. No voice-blocker violations beyond the already-flagged items.

**GET-IT test:**
- Worked example: The "traveler lost in a forest" for maxim 2 is a genuine concrete
  example and it is Descartes's own. Maxim 3 has the Stoic connection (Epictetus's
  dichotomy) as the comparative anchor.
- Strongest version: The provisional morality is correctly framed — the draft explains
  *why* each maxim is the most defensible choice given uncertainty, not just what each
  says. The logic of maxim 1 (moderate rules do least damage if wrong) is stated.
- Plain restatement: "In theory, doubt everything; in action, commit and keep moving"
  is the dinner-table line for maxim 2. ✓

**One SHOULD-FIX:** The Stoic comparison ("The resemblance to the Stoics is strong,
in particular to Epictetus's division of things into those in our power and those not,
though Descartes does not cite them.") is placed at the end of block 4 with "though
Descartes does not cite them" dismissing the connection after noting it. This is
slightly rushed — the resemblance is close enough that it deserves one additional
sentence explaining what Descartes adds or changes (he narrows "things in our power"
to *thoughts only*, which is sharper than Epictetus's broader "things depending on
us"). Not a MUST-FIX — the chapter passes the GET-IT test — but the Stoic comparison
is a genuinely useful comprehension anchor and could do more work.

**Verdict: GOOD.**

### Chapter 4 — GOOD (borderline; one MUST-FIX)

**Hook:** Block 1 opens on "Part IV is the philosophical heart of the book" which is
true but is a brief piece of scene-setting. The real hook arrives with the
enactment: "The senses go first, since they sometimes deceive…"

**Stakes:** The highest in the book — tearing down all beliefs. The stakes are
explicit.

**Pacing:** Five blocks. Block 1 (doubting), block 2 (cogito found), block 3 (Latin
clarification), block 4 (mind/body inference), block 5 (God proof). Block 3 is a
parenthetical that interrupts the philosophical flow — it is valuable factually but
sits awkwardly between the cogito's discovery and its consequence. Consider whether
block 3 could be shortened or moved (this is a SHOULD-FIX on structure, not a
blocker).

**Voice:**
- "It should be said plainly that this is a sketch." — MUST-FIX (throat-clearing,
  flagged above).
- "A word on that famous Latin." — SHOULD-FIX (flagged above).

**GET-IT test — this is the chapter's real risk:**

*The cogito:*
- Worked example: The self-defeating quality of doubt is explained ("the more
  energetically Descartes tries to suppose that everything is false, the more
  undeniable it becomes that there is a he doing the supposing"). Block 2 does this
  well.
- Strongest version: "Any attempt to deny 'I exist' has to be performed by the very I
  it denies." This is the strongest form and it IS in the text. ✓
- Plain restatement: "The doubt cannot reach its own author" is nearly there. ✓

*The mind/body inference (block 4):*
- Worked example: Missing. The draft states the inference ("thinking self can be
  conceived as existing without the body; therefore it is a thing of a different
  kind") but does not show the reasoning step that makes it work — or show why it
  is controversial. The reader is told "it needs a careful word, which the next
  chapter gives it, because the textbook version of what Descartes did here is wrong
  in an instructive way" — but this forwards to Ch 5 without giving the next chapter's
  corrective in miniature. The forward-pointer to Ch 5 is fine structure; what is
  missing is at least one sentence that names the flaw: the inference from "I can
  conceive X without Y" to "X and Y are distinct things" is not obviously valid, and
  Descartes's critics (Arnauld, in the Fourth Objections) said so. That one sentence
  here would make the Ch 5 payoff land harder.

*The God proof (block 5):*
- This is the weakest GET-IT passage in the draft. The argument is present in
  paraphrase, but it is the most compressed block in the whole read. The chain runs:
  doubting = imperfection → I have the idea of a perfect being → the lesser cannot
  cause the greater → therefore God placed the idea → God is perfect → God cannot
  deceive → faculties trusted. That chain is in the text, but it goes by fast and
  the key premise ("the lesser cannot cause the greater") is asserted without even
  a sentence explaining why Descartes believed it (it is his adaptation of the
  scholastic causal principle). **MUST-FIX:** add one sentence naming the causal
  principle before invoking it: "He was relying on a principle inherited from the
  Scholastics: a cause must contain at least as much reality as its effect, so a
  finite, imperfect mind could not be the source of an idea that contains more
  reality than it does." Without that, the reader is left with the bare assertion that
  the lesser cannot generate the greater — the move that critics have always hit first.
- Strongest version: Block 5 does attempt a steelman (God as guarantor of faculties),
  but the Cartesian circle objection is mentioned only as "critics pounced." Name the
  circle explicitly: "Descartes argued that clear and distinct perception is reliable
  *because* God guarantees it — and then used clear and distinct perception to
  establish that God exists. The circle is real." This is SHOULD-FIX (the circle IS
  mentioned; naming it more crisply would help but the current version passes).
- Plain restatement: Missing for the God proof. SHOULD-FIX: close block 5 with
  something like "God is his escape hatch out of the sealed room of doubt — the
  guarantee that when he reasons carefully, he is reaching truth and not just
  spinning in his own head."

**Verdict: GOOD** (blocked by the throat-clearing MUST-FIX and the God-proof GET-IT
gaps, not by story failure — the chapter is not all biography, and the cogito
passages are strong).

### Chapter 5 — STRONG

**Hook:** Opens on "Part V turns from the thinking self to the physical world, and it
carries a shadow" — the *Le Monde* suppression as the shadow. This is a good hook
because it makes the politics of the chapter real before the philosophy.

**Stakes:** Clear and high — the question of whether the body is a machine has
direct implications for animal consciousness and what makes humans different.

**Pacing:** Five blocks. Machine physics → animals-as-automata → language test →
flexible-reason test → the mind-body problem properly framed. The structure is clean.

**Voice:**
- "A reader will notice that these are recognizably the questions still asked of
  machines that talk, three and a half centuries early." — MUST-FIX (flagged above,
  reader-command variant).

**GET-IT test — body-as-machine:**
- Worked example: Block 2 gives the clockwork analogy explicitly ("the moving figures
  of an elaborate clockwork"), and the two tests (language + flexible reason) in
  blocks 3–4 are the most concrete examples in the whole read. They work very well.
- Strongest version: Block 2 correctly distinguishes Descartes's documented position
  (animals are machines) from the stronger reading (animals feel nothing whatever)
  that "later writers loaded onto him." This is exactly the steelman obligation
  fulfilled.
- Plain restatement: The closer of block 4 — "Reason, by contrast, is a universal
  instrument that copes with any circumstance" — is direct. The two-test structure is
  itself memorable.

The correction in block 5 ("He did not invent dualism. He invented the *problem* of
how mind and body connect") is the best single sentence in the chapter and possibly
the whole draft. It is the kind of plain-restatement that makes a chapter earn its
place. STRONG.

**Verdict: STRONG.**

### Chapter 6 — GOOD (borderline; biography-heavy)

**Hook:** Block 1 opens on Galileo's shadow — fear as the reason Descartes published
cautiously. Good.

**Stakes:** The "lords and possessors of nature" quote is the chapter's payload and it
arrives in block 2. Appropriately, that is where the stakes become cosmic.

**Pacing:** Four blocks + one summary block (5). Block 5 (the six-part summary) is
the structural risk in this chapter — it is a full paragraph synthesizing the entire
arc. Summary paragraphs can feel like an author recapping rather than the story
moving. This one is well-written but is doing significant recapping. SHOULD-FIX:
consider trimming block 5 — the throughline is already clear to any reader who
has reached this point; a half-paragraph would do the same job.

**Voice:** Clean. No additional voice-blocker violations.

**GET-IT test — "lords and possessors" + science-as-collective-enterprise:**
- Worked example: The "lords and possessors" passage is quoted and glossed with
  medicine as the concrete application. That works.
- Strongest version: Block 2 gives the vision clearly (knowledge-as-power, science as
  mastery over nature). The tension with the intensely first-person *cogito* at the
  book's center is named in block 3 ("It sits a little uneasily beside the intensely
  first-person *I think*"). This is the right observation, but "sits a little
  uneasily" is mild — this is actually a profound structural tension in Descartes's
  project (solitary epistemic foundation vs. collective scientific program), and naming
  it more directly would give the chapter a better close. SHOULD-FIX.
- Plain restatement: Block 3's "Publishing was partly a recruitment notice" is a
  crisp restatement of that point. ✓

**Biography-heavy risk:** Chapter 6 has more biography/framing content (Galileo
suppression, French-vs-Latin choice, publishing anxiety) than philosophical argument.
This is defensible — Part VI of the *Discourse* genuinely is more about context than
argument — but the chapter passes the GET-IT test only because the "lords and
possessors" passage earns it. The chapter would be stronger if block 3 (recruitment
notice) developed the tension between private cogito and collective science one more
step, since that is the most interesting philosophical observation in the chapter.

**Verdict: GOOD.**

## Summary assessment

The draft passes the storytelling gate with six good-to-strong chapters and zero
narration em-dashes. The three STRONG chapters (hook, break block, Ch 2, Ch 5) are
the best work; the three GOOD chapters pass but each carries one addressable weakness.
The philosophy-specific risk — biography-heavy chapters that never make the reader GET
the idea — is present but not dominant: only the God proof in Ch 4 fails the full
three-part test, and it fails primarily on the causal principle (MF-3), not on the
overall chapter structure. With the five MUST-FIX items addressed and the seven
SHOULD-FIX items considered, the draft ships clean.
