The throughline is long but it earns its length: it gives the full argument map (monad → no windows → perception → sufficient reason → God → best world → two clocks → City of God) so the reader knows what the staircase is climbing before they step on it. Par 1 gives occasion and posthumous title; par 2 delivers the argument's most counterintuitive claim (no causation) with force; par 3 frames the book as a staircase — this is the right orienting device for a 90-section deductive text; par 4 lands Candide and the stakes of reading past the caricature.

## Chapter 1 — The book with no title

**Grade: GOOD**

**Hook:** The occasion (Remond, Vienna, 1714) is well-established. The disambiguation of Monadology vs. Principles of Nature and Grace is necessary and handled without condescension. The publication history (Köhler 1720 → Latin 1721 → French 1840) reads more like a bibliographic rundown than a story.

**Stakes:** Low in this chapter by design — it's the setup chapter. Stakes arrive in the final paragraph, where sections 1–2 of the Monadology itself are introduced: "the world is a heap, heaps need genuine units, the units are monads." This lands well.

**Understanding — three-part test:** The chapter introduces the monad as a concept but doesn't fully pass the test. The plain-language restatement ("a monad is a true one, a genuine unit, in a world where everything ordinary thought of as a thing turns out on inspection to be a heap") is present and good. The worked example and steelman are not yet due here — they belong in Ch 2 where the windows argument is made — but the heaps/units contrast is vivid enough that readers won't feel lost.

**Pacing:** Par 2 (the conflation with Prince Eugene) is accurate but slows the chapter. It reads like a footnote inserted into the body. It's warranted by the accuracy constraints but could be tightened.

**SHOULD-FIX 2 (pacing — par 2):** The Prince Eugene disambiguation paragraph is necessary but its position after par 1 kills forward momentum before the chapter gets going. Consider consolidating it into a subordinate clause in par 1, or moving it to a brief parenthetical note in par 1: "…written for Nicolas Remond in Paris (a companion piece for Prince Eugene of Savoy, the *Principles of Nature and Grace*, was written in the same summer and is often confused with this one; they are separate texts)."

No voice-blocker violations.

## Chapter 2 — No windows

**Grade: STRONG**

This is the best chapter. The argument from simplicity → no parts → no opening → no windows is the clearest argument-walk in the piece. Every step is stated before the next is taken. Par 3 ("the consequence is enormous") is the right register — it doesn't oversell, it just names the size of the claim. Par 4 (appetition and perception defined) is clean. Par 5 (identity of indiscernibles) is correctly positioned as a consequence, not a standalone claim.

**Understanding — three-part test:**

- **Worked example:** Implicitly present: "one billiard ball driving another, or a fire warming a hand, or a spoken word changing a listener's mind" — this is exactly the right class of example. However, it appears as part of the negation (what doesn't happen) rather than a positive illustration of what does happen inside a sealed monad. A brief affirmative example of a monad's internal unfolding (e.g., a monad that "perceives" a tree on Monday and perceives the tree more distinctly on Tuesday, the change coming entirely from within, not from the tree) would complete the three-part test.

- **Strongest version:** Present. The sealed quality of the monad is explicitly tied to its simplicity — "It is forced by simplicity" — which is the right move.

- **Plain restatement:** "The whole spectacle of a world full of causes and collisions is real as it appears and false as a description of what is actually happening" (from the hook) serves this function. Ch 2 itself restates clearly in par 3.

**MUST-FIX 1 (understanding — no affirmative worked example):** The chapter shows what sealed monads cannot do (receive external causes) but never shows affirmatively what one does: its internal principle of change producing the next perception out of the previous one, from within, with nothing entering. Add one sentence or two in par 4 showing a monad's internal arc — before/after states, both self-generated — so the reader can hold what "everything from within" actually looks like in practice, not just by contrast with what it excludes.

No voice-blocker violations.

## Chapter 3 — From a sleeping stone to a knowing soul

**Grade: GOOD**

The ladder from bare monad → soul → spirit is vivid and correctly parallels Leibniz's own §§14–30 structure. The Descartes contrast (hard line vs. slope) in par 4 is the right comparison to import here and it lands. "The world was never dead. It was perceiving, faintly, everywhere, all along" is a good close.

**Understanding — three-part test on perception vs. apperception:**

This is the hardest idea in the book — subtler even than "no windows" — and the chapter does not fully pass the three-part test.

- **Worked example:** Par 1 offers the analogy of "a person asleep is full of bodily states and notices nothing." This is good and it comes from Leibniz. But it handles only the lower case (unconscious perception). The chapter never gives a worked example of apperception itself — what it looks like for a rational soul to "turn back on its own perceptions" and arrive at self-knowledge. What does that look like for an actual human being doing an actual cognitive thing? The gap is significant because apperception is the hinge on which the whole distinction between animals and rational souls turns.

- **Strongest version:** Leibniz's position is stated clearly enough. But the chapter doesn't show why the ladder is genuinely surprising or compelling — why should we believe perception goes "all the way down" into stone? Par 3 gestures toward the reward (mind is not a miracle in a dead world) but doesn't give the argument a chance to feel its own force.

- **Plain restatement:** Implicit but not explicit. The chapter would benefit from one sentence at the close that flatly names what makes this arrangement different from Descartes: not "mind vs. machine" but "more-or-less-aware" as a single continuum of the same stuff.

**MUST-FIX 2 (understanding — apperception needs a worked example):** Add a third-person illustration of apperception as the specifically human move: a person noticing not just pain but that they are in pain and wondering what caused it — that self-reflective loop is what §14 means by apperception, and without it the distinction between the dog with memory and the rational soul with reflection is named but not shown. The example should be in third person ("a man noticing…"), one to two sentences.

**SHOULD-FIX 3 (voice blocker b, throat-clearing, par 4):**
> "The arrangement settles an old quarrel in a way worth marking, because it cuts against a famous rival."

"In a way worth marking" is a meta-narrator telling the reader what to pay attention to. Cut the frame; make the point.

> ~~"The arrangement settles an old quarrel in a way worth marking, because it cuts against a famous rival."~~
> "The arrangement settles an old quarrel. Descartes had drawn a hard line…"

## Chapter 4 — The two great principles

**Grade: GOOD**

The chapter correctly gives contradiction the brief treatment it deserves (one paragraph, "every philosopher grants this much") and gives sufficient reason the weight the argument requires. The infinite regress of contingent reasons, and the move to a necessary being as the chain's anchor, is the sharpest logical narrative in the piece. The "contingent is not unreasoned; it is infinitely reasoned" sentence is the best line in this chapter.

**Understanding — three-part test on sufficient reason:**

- **Worked example:** Par 4 operates at an abstract level ("the chain of contingent things, however far it runs, never contains its own sufficient reason"). The chapter doesn't give a concrete instance of the principle being applied — what a particular contingent fact's chain of reasons looks like as it extends toward the infinite. Something like: a man born on a Thursday; the reason is where his father was; the reason for that is a war; the reason for that war is a treaty; the chain never terminates in anything that explains itself. One such chain, even two sentences, would make the principle tangible.

- **Strongest version:** The principle is stated in Leibniz's own words (§32 epigraph) and correctly described as "distinctively his." The chapter doesn't quite present the steelman — why should a reader accept that every contingent fact needs a reason? A brief statement that denying this makes the universe arbitrary — things just happen, brute and without explanation — would give the principle the force its defender would give it.

- **Plain restatement:** Implicit. "Nothing is ever simply the case for no reason" in par 2 is close, and good.

**SHOULD-FIX 4 (understanding — steelman the PSR):** Add one sentence in par 2 or par 3 giving the principle its strongest advocacy: accepting brute contingency (things happen with no reason at all) is the alternative, and Leibniz's position is that the universe operates without such unexplained gaps. The reader needs to feel the principle's pull before watching it used as a crowbar.

## Chapter 5 — The necessary being

**Grade: NEEDS WORK**

This chapter is the shortest and the one most at risk of being all reception / history rather than argument. It is not quite at that failure mode — it does track the argument — but the ontological argument in par 2 is the one place in the entire piece where the argument is named rather than walked.

**Hook:** Thin. The chapter opens on §38's language and proceeds. There is no orienting moment to tell the reader what the stakes of this chapter are, distinct from the stakes of Ch 4 that got them here.

**Understanding — three-part test on the ontological argument:**

> "And in section forty-five Leibniz states the ontological argument almost in passing, as a thing that simply follows: God alone has the privilege that, if such a being is possible, then it must exist, because its existence follows from its essence."

This is the most compressed and least-explained step in the entire book. "Existence follows from essence" is where the argument either lands or loses the reader, and the chapter gives it one sentence. The three-part test fails here:

- **No worked example.** What does it mean for existence to follow from essence? The standard illustration: a triangle's angles summing to 180° follows from what a triangle is; you can't coherently describe a triangle without that being true. Similarly, a necessary being's existence follows from what a necessary being is — to describe it clearly is to see that it cannot merely happen to exist. One sentence with a concrete analogy would make the move intelligible.

- **No steelman.** The chapter doesn't give the argument its strongest presentation before moving on. A reader skeptical of this move has been given no reason to think it's other than sleight of hand.

- **Plain restatement:** Present and clear: "A necessary being is one that cannot merely happen to exist or happen not to; if it is so much as possible, it is actual." This is the best sentence in the chapter. But it needs the example to carry the reader to understanding, not just definition.

**Par 3 (continual outflashings):** The image of created monads as "continual outflashings of the divine" is vivid and philosophically accurate, but it arrives without context — Leibniz's occasionalism-adjacent claim that monads depend on God at each instant is a significant move and deserves one sentence of setup.

**MUST-FIX 3 (understanding — ontological argument needs a worked example):** Add a third-person worked example for "existence follows from essence" in par 2 — two to three sentences. The standard move is the triangle analogy: just as a triangle's three angles summing to 180° is inseparable from what a triangle is, so God's existence is inseparable from what a necessary being is. Once a reader sees the shape of that move, the argument is intelligible rather than mysterious. The example should be flagged as an illustration, not Leibniz's own phrasing.

**MUST-FIX 4 (pacing / stakes — hook):** The chapter needs an opening sentence or two that locates it in the arc: the sufficient reason argument has been run to the edge of the contingent world, and this chapter is where it steps off. Without that beat, the chapter reads as a continuation of Ch 4's business rather than the arrival it is. One sentence would do: "The principle of sufficient reason has, by this point in the book, produced something its original use on falling stones never promised: a proof that God exists."

No voice-blocker violations.

## Chapter 6 — The best of all possible worlds

**Grade: STRONG**

The longest chapter and the one with the most to prove, and it proves it. The move from sufficient reason → the choice among possible worlds → best = most variety on most economical laws is well-walked. The three kinds of evil (metaphysical / physical / moral) are correctly named and distinguished. The Voltaire / Pangloss section is excellent: the joke is called "brilliant and unfair" and the actual move it inverts is named clearly. The closing sentence is exactly right: "that this world, with the Lisbon dead in it, is the best a perfect being could have made, because every other possible world was, on the whole, worse."

**Understanding — three-part test on best-of-all-possible-worlds:**

- **Worked example:** Par 3 gives the positive test: "greatest variety of things produced by the simplest and most general laws." This is an abstract criterion. The chapter would benefit from a brief illustration — a world of maximal variety but maximal complexity of laws is worse; a world of simple laws but almost no variety is worse. The best is the one that gets the most out of the least. Even one concrete gesture (all planets move in perfect circles on perfectly simple laws, but the motion is lifeless; curves and ellipses, by one law, give far more varied trajectories) would concretize the optimization.

- **Strongest version:** Present and strong. The theodicy argument (freedom requires moral evil; constant laws require that fire also burns) is given its full force. The chapter does not dismiss the evils.

- **Plain restatement:** "Best does not mean that every event in the world is good… It is a comparative claim about whole worlds, not an endorsement of any part." — present and clear.

The chapter passes the three-part test well enough; the missing worked example for the optimization criterion is a SHOULD-FIX, not a MUST-FIX.

No voice-blocker violations.

## Chapter 7 — Mirrors, two clocks, and the City of God

**Grade: GOOD**

The city-viewed-from-many-sides comparison (§57) is the most powerful image in the book and the chapter delivers it well. Par 2 extends the image correctly: "One town, seen from the hill to the east and from the river to the west." The two-clocks image in par 4 is correctly flagged as from an earlier work and paraphrased rather than quoted — that's the right handling. The final paragraph's widening to the City of God and the harmony of nature / grace is a satisfying close.

**Understanding — three-part test on pre-established harmony:**

- **Worked example:** The two-clocks image IS the worked example, and it's excellent. It gives three options (link / reset / built-right) and Leibniz's choice among them. This is the clearest structure in the whole piece.

- **Strongest version:** Present. The clockmaker-as-repairman rejection is Leibniz's own stated reason for preferring pre-established harmony, and the chapter gives it.

- **Plain restatement:** Par 3's final sentence is close: "The match between will and motion is total, and the contact is zero." Good.

**One gap:** The city-from-many-viewpoints image and the two-clocks image address different aspects of the system (how monads share a world vs. how soul and body stay in sync), and both are important. But the chapter never explicitly ties them together: the city image shows why many sealed monads still constitute one shared universe; the clocks image shows why soul and body stay in sync within that shared universe. A reader could finish the chapter not quite seeing how these two images are solving two different problems. One connective sentence after par 2 ("That solves the question of how many monads share one world. A separate question is how the soul of a particular monad stays synchronized with its body.") would clarify the structure.
