## Item 1: Diogenes/Alexander "stand out of my sunlight" — ch6 [VERIFY] tag

**Status: CONFIRMED — [VERIFY] TAG MAY BE CLEARED. One wording note.**

The anecdote is real and the citation is correct. Diogenes Laertius VI.38 is the standard source for the exchange at the Craneum, where Alexander offers Diogenes any boon and Diogenes replies "Stand out of my light" (Perseus text: "Stand out of my light"; Wikiquote/Plutarch tradition: "Stand a little out of my sunshine"). The draft renders it "stand out of his sunlight" — that is an accurate paraphrase of the sense and is within the range of translations in circulation; it is not a fabrication. The parallel source is Plutarch, *Alexander* 14, which confirms the story has double ancient attestation — DL VI.38 is the right primary citation.

**Framing check:** the draft correctly frames it as "the much-later tradition" and uses the conditional "told Alexander the Great … only to stand out of his sunlight [VERIFY …]". This is the right framing: the anecdote is not contemporary (DL wrote ~3rd century AD, ~600 years after Diogenes); Plutarch is earlier (~1st/2nd c AD) but still hundreds of years on. Calling it "late legend" or "much-later tradition" is accurate and appropriate.

**The [VERIFY] tag may be CLEARED.** No correction needed to the substance or framing. The citation "Diogenes Laertius VI.38" is correct.

## Item 2: Xenophon *Symposium* 2.10 — Xanthippe / "get along with anyone"

**Status: CONFIRMED — substance and attribution correct.**

Perseus (Xenophon, *Symposium* 2.10) contains exactly this exchange. Antisthenes asks Socrates why he doesn't train Xanthippe himself instead of living with "the hardest woman to get along with of all the women there are." Socrates responds by analogy to horsemen who choose high-spirited horses: "My course is similar. Mankind at large is what I wish to deal and associate with; and so I have got her, well assured that if I can endure her, I shall have no difficulty in my relations with all the rest of human kind."

The draft paraphrase — "he chose a difficult partner on purpose, reasoning that if he could learn to get along with *her*, he could get along with anyone at all" — accurately captures the Xenophon text. Attribution to Xenophon's *Symposium* (not DL legend) is correct. The contrast the draft draws ("the contemporary Xenophon presents her quite differently … in his *Symposium* he has Socrates explain…") is accurate: this is unambiguously Xenophon, not the later DL caricature tradition.

## Item 3: Four corrected quotes

### 3a: Prytaneum epigraph (ch5) — "no reward so fitting"

**Status: CONFIRMED with minor wording flag — NOT a factual error, both phrasings are in circulation.**

The draft epigraph reads: *"There can be no reward so fitting as maintenance in the Prytaneum…"*

The MIT Internet Classics Archive (the most widely cited online Jowett text) gives: *"There can be no **more fitting** reward than maintenance in the Prytaneum…"* — but the lexundria.com rendering of Jowett gives "no reward so **fitting**." Both versions are traceable to different print editions of Jowett's translation, which he revised multiple times. The sense is identical. This is **not a factual error** and does not need correction, but the author should be aware that "no more fitting reward" is the more commonly reproduced Jowett phrasing if source consistency matters.

### 3b: Cicero V.4 — paraphrase, no quote marks

**Status: CONFIRMED.**

The Cicero passage appears in the `brk` section: *"Cicero, looking back across three hundred years, summed it up: Socrates called philosophy down from the heavens, settled it in the cities and even the homes of men, and compelled it to ask about life and right and wrong."* No quotation marks are used; it is clearly attributed as a paraphrase summary. The Latin of Tusculan Disputations V.10/V.4 is: *"Socrates primus philosophiam devocavit e caelo…"* — the draft paraphrase faithfully captures all three clauses (down from heaven / into cities and homes / ask about life and right and wrong). Correct.

### 3c: Soul-care quote — "and your properties" vs Jowett's "or your properties"

**Status: WRONG — MUST-FIX (minor but it is a verbatim quotation).**

In ch4 body prose, Socrates is directly quoted: *"persuading you all, old and young alike, not to take thought for your persons **and** your properties, but first and chiefly to care about the greatest improvement of the soul."*

Jowett's Apology 30a reads: *"not to take thought for your persons **or** your properties."* The conjunctive "and" in the draft is an error introduced in transcription — Jowett uses the disjunctive "or." Because this is presented as a verbatim Plato/Jowett quotation (inside quote marks in prose), it must match the source. Fix: change "and" to "or."

*(The ch4 epigraph — "…first and chiefly to care about the greatest improvement of the soul." — is a partial quote using an ellipsis and does not contain the "and/or" word; it is correct as is.)*
