### Tag 1 — Hook: scientific revolution / Copernicus / Galileo

**R2 text (hook, lines 23–30):**
> "The old framework had been Aristotle's, taught in every university: a cosmos of
> fixed spheres with the earth at the center, things moving because it was their
> nature to seek their proper place. Then Copernicus moved the earth, Galileo's
> telescope and falling bodies broke the Aristotelian physics, and a new picture
> took hold of a universe running on matter, motion, and mathematics rather than
> purposes. … By the 1630s the old certainties were rubble, and 'starting from
> scratch' was not a stunt but a necessity."

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

**Sources:** Britannica (Galileo); SEP (Galileo); NASA Earth Observatory (Orbits
History); World History Encyclopedia (Astronomy in the Scientific Revolution).

All specifics check out:
- *Aristotelian framework*: the fixed-spheres geocentric cosmos, with natural-place
  motion, is accurately described as the taught university framework.
- *Copernicus moved the earth*: the heliocentric proposal (*De revolutionibus*, 1543)
  is unambiguously accurate.
- *Galileo's telescope*: his telescopic observations of Jupiter's moons, Venus phases,
  and the Moon's surface are well-documented challenges to the Aristotelian/Ptolemaic
  picture.
- *Galileo's law of falling bodies*: his mathematical formulation of the law of falling
  bodies (published in *Two New Sciences*, 1638, though worked out earlier) directly
  undermined Aristotelian natural-motion physics.
- *New picture of matter, motion, and mathematics*: the standard characterization of
  the mechanical philosophy that replaced Aristotle.
- *"By the 1630s"*: Galileo's condemnation was 1633; Descartes' *Discourse* was 1637.
  The framing is accurate: the revolution was the backdrop when Descartes published.

One minor precision note: the text says "Galileo's telescope and falling bodies broke
the Aristotelian physics" — strictly, the *Two New Sciences* (the falling-bodies work)
appeared in 1638, one year after the *Discourse*. But Galileo had established and
circulated the results earlier, and by 1637 the Aristotelian framework was widely
contested; the text's framing is accurate at era altitude.

**No change required.**

### Tag 5 — Ch 5: Francis Bacon as empiricist forerunner

**R2 text (Ch 5, lines 572–576):**

**Sources:** SEP (Francis Bacon); Britannica (Francis Bacon); UBC physics notes

- Francis Bacon (1561–1626): dates confirmed.

  says he "named" a method. This is precisely accurate: Bacon articulated and
  promoted the inductive observational method without developing a full theory of
  mind, which is what the draft says Locke added.

### Tag 6 — Ch 6: Hume's "Of National Characters" racist footnote

**R2 text (Ch 6, lines 793–801):**
> "In a footnote to his essay 'Of National Characters' (1748), and one he sharpened
> rather than softened in a later revision, Hume wrote that he was 'apt to suspect
> the negroes, and in general all the other species of men... to be naturally
> inferior to the whites.'"

**Verdict: ❌ WRONG on the revision-direction claim — MUST-FIX**

**Sources:** David Hume Institute blog; davidhume.org (full text, NC 20n6.1);
Siris blog (full textual history of the footnote across editions); ResearchGate
(Hume's Revised Racism Revisited); muse.jhu.edu (article 383246).

**What the sources establish:**

1. *The footnote was NOT in the original 1748 publication.* The essay "Of National
   Characters" was first published in 1748, but the racial footnote was **added in
   the 1753 edition** — not present in 1748. (The draft says the essay is "1748" in
   the parenthetical, which is the title/essay date, so that is not wrong by itself;
   but the parenthetical implies the footnote was in the 1748 original, which it was
   not.)

2. *The 1753 version is the original footnote form*, reading: "I am apt to suspect
   the negroes, and in general all the other species of men (for there are four or
   five different kinds) to be naturally inferior to the whites." This is the wording
   the draft quotes.

3. *The revision NARROWED (softened) the claim, not sharpened it.* The 1777
   posthumous revision removed "and in general all the other species of men" —
   narrowing the claim to Black people alone — and changed "never was a civilized
   nation of that complexion" to "scarcely ever was." The revised version is
   *narrower* in scope (no longer sweeping in "all other species") and slightly softer
   in wording ("scarcely ever" rather than "never"), though the core racial hierarchy
   claim about Black people was retained.

4. *The draft says Hume "sharpened rather than softened" the footnote in revision.*
   This is the reverse of what happened: the 1777 revision narrowed and softened the
   wording. If anything, the revision made the claim *less* sweeping (dropped all
   other non-white groups) while retaining the core anti-Black claim.

**MUST-FIX.** The claim that Hume "sharpened rather than softened" the footnote in
revision is factually wrong. The revision narrowed the scope (from all non-white
people to Black people only) and softened one key word ("scarcely ever" vs. "never").

**Recommended fix:** Change the framing of the revision to accurately reflect what
happened. Two options:

*Option A (simple excision):* Drop the revision claim entirely. The core fact — that
Hume published this racial hierarchy claim under his own name — stands without
needing to characterize the direction of the revision.

> "In a footnote to his essay 'Of National Characters,' first added in a 1753
> revision of the essay, Hume wrote that he was 'apt to suspect the negroes, and in
> general all the other species of men... to be naturally inferior to the whites.'"

*Option B (accurate revision direction):* Correct the direction.

> "In a footnote to his essay 'Of National Characters,' added in 1753, Hume wrote
> that he was 'apt to suspect the negroes, and in general all the other species of
> men... to be naturally inferior to the whites.' A later revision narrowed but did
> not retract the core claim."

Option A is cleaner for narrative flow. Option B is more precise but slightly more
complex. Both are accurate.

**Additional note:** The year "1748" in the draft's parenthetical — "(1748)" — refers
to the essay's original publication date, which is correct. But readers may infer the
footnote was in the 1748 text. A coordinator note: if precision is desired, the clean
fix is to say the footnote was "added in 1753 to his 1748 essay 'Of National
Characters.'" This is accurate and removes any inference that the footnote appeared
in the 1748 original.

### Tag 7 — Ch 7: du Châtelet and the vis viva dispute

**R2 text (Ch 7, lines 912–919):**
> "The dispute of the day was over what quantity nature conserves when bodies
> collide, the so-called *vis viva* or 'living force': the Newtonian-Cartesian camp
> measured it one way (mass times velocity), the Leibnizians another (mass times
> velocity *squared*). Du Châtelet sided with the Leibnizians, against the prevailing
> consensus, and she turned out to be right: the squared quantity is what we now call
> kinetic energy."

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED — with one precision note**

**Sources:** ResearchGate (Vis Viva Dispute 1959 article); ResearchGate (Leibniz's
Quantity of Force: A 'Heresy'? Emilie du Châtelet's *Institutions* in the Context of
the Vis Viva Controversy); Wikipedia (Kinetic energy); IOP (History of energy);
Medium / Vis Viva Controversy history; Urania (du Châtelet scientific works).

All main claims confirmed:
- *Newtonian-Cartesian camp = mv; Leibnizian camp = mv²*: ✅ CONFIRMED. "The vis
  viva controversy was a dispute about whether the Cartesian quantity mv or the
  Leibnizian quantity mv² was the true measure of force." (ResearchGate, 1959 article.)
- *Du Châtelet sided with the Leibnizians*: ✅ CONFIRMED. Her *Institutions de
  Physique* (1740) argued for the Leibnizian mv².
- *Against the prevailing consensus*: ✅ CONFIRMED. The Dutch physicist Willem
  's Gravesande experimentally showed mv² was correct around 1720, but the Newtonian
  camp remained dominant, and du Châtelet's 1740 alignment with the Leibnizian
  position was against the mainstream in France.
- *She turned out to be right — kinetic energy*: ✅ CONFIRMED. The kinetic energy
  formula is ½mv² (the half being a later refinement by Coriolis in 1829); the
  squared-velocity relationship is what was vindicated. Wikipedia (Kinetic energy)
  and the IOP history of energy confirm this.

**Precision note on 's Gravesande:** The revision notes flag "if the 's Gravesande
experimental work detail is later added." The draft's r2 text does not mention
's Gravesande by name; it says du Châtelet "sided with the Leibnizians" and was
right, which is accurate as written. If a future integrator wants to add 's Gravesande,
his role was to provide the experimental confirmation that du Châtelet drew on — both
are accurate characterizations.

**No change required.**
