## Ordered gloss-gap list — MUST-FIX on top

### MUST-FIX

**MF-1 — Throughline: "substance" used before it is defined.**
The throughline opens: "the world is ultimately made of simple substances, partless and immaterial." This is the very first sentence of the text. A 15-year-old does not know what "substance" means in a philosophical sense. In ordinary English a substance is a material (like a chemical). In philosophy it means "a basic, self-standing thing that exists in its own right, not as a property of something else." The distinction matters because the argument of the whole book depends on monads being substances (independent things) rather than properties or parts of something larger. The word is used throughout the throughline and hook without ever being defined.
**Fix:** In the throughline or at the latest in Ch 1 para 5 (where section §1–2 of the Monadology is discussed), add one parenthetical: "simple substances (self-standing basic things that exist in their own right, not as qualities of something else)." Subsequent uses then carry.

**MF-2 — Throughline / Ch 7: "pre-established harmony" used in the throughline without any gloss.**
The throughline uses "soul and body keep perfect time without ever touching, like two clocks synchronized at the creation" — that image is good. But three lines later the throughline says nothing about what the technical term itself is, and Ch 7 para 3 introduces the phrase "pre-established harmony" as the name of the doctrine but provides only a restatement of what was said in the "two clocks" image. A newcomer who reads Ch 7 and hits "This carries the system to its most distinctive doctrine, pre-established harmony" gets no new grip on the term; they get the same image again. The term needs a one-line name-plus-definition at its formal first use.
**Fix (Ch 7 para 3):** At first use of "pre-established harmony" add: "pre-established harmony (the doctrine that God synchronized every monad's internal script at creation so that soul and body — and all monads — always agree without ever affecting one another)." The image of the two clocks is fine as illustration but cannot do double-duty as the definition.

**MF-3 — Hook / Ch 7: "final causes" and "efficient causes" — used without definition.**
Ch 7 para 4 closes with: "souls act by the laws of final causes, through ends and means, and bodies act by the laws of efficient causes, through motions." These are Aristotelian technical terms that appear here without any explanation. An efficient cause is the push or event that produces an effect (the fire that burns). A final cause is the purpose or goal toward which something aims (the fire that is set to cook food). The distinction is load-bearing: the whole closing argument of Ch 7 (that the realm of nature and the realm of grace can run on different logic and still agree) depends on this. A newcomer reading "the laws of final causes, through ends and means" has no idea what that means.
**Fix:** Add a gloss in Ch 7 para 4 at first use: "final causes (goals or purposes — the 'in order to' kind of explanation) … efficient causes (pushes and mechanisms — the 'because of' kind of explanation)." Alternatively, introduce the distinction earlier, in Ch 4 para 1, where the principles of reasoning are laid down and could carry a brief note about why two kinds of explanation matter.

**MF-4 — Ch 2: "apperception" defined correctly but the distinction from perception is murky at first use.**
Ch 3 para 1 (the chapter on the hierarchy of monads) states: "Perception is the having of a state that pictures the world. Apperception is the further thing of being aware that one is having it, the reflective notice a mind takes of its own contents." This is a good attempt. But the epigraph for Ch 3 uses both terms before this gloss arrives, and even within the paragraph the phrasing "reflective notice a mind takes of its own contents" is slightly abstract for a 15-year-old. The key test: can the reader say in one sentence what the difference is? Something like: "perception is having an inner picture of the world; apperception is noticing that you are having the picture." The current gloss passes but thinly; the "reflective notice a mind takes of its own contents" phrase is one clause removed from plain English and may not land.
**Fix (MUST-FIX because the rest of Ch 3 depends on this distinction):** In Ch 3 para 1, after "the reflective notice a mind takes of its own contents," add: "Most of us, most of the time, are not watching ourselves think; we just think. That watching-yourself-think is apperception." The current text implies this but does not say it plainly.

**MF-5 — Ch 2: "appetition" introduced without a working definition.**
Ch 2 para 4 introduces the word "appetition" with: "The internal force that carries it from one perception on to the next he calls appetition, an inner striving." The phrase "inner striving" is adequate, but "appetition" is an unusual word that many adult readers have never encountered. A 15-year-old will memorize it as a vocabulary item without grasping what work it does. The question a newcomer will have is: striving toward what? The answer (toward the next perception, driven by the monad's own nature — not desire for anything external) is not given. Without this, the student of a monad "full of bodily states and notices nothing" analogy (coming one paragraph later in Ch 3) does not connect.
**Fix:** In Ch 2 para 4, at the word "appetition," add: "appetition (an inner drive — not toward any external object, since the monad has no windows, but toward the next state that its own nature produces, like a spring unwinding step by step)."

**MF-6 — Ch 2 final para: "identity of indiscernibles" — used as a named doctrine without a plain-language statement of what it says.**
Ch 2 para 5 ends: "This is the seed of one of his most striking claims, the identity of indiscernibles, the doctrine that nature never makes perfect duplicates." The phrase "identity of indiscernibles" is introduced and then immediately restated as "nature never makes perfect duplicates," which is a good plain restatement. But the draft uses the formal name first, then the restatement — and a 15-year-old encountering "identity of indiscernibles" cold will need the plain version first. Additionally, the logical skeleton ("if two things share every property, they are not two things but one") is not stated, only illustrated by inference.
**Fix:** Reverse the order: "this is the seed of one of his most striking claims: nature never makes perfect duplicates (what philosophers call the identity of indiscernibles — if two things are alike in every single way, down to every perception, there is no fact that makes them two, so they must be one)." That way the idea lands, then the name follows.

**MF-7 — Ch 3: Descartes cross-reference assumes prior reading.**
Ch 3 para 4 says: "Descartes had drawn a hard line through the living world: human minds think and are conscious, while animals are mere machines with nothing going on inside, no perception at all (the [Descartes](/philosophy/thinker/descartes) read)." The parenthetical link to the Descartes page is a reasonable gesture, but within this sentence the phrase "the Descartes read" is treated as a sufficient identification. A newcomer who is reading this as a standalone WORK read has no idea who Descartes is. The sentence attributes a view to Descartes but gives no identification — not when he lived, not that he was a philosopher Leibniz was directly responding to, not even a one-line "who."
**Fix (MUST-FIX because the Descartes contrast is load-bearing for the whole Ch 3 argument):** Expand the parenthetical or add a clause: "Descartes, the French philosopher of the generation before Leibniz who is credited with the sharp division between thinking mind and mechanical body." The link is fine to keep; the identification cannot be outsourced to it.

**MF-8 — Ch 3: "spirits" as Leibniz's technical term.**
Ch 3 para 3 says: "At the top of the ladder are the monads he calls spirits, the rational souls." The word "spirits" here is Leibniz's own technical term for the highest grade of monads, and the draft signals this by saying "the monads he calls spirits." But a newcomer may take "spirits" as a synonym for ghosts or supernatural beings, especially because the sentence follows talk of "souls." The technical meaning (rational self-aware monads — human and divine — as distinct from mere animal souls) needs to be anchored.
**Fix:** Add a brief gloss: "spirits (his term for rational, self-aware monads — the highest grade, which includes human souls and God — not a supernatural category but simply the clearest mirrors)." One phrase removes the ghost-association.

**MF-9 — Ch 4: "truths of reason" and "truths of fact" introduced as category names before the distinction is explained.**
Ch 4 para 1 says: "the principle of contradiction … governs one whole class of truths, the truths of reason, the necessary ones, true in every possible world." The category name "truths of reason" appears as the label for a concept that is then explained in the following sentences. For a newcomer this is backwards: the name arrives before the concept is set up. Likewise "truths of fact" in para 2 is introduced as if the reader already knows this is a philosophical category.
**Fix:** Give the concept first, then the label. In para 1: "… one whole class of truths — the necessary ones, true in every possible world, which he calls truths of reason." In para 2: "… the contingent ones, true in this world but not in every possible world, which he calls truths of fact." This is a small reordering but a meaningful one for a newcomer.

**MF-10 — Ch 4 / Ch 5: The leap from "the series of contingent things needs a sufficient reason outside itself" to "a necessary being must exist" needs one explicit bridging sentence.**
Ch 4 para 4 makes the move: "The total series of contingent things must therefore have its sufficient reason outside the series, in something that is not itself contingent, something whose existence needs no further reason because it exists necessarily, by its own nature." This is the most important logical step in the whole book and it is also the place where a 15-year-old is most likely to lose the argument. The bridge between "needs a reason outside itself" and "exists necessarily" is implicit. The text does not explain what "exists necessarily" means. A newcomer will read "something that exists necessarily" and not know what that means. Does it mean "something that always existed"? "Something that cannot fail to exist"? "Something that is its own reason for existing"?
**Fix:** Before "something whose existence needs no further reason," insert: "something that does not depend on anything else for its existence, that cannot fail to be — necessary here means the opposite of contingent: not 'existing because of some prior cause' but 'existing just because it is what it is.'" This is the key conceptual distinction the ontological argument and the rest of Ch 5 rest on. Without it, the word "necessary" sounds like an intensity claim ("very important") rather than a logical category.

**MF-11 — Ch 5: "ontological argument" used as a named doctrine without explanation of what the argument is.**
Ch 5 para 2 says: "in section forty-five Leibniz states the ontological argument almost in passing, as a thing that simply follows: God alone has the privilege that, if such a being is possible, then it must exist, because its existence follows from its essence." The ontological argument is named and the core claim is stated. However, the phrase "ontological argument" is a philosophy-class term that carries enormous weight in the history of ideas, and a 15-year-old newcomer reading this will either (a) not have heard the term, and need it labeled as a named argument, or (b) have heard it vaguely as "the argument that God must exist" with no more content. The draft gives the claim but does not tell the reader what makes this argument distinctive as a TYPE of argument (one that tries to prove God exists from the concept of God alone, without reference to any experience of the world).
**Fix:** Add one gloss at "ontological argument": "the ontological argument (the attempt to prove God's existence from the very definition of God, without needing to look at any evidence from the world — if a necessary being is so much as possible, the argument runs, then it must be real, because a merely possible necessary being is a contradiction)." The current text gives the substance of the claim but never tells the reader why this argument has a famous name or what type of argument it is. The word "ontological" (from Greek for "being") never gets any purchase without this.

**MF-12 — Ch 5: "eminently" used in a technical philosophical sense with only a partial gloss.**
Ch 5 para 1: "The word eminently is doing precise work. It does not mean God contains the changes the way a box contains its contents. It means God contains them as a source contains what flows from it, the way the cause of many effects holds those effects in a higher and unified form." This is a genuine effort. But the gloss stops at the metaphor (a source containing what flows from it) without stating the scholastic technical meaning. "Eminently" in this tradition means "contains in a superior mode" — the cause contains the effect in a richer, higher form. Without this, the "higher and unified form" phrase is suggestive but not quite transparent. The bigger risk is that the reader thinks "eminently" just means "prominently."
**Fix:** Add after "higher and unified form": "—not as contents in a box but as an architect 'has' the building before it exists, in a richer form than the stones have it." The box-vs-architect distinction makes the two senses concrete. Alternatively, simply define: "eminently (in a higher and more concentrated form — the way heat 'has' fire in itself before the fire is struck)."

### SHOULD-FIX

**SF-1 — Throughline: "plenum" is in the gate-defined terms list but does not appear in the draft at all.**
The task brief listed "plenum" as a term to flag. Leibniz's concept of the plenum (that space is fully packed, no true vacuum) does not appear in this work read. This is an absence, not an error — the *Monadology* itself does not centrally use the term. No fix needed in the draft, but this is noted: if the thinker read covers the plenum, a brief cross-pointer here might help readers who arrive from that page.

**SF-2 — Ch 1 para 2: "Prince Eugene of Savoy" — named but only identified as "the great military commander."**
"Prince Eugene of Savoy, the great military commander" — this is a one-line identification, which is gate-adequate. But a 15-year-old may not know who Prince Eugene was even with that label (he was one of the most celebrated generals in Europe, commander in the War of the Spanish Succession). The draft's purpose in naming him is narrow (to distinguish the two Vienna texts), so the current one-liner is sufficient. SHOULD-FIX only if the thinker read gives more context that readers might want here.

**SF-3 — Ch 2 para 2: "section seven is the hinge of the entire book" — structural announcement.**
"Section seven is the hinge of the entire book" is a mild throat-clearing phrase. The gate-2 voice rules flag reader-directed structural announcements; this one is close to the banned "here is the part people miss." It could be cut ("Section seven draws the conclusion that names the system: the monads have no windows") without any loss. Not a clarity issue — flagged here as a secondary gate-2 observation that the clarity audit noticed.

**SF-4 — Ch 6 para 3: "theodicy" defined well but late.**
"Theodicy" appears in Ch 6 para 4 with a full parenthetical definition ("an attempt to justify the ways of God in the face of evil"). It is defined adequately at point of use, which is correct. However, the term also appears in the draft's file header comments (where it references the Theodicy of 1710) and in the throughline's mention of Leibniz's "big published book." A newcomer who has been wondering what the Theodicy is for the whole read gets the definition only in Ch 6. This is passable but barely. SHOULD-FIX: a one-phrase gloss on the Theodicy's title in Ch 1 ("the Theodicy — his 1710 book on why a good God allows evil") would close the gap earlier.

**SF-5 — Ch 6 para 6: "Panglossian" defined only implicitly.**
"Panglossian still means fatuous optimism." This is the only definition offered. A newcomer who has read Voltaire will get it; one who has not will get "fatuous" (which might itself be new) but will not quite understand why one novel created a lasting adjective. The draft has told the Pangloss story fully in para 5, so the payoff here works — this is a genuine SHOULD-FIX not a MUST-FIX. A one-phrase anchor ("Panglossian — the adjective the novel burned into the language — still means...") would make the coinage explicit.

**SF-6 — Ch 7 para 5: "City of God" — used as Leibniz's term but the phrase is also Augustine's famous title.**
"What Leibniz calls, in section eighty-six, the City of God." A newcomer who has read the faith-reason era article (and the Augustine chapter) will recognize this phrase as the title of Augustine's major work. The draft does not note the echo. This is not a clarity error — the City of God as a concept (a moral community under God) is coherent from context. But the coincidence with Augustine's title may cause a moment of confusion ("wait, is this a reference to Augustine?"). A brief parenthetical ("borrowing Augustine's own term") would close the potential confusion and also serve as a correct intellectual genealogy note.

**SF-7 — Hook para 2: "immaterial points he calls monads" — "immaterial" not defined.**
"Immaterial points" — immaterial means without matter, non-physical. A 15-year-old will likely understand this from context, but the contrast (material = takes up space / immaterial = does not take up space) is the crux of the whole system and could use one explicit anchor. This is a SHOULD-FIX because the break block's argument about divisibility (para 2) effectively defines immaterial by implication — but a one-word gloss ("immaterial, meaning they take up no space and have no physical size") at first use would prevent any reader stumbling.

## Summary assessment

The draft is an unusually well-written and careful piece of philosophical exposition, and most of it would be followable by a determined 15-year-old. The throughline, break block, and Ch 1 are very close to CLEAR; Chs 6 is the strongest chapter by clarity standards (the Pangloss / theodicy section is a model). The book's structure — each conclusion resting on a stated premise — is reflected in the writing, and the "staircase" metaphor the hook uses is honored.

The MUST-FIX list (12 items) clusters in three areas:

1. **Technical terms used before they are defined** (substance in the throughline; truths of reason / truths of fact as category names in Ch 4; appetition, the identity of indiscernibles, "spirits" in Ch 2–3; "eminently" in Ch 5; final/efficient causes in Ch 7). These are all fixable with one-phrase inline glosses at point of first use.

2. **The single largest logical leap in the book is underserved** (MF-10): the move from "the series of contingent things needs a sufficient reason outside itself" to "therefore a necessary being exists" is the pivot on which Ch 4 and all of Ch 5 turn, and the draft's explanation of what "necessarily existing" means is implicit rather than stated. A newcomer who does not already know this distinction will follow the words but lose the argument. This is the highest-priority fix.

3. **One named philosophical argument is not explained as an argument** (MF-11): the ontological argument is named and its claim is stated, but a newcomer is not told what TYPE of argument it is or why it has a famous name. Without this, the phrase "the ontological argument" sounds like a chapter heading, not a notorious logical move.

4. **One person introduced without a who-they-are** (MF-7): Descartes appears in Ch 3 as a named contrast figure with no identification beyond a hyperlink to another page.

None of the MUST-FIX items require structural rewriting; all are inline-gloss additions or short bridge sentences. No chapter is LOST. The pre-established harmony, the best-of-all-possible-worlds, and the no-windows arguments are all walked in sufficient step-by-step detail for a newcomer to follow the logic without gaps.

**Overall gate verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (PASS-WITH-FIXES). 12 MUST-FIX items, all mechanical. No chapter requires structural rewrite on clarity grounds. MF-10 (the necessary being / necessary existence distinction) is the highest-stakes fix.**
