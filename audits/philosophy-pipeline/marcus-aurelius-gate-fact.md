**Draft text (Ch-6, in substance, not verbatim — correctly not quoted directly):**

**Verified Long text (Lexundria XI.3 fetch + Stoic Breviary 11.3 page, 2026-06-13):**
> "What a soul that is which is ready, if at any moment it must be separated from the body,
> and ready either to be extinguished or dispersed or continue to exist; but so that this
> readiness comes from a man's own judgment, not from mere obstinacy, as with the
> Christians, but considerately and with dignity and in a way to persuade another, without
> tragic show."
> — *Meditations* XI.3, trans. George Long

**Critical finding — INTERPOLATION QUESTION:**

The phrase "as with the Christians" in Long's text IS present in the manuscript tradition
Long translated from — but it is **widely flagged by modern scholars as almost certainly a
later interpolation** (a marginal gloss by an early Christian or anti-Christian reader that
migrated into the text). Gregory Hays's Modern Library translation introduction states the
passage is "almost certainly a later interpolation" and that "there is no reason to think
Marcus had the Christians in mind here." An academic paper specifically addressing this
passage — "Questioning the 'Opposition' and *Interpretatio Christiana* in Marcus Aurelius
(Med. 11.3)" — questions whether the passage was ever Marcus's own wording. The fact pack's
§5 note flags this accurately ("verify Long's exact wording... before quoting this passage
in prose") but does NOT mention the interpolation question.

**The draft handles this correctly** — it does NOT quote XI.3 verbatim; it uses substance
only ("'not from mere obstinacy' of the kind he associates with them"). This is exactly the
right call given the textual uncertainty.

**Assessment (two-part):**

(a) **Long wording verification:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Long's text does say "not from mere
obstinacy, as with the Christians." The word "Galileans" does NOT appear in XI.3 in Long —
that term is used by the Emperor Julian, not Marcus. The fact pack §5 note's reference to
"Galileans" as a possible reading reflects some secondary scholarship but is NOT in Long's
XI.3 text. The draft correctly does not use "Galileans." ✅

(b) **The interpolation issue is a MISSING FRAMING FLAG.** The draft says "a passage
*usually read* as a swipe at Christians" — which gestures at interpretive controversy but
does NOT tell the reader that many modern scholars think the phrase "as with the Christians"
was NOT written by Marcus at all. This is a framing gap: calling it "his own notes" that
"regard dying for the Christian faith with cool contempt" is stronger than the textual
evidence warrants if the phrase is a gloss. See FINDING F-1 below.

**Tag resolution:** ✅ Long wording confirmed; Christians reference is in the Long text;
"Galileans" is NOT in XI.3 in Long (correct that the draft avoids it). But an additional
finding is generated — see F-1 under Framing below.

#### [VERIFY] 3 — Empire population ~60 million (hook, Ch-0)

**Draft text:**
> "...master of an empire of something like sixty million people..."

**Verification (web research, 2026-06-13):**

Scholarly estimates for the Roman Empire's population in the 2nd century AD range widely.
The Wikipedia "Demography of the Roman Empire" article summarizes:
- Estimates span **59 to 76 million** for the 1st–2nd centuries
- Kyle Harper's leading contemporary estimate: **75 million** at the empire's peak
  (just before the Antonine Plague struck around AD 165)
- Harper models approximately 60 million under Augustus, growing at ~0.1%/year to reach
  ~75 million by Marcus's reign
- The "70–75 million" range represents the strongest modern scholarly consensus for Marcus's
  era
- The population then *declined* from the Antonine Plague

**Assessment:** 🟡 SOFT WARNING — the "sixty million" figure is conservative versus the
current scholarly consensus (Harper and others put the peak at ~70–75 million). It is not
wrong in the way a made-up number is wrong — it is within the lower end of the accepted
range — but it undersells the scale. The fact pack correctly flagged it as needing
verification.

**Recommended fix (SHOULD-FIX):** Change to "something like seventy million people" or
"somewhere between sixty and seventy-five million people, depending on who is counting"
(the imprecision is honest since estimates genuinely range). "Sixty million" is technically
defensible but is the old, lower figure; modern scholarship favors 70–75 million at the
2nd-century peak.

**Tag resolution:** 🟡 Not wrong, but the preferred modern figure is higher. See F-2.

### FR-1 — The Lyon martyrs / XI.3 "cool contempt" framing ❌ MUST-FIX

**Draft text (Ch-6):**
> "his own notebook is not kind to people who die for their faith... [VERIFY: Long's exact
> wording of Meditations XI.3]... So the fair statement is this: Christians were persecuted
> and killed under his reign; whether that reflected his personal orders or local officials
> acting under general Roman policy is genuinely debated by historians; and his own private
> notes regard dying for the Christian faith with cool contempt rather than respect."

**Problem:** The draft correctly does not quote XI.3 verbatim, but the surrounding framing
attributes the "cool contempt" remark directly and without qualification to Marcus's own
"private notes." This is stronger than the textual evidence warrants. Modern scholarship —
including Gregory Hays's widely-used translation introduction — judges the phrase "as with
the Christians" in XI.3 to be "almost certainly a later interpolation" (a marginal gloss by
a later reader that migrated into the text over the manuscript tradition). An academic paper
specifically challenges the assumption that Marcus was even contrasting himself with
Christians here. The phrase is present in Long's text and in the manuscript Long used, but
its authenticity as Marcus's own words is seriously disputed by the field.

Framing it flatly as "his own private notes regard dying for the Christian faith with cool
contempt" without noting this textual debate is a DISTORTED picture — it presents as settled
something that is contested at the manuscript level.

**Required fix:** Add a one-sentence note that scholars dispute whether "as with the
Christians" is actually Marcus's wording at all — it may be a later reader's gloss that
drifted into the manuscript. Something like: "The one passage that seems to say this
directly (XI.3) is in the manuscript Long used, but modern scholars including the Hays
translation introduction judge the phrase 'as with the Christians' to be almost certainly a
later interpolation, not Marcus's own words — so the contempt attributed to him from that
note must be held loosely."

This does not require removing the passage's discussion — it is relevant and honest to
mention. It requires ONE caveat sentence so the reader understands the textual situation.

**Severity:** ❌ MUST-FIX. Framing manuscript-contested material as "his own private notes"
without caveat crosses the line from fair to distorted.
