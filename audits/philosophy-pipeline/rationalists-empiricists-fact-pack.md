# Fact pack — "The rationalists and the empiricists" (Philosophy era 3, kind = ERA)

**Scope:** Western philosophy from Descartes (1637) through Hume (d. 1776) — the
century and a half in which thinkers tried to establish what reason alone can know
(the rationalists) and what experience can actually deliver (the empiricists), and
ended with Hume's fork nearly splitting philosophy clean in two.

## 0. Chapter-arc assessment (requested flag)

**Proposed arc: 7 chapters.** The material argues for this structure (finalized
with rationale):

1. **"The man in the stove-heated room" — Descartes** (~1637–49): method of doubt,
   the evil genius, the cogito, mind-body dualism, the wax argument, God as
   guarantor of clear and distinct ideas. **Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia enters
   here** — her May 1643 letter is the interaction problem in its sharpest form
   and Descartes' most famous non-answer; she belongs in the Descartes chapter,
   not a sidebar.

2. **"God or Nature" — the rationalist pole I: Spinoza** (~1661–77): the geometric
   method, substance monism (one substance = God = Nature), the two attributes we
   can know (thought and extension), determinism vs. freedom, the intellectual love
   of God, *sub specie aeternitatis*, the *Ethics* as worked example. The 1656
   excommunication (herem) as the era's first act.

3. **"The windowless clocks" — the rationalist pole II: Leibniz** (~1686–1714):
   monads (the "no windows" image), pre-established harmony (why the clocks run
   in sync), the principle of sufficient reason, the principle of
   non-contradiction, the best of all possible worlds (and its argument, not just
   the Candide parody). The Spinoza–Leibniz contrast: they start from the same
   inheritance and reach opposite conclusions about substance.

4. **"Nasty, brutish, and short" — the political turn: Hobbes and Locke** (~1651–
   1689): philosophy's descent into politics; the state of nature (two versions),
   the social contract (two very different ones), the commonwealth, Locke's consent
   and property, Locke's toleration. **Folded here, not a ghetto chapter:** these
   are empiricist moves applied to the human animal in society. Bridge: Locke's
   *Essay* on the same page as his *Two Treatises* — the empiricist epistemology
   and the contract politics are the same mind at work.

5. **"White paper" — Locke's epistemology and Berkeley's escape** (~1689–1710):
   Locke's Essay in depth (white paper / tabula rasa mythology, the actual "empty
   cabinet," primary/secondary qualities, the limits of knowledge), then Berkeley
   as the thinker who takes Locke's empiricism seriously and finds it leads
   somewhere Locke didn't intend — immaterialism (*esse est percipi*). **Chapter
   arc: Berkeley is not a crank; he is Locke's most rigorous student.**

6. **"Woken from its dogmatic slumber" — Hume's fork I** (~1739–48): the bundle
   self, the missing shade of blue, the is/ought gap, causation and constant
   conjunction, "reason is the slave of the passions." The Treatise as the
   full-system version; the Enquiry as the refined public statement.

7. **"Commit it then to the flames" — Hume's fork II and the era's question**
   (~1748–76): the design argument (the Dialogues), miracles, religion handled
   carefully (never calling himself an atheist — explain why and why that matters),
   Hume's fork as a clean closing image. The handoff to era 4: what Kant does with
   the question Hume leaves open. **Émilie du Châtelet** enters here as the
   person who had already built the bridge — Newtonian mechanics + Leibnizian
   metaphysics, published 1740 and 1759, while Hume was writing — and the era's
   canonical example of a thinker dropped from the story because she was a woman.

**Note on structure:** The rationalist/empiricist split is the textbook frame, and
the chapter arc respects it — but §0-assessment flag: the split is partly
retrospective. Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz, Locke, Berkeley, and Hume are all
responding to the same post-Galilean question (what can reason establish with
certainty?); the "school" labels solidify with Kant's *Critique*. The break block
(§5) should frame it as one argument across six minds, not two rival camps — the
camp model, taught for two centuries, makes Hume look like the empiricist answering
the rationalists, when he's actually reading Locke.

## 2. Position summaries (steelmanned; ⚠️ = potted-summary trap pre-flagged)

### Descartes

The opening move: a soldier in a stove-heated room in winter, 1619. Descartes'
own *Discourse on the Method* (Part II) describes withdrawing from the world to
think — the "poêle" (heated stove-room) detail is in the French original; the
Veitch translation suppresses the word "stove" but describes the winter retreat.
**Ship as his own account** (§4 ledger): "as Descartes tells it in the Discourse."

**Method of doubt:** not skepticism for its own sake — it's a procedure for finding
what CAN'T be doubted. Three levels: senses (perceptual illusions); dreams (how do
I know I'm awake?); the evil genius (what if an all-powerful deceiver ensures I am
wrong even in mathematics?). Each level strips another apparent certainty. Worked
example: run all three in sequence. The evil genius is the terminal doubt — not a
belief Descartes holds, but the strongest possible challenge he can construct.

**The cogito (source precision, mandatory):**
- *Discourse on the Method*, Part IV (1637, French): "je pense, donc je suis" —
  the French; the Veitch English translation renders it "I think, therefore I am"
  with the Latin "COGITO ERGO SUM" in parentheses immediately after. The Latin in
  the Discourse is Veitch's own gloss, not Descartes' original text.
- *Meditations on First Philosophy*, Meditation II (1641, Latin): the argument is
  present but NOT as a syllogism — the Molyneux translation (1680) renders it "I
  am, I exist — 'tis true" / "whenever this sentence I am, I exist, is spoken or
  thought of by Me, 'tis necessarily True." The famous "ergo" (therefore) is absent
  from the *Meditations* form.
- *Principles of Philosophy*, Part I, Article 7 (1644, Latin): **this is where
  "cogito ergo sum" appears as an explicit Latin axiom**, established as the
  first principle of his philosophy.
⚠️ **TRAP: writers often cite "Cogito ergo sum" and point to the *Meditations* as
the source.** The *Meditations* carries the argument; the explicit Latin phrase as
axiom is in the *Principles*. The *Discourse*'s Veitch translation adds it
parenthetically. Get the locus right.

**Mind-body dualism:** Descartes' precise claim: the mind (res cogitans, thinking
substance) and body (res extensa, extended substance) are two entirely distinct
substances with no properties in common — the mind is unextended, the body
unthinking. The "pineal gland" is where Descartes locates their interaction (a
real Descartes claim — §4 ledger — not a later caricature).
⚠️ **TRAP: "Descartes invented mind-body dualism."** Plato separates soul and body
(*Phaedo*; Forms vs. particulars); the Scholastics distinguished soul from body;
Augustine's *Confessions* is about the divided interior life. What Descartes does
that is NEW and specific: (1) the dualism is between two complete substances (not
degrees of being); (2) the framing is mechanistic — the body is a machine, the
mind is its ghost; (3) this makes the *interaction problem* acute in a way it
wasn't before (how does the unextended cause motion in the extended?). Plato's
soul participates in the Forms; it doesn't have to move a clockwork body.

**God as guarantor:** after the cogito, Descartes argues for God's existence (using
an argument from the idea of infinite perfection; a version of the causal
adequacy principle — an idea of infinite perfection requires an infinitely perfect
cause). Crucially, the existence of a non-deceptive God then guarantees that clear
and distinct ideas are true — this is the engine that lets Descartes rebuild
knowledge. ⚠️ The circularity (the "Cartesian circle"): God guarantees clear and
distinct ideas, but the proof of God relies on clear and distinct ideas — a famous
objection raised in the *Objections and Replies* (Arnauld) and never fully resolved
by Descartes.

**The wax argument (Meditation II):** a piece of fresh beeswax has taste, smell,
color, shape, sound — all sensory qualities. Hold it near a fire: all those
qualities vanish. Is it the same wax? Yes — but everything we identified it by
is gone. What remains? Only something extended, flexible, movable. The argument:
our knowledge of material objects as such is not sensory but intellectual — we
grasp the wax by the mind, not the senses.

### Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia

She is not a sideline; she got the best objection in. Her letter to Descartes of
16 May 1643 (first letter) poses the interaction problem with precise attention to
his own physics: how can a non-extended, non-material, non-local substance (the
mind) cause motion in an extended, material, located substance (the body)? In
Cartesian mechanics, causation requires contact and extension — the mind has
neither. Descartes' responses fall back on the example of "heaviness" (gravity)
moving the body — **she immediately catches this**: he had already rejected
occult qualities like heaviness in his physics. He is invoking exactly what he
was supposed to have demolished. He never provided a satisfactory answer. This
exchange **is** the mind-body problem in its modern form.
⚠️ **FRAMING GATE:** Do not slot her as "a woman who asked Descartes a question."
She is the thinker who identified the load-bearing flaw in the most influential
philosophical system of the century, and Descartes' failure to answer her is as
philosophically significant as the system itself. Ship her as a thinker.
**Key passage:** PARAPHRASE-ONLY (correspondence not in PD English online).

### Spinoza

**The geometric method:** the *Ethics* opens with definitions and axioms like
Euclid's *Elements* — Propositions proceed by "Q.E.D." It is either the bravest
or the most grandiose act in the history of philosophy, depending on your mood.
Worked example for the reader: walk Proposition XIV through its proof.

**The one substance:** Spinoza's universe has exactly one substance —
self-caused, infinite, necessarily existing — and that substance is God, which
is identical with Nature. Finite things ("modes") are not things alongside God
but ways in which the one substance expresses itself. From Elwes, Ethics Part IV
Preface (VERIFIED, §3): "God or Nature." From Part I, Prop. XIV: "Besides God no
substance can be granted or conceived."

**Thought and extension:** the one substance can be known under infinitely many
attributes, of which humans know two — thought and extension (mind and matter).
They are parallel aspects of the same thing, not two separate substances. This is
Spinoza's answer to the interaction problem: mind and body never interact because
they are not two things; they are two ways of describing one event. Worked example:
think of a mental event (deciding to raise your arm) and the corresponding physical
event (the neurons firing) — for Spinoza these are the same event under two
descriptions.

**Determinism:** in the one substance, everything follows necessarily from its
nature. There is no free will in the sense of undetermined choice — but Spinoza
argues there is genuine freedom: acting from your own nature rather than being
pushed by external causes. The free person is not the undetermined person; it is
the person acting from reason.

**The intellectual love of God / sub specie aeternitatis:** the *Ethics* ends
in a strange place — a theory of human blessedness. To see things "under the form
of eternity" (from Elwes/Wikisource, Ethics Part V, Prop. 29 — VERIFIED, §3) is
to see them as necessary expressions of the one substance, which is its own kind
of peace. The "intellectual love of God" follows.

⚠️ **TRAP 1: "Spinoza was an atheist."** He has been called both "God-intoxicated
man" (Novalis, c. 1800 — LATE attribution; Novalis as the source is conventional
in secondary literature but the primary locus was not pinned) and an atheist. The
most careful answer: Spinoza radically redefines God (as Nature, not a person
above Nature), which made him look atheist to his contemporaries and theist to
certain Romantics. He is best described as a **naturalist** who identifies God
with Nature — the label "pantheist" is standard but imprecise.

⚠️ **TRAP 2:** The 1656 herem (excommunication). We do NOT know its specific cause —
the document alleges "monstrous deeds and abominable heresies" without naming them;
the specific grounds are unknown (SEP). Do not invent a reason.

### Leibniz

**The monads:** the ultimate constituents of reality are not atoms (extended, and
therefore divisible) but *monads* — simple, unextended, individual substances that
"have no windows through which anything may come in or go out" (VERIFIED, §3).
Everything that seems like compound matter is built from monads. Each monad reflects
the entire universe from its own perspective, making it a "perpetual living mirror
of the universe" (VERIFIED, §3).

**Pre-established harmony:** because monads have no windows, they cannot
interact. How then do mind and body seem to track each other? Leibniz: God
established at creation a harmony between all substances, like two clocks set to
the same time — they appear to interact but only because they were pre-calibrated.
(VERIFIED, §3: "preestablished harmony.")

**Principle of sufficient reason:** "no fact can be real or existing and no
statement true unless it has a sufficient reason why it should be thus and not
otherwise" (VERIFIED, §3 — Monadology §32). This is the knife Leibniz uses against
everything: nothing just IS without a reason. Applied to the universe: why does
anything exist rather than nothing? Why THIS universe rather than another? There
must be a sufficient reason.

**The best of all possible worlds:** The argument (not just the Candide punchline):
God has understanding (surveys all possible worlds), will (can choose among them),
and goodness. A perfectly rational, benevolent, omnipotent God must choose
optimally — the actual world is therefore the best possible combination of simplest
laws and richest phenomena (SEP: *Discourse on Metaphysics* §6). The *Theodicy*
(1710) works this out at length.
⚠️ **TRAP: the Voltaire/Pangloss parody** ("all is for the best in the best of all
possible worlds" — *Candide*, 1759) is the cultural reception, not the argument.
Dr. Pangloss is Voltaire's comic inflation of Leibniz's actual position, which is
a serious theodicy: the world contains evil because a world without the possibility
of evil would be a worse world (fewer laws, fewer phenomena, less freedom). State
Leibniz's argument first, then note that Voltaire — writing after the 1755 Lisbon
earthquake — had a point about the gap between the argument and the evidence.

**Spinoza vs. Leibniz contrast (structurally important):** both start from
Descartes, both are rationalists, both try to solve the interaction problem. Spinoza
dissolves it by making mind and body aspects of one substance; Leibniz avoids it by
making monads windowless and pre-harmonized. Result: Spinoza's universe is
deterministic and God-is-everything; Leibniz's universe is pluralistic and
God-stands-above-creation choosing the best arrangement.

### Hobbes

**Materialism:** Hobbes is a thoroughgoing materialist — everything, including
thought, is matter in motion. This makes him the odd one out in the rationalist
era: he's not doing epistemology, he's telling you the world is already explained.
The mind-body problem doesn't arise when the "mind" is just the body thinking.

**State of nature:** without a sovereign, life is "continuall warre... And the life
of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short" (VERIFIED, §3 — *Leviathan*
ch. 13; archaic spelling preserved in quote). This is not just a historical claim
— it is what would be true if the authority holding everything together were removed.

**The social contract (Hobbes version):** people authorize a sovereign (a person or
assembly) to act in their name in exchange for security. Once authorized, the
sovereign's power is absolute — the contract is between subjects, ceding authority
to the sovereign, not a contract between subjects and sovereign (the sovereign
makes no promise). The *Leviathan* as artificial man: "that great LEVIATHAN called
a COMMON-WEALTH, or STATE... is but an Artificiall Man" (VERIFIED, §3).

⚠️ **TRAP:** Hobbes is often read as a conservative apologist for absolute monarchy.
He's making a philosophical argument: any stable government is better than none,
because the alternative is the war of all against all. His sovereign could be a
democratic assembly — absolutism is structural, not about kings. Steelman: the
argument for *stability* over *legitimacy* is a genuine philosophical position with
a clear payoff when you look at the English Civil War he lived through.

### Locke

**Epistemology (the Essay):** the mind begins as white paper — "void of all
characters, without any ideas" (VERIFIED, §3 — *Essay* II.1.2). All ideas come
from experience: sensation (the external world) or reflection (the mind observing
its own operations). There is no innate knowledge.
⚠️ **TRAP: "tabula rasa" is Locke's own phrase.** It is NOT. Locke's images are
"white paper" (*Essay* II.1.2, VERIFIED) and "empty cabinet" (*Essay* I.2.15,
VERIFIED). The Latin *tabula rasa* is ancient (traceable through Aquinas to
Aristotle's *De Anima*) and was applied TO Locke by later commentators. He never
uses it. The fact pack uses Locke's actual words.

**Primary and secondary qualities:** qualities entirely inseparable from the object
(solidity, extension, motion or rest, number, figure) are primary; powers to produce
sensory effects in us (color, sound, taste, smell) are secondary. The crucial point
(VERIFIED, §3 — *Essay* II.8.15): secondary quality ideas "have no resemblance of
them at all. There is nothing like our ideas, existing in the bodies themselves."
Colors are not in the apple; they're in the interaction between the apple's primary
qualities and your sense organs.

**The social contract (Locke version):** the state of nature is NOT Hobbes' war —
it is a condition of natural liberty governed by a law of nature that no one may
harm another in "his life, health, liberty, or possessions" (VERIFIED, §3 —
*Two Treatises* II.6). People consent to form governments to better protect these
natural rights. The crucial difference from Hobbes: the consent comes from the
people; government exists to serve their interests; if it fails, the people may
dissolve it (Chapter XIX, *Two Treatises* — VERIFIED by title, §3). Locke's triad
is life, liberty, and **estate** (property) — not Jefferson's life, liberty, and
the pursuit of happiness (that's the Declaration's revision of Locke).

**Toleration:** the *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) argues that the civil
government's power extends only to civil interests — magistrates have no jurisdiction
over souls. Religion is a matter of inner conviction; coercion cannot produce
genuine belief and therefore can never serve the religious purpose it claims.

### Berkeley

⚠️ **THE trap, pre-empted:** Berkeley does NOT deny that tables, chairs, and
trees exist. He denies that they exist as *mind-independent matter*. The world of
experience is entirely real; what Berkeley rejects is the philosophical postulate
of a material substance underlying and causing those experiences. As he argues:
if matter is defined as something existing outside any mind, we can never
experience it — so what work is the concept doing? The common-sense response
("but I walked INTO the table") Berkeley would say proves his point: you
experienced the table; you did not experience mind-independent matter.

**"Esse est percipi":** Berkeley's Latin tag in Section 3 of the *Principles*:
"Their ESSE is PERCIPI, nor is it possible they should have any existence out of
the minds or thinking things which perceive them" (VERIFIED, §3 — mixed Latin/
English as Berkeley wrote it; the fully Latin "esse est percipi" is the
standardized later version). "To be is to be perceived" is the standard English
paraphrase — accurate but not Berkeley's own phrasing.

**Why this is a move on Locke:** Locke admitted that secondary qualities exist only
in the mind. Berkeley asks: why stop there? If we examine what we mean by
"primary qualities in matter," we find we can only ever know our ideas of them —
and if the ideas are in the mind, and the supposed matter is only ever known
through ideas, what is matter adding to the picture? It's Locke's empiricism
done consistently.

**God and other minds:** because "esse est percipi," trees exist even when I'm
not looking — because God perceives them continuously. Berkeley is not arguing
the world winks in and out of existence; he is arguing that the world's continuity
requires a perceiving mind that is always present. (Verse attributed to Knox: "There
was a young man who said 'God / Must think it exceedingly odd / If he finds that
this tree / Continues to be / When there's no one about in the quad.' / Dear Sir:
Your astonishment's odd / I am always about in the quad / And that's why the tree /
Will continue to be / Since observed by, Yours faithfully, God." — a later joke,
not Berkeley's, but an accurate summary of his move; ship as a later verse, not
Berkeley's.)

### Hume

**The fork (Enquiry IV, VERIFIED, §3):** all objects of human reason divide into
two and only two kinds:
- **Relations of ideas** — true by the content of the ideas alone (mathematics,
  logic); "discoverable by the mere operation of thought, without dependence on
  what is anywhere existent in the universe."
- **Matters of fact** — known through experience; their negation is always
  conceivable (possible).
⚠️ A claim that fits neither category — neither demonstrably true by reason nor
supported by experience — is neither. **This is what the Enquiry's final
"commit it to the flames" passage applies** (VERIFIED, §3): "Does it contain any
abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain any
experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. Commit it then
to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."

**Causation:** this is the fork's most devastating application. We observe constant
conjunction: fire is always followed by burning; billiard balls collide and the
second one always moves. But we never observe the necessary connection itself —
only the sequence. "Motion in the second Billiard-ball is a quite distinct event
from motion in the first; nor is there anything in the one to suggest the smallest
hint of the other" (VERIFIED, §3 — *Enquiry* §4). Our belief in causation is
produced by **custom or habit**: "This principle is Custom or Habit... we always
say, that this propensity is the effect of Custom" (VERIFIED, §3 — *Enquiry*).

**The bundle self:** "what we call the self" — look inside and you find only a
stream of perceptions, never a stable perceiver: "they are nothing but a bundle or
collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable
rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement" (VERIFIED, §3 — *Treatise*).

**Is/ought gap (*Treatise* III.1.1):** "In every system of morality... I am
surprized to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and
is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought
not. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence"
(VERIFIED, §3). The gap: you cannot derive what OUGHT to be from statements about
what IS without a further premise that connects them. Every moral system that
pretends to derive "should" from "is" has made an illicit move.

**Reason is the slave of the passions:** VERIFIED, *Treatise* (§3): "Reason is,
and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other
office than to serve and obey them." Not nihilism: Hume thinks reason tells us
how to get what we want; desire supplies the ends.

**The missing shade of blue:** the one concession Hume makes against his own
empiricism — if you have seen every shade of blue except one and are shown a
spectrum with a gap, you can probably imagine the missing shade without having seen
it. "I believe there are few but will be of opinion that he can" (VERIFIED, §3 —
*Treatise*). Hume flags it as so singular it doesn't undermine the theory. It has
bothered philosophers ever since.

**Religion:** Hume never called himself an atheist — his public positioning was
deliberately ambiguous throughout his life. In the *Natural History of Religion*
(1757) he explains religion genetically (polytheism comes from fear; monotheism
comes from a different psychological tendency). In "Of Miracles" (*Enquiry* §10):
"no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such
a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it
endeavours to establish" (VERIFIED SEP/secondary — confirm wording at integration
from Gutenberg #9662 §10). The *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (posthumous
1779): Philo (the skeptic) systematically destroys the design argument; Cleanthes
defends it weakly; Pamphilus narrates. Hume's own view is implied but never
stated — and that's deliberate.
⚠️ **TRAP: "Hume was an atheist."** He never said so. His careful positioning was
not cowardice (he could say what he thought when he chose) but epistemic honesty:
the evidence for or against God's existence fell under "matters of fact," and the
evidence was not sufficient. He died with notable composure — Adam Smith's letter
is the documented record (§4).

### Émilie du Châtelet

⚠️ **FRAMING GATE (era 3's women-erasure instance):** Du Châtelet is not a
biographic curiosity. Her *Institutions de Physique* (1740) is a philosophical
text that argues Newton's mechanics needs Leibnizian metaphysical foundations —

Newton's *Principia* (still the standard French edition) includes her own
commentary. She died at 42, days after giving birth. The era's chapter must name
her and give her argument, not just her story.

## 5. "Why this is a break" block material

**The break: the God problem goes inside.**

**BEFORE** (the era-2 frame, Anselm's formulation — VERIFIED in era-2 pack §3):
"I believe in order to understand." The starting point is given by faith; reason
works within it. For a thousand years philosophy was the servant of theology;
the questions you could ask, and the answers you could give, were constrained by
what revelation had already settled.

**AFTER — the method changes everything.** Descartes is not a skeptic; he's using
doubt as a METHOD. He ends up with God, soul, and matter — the medieval inheritance
— but he arrives at them differently: from a first-person, starting-from-scratch
reconstruction that owes nothing to scripture as a premise. The question shifts from
"what does revelation tell us?" to "what can I establish by reason alone, starting
from nothing I haven't personally verified?"

**The concrete move:** Descartes puts method before content. He doesn't assume the
existence of God, matter, or other minds — he derives them (or tries to). That
attempt is what makes the era tick: rationalists trust reason to derive the world's
structure; empiricists trust experience to constrain what reason can claim; Hume
uses empiricist tools on the rationalists' claim and finds it can't be verified.
The era opens in 1637 with a soldier deciding to doubt everything and ends in
1776 with a philosopher politely refusing to call himself an atheist — but having
committed every foundational claim of the tradition to the flames.

**Before/after contrast for the break block (concrete):**
- BEFORE: Aquinas' *Summa Theologiae* begins "I answer that..." — the framework is
  already given; philosophy works inside revelation.
- AFTER: Descartes' *Meditations* begins "I will now close my eyes, stop up my
  ears, call in all my senses..." (Molyneux) — the starting point is solitary,
  interior, beginning from scratch.

**The twist that makes it a story, not a theory:** the empiricist critique of
rationalism is not that reason is bad — it's that reason without experience is
overreaching. Hume is not anti-reason; he's applying reason to the question of
what reason can do. The era ends in a kind of knot that no one in it could untie —
which is why Kant (era 4) read it as a crisis requiring a completely new
architecture.

## 9. APOCRYPHA BLACKLIST — famous fakes the author must NOT use

1. **"Cogito ergo sum" in the *Meditations* as a formal axiom** — the
   FORMULATION as a Latin axiom is from the *Principles of Philosophy* (1644), not
   the *Meditations* (1641). The *Meditations* contains the argument in first-person
   narrative form ("I am, I exist" — Molyneux VERIFIED §3). Writers routinely
   attribute the Latin slogan to the *Meditations*; get the locus right. The Veitch
   Discourse translation adds "COGITO ERGO SUM" parenthetically as a gloss.

2. **"War of all against all" (Bellum omnium contra omnes) as Hobbes' phrase in
   *Leviathan*** — the Latin tag was NOT in the *Leviathan* (1651). Hobbes uses
   "every man, against every man" (VERIFIED §3). The Latin formulation is a
   retroactive scholastic-sounding gloss. Use the actual Hobbes wording.

3. **"Tabula rasa" as Locke's own phrase** — Locke uses "white paper" and "empty
   cabinet" (both VERIFIED §3). The Latin tag is later application. Do not put it
   in quotation marks as Locke's.

4. **"The world exists in my mind" as Berkeley's position** — Berkeley denies
   *mind-independent matter*, not existence. Tables exist; they exist as ideas in
   minds. Common-sense objects are preserved; the philosophical postulate of
   mind-independent material substance is rejected. The caricature ("he bumped into
   things to prove the world exists") is §2 trap; §9 blacklists the blunter
   caricature.

5. **"Best of all possible worlds" as a Leibniz quotation or Monadology phrase** —
   does NOT appear verbatim in the *Monadology*; the spirit is there (§§53–58) but
   not the catchphrase. The Voltaire/Pangloss version ("all is for the best in the
   best of all possible worlds") is *Candide* (1759), not Leibniz. Use to describe
   the position; do not quote-mark as Leibniz.

6. **"Hume was an atheist"** — he never said so, and the evidence doesn't
   support asserting it on his behalf. His stated position in the *Enquiry* is
   "mitigated skepticism" — insufficient evidence for traditional theological claims.
   The *Dialogues* is careful not to put Hume's own views in any character's mouth.
   Use "irreligious skeptic" or "philosophical skeptic about religion" with care.

7. **"I never asserted so absurd a proposition as that anything might arise without
   a cause" — NOT in Hume's *Dialogues* or any confirmed source** (CONFIRMED ABSENT,
   §3 search). Widely quoted on the internet; absent from the text. Do not use.

8. **"Hume woke Kant from his dogmatic slumber"** — Hume did no such thing as
   an action. The "dogmatic slumber" line is Kant's own phrase about himself in the
   Prolegomena (1783): "I openly confess, the suggestion of David Hume was the very
   thing, which many years ago first interrupted my dogmatic slumber" (VERIFIED, §3).
   Ship as: "Kant would later write that Hume's challenge 'interrupted my dogmatic
   slumber.'" It is NOT a line about Hume; it is a line BY Kant ABOUT Kant.

9. **Descartes "proved the existence of God" conclusively** — Descartes argued
   for God; his argument (from the idea of infinite perfection as requiring an
   infinitely perfect cause) was immediately challenged by Arnauld (the "Cartesian
   circle") in the Objections and Replies. The argument is real; its success is
   not. Frame as: Descartes argued for God's existence from the cogito — and the
   debate started before the ink was dry.

10. **The "stove-heated room" as Descartes' dream night** — the *Discourse*
    describes a winter retreat and methodological meditation; the "stove" detail is
    in the French original ("poêle"); there are ALSO three famous dreams Descartes
    reportedly had that same night (November 10, 1619), preserved in Baillet's
    biography (1691, 72 years after the fact). The dreams and the stove are
    SEPARATE: the dreams come from Baillet's hagiographic biography (LATE-TRADITION);
    the stove is in the *Discourse* itself. Do not collapse them.

11. **"Spinoza was expelled for reading Descartes"** — the grounds for the
    1656 herem are unknown (the document itself doesn't say — SEP confirmed).
    Do not invent reasons; the mystery is part of the story.

12. **The "God is dead" line in this era** — that's Nietzsche, era 5. Do not
    foreshadow with it here.

13. **Locke's "consent of the governed"** — not Locke's phrase (confirmed ABSENT,
    §3). His phrase is "consent of the people" (preface). "Consent of the governed"
    is Jefferson's *Declaration*. Do not attribute to Locke.

14. **"Berkeley proved the existence of God from his idealism" as a knockdown** —
    Berkeley's argument for God's continuous perception is structurally required by
    his immaterialism, but it is not a proof most philosophers found convincing then
    or since. Frame as: Berkeley held that God's perception explains the permanence
    of the world; the argument is real and has the structural moves described in §2.

15. **Elisabeth of Bohemia as merely "Descartes' fan" or "correspondent"** — she
    is the author of the sharpest objection the mind-body dualism ever received.
    She is a thinker. The framing gate applies.

### §11.8 — Du Châtelet and the vis viva dispute (Ch 7)

**Claim:** In the vis viva dispute, the Newtonian/Cartesian camp held the conserved
quantity was mv (mass times velocity) and the Leibnizian camp held it was mv²
(mass times velocity squared). Du Châtelet sided with the Leibnizians, against the
prevailing consensus, and she turned out to be right: the squared quantity is what
we now call kinetic energy.

**Status:** CONFIRMED

**Sources:** ResearchGate (Vis Viva Dispute, 1959 article by Iltis / Costabel);
ResearchGate / SpringerLink (Leibniz's Quantity of Force: A 'Heresy'? Emilie du
Châtelet's *Institutions* in the Context of the Vis Viva Controversy); Wikipedia
(Kinetic energy); IOP (History of energy); Urania (du Châtelet scientific works).

**Key detail:** The Dutch physicist Willem 's Gravesande experimentally demonstrated
the mv² relationship around 1720 using balls dropped into clay. Du Châtelet drew on
's Gravesande's experimental work in her *Institutions de Physique* (1740) to argue
for the Leibnizian position. The ½ coefficient (kinetic energy = ½mv²) was added
by Coriolis in 1829; the squared-velocity relationship du Châtelet defended is what
was vindicated.

**Framing rule:** the draft correctly describes du Châtelet as making "an independent
intervention in an open argument" — she was not merely a transmitter. Her
*Institutions* synthesized the experimental and metaphysical arguments for mv² in
a way that advanced the debate.
