## MUST-FIX list

**MF-1 (Ch 1) — Throughline established late; Chapter 1's Augustine section does not run the three-part test.**
Augustine is the era's first major thinker and the one who names the era's central problem (two loyalties). The chapter gives biography and rhetoric (the rhetorical structure of the *Confessions*, the "divided will," the anti-cartoon work) but never runs a concrete worked example of what Augustine's actual argument looks like in motion. What IS the argument that Neoplatonism gives him that he couldn't get elsewhere? What step does he take that scripture alone could not supply, and what step does philosophy alone fail to supply? The reader understands Augustine is important and understands the emotional texture of the *Confessions* — but does not actually understand the intellectual MOVE he made. Specifically: the privation theory of evil (evil as absence of good, derived from Plotinus) is named in passing ("evil is not a thing in itself but a lack, a privation, the way darkness is just the absence of light") but never worked. That IS the worked example the chapter needs — run it one more move: why does that matter? What does it solve that the Manichaean evil-as-substance view couldn't? The dinner-table restatement is half-there (the restlessness line, the destination-fixed-before-the-journey starts), but the reader is left with the FEELING of Augustine's problem, not the argument.

This is the Proof run 1 pattern the spec warned to check — a final chapter with biography and no worked example. Here it is the FIRST chapter, which sets the throughline stakes. MF-1 is not about length; one tight paragraph running the privation move would close it.

**MF-2 (Ch 3 and Ch 4, joined) — Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism argument is stated but not run to its philosophical conclusion inside the chapter. The cross-era callback to Hume lands before the chapter gives the reader the full force of the argument.**
The flying man (Avicenna, Ch 3) is run beautifully — this is a model worked example. Al-Ghazālī's causation argument is also set up clearly (fire and cotton, the absence of observed necessity). But the chapter cuts away to "hold this thought, because seven hundred years later a Scottish skeptic named Hume will rediscover that you never observe necessity" BEFORE giving the reader the full force of al-Ghazālī's own conclusion for the Islamic philosophy story. The forward-pointer to Hume is good. The problem is that it replaces rather than follows the argument's landing. The reader should first feel WHY this argument is devastating to Avicenna's project (if causal connections aren't necessary, the whole chain-of-causes argument for the Necessary Existent frays — because that argument depends on effects necessitating causes), and THEN the Hume pointer. As written, the reader gets the argument's setup and the future payoff but not the present-tense crisis it creates in Chapter 3's story. One sentence connecting al-Ghazālī's occasionalism back to Avicenna's God-proof would close this, and it would also make Chapter 7's "al-Ghazālī's point arriving in Latin" callback land harder.

## SHOULD-FIX list

**SF-1 (Ch 1) — The Hypatia opening is strong on the legend-clearing work but slightly long on that clearing relative to what it sets up for Augustine.**
The three-paragraph legend-clearing block on Hypatia (no, she wasn't killed for being a scientist; no, the Library; no, the Hubbard quotes) is thorough and admirably honest. But it runs slightly past the structural purpose: the chapter's real work is establishing that Greek philosophy crossed into Augustine's Christianity via Plotinus, and by the time the reader reaches Augustine, the Hypatia material has spent most of the chapter's real estate. The legends warrant clearing, but the third paragraph ("What survived her was the thing that would matter most...") is the hinge — everything before it is scene-setting for that sentence. Consider compressing the first two paragraphs slightly so the Augustine section has more room for MF-1's worked example.

**SF-2 (Ch 5) — Héloïse section is the best writing in the chapter, but the argument she makes (disinterested love, incompatibility of contemplative and domestic) is told as paraphrase-from-Abelard rather than as philosophy in its own right.**
The draft names her arguments (love should be disinterested; the contemplative life and the domestic life are incompatible) but doesn't run them the way it runs Anselm's argument or even Abelard's Sic et Non method. This is partly justified by the sourcing caveat (we have Abelard's report, not her direct text), but the result is that Héloïse gets more biography-shaped treatment than argument-shaped treatment in a section that has just spent several paragraphs on Anselm's argument run slowly. A single sentence asking the reader to feel the force of her disinterested-love claim would bring her up to the chapter's own standard. ("What would it mean to love someone purely, with no contract doing the holding? That is not a swoon — it is a claim about what makes love real.")

**SF-3 (Ch 6) — "Grace does not destroy nature but perfects it" is the era's clearest synthesis line, but the analogy used to unpack it (the roof completing a house) is slightly off.**
The draft says faith completes reason "the way a roof completes a house rather than crushing it." The problem with the roof analogy is that a house works without a roof (you can live in a roofless structure) but reason, on Aquinas's account, cannot reach the Trinity at all without revelation — revelation doesn't just complete what's already working, it supplies something reason has no access to. The chapter itself says this correctly two sentences earlier ("others only revelation can give us... which no argument could have deduced"), but the roof image suggests only degree, not kind. Aquinas's own metaphors tend toward light and sight (natural reason sees by natural light; faith adds a higher light). Consider trading the roof for something that better captures supplied-capacity rather than just-added-layer.

**SF-4 (Ch 2) — The "quiet centuries" section does excellent structural work but the Eriugena paragraph runs slightly against the chapter's own stated purpose.**
The chapter says "there was one exception, strange and isolated, and naming him keeps the 'quiet centuries' claim honest rather than absolute" — and then the Eriugena paragraph is the chapter's most purely enjoyable writing (the pen-death legend, the table joke). The joke earns its place. But the paragraph slightly overweights the charming legend material relative to what Eriugena actually argued (the *Periphyseon*'s vision of reality flowing from and returning to God). Since that emanationist picture is directly relevant to Neoplatonism, and since Neoplatonism is the throughline running from Plotinus through Augustine, a one-clause note on what the *Periphyseon* actually claimed would justify Eriugena's presence structurally, not just as a nice anecdote. As it stands the reader knows Eriugena was unusual and funny; they don't know why his system mattered.

**SF-5 (Ch 7) — The closing synthesis ("what the era settled") runs the throughline genuinely well, but the Descartes hand-off sentence is slightly over-prepared.**
The last paragraph names Descartes twice ("A Frenchman in a stove-heated room" in the body and the close "In 1619 a young Frenchman named Descartes..."), which means the era's close uses Descartes as both the villain in Ockham's logic ("this is al-Ghazālī's point, and a Frenchman will soon wonder...") and the opening image of era 3. The double-mention is not a contradiction and the era-3 hand-off is exact and clean. But the first Descartes mention ("a Frenchman in a stove-heated room will soon wonder whether an all-powerful being might be deceiving him about everything") arrives in the middle of the Ockham voluntarism argument and slightly deflates the immediacy of the era's own crisis by pointing immediately to its future resolution. Restructuring so the Descartes forward-pointer appears only in the closing synthesis paragraph would keep the Ockham section's dread intact and give the hand-off more weight when it lands.

## Long-chapter ruling

**AUTHOR-FLAGGED: Ch 3 (~1,500 words) and Ch 5 (~1,600 words) exceed the 700–1,100 word brief. Are the overruns earned?**

**Ch 3: LENGTH IS EARNED.**
The chapter carries four thinkers (al-Kindī, al-Fārābī, Avicenna, al-Ghazālī) plus the translation-movement context — none of which can be safely compressed without silently dropping a link in the influence chain. Avicenna alone requires the flying man AND the essence/existence distinction to set up both Aquinas (Ch 6) and Descartes (era 3 hand-off). Al-Ghazālī requires both the breakdown narrative AND the philosophical argument to avoid the potted-summary trap the spec explicitly warns against ("al-Ghazālī killed Islamic philosophy" is the caricature this chapter must undo, and undoing it takes space). No passage is padding; every section is doing structural work for a chapter that comes later. The one cut I would make if length were truly a constraint: the three-sentence comparison of al-Ghazālī's autobiography to Augustine's *Confessions* could be trimmed to one sentence, saving ~40 words, though the comparison is genuinely useful for the era's throughline (both are constructed conversion narratives; both frame a crisis between argument and a higher certainty). **Ruling: earned. Run at current length.**

**Ch 5: LENGTH IS MARGINALLY EARNED — with a specific cut available.**
The chapter carries Anselm (argument run in full), Abelard (Sic et Non method run, universals fight, Héloïse), the university institution, Peter Lombard, and Bernard of Chartres. Anselm and Abelard both require their arguments run step by step — these are the chapter's primary gate-2 obligations, and neither can be compressed. The Héloïse section is the chapter's best writing and correctly does the framing-gate work (the tradition remembers her as a romantic heroine; she was a formidable thinker). The institutional context (scholasticism, the university origins, the Sentences) is load-bearing for Ch 6's Aquinas. The Bernard of Chartres close is genuinely good but could be cut to one sentence if the chapter needs trimming. The largest available cut is about 80 words in the Héloïse section, specifically the concluding summary ("She became abbess of the Paraclete...remember her as a philosopher who happened to be in a famous romance, not the other way around") — the instruction to the reader is correct in sentiment but mildly redundant after the argument has already been run. Cutting it would save ~60 words and trust the reader more. **Ruling: earned at current length, with a clean optional trim of ~60–80 words available in the Héloïse close if the coordinator wants it.**

## Biggest concern

**The chapter that most risks the Proof run 1 pattern (biography without thinking) is Ch 1, not Ch 7.** Chapter 7's close was the failure point in the greeks-draft-r2 precursor, and this draft solved it: Ch 7 is STRONG throughout, the throughline resolves, the hand-off lands. But Ch 1, the Augustine chapter, has the same structural vulnerability that Ch 7 had before revision: Augustine's intellectual biography is told beautifully (Plotinus, the *Confessions*, the divided will, the anti-cartoon clearing), but the reader finishes Ch 1 having felt the era's stakes without having understood Augustine's actual argument. Since Ch 1 establishes the era's central problem, a worked example here is not a nice-to-have — it is what makes the throughline's first note sound. MF-1 addresses this directly and is the draft's one blocking item before ship.
