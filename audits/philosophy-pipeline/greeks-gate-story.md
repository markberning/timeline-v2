## MUST-FIX list

1. **Ch 7: No worked example for any Hellenistic prescription.** The Epicurean "death is nothing to us" quote is rendered admiringly but not *walked*. The three-part test fails: the example barely runs, the strongest version of the reasoning isn't presented, and there is no plain restatement the reader can carry. The Stoic control-line likewise receives a paraphrase but no demonstration. This is the only chapter where a central idea is asserted instead of taught. (See Ch 7 §Three-part test.)

2. **Ch 7: Throughline drop.** The chapter is the payoff for a thousand years of argument, yet it never explicitly delivers the promised resolution (or non-resolution) of the questions the hook raised: "what does the world behave by, can we know, how do we live?" Cicero, Lucretius, Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus, Boethius, and the Arabic handoff are all covered in sequence, but there is no sentence that says: here is what the Greeks finally decided, or here is why they didn't decide. The chapter reads as a relay baton list, not a conclusion.

3. **Ch 3: The Socratic elenchus example stops one move too early.** The run-through of the courage example is genuinely excellent — but it ends right at the moment of greatest interest (courage = endurance + good sense) without showing how *that* definition also fails. The reader may walk away thinking Socrates won the argument rather than understanding that the elenchus never lands a positive definition, only strips the false ones. That asymmetry is the *scandal* of Socratic method — the man who made everyone smarter never told them the answer — and the draft points at it but doesn't land it.

4. **Ch 7: Neoplatonism and Boethius are named but not taught.** The draft mentions "a mystical, religious reworking of Plato that scholars call Neoplatonism" in a subordinate clause. Neoplatonism is not explained, the thinker (Plotinus) is not named, and no characteristic idea is conveyed. Given the era's centrality to the transmission story and to "Faith meets reason" (Ch 2 of the full series), this is a thin exit from the Greek era. Either teach the move briefly or drop the name — a label without content is noise.

5. **Ch 6: Stoic framing undersells the exercise.** The Stoic control exercise (Epictetus' distinction) is quoted and summarized but never walked on a concrete example. No situation is set up, no line drawn, no judgment examined. The Epicurean consolation ("death is nothing to us") is quoted verbatim and the argument is gestured at, but the prose says "sit with that" rather than actually running the argument step by step for the reader. Both ideas deserve their own worked example before the reader hits the end of the section.

---

## SHOULD-FIX list

6. **Ch 7: Seneca's death is the chapter's best scene and is under-exploited for the throughline.** The Tacitus account of Seneca's slow death is vivid and earned, but the prose doesn't complete the thought: did he demonstrate the Stoic thesis or not? The draft says he died "with composure" but the Tacitus passage implies it was protracted and not entirely serene. Committing to the complexity here — the test of the doctrine, the gap between principle and practice, what Tacitus saw vs. what Seneca claimed — would make the chapter's best moment fully land.

7. **Ch 3: No comparison for the Sophists.** Ch 3 rehabilitates the Sophists fairly, but it does so without a comparison that would let the reader actually feel why they were threatening. The closest analog is the modern debate-coach who teaches how to argue *any* side — comparing Protagoras to that figure would make the civic scandal (you can be taught to win a bad case) visceral.

8. **Ch 6: Hipparchia paragraph positioned poorly.** The Hipparchia paragraph is good and deserves its place, but it comes after the chapter's conclusion has essentially been made. Structurally it reads as an afterthought. Consider integrating it into the Cynics passage where her story actually belongs, rather than appending it after the Epicureans.

9. **Hook / Era block: The throughline promise is slightly narrower than the actual content.** The hook promises a story about "what the gods didn't make the world behave" → what does / can we know / how to live. Chapters 1–2 deliver the first question, Chapter 3–4 deliver the second, Chapters 5–6 the third. This is fine. What's missing is a single moment, probably in Ch 7, where the reader is shown whether that argument reached a conclusion or consciously handed off open. The hook created a debt the ending doesn't fully pay.

10. **Ch 5: "Teleology" paragraph risks altitude creep (thinker-deep).** The paragraph on teleology is excellent for a thinker-level read, but this is an era page. The four causes and eudaimonia are the right depth; teleology as a running theme in Western science for two thousand years is already pressing toward a Thinker: Aristotle page. Consider trimming to keep the era page at era altitude.

11. **Ch 2: The Zeno paradox resolution is promised but not delivered.** "A knot mathematicians didn't fully cut for two thousand years" — this is a wonderful phrase, but the reader is left with no idea what the cut was (infinite series, calculus, Cauchy's epsilon-delta). A single sentence resolution would pay off the cliffhanger and build trust that the writers know the ending.

### Chapter 1 — Before philosophy, and the move that started it
**Grade: STRONG**

**Three-part test:**
- *Worked example:* YES, and unusually strong. The Anaximander courtroom image, the Anaximenes mechanism (air thins/thickens through stages), and the three-generation-correction arc are all genuine demonstrations, not assertions. The reader watches the method in action. Pass.
- *Strongest version:* YES. "Put plainly, philosophy begins not when someone gets the right answer, but when someone offers a kind of answer you are allowed to argue with." This is the steelman and the restatement at once. Pass.
- *Plain restatement:* YES. The final paragraph delivers it clean. Pass.

**Throughline:** Strong. The chapter earns its position as the birth of the enterprise, and the Pythagorean forward-hook ("hold that thought, two chapters from now Plato builds a universe on it") is a clean bridge.

**Voice:** The dry wit is well-calibrated here — "His answer was wrong. That barely matters." The Anaximander fragment ("a courtroom image") is exactly the comparison-as-comprehension-tool the pipeline asks for. No em-dashes, no bold-for-drama.

**Minor observations:**
- "We should be honest about how little we have of him" is a mild meta-narrator wobble but barely noticeable.
- The Burnet attribution is good scholarly hygiene, though it reads slightly academic; this is a SHOULD-FIX only.

### Chapter 2 — The problem of change
**Grade: STRONG**

**Three-part test:**
- *Worked example:* YES for both Heraclitus and Parmenides. The river identity argument is done right (river stays the same river *because* different water flows, not despite it). Parmenides' argument is walked step-by-step. The Achilles paradox is the best worked example in the draft.
- *Strongest version:* YES. "Feel the pull of that before you reject it, because the smartest people of the next two centuries couldn't simply wave it away." This is the explicit steelman instruction the pipeline requires, and it is executed in the prose itself, not just gestured at. Pass.
- *Plain restatement:* YES. "So: Heraclitus says change is the deep truth of things; Parmenides says change is impossible. Both have arguments. The rest of Greek thought is largely the project of buying back the ordinary world from Parmenides." Clean. Pass.

**Throughline:** The "buying back the ordinary world" framing is excellent — it creates immediate stakes for Chapters 3–5 and keeps the reader oriented.

**Voice:** The Democritus quote ("by convention sweet...") is deployed correctly — stated, then its disturbing implication named. Comparison: the brief cheer/caution on atomic theory ("Resist the cheer") is well-placed dry wit.

**Minor observations:**
- The Zeno paradox resolution is promised but not delivered (see SHOULD-FIX #11).
- "One more figure belongs to this chapter, slightly off to the side" is a mild meta-narrator flag. Cut "belongs to this chapter" and it reads cleaner.

### Chapter 3 — The Sophists and Socrates
**Grade: GOOD**

**Three-part test:**
- *Worked example:* PARTIAL. The elenchus example (courage) is the draft's most pedagogically explicit passage, and it is very good up to the point it stops. The reader gets the method — confident definition, patient testing, definition collapses — but not the scandal: that Socrates never gives a positive answer. The method's most unsettling feature (it only destroys, it doesn't build) is named in the following paragraph but not *felt* in the example. A single additional move in the elenchus run (showing the "endurance + good sense" definition also fail) would close the loop.
- *Strongest version:* YES for the Sophists: the rehabilitation is genuine and the Protagoras measure-doctrine is presented at full strength. For Socrates, the divine-assignment frame is given its full weight without endorsement. Pass.
- *Plain restatement:* YES for Sophists; YES for elenchus as practiced. The bumper-sticker correction ("I know that I know nothing" vs. the real line) is a strong comprehension win.

**Throughline:** The political context (democracy → persuasion as power → the Sophists) is excellent setup and genuinely serves comprehension.

**Voice:** The Aristophanes note ("a ridiculous quack who studies the clouds") and the Plato-smear origin story are both good dry wit. No em-dashes.

**Concerns:**
- The trial section is vivid and honest, but the paragraph listing the political context of the trial (Athens had lost the war, several of Socrates' companions were traitors) is dense. The reader may lose the argument thread. Consider whether the trial complexity earns its length at era altitude.
- No comparison for the Sophists (see SHOULD-FIX #7).

### Chapter 4 — Plato

### Chapter 6 — Philosophy as a way of life

**Three-part test:**
- *Worked example (Stoics):* PARTIAL. The Epictetus control-line is quoted and the "disturbed not by things but by judgments" follow-up is quoted. But neither is demonstrated. No situation is set up, no line is drawn, no judgment is examined. The reader gets the theory without seeing it work. **Throughline:** The Hellenistic context (small subject in a vast unstable world, philosophy as therapy) is well set. The "three rival gyms" image is good.

**Concerns:**

### Chapter 7 — Rome and the long afterlife

The three-part test fails on the central ideas of the chapter.

The closing paragraph (Arabic transmission handoff) is clean for a series but doesn't close the loop for a reader who just finished this era page.

**Throughline:** This is the chapter's core problem (MUST-FIX #2). The chapter covers a lot of ground efficiently but reads as relay rather than resolution. The Cicero line ("the whole business of philosophy is learning how to die well") is the closest thing to a throughline statement, but it is Cicero summarizing Socrates, not the narrator landing the era's conclusion.

**Voice:** The Seneca death scene (SHOULD-FIX #6) is the chapter's best moment. Tacitus as source is correctly flagged. The "people still read them on the train" line is a rare earned modern reference. No em-dashes.

**Concerns:**
- MUST-FIX #1 (no worked example for Hellenistic prescriptions).

- MUST-FIX #4 (Neoplatonism named but not taught, Plotinus absent).

## Era-altitude check

The draft is largely well-calibrated at era altitude — it picks signature ideas and renders them vividly without pre-spending the future thinker-level deep reads. The following passages are the closest to over-spending:

- **Ch 5 teleology paragraph** (SHOULD-FIX #10): the "Western science for two thousand years" span belongs to a Thinker: Aristotle page, not an era sweep.
- **Ch 3 trial complexity**: the political texture of the trial is genuinely era-level material, but the accumulation of detail (the vote count, the free-meals proposal) is on the edge of thinker-deep.
- **Ch 6 Chrysippus name-drop**: "the school's great systematizer, Chrysippus... the saying went that without him there would have been no Stoa" — this is correct, but at era altitude it is a distraction. Chrysippus earns this space on a School: Stoicism page.

Everything else — the pre-Socratics, the Cave, the Forms, the four causes, the virtue-as-habit, the control-line, the death-consolation — is pitched correctly for the era.

---

## Single biggest concern

**Chapter 7 has no worked example and no throughline resolution, and it is the chapter the reader closes the era on.**

The era hook promises a thousand-year argument. Chapters 1–6 deliver the argument, vividly and at the right altitude. But Chapter 7 drops the ball at the finish line: it names ideas without teaching them (the Stoic prescription appears twice across Chs 6 and 7 and is never actually demonstrated), it lists thinkers in relay rather than landing a conclusion, and it never circles back to the opening bet — what did the Greeks finally do with the move Thales made? The reader who closes this page knows the history. They may not feel that they *understand* anything they didn't understand about dying or living or what the world behaves by. That gap is this draft's primary repair target.
