## Ordered gloss-gap list — MUST-FIX on top

### MUST-FIX

**MF-1 — Ch 2: "scholastics" / "scholasticism" — first use is backward.**
The term "scholastics" appears in Ch 2 at the Boethius tag ("last of the Romans and the first of the scholastics") *before* "scholasticism" is defined. That tag is correctly flagged as a label nobody can source — but the draft never defines the word at that moment. A newcomer has no idea what a "scholastic" is until Ch 5, three chapters later, where scholasticism gets its inline gloss. Because Ch 2 treats the word as already known, the newcomer hits the tag and gets nothing. **Fix:** either move the gloss forward, or simply omit "scholastics" from Ch 2 (the draft already discredits the tag as unverifiable) and let Ch 5 introduce the term cleanly.

**MF-2 — Ch 2: "monasteries" vs "monks" — partial, but one slip.**
"Monasteries" and "monks" are defined inline ("communities of monks, men who withdrew from ordinary life to pray, work, and study under a shared rule") — that is good. But "Benedictine houses" is used two sentences later with no gloss. Benedictine is a specific monastic order (founded by Saint Benedict; the dominant form of Western monasticism). A newcomer reading "the Benedictine houses" hears a proper noun whose significance they cannot parse.
**Fix:** one-phrase gloss: "the Benedictine houses (monasteries following Saint Benedict's sixth-century rule, the most widespread in western Europe)."

**MF-3 — Ch 2: "Porphyry (a student of Plotinus)" — the gloss is fine, but the *problem of universals* is introduced in Ch 2 and its stakes are NOT explained here.**
The universals problem is named in Ch 2 with "Hold it; it detonates in three chapters." That is a clever structural move. The issue is that the chapter gives no intuition pump at this point — no one-sentence "here's why this matters" — so the promise of detonation lands as a shrug for a newcomer. The full concrete example (two white sheets) arrives correctly in Ch 5. But there is a three-chapter gap where readers who lose the thread will have no handle on why they should care. This is borderline SHOULD-FIX for gate 4 (the argument is not in play yet), but given that it is the era's "longest-running fight," a one-sentence stake at Ch 2's introduction is better than a structural tease with no emotional hook.
**Fix (MUST-FIX given "detonates" promise):** after naming the problem in Ch 2, add one sentence of stakes — e.g., "Whether the categories we think in are real discoveries or just convenient labels will later determine whether faith and reason can stay on the same map."

**MF-4 — Ch 3: "occasionalism" — word not defined at its point of use.**
The draft correctly says (at the close of the al-Ghazālī passage): "This occasionalist argument ships as paraphrase." But "occasionalist" appears here as a label the newcomer has never seen, used as if it is a known term. The argument itself is explained well (God creates the burning alongside the fire each time), but the word "occasionalism" is dropped without a gloss. A newcomer reading this will understand the idea but not know what the word means or why it matters as a named position.
**Fix:** either gloss it inline ("this position — called occasionalism, the view that only God directly causes events and apparent cause-and-effect is just God's regular sequence — ships as paraphrase") or drop the word "occasionalist" from that parenthetical and let the argument stand without the label (the label is not load-bearing here).

**MF-5 — Ch 4: "the Law" used for both Islamic and Jewish law without clear differentiation.**
The word "the Law" appears multiple times in Ch 4 referring to Islamic religious law (from the Averroes passage), and then Maimonides is described as having been "trained to believe in the truth of our holy Law" (Jewish law, the Torah). A reader with no religious background may not realize these are two different legal traditions from two different scriptures. Maimonides' "Law" is the Torah and the body of Jewish legal tradition (halacha); Averroes' "Law" is Islamic religious law (sharia). The draft never names either specifically in the moments where "the Law" is used.
**Fix for the Islamic occurrence:** when Averroes first says scripture "makes observation by reason obligatory," a parenthetical identifying "the Law" as the body of Islamic religious law would close the gap.
**Fix for the Maimonides occurrence:** when "our holy Law" appears, at minimum name it: "trained to believe in the truth of our holy Law — the Torah and the vast body of Jewish legal tradition built on it."

**MF-6 — Ch 5: Abelard's condemnation mechanics — "condemned" is used without explaining what a condemnation meant.**
"He got him condemned twice, at Soissons in 1121 and again at Sens around 1140." A newcomer does not know what it means to be condemned. Was it excommunication? Execution? Being banned from teaching? The consequences are unclear, so the line "the man lost" (even though his method won) is an empty punch. Excommunication is defined clearly in Ch 6 ("formal expulsion from the Church, cutting a person off from its sacraments and community"), but it comes a chapter too late.
**Fix:** one-phrase gloss on the Soissons condemnation: "condemned (forced to burn his own book and barred from teaching the position)." This is also more specific and more shocking than the bare word.

**MF-7 — Ch 6: "Trinity" dropped without definition.**
"Others only revelation can give us (that God is a Trinity, three persons in one being, which no argument could have deduced)." The gloss in parentheses is present — that is the right instinct — but "three persons in one being" will leave a newcomer with no religious background genuinely puzzled. Three persons sharing one being is a claim that needs one more sentence to register as meaningful rather than just strange. A newcomer needs to know this is the specifically Christian doctrine that God is simultaneously Father, Son (Jesus), and Holy Spirit — and that it is not a claim any non-Christian tradition accepts. Without this, the point that "no argument could have deduced" this is meaningless.
**Fix:** expand to: "that God is a Trinity — three distinct persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, who are simultaneously one God, a specifically Christian claim that Jewish and Muslim thinkers rejected — which no argument could have deduced."

**MF-8 — Ch 7: Avignon — used without any geographical or institutional context.**
"Summoned to the papal court at Avignon in 1324..." A newcomer does not know why the papal court would be in Avignon (a city in southern France) rather than in Rome. This is load-bearing: it feeds directly into the "papacy split and discredited" line at the chapter's close. The Avignon papacy (1309–1377, when the popes relocated from Rome to Avignon under French political pressure) is a piece of assumed history the draft never explains.
**Fix:** one clause: "summoned to the papal court at Avignon — where the papacy had relocated from Rome, under French political pressure, a move that many Christians considered a scandal —."

**MF-9 — Ch 7: heresy → excommunication sequence is confusing.**
"Summoned to the papal court at Avignon in 1324 on suspicion of heresy" — heresy is used without definition here. Then: "was excommunicated" — excommunication was defined in Ch 6, which is fine. But "heresy" was also used without a definition in Ch 6 ("the bishop of Paris condemned 219 propositions on pain of excommunication") and in Ch 7 Ockham is charged with it, and the charge is immediately complicated ("studied the pope's pronouncements, and concluded the pope was himself a heretic"). A newcomer reading this will not understand the inversion — the accused declaring his accuser a heretic. The drama of this section depends entirely on understanding what heresy means (believing something the Church formally declares false) and why a friar could throw that charge back at a pope.
**Fix:** heresy needs a gloss at first use in Ch 6 or Ch 7: "heresy (holding a belief the Church had officially declared false — a charge that could mean losing one's teaching license, being forced to recant, or excommunication)." Then the Ockham inversion lands with force.

### SHOULD-FIX

**SF-1 — Era hook: "Western world changed its mind about books" — Judaism is named third.**
The hook lists Christianity, then Islam, then "a renewed Judaism." The phrase "renewed Judaism" is slightly opaque for a newcomer. Judaism did not "renew" the way the draft implies — rather, post-Temple Judaism (after 70 CE) saw a renewed emphasis on textual study and the authority of the rabbis and the Talmud. The draft might instead say "a Judaism increasingly anchored in the Torah and the rabbinic tradition built on it" — or simply "Judaism" without the "renewed," letting Ch 4 introduce the specifics. Not a MUST-FIX because this is only a hook-level mention, but the word "renewed" may imply a reset or reformation that a newcomer could misread.

**SF-2 — Ch 1: "bishop" — used without definition.**
"Alexandria was torn between its Christian bishop, Cyril, and the Roman governor, Orestes." Bishop is used without a one-phrase gloss. For a newcomer with no religious background, "bishop" is an administrative rank whose scope is not obvious. The draft defines pope/papacy mechanics only in Ch 7. A brief gloss here ("bishop, the church official governing a city or region") would help, especially because the bishop's political rivalry with the governor is the whole point of the passage.

**SF-3 — Ch 2: "Frankish" / "Charlemagne" — assumed-known context.**
"The Frankish emperor Charlemagne, organized by an English scholar named Alcuin." The draft assumes readers know who Charlemagne was and what Frankish means. Era-1 readers of the Greeks chapter would not necessarily know this. A one-phrase gloss ("Charlemagne, the Frankish — western European — king who built a short-lived empire and sponsored a revival of learning") would close the gap. Not MUST-FIX because the paragraph's point (books were preserved) lands without it.

**SF-4 — Ch 3: "Sufi (a practitioner of Islamic mysticism, which pursues God through direct experience rather than doctrine)" — this gloss is good.**
No fix needed. Flagged here as positive confirmation that the draft handles this term correctly.

**SF-5 — Ch 3: "caliph is a successor to the Prophet Muhammad as head of the Muslim community" — good, but "the Prophet" is used without identifying Muhammad first.**
The draft defines caliph as "successor to the Prophet Muhammad" — but "the Prophet" appears one clause before without the name: "the Abbasid caliphs (the rulers of the Islamic empire from Baghdad; a caliph is a successor to the Prophet Muhammad)." The name Muhammad is IN the gloss itself, so the newcomer does get the name. But the word "the Prophet" (with capital P, implying an already-known figure) is used before the name is given. This is a minor sequencing issue: the clause should read "successor to Muhammad, the founder-prophet of Islam" rather than "successor to the Prophet Muhammad."

**SF-6 — Ch 3: "the Qur'an" — never named explicitly.**
The draft refers to "a text he believed God had written" (in the break block), "a message from God" (in the hook), and "the Law" (for Islamic law), but the word Qur'an never appears in the draft. For a newcomer, it would be useful — once — to name the Muslim holy text by name. This is SHOULD-FIX rather than MUST-FIX because the concept (God-authored scripture) is clear from context throughout, and the chapter is not centrally about the Qur'an's content. But naming it once in Ch 3 would remove the ambiguity.

**SF-7 — Ch 4: "vizier" used without gloss.**
"Court physician to a sultan's vizier." Vizier (a high administrative official, effectively a prime minister under a Muslim ruler) is not defined. The information is not load-bearing — the point is that Maimonides was at a court — but a newcomer will encounter an unfamiliar word. One-phrase gloss or substitute "a sultan's chief minister."

**SF-8 — Ch 4: "al-Andalus" — the draft defines it inline; that is correct and good.**
"Córdoba in Muslim Spain (al-Andalus, the name for the Iberian territory under Muslim rule)" — this is well-handled. Flagged as a confirmation.

**SF-9 — Ch 5: "cathedral schools" — used without explanation.**
"Cathedral schools swelled." A newcomer does not know what a cathedral school is (a school run by a cathedral — a major church — that predated universities and trained clergy and administrators). Not MUST-FIX because the paragraph immediately introduces universities and the cathedral school is background, but a one-phrase gloss would help.

**SF-10 — Ch 5: "liberal arts" — used without gloss.**
"Paris in theology and the liberal arts." The medieval liberal arts (grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy — the trivium and quadrivium) are not defined. A newcomer hearing "liberal arts" will assume the modern meaning (a broad, non-vocational curriculum). Not MUST-FIX because it is incidental, but "the liberal arts" meant something very specific in the medieval university context and deserves a parenthetical.

**SF-11 — Ch 5: "Norman monastery" — geographic context.**
"A Norman monastery" — Norman means from Normandy, the northern French region whose rulers conquered England in 1066. This is slightly assumed. The draft identifies Bec as "in Normandy," which is fine for those who know the geography, but "in northern France" would close the gap for a newcomer.

**SF-12 — Ch 5: "Sentences" — good gloss, correctly placed.**
"Peter Lombard assembled the Sentences, a vast, organized collection of theological questions with the authorities arrayed under each." This gloss is adequate. The summa format is glossed via Aquinas in Ch 6 ("a vast, orderly attempt to lay out the whole of Christian theology as a reasoned system, written as a beginner's textbook"). Both are adequate.

**SF-13 — Ch 6: "Cistercian abbey" — used without gloss.**
"He died... at a Cistercian abbey." A newcomer does not know what Cistercian means. It is a religious order (a stricter reform of the Benedictines). Not load-bearing — Aquinas died at a monastery is all the reader needs — but one word ("monastery") in place of "Cistercian abbey" would be cleaner.

**SF-14 — Ch 6: "Franciscan" and "Dominican" — Franciscan is defined ("an order of mendicant preachers"), Dominican is defined ("a member of a preaching religious order; a friar, unlike a cloistered monk, lived and worked out in the towns") but the definitions are split. Franciscan appears in Ch 7 for Ockham. Both definitions are adequate taken individually; they are just never side by side, which means a newcomer who misses one will not get the contrast. SHOULD-FIX: note that Franciscans and Dominicans were the two main mendicant orders, and both were defined — the contrast (Franciscans sworn to poverty, Dominicans focused on preaching) is load-bearing in Ch 7's Ockham dispute.**

**SF-15 — Ch 7: "summa" (title format) — not explicitly defined.**
The *Summa Theologiae* is named in Ch 6 and its genre described ("a vast, orderly attempt"). But the word *summa* itself (meaning "a complete account" or "summary of knowledge" — a medieval genre of comprehensive, structured inquiry) is never glossed. SHOULD-FIX for completeness; the descriptive phrase is adequate for a newcomer.

**SF-16 — Ch 7: "voluntarism" — glossed but gloss slightly thin.**
"This is voluntarism (the view that will, not reason, is fundamental, so that things are good because God wills them, rather than God willing them because they are good)." The gloss is present and the distinction is stated. The issue is that a newcomer may not fully grasp WHY this matters — the gloss identifies the position but not the stakes (if things are only good because God wills them arbitrarily, then morality is just divine command, and there is no rational basis for it independent of revelation). This is borderline gate 2 territory (does the idea land?) rather than gate 4 (is the word defined?), so SHOULD-FIX rather than MUST-FIX.

## Summary assessment

The draft is overwhelmingly clear at the narrative level. It defines most era-specific terms at their point of first use, handles the three-religion landscape better than any comparable text I've seen (the reader is never left guessing which tradition they are in), and the argument-walk chapters (Anselm's ontological argument, the Five Ways, Avicenna's flying man) all carry the reader through each step without unexplained jumps. The flying man argument in particular is a model of how to explain a philosophical thought experiment cold.

The MUST-FIX list (9 items) is concentrated in three areas: (a) terms used before their gloss arrives (scholastics in Ch 2, occasionalism in Ch 3, heresy in Ch 6–7), (b) "the Law" used for both Islamic and Jewish law without distinguishing them (Ch 4), and (c) assumed institutional context that a non-Christian, non-religious newcomer will not have (Avignon, Trinity, condemnation-mechanics).

None of the MUST-FIX items require rewriting argument passages; all are fixable with one-phrase inline glosses or small additions. The SHOULD-FIX list is genuine quality improvement but not clarity-blocking.

The draft does NOT fail on: the three-religion structure (handled cleanly throughout), the leap across centuries and geographies (WHEN and WHERE are almost always named), person introductions (every first mention has a one-line who-they-are), or the core arguments (the ontological argument, the First Way, the flying man, the universals fight, and Ockham's razor are all walked step by step with no mysticism gaps).

**Overall gate verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (PASS-WITH-FIXES). All 9 MUST-FIX items are mechanical inline-gloss additions. No chapter needs structural rewrite on clarity grounds.**
