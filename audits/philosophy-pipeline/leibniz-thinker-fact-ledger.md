## A. Biography (CONFIRMED)
- Born **1 July 1646, Leipzig** (Saxony, Holy Roman Empire). Died **14 November
  1716, Hanover**. (Britannica, MacTutor, Wikipedia.) — CONFIRMED
- Father a professor of moral philosophy at Leipzig; died when Leibniz was six;
  Leibniz taught himself Latin and read freely in his father's library as a boy.
  — CONFIRMED (MacTutor/Britannica)
- Studied law + philosophy at Leipzig and Jena; doctorate in law from **Altdorf**
  (1666–67) after Leipzig reportedly declined to grant it (he was thought too young).
  — CONFIRMED (the Altdorf detail; the "too young" reason is the standard account)
- Entered service of the Elector of Mainz (1667). 1672–76 in **Paris** on a
  diplomatic mission; there met Huygens, was drawn deep into mathematics, and did the
  core calculus work. Visited London (1673), shown to the Royal Society. — CONFIRMED
- From **1676 to his death (40 years)** served the **House of Brunswick-Lüneburg
  (Hanover)** as **librarian, councillor, and court historian** (tasked with a
  history of the Brunswick dynasty he never finished). — CONFIRMED
- Polymath: philosophy, mathematics, law, diplomacy, history, geology, engineering,
  Chinese studies, library science. Wrote thousands of letters across Europe; founded
  and was first president of the **Berlin Academy of Sciences (Prussian Academy,
  1700)**. — CONFIRMED
- Built the **Stepped Reckoner** (machine doing all four arithmetic operations;
  designed 1672–74, built later, the **Leibniz wheel** its key part). — CONFIRMED
- **Funeral, 1716:** out of favor at court (the elector George Ludwig had just become
  **King George I of Great Britain** and left Leibniz behind in Hanover, partly over
  the Newton dispute); his funeral was poorly attended. Standard account: of the
  whole court, **only his secretary/servant (Eckhart/Eccard) followed the coffin.**
  — CONFIRMED as the standard biographical account; framed as such, not embellished.

- **Leibniz-Clarke correspondence**, 1715–16 (with Samuel Clarke, defending Newton);
  published 1717 in Clarke's bilingual edition. — CONFIRMED

## C. Verified QUOTES — Monadology, trans. Robert Latta (1898, PD)
Source URL (load-checked, used for verbatim): 
https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Monadology_(Leibniz,_tr._Latta)
All quotes below are Latta's exact words, cited by §:
- **§1** — "The Monad, of which we shall here speak, is nothing but a simple
  substance, which enters into compounds."
- **§3** — "Now where there are no parts, there can be neither extension nor form
  [figure] nor divisibility."
- **§7** — "The Monads have no windows, through which anything could come in or go
  out." — the famous "windowless" line.
- **§17** — "Moreover, it must be confessed that perception and that which depends
  upon it are inexplicable on mechanical grounds"
- **§22** — "…its present is big with its future" (Latta: "its present is big with
  its future"). Used to show each monad unfolds from within.
- **§32** — "And that of sufficient reason, in virtue of which we hold that there can
  be no fact real or existing, no statement true, unless there be a sufficient
  reason, why it should be so and not otherwise." — the principle of sufficient
  reason, in Leibniz's own statement.
- **§53** — "Now, as in the Ideas of God there is an infinite number of possible
  universes, and as only one of them can be actual, there must be a sufficient reason
  for the choice of God."
- **§55** — "Thus the actual existence of the best that wisdom makes known to God is
  due to this, that His goodness makes Him choose it, and His power makes Him produce
  it." — the best-of-possible-worlds choice, stated as a balance of wisdom/goodness.
- **§56** — "Each simple substance has relations which express all the others, and,
  consequently, that it is a perpetual living mirror of the universe." — the mirror.
- **§57** — "And as the same town, looked at from various sides, appears quite
  different and becomes as it were numerous in aspects" — the town-from-many-sides
  image of one universe seen from many monadic points of view.
- **§58** — "By this means there is obtained as great variety as possible, along with
  the greatest possible order; that is to say, it is the way to get as much perfection
  as possible." — best world = the maximum of variety with order (the balance, not
  "every part is good").
- **§62** — "Thus, although each created Monad represents the whole universe, it
  represents more distinctly the body which specially pertains to it"
- **§78** — "The soul follows its own laws, and the body likewise follows its own
  laws; and they agree with each other in virtue of the pre-established harmony
  between all substances." — pre-established harmony stated for mind+body.

## D. Leibniz-Clarke correspondence — trans/own English by Samuel Clarke (1717, PD)
Used carefully; identity of indiscernibles.
- "There is no such thing as two individuals indiscernible from each other." (Fourth
  Paper, §4.) — widely quoted from Clarke's PD edition; framed as Leibniz's statement
  of the **identity of indiscernibles** (no two genuinely distinct things can share
  ALL properties; if they did they would be one thing). — CONFIRMED wording in the PD
  correspondence; quoted.
- The **two-leaves anecdote** (the Herrenhausen garden; a courtier, challenged by the
  Electress/Princess Sophie, hunts for two identical leaves and fails) — appears in
  the Fourth Paper as Leibniz's own illustration. The courtier is sometimes named
  (Carl August von Alvensleben) in later accounts; the read FRAMES it as the anecdote
  Leibniz tells, names Sophie's garden, does not assert the courtier's name as
  certain. — CONFIRMED that the leaves illustration is Leibniz's own in the
  correspondence.

## E. Two clocks (pre-established harmony) — CONFIRMED concept, PARAPHRASED
- The **two-clocks analogy** is Leibniz's, from the 1696 clarification of the "New
  System." Three ways two clocks stay in agreement: (1) mutual influence (one drives
  the other) — rejected; (2) a craftsman constantly resetting them — Leibniz's name
  for the **occasionalist** view (God intervenes each moment), rejected as needing
  perpetual miracle; (3) made so perfectly at the start that they keep time forever on
  their own — **pre-established harmony**, his answer. — CONFIRMED (IEP, Yale Leibniz
  passages). PARAPHRASED (no confirmed PD English of the exact 1696 passage used as a
  quote); the Monadology §78 quote carries the harmony claim verbatim.

## F. Sufficient reason + identity of indiscernibles (CONFIRMED)
- **Principle of sufficient reason (PSR):** nothing is so without a reason why it is
  so and not otherwise — Monadology §32 (quoted above). One of Leibniz's two great
  principles (the other: non-contradiction, §31). — CONFIRMED
- **Identity of indiscernibles** follows from PSR: if two things shared every
  property, God would have had no sufficient reason to put this one here and that one
  there, so they cannot be two; they are one. — CONFIRMED as Leibniz's own derivation
  (Clarke corr.; SEP/IEP).

## G. Best of all possible worlds / theodicy — CONFIRMED, FRAMED PRECISELY
- *Theodicy* (1710): God, before creating, surveys the **infinitely many possible
  worlds** (all the internally-consistent total histories), and being perfectly wise,
  good, and powerful, **chooses the best one** (Monadology §53–55, quoted). — CONFIRMED
- **PRECISE FRAMING (hard requirement):** "best" is about the **balance of the whole**
  — the greatest variety together with the greatest order/simplicity of laws
  (Monadology §58, quoted) — NOT a claim that every individual event is good or that
  suffering is good. Evil is the **necessary cost** of the best overall package: a
  world with free creatures and lawful nature cannot exclude all evil without losing
  more good elsewhere. Leibniz distinguishes metaphysical, physical, and moral evil
  and argues local evils can serve a greater whole. The read states this is about the
  WHOLE's balance, never "everything that happens is good." — CONFIRMED (SEP "Leibniz
  on the Problem of Evil"; Britannica).
- **Voltaire / Candide / Dr Pangloss (1759):** Voltaire's satire — written after the
  1755 Lisbon earthquake — caricatures the doctrine through **Dr Pangloss**, who
  insists "all is for the best in this best of all possible worlds" no matter the
  horror. Scholars hold this **conflates Leibniz's metaphysical claim (evil can exist
  in the best total world) with moral complacency (do nothing, all is already
  optimal)** — a misrepresentation, the famous caricature. The read frames Pangloss
  AS the caricature, then states what Leibniz actually argued. — CONFIRMED; framed as
  caricature (Wikipedia "Candide"; scholarship via Project MUSE "Is Pangloss
  Leibniz?").

## H. Calculus + the Newton dispute — CONFIRMED, framed fairly
- Leibniz developed the **differential and integral calculus independently** in the
  mid-1670s (Paris), with the notation still used: **dx, dy, the long-S integral sign
  ∫** (Leibniz's notation, superior for computation and standard today). — CONFIRMED
- **Newton** had developed his "method of fluxions" earlier (from ~1665–66) but did
  not publish it; **Leibniz published first** — in **Acta Eruditorum, 1684** (the
  *Nova Methodus*, on the differential calculus) and 1686. — CONFIRMED
- **Priority dispute:** accusations of plagiarism flew (both directions). The Royal
  Society, with **Newton its president**, appointed an "impartial" committee in 1712
  whose report (**Commercium Epistolicum**) found for Newton — and **Newton himself
  anonymously drafted the report and its review.** — CONFIRMED. Modern consensus:
  **both invented it independently**; Leibniz's notation won. The read names the
  rigged committee honestly and credits both. — CONFIRMED (MacTutor; Royal Society
  histories).
- Honest-warts: the dispute poisoned Leibniz's last years; it is part of why he died
  out of favor. The standard account (not over-asserted).

## I. Characteristica universalis / calculus ratiocinator — CONFIRMED
- Leibniz dreamed of a **characteristica universalis**: a universal symbolic language
  in which every concept gets an exact character (built on an "alphabet of human
  thought"), so reasoning becomes **calculation** — paired with a **calculus
  ratiocinator**, a method/engine for computing inferences. His slogan-image: when
  two people disagree, instead of quarreling they could say "let us calculate"
  (*calculemus*) and settle it by reckoning. — CONFIRMED (Wikipedia; SEP). The
  *calculemus* line is widely attributed; the read frames it as Leibniz's image of the
  dream, not as a pinned verbatim PD quotation (paraphrased), unless string-checked.
- Recognized as a **forerunner of symbolic / mathematical logic** (Boole, Frege,
  computer science later). The read makes the forward-pointer cautiously (he
  anticipated the AIM; he did not build modern logic). — CONFIRMED framing.

## J. The break (era-3 contrast — steelmanned)
- **Before:** Descartes left a universe of two substances (mind, matter) that cannot
  touch (the interaction problem Elisabeth of Bohemia exposed — see the Descartes
  read); Spinoza answered by collapsing everything into **one** substance (God-or-
  Nature) — see the Spinoza hub. Both are coherent, both are steelmanned.
- **After:** Leibniz rejects BOTH. Not one substance (Spinoza) and not two-kinds-that-
  can't-touch (Descartes), but **infinitely many** simple substances (monads), none
  of which interact at all, coordinated by **pre-established harmony**. The concrete
  move: dissolve interaction entirely rather than explain it. — CONFIRMED as the
  era-3 framing; differentiated from the era read by going deeper into monads, PSR,
  indiscernibles, theodicy, the dispute, and the characteristica.
