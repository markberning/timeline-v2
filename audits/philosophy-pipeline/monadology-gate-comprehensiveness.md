## Methodology

Built from the gate 3 mandate for a WORK read: walk the text's argument, section
by section, as an argument that builds. The checklist below is derived
independently from the Monadology itself, the Latta translation (Wikisource §§1–90),
and standard secondary literature (SEP, IEP). Each item is evaluated against the
seven chapters of the draft.

## Must-cover checklist for a WORK read of the Monadology

### 1. Monads as simple substances — why simplicity is forced

**Verdict: COVERED**

Draft Ch. 1 ("The book with no title") handles §§1–2 correctly: monads as partless,
the inference that if there were no simples there would be nothing at all, the
word monad from the Greek for unit. Draft Ch. 2 ("No windows") picks up §§3–6:
no extension, no shape, begin only by creation, end only by annihilation. Well done.

**No gap.**

---

### 2. Windowlessness / no causal interaction — forced by simplicity, not assumed

**Verdict: COVERED**

Draft Ch. 2 handles §7 at length — the famous phrase, the full logical derivation
(no parts → no opening → nothing can enter or leave), and the consequence that
no monad ever acts on any other. The section correctly identifies windowlessness
as derived, not stipulated. The billiard ball / fire / spoken word examples land
the "no causation between substances" point concretely.

**No gap.**

---

### 3. Perception and appetition — the internal engine

**Verdict: COVERED — but THIN on "petites perceptions"**

Draft Ch. 2 names both perception (the monad's representing of the universe in a
unity) and appetition (the internal force driving the monad from state to state)
and correctly derives both from the no-windows constraint. The identity of
indiscernibles is correctly traced to this source.

**What is THIN:** The concept of *petites perceptions* (unconscious or micro-
perceptions, §§14, 19–21 in the text) — Leibniz's claim that perception is
everywhere below the threshold of awareness, that the sound of the sea is the
unconscious sum of infinitely many tiny wave-perceptions — is named in Ch. 3 only
as "dim and confused" perception of the bare monads. The *petites perceptions*
doctrine is a significant and distinctive move: it grounds the denial of Descartes'
claim that all mental states are conscious, and it does real explanatory work in
the ladder argument. The current treatment reduces it to a footnote when it is a
hinge.

**Verdict: SHOULD-CONSIDER adding a sentence or two on petites perceptions in
Ch. 3 — not MUST-ADD (the ladder argument survives without it), but the omission
means the anti-Cartesian force of the perception doctrine is understated.**

---

### 4. The ladder — bare monads → souls → spirits (the three grades)

**Verdict: COVERED**

Draft Ch. 3 ("From a sleeping stone to a knowing soul") walks the three grades
explicitly: bare monads (confused perception, no memory, "perpetual swoon"), souls
(add memory, the dog-flinching example, "imitates reasoning without being it"), and
spirits (apperception, reflective self-knowledge, science, morality, knowledge of
God). The distinction between perception and apperception (§14, Latta Q3 verbatim)
is stated and integrated. This chapter is the draft's cleanest comprehensiveness
win.

**No gap.**

---

### 5. The two great principles — contradiction and sufficient reason

**Verdict: COVERED — but THIN on the distinction between truths of reason and truths of fact**

Draft Ch. 4 ("The two great principles") handles §§31–32 through §§36–37 well:
the principle of contradiction stated, the principle of sufficient reason stated,
and the crowbar move from the PSR to the existence of God executed clearly. §§33–35
(the distinction between necessary truths / truths of reason and contingent truths /
truths of fact, and the asymmetry of their analyses — necessary truths terminate,
contingent truths run to infinity) are present but go by fast in the third
paragraph of Ch. 4. The asymmetry is load-bearing: it is why God alone can grasp
the full reason for any contingent fact, which matters both for the God-proof and
for the theodicy (God sees the whole infinite series; we cannot). 

**Verdict: SHOULD-CONSIDER a slightly fuller beat on the necessary/contingent
asymmetry — currently compressed to one paragraph that does the work but does not
let it breathe. Not MUST-ADD, but expanding it would close a comprehension gap
flagged in the gate-2 surface.**

---

### 6. Pre-established harmony — the two-clocks solution to soul/body

**Verdict: COVERED — but THIN on the three rival explanations**

Draft Ch. 7 ("Mirrors, two clocks, and the City of God") states pre-established
harmony at §§78–79 (Latta Q12–Q13 verbatim) and uses the two-clocks image
correctly. The three rival accounts of soul/body coordination are correctly named
and dismissed in order: ordinary causation (impossible for windowless monads),
constant divine intervention (God as repairman — rejected), and pre-established
harmony (third, accepted). This is well-structured.

**What is THIN:** The draft does not explicitly name the two rival philosophical
positions — **occasionalism** (Malebranche's solution: God intervenes on every
occasion of soul-body correspondence) and **Descartes' interactionism** (the
position Leibniz is replacing) — as the named schools they are. Occasionalism
is Leibniz's primary polemical target in the harmony literature. A WORK read
of the Monadology that does not name Malebranche/occasionalism as the defeated
alternative leaves a silent missing link: why is "constant divine intervention"
on the table? For whom was that a live option? The gate-3 mandate for philosophy
is that **the influence chain may not have silent missing links**, and this is one.

**Verdict: MUST-ADD — one sentence naming occasionalism (Malebranche) as the
rejected alternative, so the two-clocks argument lands as a three-way contest
with named rivals, not an abstract dismissal of unnamed options.**

---

### 7. God as necessary being — the cosmological argument in the PSR

**Verdict: COVERED**

Draft Ch. 5 ("The necessary being") walks §§38–40, 45 correctly: God as the
substance in which the variety of changes is contained eminently (Latta Q6 verbatim),
the uniqueness proof, the unlimited-perfection derivation, and the ontological
move at §45 (if possible then actual, because existence follows from essence). The
point that God is reached by the same instrument as everything else (PSR applied
to the whole contingent series) is explicit in the final paragraph and is a genuine
comprehensiveness win.

**No gap.**

---

### 8. The ontological argument — §45

**Verdict: COVERED — but THIN on what makes it distinctive**

The ontological argument appears in draft Ch. 5, second paragraph, correctly located
at §45 and correctly stated (God alone has the privilege that if such a being is
possible it must exist; existence follows from essence). However, the draft gives
it one sentence "almost in passing" (its own words) without flagging: (a) this
is Leibniz's own version of an argument Descartes also used in *Meditations* V;
(b) Leibniz's version adds a **possibility premise** (it must first be shown that
a necessary being is possible, i.e., that the concept is not self-contradictory)
that Descartes' version lacked — Leibniz regarded the Cartesian version as
incomplete for exactly this reason. Leibniz's refinement of the ontological
argument is a documented original contribution, not just a restatement of
Descartes, and a WORK read should flag it.

**Verdict: MUST-ADD — a sentence noting that Leibniz's §45 adds the possibility
premise Descartes omitted; this is the philosophically interesting move and a
direct engagement with a rival Leibniz explicitly criticized.**

### 9. Best of all possible worlds + theodicy + three kinds of evil

**Verdict: COVERED**

Draft Ch. 6 ("The best of all possible worlds") is the draft's most thorough
chapter. Covers: §53 (infinite possible worlds), §54 (fitness / degrees of
perfection), §55 (wisdom + goodness + power → the best), §58 (greatest variety
with simplest laws as the content of "best"), the three kinds of evil
(metaphysical / physical / moral), the caricature (Voltaire / Pangloss / *Candide*
1759 / Lisbon earthquake), and the inversion of the caricature vs. the actual
argument (explanation ≠ moral complacency). This chapter handles all the
mandatory items from the brief, including the holistic/comparative nature of the
claim.

**No gap on the theodicy core. One SHOULD-CONSIDER below (§10).**

### 10. The Lisbon earthquake as context for *Candide*

**Verdict: COVERED**

The 1755 Lisbon earthquake is named and contextualized (killed tens of thousands,
shook European confidence in a benevolent order) before *Candide* is introduced.
This is correct and necessary: without the earthquake, *Candide* reads as a
satirist's whim; with it, the caricature has a historical anchor.

**No gap.**

### 11. Each monad mirroring the universe / the town-from-many-sides image

**Verdict: COVERED**

Draft Ch. 7 handles §§56–58 in full: the perpetual living mirror (Latta Q10
verbatim), the town-from-many-sides image (Latta Q11 verbatim), and the
elucidation that there is one universe mirrored infinitely many times from
infinitely many viewpoints. The closing synthesis — the best world as the one
that secures greatest variety with greatest order — is also present.

**No gap.**

### 12. Relation to Descartes — the ladder vs. the line; the animal-machine problem

**Verdict: COVERED — but THIN on the mind-body interaction problem**

Draft Ch. 3 handles the Descartes comparison at the correct moment: Descartes
drew a hard line (humans = conscious minds; animals = mere machines), Leibniz
replaces the line with a slope (no point where perception switches on out of
nothing). The cross-link to the Descartes thinker read is present. This is the
right framing.

**What is THIN:** The draft addresses the Descartes divergence primarily through
the ladder/animals dispute, but Descartes is also relevant at the soul-body
interface: Leibniz's pre-established harmony is an explicit answer to Descartes'
unresolved **mind-body interaction problem** (how does a non-extended mind move
an extended body? Descartes never satisfactorily answered this; Princess Elisabeth
of Bohemia pressed him on exactly this point). Draft Ch. 7 notes that pre-
established harmony "dissolves the mind-body problem Descartes left open" in one
phrase, but does not name the problem. Since the Monadology's pre-established
harmony is one of its most famous moves, and since the Descartes interaction
problem is what motivates the whole inquiry, a WORK read should name it even
briefly.

**Verdict: MUST-ADD — a sentence in Ch. 7 naming the Cartesian interaction problem
(how does a non-extended mind move a body?) as the thing pre-established harmony
dissolves, not merely that it "dissolves the mind-body problem" in the abstract.**

### 13. Relation to Spinoza — one substance vs. infinite monads

**Verdict: MISSING**

The brief explicitly requires the Spinoza comparison: one substance (Spinoza's
monism, where God and Nature are the same thing and finite things are modes of the
single infinite substance) vs. Leibniz's infinite monads. This is not a peripheral
comparison. Leibniz knew Spinoza, corresponded with him (the 1676 visit to Spinoza
in The Hague), and the Monadology's pluralism of sealed individual substances is in
direct tension with Spinoza's monism. The monads are NOT modes of one substance;
they are genuine individual substances, each with its own intrinsic character.
This distinction matters for understanding why Leibniz needed pre-established
harmony at all: if he were a Spinozist, there would be no problem of individual
substances synchronizing, because they would all be aspects of one thing. The fact
that they are genuinely many is what makes the harmony philosophically surprising.
The brief flags this item explicitly; the draft contains zero mention of Spinoza.

**Verdict: MUST-ADD — a concise Spinoza contrast (one substance vs. infinite
genuinely distinct monads) that explains why the problem of inter-monad agreement
is real and why Spinoza's solution (monism) was not available to Leibniz. This is
not duplication of the thinker read (which handles the historical Leibniz-Spinoza
meeting); it is explaining the Monadology's philosophical stakes.**

**No gap.**

### 15. Matter as "well-founded phenomenon" — what happens to matter in the system

**Verdict: MISSING**

The Monadology demotes matter to a "well-founded phenomenon" — real at the level
of appearances, grounded in the aggregate perceptions of monads, but not
metaphysically fundamental (it is not itself a substance). This is essential for
the reader to understand what Leibniz is actually saying about the physical world
the new science described so successfully: he is not denying the physics. He is
saying the physics describes phenomena that are real but derivative, grounded in
the monadic substrate. The ledger (§5, Position 1) flags this explicitly: "matter,
as we know it, is composed of monads, and is itself a 'well-founded phenomenon' —
real but dependent on these ultimate simples." The draft does not use this phrase
or make this point. Without it, the reader is left uncertain whether Leibniz is
denying the existence of matter altogether (idealism in the Berkeley sense) or
doing something more subtle. The "break" block in the draft correctly says matter
"is demoted, recast as the well-grounded appearance" — but this appears only in
the break block, not in the chapter that actually walks the argument.

**Verdict: MUST-ADD — the "well-founded phenomenon" status of matter needs at
least one sentence in the main argument walk (Ch. 1 or Ch. 2) to prevent readers
from inferring that Leibniz is a simple immaterialist. The break block plants it
but the argument walk never picks it up.**

## MUST-ADD items (5 total)

**M1 — Occasionalism named (Ch. 7)**
The "constant divine intervention" option in the two-clocks argument is
Malebranche's occasionalism. Name it. One sentence: "Leibniz's primary rival here
was Malebranche, whose occasionalism held that God intervenes directly on every
occasion of soul-body correspondence, a perpetual miracle." Without this, the
reader has no idea who held the "God as repairman" position or why anyone would,
and the three-way contest that makes pre-established harmony persuasive collapses
into an abstract dismissal.

**M2 — Leibniz's possibility refinement of the ontological argument (Ch. 5)**
The draft says Leibniz deploys the ontological argument "almost in passing." A
WORK read should note that his version (§45) adds a possibility premise absent in
Descartes — Leibniz explicitly argued Descartes' version was incomplete because it
did not first demonstrate that the concept of a necessary being is self-consistent.
One sentence is enough; the point is philosophically distinctive and not in the
thinker read at argument-structure depth.

**M3 — Cartesian interaction problem named (Ch. 7)**
"Dissolves the mind-body problem Descartes left open" needs a sentence of
specification: Descartes claimed the mind (non-extended) acts on the body
(extended), but never explained how — Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed him
on exactly this in correspondence, and he could not answer. Pre-established
harmony dissolves the problem by denying interaction happens. Without naming the
problem, the reader cannot feel what the dissolution accomplishes.

**M4 — Spinoza contrast: one substance vs. infinite monads (Ch. 2 or Ch. 7)**
A concise paragraph (or substantial sentence) contrasting Spinoza's monism (God
and Nature are one infinite substance; finite things are modes, not substances in
their own right) with Leibniz's pluralism (infinite genuine individual substances,
each truly distinct). The contrast explains why the agreement problem is real for
Leibniz but dissolves for Spinoza, and why pre-established harmony is the signature
move of a pluralist, not a monist. The brief identifies this as a required item;
it is entirely absent from the draft. Does not need to restate the thinker-read
account of the Leibniz-Spinoza correspondence; a conceptual contrast is enough.

**M5 — Matter as "well-founded phenomenon" (Ch. 1 or Ch. 2)**
The break block correctly says matter is "demoted, recast as the well-grounded
appearance," but the argument walk never picks this phrase up. The WORK read
needs at least one sentence making explicit that matter is not denied but demoted —
a well-founded phenomenon, real at the level of appearances, grounded in the
aggregate perceptions of monads. Without it, readers may misread Leibniz as a
simple immaterialist (Berkeley's position) rather than a pluralist who preserves
the appearances while explaining their ground.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER items (3 total)

**S1 — Petites perceptions (Ch. 3)**
A sentence on the *petites perceptions* doctrine (§§14, 19–21) — the claim that
perception is always below the threshold of awareness in bare monads and most
animals, and that conscious perception is the rare, high end of a continuous
spectrum. This is what makes the ladder argument's anti-Cartesian force concrete.

**S2 — Necessary/contingent asymmetry (Ch. 4)**
The asymmetry that necessary truths terminate in analysis while contingent truths
run to infinity (only God can complete the chain) could use one more beat — it pays
off in the theodicy argument (God sees the whole infinite reason for this world;
we cannot) and would deepen Ch. 4's third paragraph.

**S3 — Descartes Ch. 3 comparison: add § reference**
The Descartes comparison in Ch. 3 is close to the thinker-read altitude. Adding
a specific §-cite (§§19–30) would make the depth differential visible to a reader
who has seen both pages.

## Silent missing links in the argument chain

The gate-3 mandate flags **silent missing links in the influence chain**. Two are
present in this draft:

**Link gap A (now flagged as M1):** The occasionalism option in the two-clocks
argument assumes the reader knows why "constant divine intervention" was on the
table at all. Malebranche is the missing link. The argument's three-way structure
is clear only if the three parties are named.

**Link gap B (now flagged as M4):** The draft opens the break block on the
Cartesian matter-in-motion picture as the thing Leibniz replaces. But in the
early modern landscape, Spinoza's monism is an equally important contrast to
the monad pluralism. The draft's break is Leibniz vs. Descartes; the
comprehensiveness gate requires at minimum acknowledging Spinoza's competing
answer to the same problem.

**Link gap C (now flagged as M5):** The break block correctly demotes matter but
the argument walk never closes the loop on what matter IS in the system. A reader
finishing Ch. 2 (which establishes monads as the real units) reasonably asks:
"Then what is physical matter?" The answer — well-founded phenomenon — is in the
break block but disappears from the argument chapters. This is the single most
common misreading of the Monadology (conflation with Berkeley's idealism) and the
draft's silence on it in the main text is a genuine argument-chain gap.
