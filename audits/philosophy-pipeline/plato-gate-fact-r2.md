### Tag 1 — Berengar of Poitiers (~1150) as earliest known Latin "amicus"-type formula, using Socrates

**R2 draft text (Ch 7):**
> "That snappy Latin sentence is a medieval paraphrase: the earliest known Latin
> version, using Socrates rather than Plato, turns up in the 12th-century writer
> Berengar of Poitiers, around 1150 [VERIFY: Berengar of Poitiers, c. 1150,
> earliest Latin version of the "amicus" formula, using Socrates], and it circulated
> in various forms afterward (Roger Bacon has a Socrates version around 1267; Newton
> later wrote the Plato version in a notebook)."

**Web findings:**

Wikipedia's "Amicus Plato, sed magis amica veritas" article confirms Berengar of
Poitiers as the earliest known Latin attestation. His letter to the bishop of Mende
(dated "towards 1150" by Wikipedia's Berengar article) contains:

> "Amicus est Socrates, sed magis amica est ueritas. Cum Socrate oportet sollicitum
> esse, sed magis cum ueritate."

This predates Roger Bacon's Opus Majus (c. 1267), which attributes: "Amicus est
Socrates, magister meus, sed magis est amica veritas." The Wikipedia article also
notes that Greek manuscript "Lives of Aristotle" may carry an earlier version of
the underlying sentiment, but those manuscripts carry no specific dateable Latin
text; the earliest dated Latin prose formulation remains Berengar.

The Wikipedia Berengar of Poitiers article confirms he wrote "towards 1150" — the
draft's "around 1150" is an accurate rendering. He is described as a younger
contemporary of Peter Abelard (died 1142), writing the letter after Abelard's
condemnation.

**One precision note:** The Wikipedia article on the formula notes some complexity
about even earlier analogues in unnamed manuscript traditions, but the scholarly
consensus for the earliest *dateable* Latin attestation remains Berengar. The
draft's framing "earliest known Latin version" is defensible — it does not claim
absolute first-ever, only earliest known, which the sources support.

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

Berengar of Poitiers, c. 1150, earliest known dateable Latin version of the
formula, using Socrates not Plato; Roger Bacon c. 1267 is a correct later
circulation point; Newton's Plato version is also confirmed. The draft's framing
is accurate in every detail, and it correctly preserves the "medieval paraphrase,
not verbatim Aristotle" frame.

### Tag 2 — Theaetetus: three definitions of knowledge, each rejected, dialogue ends in aporia

**R2 draft text (Ch 7):**
> "In the *Theaetetus*, Plato has Socrates ask flatly 'what is knowledge?' and
> chase three answers in turn [VERIFY: Theaetetus, three definitions of knowledge
> each examined and rejected, dialogue ends in aporia]. First: knowledge is just
> *perception*, what your senses report... Second: knowledge is *true belief*...
> Third, and closest: knowledge is true belief *plus an account*, true belief you
> can explain and back up. And that one slips through Plato's fingers as well...
> The dialogue ends in *aporia*, stuck, with no definition standing."

**Web findings:**

SEP "Plato on Knowledge in the Theaetetus" fully confirms the three-definition
structure:

1. **D1 — Knowledge is perception** (aisthêsis): Sections 151e–187a. Linked by
   Socrates to Protagoras's "man is the measure" thesis and Heraclitean flux.
   Refuted through twelve objections, particularly via the koina (common notions
   like existence and sameness) that cannot derive from sensation.

2. **D2 — Knowledge is true belief** (orthê doxa): Sections 187b–201c. Refuted
   via the jury counterexample: skilled advocates can produce true beliefs in jurors
   without those jurors having knowledge, proving true belief and knowledge are
   distinct.

3. **D3 — Knowledge is true belief with an account** (meta logou alêthê doxan):
   Sections 201d–210a. Three interpretations of what "logos" must add are examined
   (the Dream Theory and two further analyses); all collapse.

The dialogue ends in aporia with no positive definition surviving.

The draft's summary — three definitions (perception; true belief; true belief plus
an account/logos), each rejected, ending in aporia — is precisely correct. The
specific wording "an account" for logos is the standard English rendering.

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

All three definitions named correctly, each correctly described as rejected, the
aporia conclusion confirmed. The draft's framing of this as "the one place Plato
interrogates knowledge with the Forms set aside rather than pre-loaded as the
answer" is also accurate: the Theaetetus is a late dialogue in which the Forms
play no solving role.

### Tag 3 — Antiochus of Ascalon: broke with Skeptical Academy, restored positive Platonic doctrine, link to Middle Platonism; Cicero studied under him

**R2 draft text (Ch 7):**
> "The hinge is a first-century-BC philosopher named **Antiochus of Ascalon**,
> who broke with the Skeptical Academy, declared that its centuries of suspended
> judgment had been a wrong turn, and reasserted a positive, doctrinal Plato as
> the tradition's authentic core [VERIFY: Antiochus of Ascalon, c. 1st century BC,
> rejected the Skeptical Academy's suspension of judgment and restored positive
> Platonic doctrine; Cicero studied under him]. It is his dogmatic Plato, not the
> skeptics', that the Roman world inherited."

**Web findings:**

Wikipedia (Antiochus of Ascalon) and the SEP entry fully confirm every element:

- **Dates:** c. 135/130 – c. 68 BC — 1st century BC, confirmed.
- **Break with skepticism:** He rejected Academic skepticism under Philo of
  Larissa, around 88 BC, and founded his own "Old Academy," claiming to restore
  original Platonic doctrine that he argued the New Academy's skeptics had betrayed.
- **Positive doctrinal stance:** He maintained that knowledge is possible and
  asserted positive doctrines (a dogmatist), explicitly rejecting the suspension
  of judgment (epochê) characteristic of the Skeptical Academy.
- **Middle Platonism:** Wikipedia's Middle Platonism article states: "Middle
  Platonism lasted from about 90 BC — when Antiochus of Ascalon rejected the
  skepticism of the new Academy — until the development of neoplatonism under
  Plotinus in the 3rd century." He is confirmed as the first philosopher in the
  Middle Platonist tradition.
- **Cicero:** Confirmed. Cicero spent six months with Antiochus in Athens in
  79 BC; Cicero called him "the best and wisest of the Academics, and the most
  polished and acute philosopher of his age." What we know of Antiochus's doctrines
  is largely preserved in Cicero's writings.
- **Roman world inheritance:** Confirmed. The Wikipedia article states that after
  the New Academy declined, Antiochus's Old Academy was the primary institution
  continuing the Platonic tradition, and his dogmatic approach became foundational
  for Roman philosophical inheritance.

**One precision note:** The Wikipedia Antiochus article notes his philosophy was
"often more Stoic than Platonist," reflecting his eclectic synthesis. The draft's
claim that he "reasserted a positive, doctrinal Plato as the tradition's authentic
core" is accurate about his *self-presentation* and historical role, even if
scholars debate whether his Plato was genuinely Platonic. The draft does not claim
his synthesis was orthodox Platonism — it says "doctrinal Plato as the tradition's
authentic core," which is exactly how Antiochus characterized his own project. This
is a fine framing at era altitude.

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

All four sub-claims confirmed: 1st-century BC dates; rejection of Skeptical Academy
suspension of judgment; reassertion of positive Platonic doctrine; Cicero as
student. The claim that his dogmatic Plato is what the Roman world inherited, making
Middle Platonism possible, is also confirmed.

### Tag 4 — Phaedrus Theuth/Thamus myth (274b–277a): king Thamus's objection that writing breeds forgetting and the mere appearance of wisdom

**R2 draft text (Ch 7):**
> "The Egyptian god Theuth, the inventor of writing, brings his invention to the
> king Thamus and presents it as a gift that will make people wiser and improve
> their memory. Thamus turns it down. Writing, he says, will do the opposite of
> what Theuth promises: people who rely on marks on a page will stop exercising
> their memory and lose it, and worse, they will *seem* to know a great deal while
> having learned nothing, because a reader mistakes having read a thing for having
> understood it. [VERIFY: Phaedrus, the Theuth/Thamus myth, 274b-277a, the king
> Thamus's objection that writing produces forgetting and the appearance of wisdom]"

**Web findings:**

Multiple sources (GradeSaver, LitCharts, Perseus Digital Library, Jowett
translation excerpt, conversational-leadership.net) all confirm the myth's content
and locus (274b–277a) precisely:

- **Theuth's claim:** He presents writing as "an elixir of memory and wisdom" /
  "a specific both for the memory and for the wit" (Jowett).
- **Thamus's refusal:** Confirmed — Thamus turns down the gift.
- **Forgetting:** Thamus says the invention "will create forgetfulness in the
  learners' souls, because they will not use their memories; they will trust to
  the external written characters and not remember of themselves" (Jowett, from
  benschmidt.org Phaedrus PDF).
- **Appearance of wisdom:** Thamus says students "will be generally knowing
  nothing; they will be tiresome company, having the show of wisdom without the
  reality" (Jowett formulation, multiple sources). The draft's "mere appearance
  of wisdom" and "a reader mistakes having read a thing for having understood it"
  are accurate paraphrases of this objection.
- **Locus 274b–277a:** Confirmed. The Perseus Digital Library entry at page 274
  confirms this section of the Phaedrus, and GradeSaver's study guide explicitly
  labels this section "274b–277a."

The draft's retelling is an accurate prose summary of the myth. The god is called
Theuth (sometimes "Thoth" in other traditions — Jowett's Phaedrus uses "Theuth"),
and Thamus is correctly identified as the king who refuses the gift. The two
objections (forgetting; appearance rather than reality of wisdom) are both present
in the source.

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

Myth locus, characters, Thamus's two-pronged objection (forgetting + appearance
of wisdom), and Theuth's claim that writing improves memory — all confirmed against
the Jowett text at 274b–277a.

### Tag 5 — Timaeus cosmology: Demiurge shaping the world looking to the Forms; dialogue traveled separately into the Latin Middle Ages

**R2 draft text (Ch 7):**
> "(Plotinus also leaned on Plato's one cosmological dialogue, the *Timaeus*, where
> a divine craftsman shapes the visible world using the Forms as his blueprints;
> that dialogue, which connects the Forms to a story of creation, traveled
> separately into the Latin Middle Ages and did as much as any single text to make
> Platonism feel congenial to Christian cosmology.) [VERIFY: Timaeus, a divine
> craftsman shapes the visible world using the Forms as blueprints; the dialogue
> traveled separately into the Latin Middle Ages]"

**Web findings:**

SEP "Plato's Timaeus" and associated sources confirm both sub-claims:

**Sub-claim 5a — Demiurge shapes the world using the Forms as blueprints:**
SEP confirms: "the Craftsman, imitating an unchanging and eternal model, imposes
mathematical order on a preexistent chaos to generate the ordered universe." The
Forms are the model ("something that always is") the craftsman looks to. This is
the standard description of the Timaeus cosmology in scholarship, confirmed by
multiple sources (SEP, IEP, Wikipedia Demiurge article).

**Sub-claim 5b — Traveled separately into the Latin Middle Ages:**
SEP explicitly states: "Calcidius' Latin translation in the fourth century CE made
it the only text of Plato available in the Middle Ages in the Latin West until the
twelfth century." The Cambridge University Press book *Plato's Timaeus and the
Latin Tradition* further confirms this reception history. The Calcidius/Chalcidius
partial translation (to 53c, c. 321 CE) was the primary vehicle.

**Sub-claim 5c — Made Platonism congenial to Christian cosmology:**
Confirmed by multiple sources. The search results note: "shaping Christian doctrines
of creation, time, and divine providence in authors such as Augustine." The 12th-
century School of Chartres (Thierry of Chartres, William of Conches) wrote
extensive Christian commentaries on the Timaeus, interpreting it in light of the
Christian faith and connecting it to Genesis and creatio ex nihilo. The Cambridge
book title is explicit: "Greek Philosophy, Latin Reception, and Christian Contexts."

**One precision note:** The draft says "Plato's one cosmological dialogue." This is
accurate in the sense that the Timaeus is the only full-length Platonic cosmogony;
the Critias is fragmentary and tells the Atlantis story rather than cosmology proper.
"One cosmological dialogue" is a defensible characterization at this level.

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

Both sub-claims confirmed: the Demiurge uses the Forms as blueprints (looking to
an "unchanging and eternal model"); the Timaeus traveled separately into the Latin
Middle Ages via Chalcidius's translation, where it shaped Christian cosmological
theology. The sentence in the r2 draft accurately captures both.

> alongside the great rocks and great men it was supposed to explain. So now you
> have a new collection of great things: the originals *and* the Form. And by the

> will all be great,' is the engine: each new collection of great things shares

1. The full unelided Jowett text is restored: "by virtue of which they will all be

2. The step-warrant is made explicit *before* the quote (Greatness itself counts
