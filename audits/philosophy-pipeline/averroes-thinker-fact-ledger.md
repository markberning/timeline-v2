## ★ THE LOAD-BEARING CORRECTION: the double-truth myth
Averroes did **NOT** hold "double truth" (that one proposition can be true in
philosophy and false in theology, or vice versa). He held the **opposite**: there
is ONE truth, reached two ways (demonstration and revelation), reconciled by
allegorical interpretation where they seem to clash. The read states this plainly
in the hook, the break block, and chapter 5.
- IEP "Ibn Rushd": the double-truth doctrine "was not held by Ibn Rushd himself but
  was an innovation of the Averroists." [MYTH-CORRECTED]
- Wikipedia "Averroism" (verbatim, load-checked): "Modern scholarship has shown,
  however, that no Latin Christian medieval thinker ever upheld the 'double truth'
  theory." And: "This idea differed from that of Averroes, who taught that there is
  only one truth, but reached in two different ways, not two truths."
- Origin of the label: Tempier's 1277 condemnation **preamble** accused his targets
  of maintaining philosophical stances irreconcilable with Catholic dogma while
  still professing the faith; "Modern historians called this the 'double truth'
  theory." (Wikipedia "Averroism", verbatim.) It is a polemic put in opponents'
  mouths, not a doctrine anyone professed.
- URLs (HTTP-checked via WebFetch this session, all resolved):
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-rushd/ · https://iep.utm.edu/ibnrushd/ ·
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averroism

## BIOGRAPHY & DATES
- Born **1126, Córdoba** (al-Andalus); died **1198, Marrakesh**. [DOCUMENTED]
  (Wikipedia gives precise 14 April 1126 / 11 December 1198; SEP/IEP give 1126
  Córdoba / 1198. The read uses "1126" / "1198".)
- A **dynasty of judges**: his grandfather Abu al-Walid Muhammad (d. 1126) was
  **chief qadi (judge) of Córdoba**; his father held the same office. The grandfather
  died the year Averroes was born. [DOCUMENTED — IEP, corroborated Wikipedia]
- Own career: qadi in **Seville (1169)**, qadi in **Córdoba (1171)**, qadi again in
  Seville (1179), **chief qadi of Córdoba and court physician (1182)**, succeeding
  Ibn Tufayl as physician. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia] (Read states judge/physician,
  chief qadi of Córdoba, court physician — the sequence is held at summary level.)
- The **Almohads**: the Berber Muslim dynasty from North Africa that displaced the
  Almoravids in al-Andalus (Córdoba by 1146); his patrons and, later, his
  persecutors. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Origin of the commentaries (~1169)**: Ibn Tufayl (philosopher, author of *Hayy
  ibn Yaqzan*) introduced Averroes to the caliph **Abu Yaqub Yusuf** (r. 1163-1184).
  When the caliph complained about the difficulty of understanding Aristotle, Ibn
  Tufayl recommended Averroes to explain/clarify Aristotle. The COMMISSION is
  documented (SEP: the caliph "officially asked Ibn Rushd to comment on Aristotle's
  works and render them accessible"). The vivid verbatim-dialogue version is from the
  later biographer al-Marrakushi and is [TRADITIONAL] — the read frames it as the
  story/tradition, never quotes the dialogue.
- Medical work: ***Kitab al-Kulliyat fi al-Tibb*** ("The General Principles of
  Medicine"), Latinized ***Colliget***; a 7-book medical encyclopedia, later used as
  a European university text. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia "Colliget"]
- **Disgrace (c. 1195)** under caliph **Abu Yusuf Yaqub al-Mansur**: a tribunal
  condemned his teachings, ordered his books burned, and banished him to **Lucena**.
  Scholars read it as politically motivated (the caliph rallying conservative jurists
  for a military campaign). [DOCUMENTED as event; political-motive reading flagged as
  INTERPRETATION.] Restored to favor before his death. [DOCUMENTED]
- Death **Marrakesh 1198**; body later moved to **Córdoba** for reburial.
  [DOCUMENTED for the move.] The tradition that Ibn Arabi witnessed the return of the
  body is [TRADITIONAL] — the read does not use it.

## THE COMMENTARIES
- **Three levels**: short/epitome (*jami*/*jawami*), middle/paraphrase (*talkhis*),
  long/line-by-line (*tafsir*/*sharh*, the "great commentaries"). [DOCUMENTED]
  SEP caveat honored: "recent scholarship has doubted whether this neat threefold
  distinction adequately captures" his approach — the read presents it as the
  standard schema, not absolute.
- **He did not know Greek**; he worked from existing **Arabic translations** of
  Aristotle (themselves often via Syriac), not the Greek. [DOCUMENTED] (This is the
  same correction the era-1 read's r2 fact-check made for the Arabic transmission.)
- **"The Commentator"**: Latin Christian writers called him simply *Commentator*, as
  they called Aristotle simply *Philosophus* ("the Philosopher"). [DOCUMENTED —
  Wikipedia: "led to Averroes being referred to simply as 'The Commentator'".] The
  paired scholastic convention is standard usage.

## THE AL-GHAZALI DUEL
- **al-Ghazali (c. 1056-1111)** wrote ***Tahafut al-Falasifa* (The Incoherence of
  the Philosophers)**, attacking the Aristotelian *falasifa* — chiefly **Avicenna
  and al-Farabi** — across **twenty discussions**. [DOCUMENTED — SEP "al-Ghazali".]
  (The read uses "around 1058-1111"; SEP notes birth uncertainty c.1056.)
- Of the twenty, al-Ghazali condemned **three as outright unbelief (kufr)**: (1) the
  **eternity of the world**, (2) that **God knows only universals, not particulars**,
  (3) **denial of bodily resurrection**. [DOCUMENTED — SEP "al-Ghazali" verbatim
  list; IEP corroborates.]
- **Occasionalism / fire-and-cotton** (17th discussion): the connection between cause
  and effect is not necessary but a habit God customarily maintains; when fire
  touches cotton it is **God who creates the burning**, not the fire by an inherent
  power. [DOCUMENTED — SEP "al-Ghazali" quotes the fire/cotton passage as his.] The
  read PARAPHRASES (the standard Marmura translation is in copyright).
- Averroes's reply: ***Tahafut al-Tahafut* (The Incoherence of the Incoherence)**, a
  point-by-point rebuttal (composed c. 1180/81 per SEP) that defends Aristotelian
  causation and accuses al-Ghazali of arguing dialectically rather than
  demonstratively. [DOCUMENTED — SEP "Ibn Rushd".]

## THE DECISIVE TREATISE (*Fasl al-Maqal*)
- Full title ~ "The Decisive Treatise, Determining the Connection Between the Law
  (religion) and Wisdom (philosophy)." [DOCUMENTED]
- Core argument: philosophy is permitted, even obligatory, for those capable of
  demonstrative reasoning; the Law and demonstrative reason cannot truly conflict
  because **truth does not contradict truth**; where the literal sense of scripture
  appears to conflict with a demonstrated conclusion, scripture must be interpreted
  allegorically (*ta'wil*). [DOCUMENTED — SEP; Hourani text.] PARAPHRASED in the
  read (Hourani translation in copyright).
- **Three classes of people**, each suited to a mode of assent: rhetorical (the
  majority; take scripture at its apparent sense; should NOT be given allegorical
  interpretation), dialectical (the theologians, *mutakallimun*), demonstrative (the
  philosophers, qualified to interpret allegorically). [DOCUMENTED — in the Fasl
  text.] Averroes blames al-Ghazali for spreading allegory to the masses. [DOCUMENTED]

## UNITY / UNICITY OF THE INTELLECT
- Drawing on Aristotle's *De Anima* (worked out in the **Long Commentary on the De
  Anima**, his most mature position), Averroes argues there is **one single shared
  "material intellect"** (also "potential"/"possible" intellect) for all of humanity,
  not one per person. [DOCUMENTED]
- The two intellects: the **material/potential intellect** receives universal forms
  (and must be immaterial/separate/single so that what it receives stays universal
  rather than being individualized by a particular person's matter); the
  **agent/active intellect** abstracts/actualizes the intelligibles. The individual
  connects to thought via the **images (phantasms)** generated by his own body and
  senses. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia unity-of-intellect page + SEP.]
- Controversial consequence: appears to deny **personal individual immortality** —
  what survives is the shared intellect, not the individual. [DOCUMENTED]
- Averroes acknowledged the view's novelty (SEP reports him writing the conclusion
  came "after long reflection and intense care, and I have not seen it in anyone else
  before me" — Taylor 2009 translation in copyright; PARAPHRASED in the read).
- Rebuttal: **Aquinas, *De Unitate Intellectus contra Averroistas* (1270)**, written
  specifically against this doctrine; Aquinas argues the intellect is the form of the
  individual body so that "this man" genuinely thinks. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia;
  isidore.co/aquinas/english/DeUnitateIntellectus.htm. Zedler 1968 translation in
  copyright; PARAPHRASED.]

## LATIN AVERROISM & AFTERLIFE
- Commentaries translated into Latin **from the 1220s**, notably by **Michael Scot**
  (Toledo / for Frederick II). [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia "Michael Scot".]
- **"Latin Averroism"**: a current in the **Paris arts faculty**, associated with
  **Siger of Brabant** and **Boethius of Dacia** (radical/heterodox Aristotelians;
  scholars increasingly prefer "radical Aristotelianism"). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Condemnations**: Bishop **Étienne Tempier** of Paris condemned 13 propositions in
  **1270** and 219 in **1277**. [DOCUMENTED] (Consistent with the era-2 read's
  finding that the 1277 document names no individuals — Siger/Boethius are identified
  by later historians.)
- **Jewish reception**: near-contemporary **Maimonides (1138-1204)** admired him;
  works translated into Hebrew; **Gersonides** wrote super-commentaries on Averroes
  (c. 1321-24). [DOCUMENTED — Steven Harvey, Cambridge Companion; SEP "Gersonides".]
  Used lightly in the read.

## INFLUENCE CHAIN (the gated spine)
- **Answered (upstream)**: Aristotle (the master he recovered); al-Farabi and
  **Avicenna** (the earlier *falasifa*; Averroes corrects Avicenna for diluting
  Aristotle with Neoplatonism); al-Ghazali (the antagonist).
- **Answered by (downstream)**: **Aquinas** (*De Unitate*, 1270, against the unity of
  intellect — but absorbed much of Averroes's Aristotle); the Latin Averroists
  (Siger, Boethius of Dacia); Tempier (condemnations); the Jewish tradition
  (Maimonides, Gersonides).
