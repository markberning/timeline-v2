## Ordered gloss-gap list — MUST-FIX on top

### MUST-FIX

**MF-1 — Ch 5 (Berkeley): "idealism" and "immaterialism" — both missing, and this is the worst gap in the draft.**
Berkeley's position is the philosophy vertical's landmine term: the pipeline spec flags "idealism (which does NOT mean having ideals)" as a must-catch. The draft does fine work explaining Berkeley's actual claim — the table exists, mind-independent matter does not — but it never once uses the words "idealism" or "immaterialism," the standard labels for his position. The gap matters because:
(a) the next era (Kant) introduces German Idealism, and a reader who never learned what idealism means in philosophy cannot follow that transition;
(b) the draft's closing summary in Ch 7 uses neither label, so the word "idealism" arrives at the era's close (in the throughline implications for Kant) without having been defined anywhere.
The irony is that the draft sets up the definition perfectly — "what he denies is something much narrower... that the table exists as mind-independent matter" — and then does not name what that denial is called.
**Fix:** At the moment Berkeley's conclusion is stated ("their being is their being perceived"), add: "This position — that reality consists entirely of minds and their ideas, with no mind-independent matter — is called **idealism** (nothing to do with having high ideals; it is named after 'ideas', not aspirations) or, in Berkeley's own preferred word, **immaterialism**. It is the most counterintuitive conclusion in the era, and also the most logically airtight follow-through from Locke's own starting point."

**MF-2 — Ch 1 (Descartes): "substance" appears in the dualism gloss before it is defined.**
"This is Descartes' **dualism**: mind and body are two completely distinct substances ('substance' here meaning a basic kind of stuff that can exist on its own)." The gloss is present — that is the right instinct — but it arrives inside the sentence that USES the word, so the reader meets the term and its definition simultaneously in a dense parenthetical. This alone would be fine, except: Ch 2 opens immediately with "Descartes had left two big problems on the table" and then the word "substance" drives the entire Spinoza chapter (where its strict sense — a thing that depends on nothing else — is the engine of Prop. XIV). The Ch 1 gloss is too casual ("a basic kind of stuff") for the precise logical work the word must do in Ch 2.
**Fix:** Strengthen the Ch 1 gloss slightly: "('substance' here meaning a basic, self-standing kind of stuff — something that can exist entirely on its own, without depending on anything else to be what it is)." This prepares the reader for Spinoza's tighter version in Ch 2, which can then sharpen it further rather than having to backfill.

**MF-3 — Ch 1 (Descartes): "rationalism" / "rationalist" — used but never explicitly defined at first use.**
The break block warns that the rationalist/empiricist split "hardened mostly in hindsight," which is honest. But the actual word "rationalist" is used in Ch 1's last paragraph ("The rationalists are off and running") as if it were already a known label. It was introduced one paragraph earlier in the break block, but only as a team-label — never defined as a position. A newcomer still does not know what a rationalist *believes*.
The pipeline's landmine list requires: "rationalism" defined at first use.
**Fix:** At the break block's first use of "rationalist" (or at the Ch 1 payoff line), add a one-sentence definition: "A rationalist, in the technical sense these chapters use, is a philosopher who believes reason alone — without relying on sensory experience — can establish real knowledge about the world." (The empiricist definition is handled beautifully in Ch 5's opening; the rationalist definition needs the same treatment here.)

**MF-4 — Ch 5 (Berkeley): the argument step from Locke's primary/secondary split to Berkeley's conclusion has a skipped move.**
The draft says: "Berkeley asks the obvious next question: why stop there? Look at what you actually have access to when you know an object. You have your ideas of it, its perceived shape, perceived size, perceived solidity. Every primary quality reaches you as an idea in your mind just as much as every secondary one does." This is correct as a summary, but the logical step that makes it work — *why* a primary quality only reaches you as a mental idea — is skipped. A newcomer might reasonably object: "But I can touch the shape and size. That's not just in my mind." Berkeley's answer is that touch itself is a sense perception, an idea in the mind, just as sight is. The draft assumes the reader already knows that all sensory data is mediated.
**Fix:** Insert one sentence before "Every primary quality reaches you as an idea in your mind": "After all, you never experience the shape of something directly — you see it or feel it, and both seeing and feeling are just more perceptions happening inside your mind."

**MF-5 — Ch 6 (Hume): "induction" / "the problem of induction" — the term appears, the problem is explained, but the word is never glossed.**
"This is the **problem of induction**: all our reasoning about matters of fact assumes the future will resemble the past, but that assumption is itself just a matter of fact, which we can only support by appeal to experience, which is what we were trying to justify." The problem is explained correctly and clearly. But the word "induction" is never defined. A newcomer knows what "deduction" feels like from geometry (if A then B; A; therefore B), but induction — reasoning from past observations to future expectations — is a different logical operation that has not appeared in this era's narrative before.
**Fix:** Before or at the "problem of induction" tag, add: "(Induction is the logical move of drawing a general rule from particular cases — you've seen fire burn a thousand times, so you conclude fire always burns. It is the engine of almost all practical science and everyday reasoning.)" Then the "problem" of induction lands with real force: the engine of all science has no logical foundation.

**MF-6 — Ch 2 (Spinoza): "determinism" is implied throughout but never named.**
"If everything is the one substance unfolding by its own nature, then everything that happens follows necessarily, the way the triangle's angles follow from its definition. There is no free will in the sense of a choice that could have gone otherwise." This is a perfect explanation of hard determinism. But the word "determinism" never appears. This matters because: (a) it is the term Spinoza is famous for endorsing; (b) it is the term Leibniz is reacting against when he insists God *chose* this world from many possible ones; (c) the next era will require the newcomer to know the word.
**Fix:** After "There is no free will in the sense of a choice that could have gone otherwise," add: "This is **determinism** — the view that every event, including every human decision, is the necessary consequence of prior causes, with no genuine alternatives."

**MF-7 — Ch 5 (Locke): "a priori / a posteriori" — defined, but the parenthetical is a fraction too long and may lose readers mid-sentence.**
"*a priori* means knowable independently of experience, by thought alone, the way you know all bachelors are unmarried without having to survey any; *a posteriori* means knowable only through experience, the way you know it is raining by looking." This is genuinely good. The bachelor example is exactly right. The problem is structural: both definitions are buried inside a parenthetical inside the empiricism definition, making three nested ideas simultaneous. A newcomer at normal reading speed may absorb "empiricism" and then skim the Latin.
**Fix (MUST-FIX, low effort):** Break into two sentences. End the empiricism definition sentence. Then: "Two pieces of jargon that will carry their weight here: *a priori* means knowable by thought alone, before any experience — the way you know all bachelors are unmarried without surveying any; *a posteriori* means knowable only through experience — the way you know it is raining by looking out the window."

**MF-8 — Ch 3 (Leibniz): "monism" never named, despite Spinoza being its canonical example.**
The draft sets up the contrast: "Where Spinoza had one substance, Leibniz would have infinitely many." Spinoza's one-substance position is the philosophical definition of monism (the view that reality is ultimately one kind of thing, or one thing). Leibniz's infinitely many substances make him a pluralist. Neither word is used. This matters because: (a) the draft's closing paragraph explicitly invites the reader to "set the two rationalist poles side by side," and the contrast is more memorable with its labels; (b) "monism" is a term the next era will require (German Idealism is sometimes called absolute idealism, in dialogue with Spinozistic monism).
**Fix:** At Spinoza's conclusion paragraph ("There is exactly one substance"), add: "The label for this position is **monism** — the view that reality is, at bottom, one single thing or one kind of thing — and Spinoza's is the most thoroughgoing version in the Western tradition."

### SHOULD-FIX

**SF-1 — Era hook: "rationalists who trust reason and the empiricists who trust experience" — the gloss is accurate but slightly unsharp.**
The break block glosses the two teams correctly and warns against the tournament framing. But "trust reason" and "trust experience" are thin as standalone definitions. A reader finishing the era might still not be able to say in one sentence what the rationalist claims that the empiricist denies. SHOULD-FIX, not MUST-FIX, because the full definitions land correctly in Ch 1 and Ch 5.

**SF-2 — Ch 1 (Descartes): "dualism" vs Plato's soul-body split — the caveat is present but the distinction between Cartesian and Platonic dualism is not drawn clearly enough.**
"Be careful with the claim that Descartes invented this. Plato split soul from body, Augustine anatomized the divided inner life, the Scholastics distinguished the two for centuries." This is an important and correct caveat. But a newcomer who has read Era 1 knows Plato split soul from body — they may wonder: so what IS new? The draft says "the sharpness: not soul and body as higher and lower grades of one being, but two complete substances with nothing shared." That is the right distinction, but "higher and lower grades of one being" may be too dense for a newcomer to cash out. SHOULD-FIX: restate as "in Plato, the soul is the higher and better part of a person, but both soul and body belong to the same person; in Descartes, mind and body are two completely different kinds of stuff, like iron and water, that happen to be fitted together."

**SF-3 — Ch 2 (Spinoza): "attribute" and "mode" — both are technically defined, but the definitions arrive quickly and in sequence.**
"Attributes" as "fundamental ways of grasping" the one substance (thought and extension), and "modes" as "finite things" that are "a particular shape the ocean is taking right now" — the wave analogy is excellent. But attribute arrives in one sentence and mode in the next, and a newcomer may not realize these are the two KEY technical terms of Spinoza's system. SHOULD-FIX: bold both terms at first use (attribute is not bolded in the draft, mode is treated as passing vocabulary) and confirm the definitions are side by side.

**SF-4 — Ch 3 (Leibniz): "theodicy" — defined correctly and at the right moment.**
"The serious theodicy (the attempt to square a good God with a world full of suffering)" — this is well-handled. Flagged as a positive confirmation.

**SF-5 — Ch 3 (Leibniz): "pre-established harmony" — the clock analogy is superb, but "harmony" as a technical term could be flagged as the thinker's own label.**
The clock analogy lands perfectly. SHOULD-FIX only: note that "pre-established harmony" is Leibniz's own term for this, not a neutral description — he coined the phrase. This is a small framing point but helps a reader understand why the phrase appears in every philosophy textbook.

**SF-6 — Ch 4 (Hobbes): "sovereignty" / "sovereign" — used throughout but never explicitly glossed.**
"Sovereign" is used naturally throughout the Hobbes section and clearly means "the ruler" from context. But "sovereignty" as a philosophical concept (the idea that there must be a single highest authority in a political order, with no power above it) is never named. Not MUST-FIX because "sovereign" works as a synonym for "ruler" throughout the chapter, but SHOULD-FIX because Hobbes is one of the founding theorists of sovereignty as a concept.

**SF-7 — Ch 5 (Locke): "reflection" as the second source of ideas — the word is used in a specialized sense that may confuse.**
"Reflection, the mind turning around to watch its own operations, noticing itself remembering, doubting, willing." This is Locke's technical term for introspection. It is defined correctly in the same sentence. But the word "reflection" already means something to a newcomer (thinking carefully about something), and Locke's use is more specific (the mind *observing its own processes*). SHOULD-FIX: add one word — "reflection (what Locke calls the mind turning around to watch its own operations)" — to flag that this is Locke's specific technical term, not the ordinary word.

**SF-8 — Ch 6 (Hume): "bundle theory" — the position is explained but the label is not bolded or named as a term.**
"What we call the self is not a thing that has experiences; it is 'nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions...'" The explanation is excellent. But the phrase "bundle theory of the self" — the standard philosophical label for this position — never appears. A newcomer who encounters this term in the next era or in a conversation will not recognize it. SHOULD-FIX: after the quote, add one sentence: "This is called the **bundle theory of the self** — the self is not a container that holds experiences, but the bundled stream of experiences themselves."

**SF-9 — Ch 6 (Hume): "the missing shade of blue" — a lovely digression, but its purpose in the chapter structure is not stated.**
The missing shade of blue is presented as "a tiny honest confession against his own system." The newcomer may not know what the confession threatens — why this single exception would matter to Hume. SHOULD-FIX: one sentence after naming the problem — "If one idea can arise without a matching impression, then Hume's whole 'knowledge comes only from experience' rule has a hole in it, and Hume knows it."

**SF-10 — Ch 7 (Hume): "mitigated skepticism" — dropped without definition.**
"The honest position, given the fork, was suspension, what he called a mitigated skepticism: not enough to affirm, not enough to deny." "Mitigated skepticism" is Hume's own term for his position, and the draft defines the position (suspension, neither affirm nor deny) but never says what "mitigated" means or how it differs from full skepticism (which would deny everything). SHOULD-FIX: add one clause — "a mitigated skepticism (mitigated meaning partial: he doubted the evidence, not the possibility of knowledge altogether, unlike the ancient Pyrrhonists who doubted everything)."

**SF-11 — Ch 7 (du Châtelet): introduced without a one-line who-she-is before the substance of her work.**
"Émilie du Châtelet (1706 to 1749) was a French mathematician and physicist, and the standard French edition of Newton's *Principia*, the foundation of modern physics, is still her translation, with her own commentary." The dates and her title — mathematician and physicist — are given. But a newcomer has not heard of Newton's *Principia* yet (it is presumably coming in the next era alongside Kant). The sentence "the foundation of modern physics" gestures at the *Principia*'s significance but does not explain what it contains. A newcomer cannot understand why synthesizing Newton and Leibniz matters without a one-phrase description of what Newton did. SHOULD-FIX: one clause — "Newton's *Principia*, the book that had described the mathematical laws governing how all physical objects move — the most powerful scientific framework the century had produced."

**SF-12 — Ch 4 (Hobbes/Locke): the chapter title "Nasty, brutish, and short" is the political philosophy chapter, but the connection between political philosophy and empiricism is never explicitly stated.**
The draft says Hobbes and Locke "are usually filed with the empiricists, and that fits: instead of asking what political authority *ought* to be in some ideal heaven, they ask what it actually is." This is a good start. But a newcomer reading the chapter title and the chapter's opening paragraph might reasonably ask: why is political philosophy in a chapter labeled as part of the rationalist/empiricist story? The connection to the empiricist method — starting from observed human nature rather than abstract ideals — deserves one more explicit sentence. SHOULD-FIX: after "instead of asking what political authority ought to be," add: "This is the empiricist instinct in politics: observe first, theorize second, instead of deducing what governments must look like from pure reason."

## Summary assessment

The draft is well-structured for a newcomer. The method of doubt is walked step by step with no gaps. The cogito's history (three books, three forms) is clarified better than most academic treatments. The primary/secondary qualities distinction is explained cleanly. Hume's fork and the is/ought gap are both accessible to a first-time reader.

The MUST-FIX list (8 items) clusters around three failure modes:

1. **Berkeley's position is never labeled** — "idealism" and "immaterialism" are both missing (MF-1). This is the highest-stakes gap in the draft because idealism is the pipeline's single most flagged newcomer trap, and the word will recur in every subsequent era.

2. **Technical vocabulary arrives without its name** — "determinism" (MF-6), "induction" (MF-5), and "monism" (MF-8) are all explained but never labeled; a newcomer who finishes the chapter cannot attach the ideas to the words they will encounter in the wild.

3. **Glosses arrive slightly misaligned** — "substance" in Ch 1 is glossed too casually for the work it must do in Ch 2 (MF-2); "rationalism" is named before being defined (MF-3); *a priori/a posteriori* are buried inside a triple parenthetical (MF-7, low-effort fix).

The SHOULD-FIX list (12 items) is genuine quality improvement. The most important are SF-8 (the "bundle theory" label) and SF-10 (mitigated skepticism), both of which leave standard philosophical terms unnamed after their positions are correctly explained.

The draft does NOT fail on: person introductions (every thinker has a one-line who-they-are at first mention), argument completeness for Descartes and Hume (both walked step by step with no mystical gaps), the Spinoza wave/ocean analogy (excellent), the Leibniz clock analogy (excellent), the Berkeley table example (excellent), or the is/ought gap (clearer than most academic treatments). The de-mythologizing moves — Voltaire's line vs Leibniz's argument, the three-books cogito, the tabula rasa correction — are all handled correctly and are newcomer-friendly.

**Overall gate verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (PASS-WITH-FIXES). 8 MUST-FIX items, none requiring argument restructuring — all are inline-gloss additions or label insertions. The single highest-priority fix is MF-1: Berkeley's idealism must be named before the era closes.**
