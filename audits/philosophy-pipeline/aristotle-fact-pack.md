**THE ARISTOTLE CORPUS FIDELITY RULE (load-bearing):** Unlike Plato's polished
literary dialogues, Aristotle's surviving works are lecture notes — working
documents for use inside the Lyceum, not the elegant published writings (now lost)
that Cicero praised. The author must carry this distinction for the reader, because
it shapes every quotation: these texts are often dense, occasionally repetitive, and
sometimes read like notes awaiting final revision. This is not a weakness the author
should apologize for — it is the window into how Aristotle actually *worked*, which
is itself significant.

## 0. Chapter-arc (proposed — 7 chapters at thinker-deep altitude)

Shape follows the `kind = THINKER` template: inherited → thought → changed → answered.
The ideas are the chapters; biography provides the scene-setting.

**Proposed chapters:**

**Ch1 — "The man who checked everything"**
Aristotle's life as the frame: Stagira, Nicomachus the physician, Plato's Academy for
twenty years (what a student at the Academy actually did, what Aristotle took and what
he rejected). The move to Assos, tutoring Alexander at Mieza (~343 BC), the return to
Athens. What he found wrong with Plato's Forms: the "amicus Plato" position — deep
loyalty, clear objection. The corpus problem: lecture notes, not polished dialogues;
why we have what we have (Andronicus of Rhodes, the Apellicon rediscovery story).
The break block: how he shifts the "really real" from a separate realm of Forms INTO
the things themselves.

**Ch2 — "How things work: the four causes"**
The four-cause framework as the tool for understanding ANYTHING (Physics II.3). Bronze
statue as the worked example (material/formal/efficient/final). Why the final cause is
the controversial one — Aristotle's teleology baked into nature, not just into human
artifacts. Teleology without a divine planner: the eye that is FOR seeing without
anyone designing it. Hylomorphism — form and matter as the inseparable pair that
constitute any substance. Contrast with Plato: the Form is NOT a separate floating
entity, it is IN the thing.

**Ch3 — "What we are for: the good life"**
The Nicomachean Ethics as the system. The function argument (NE I.7 / 1098a): what
is the ergon — the characteristic activity — of a human being? Activity of soul in
accordance with virtue. Eudaimonia NOT as "happiness" (anachronism trap) but as
flourishing / living-well — a whole-life judgment about excellent activity, not a
feeling. The golden mean (NE II.6 / 1106b): virtue as the trained stable disposition
to hit the appropriate response, lying between excess and deficiency. The habit argument
(NE II.1 / 1103a): moral virtue is formed by practice, not teaching. The Durant debunk
(the "We are what we repeatedly do" fake): the pack's load-bearing apocryphal alert.

**Ch4 — "The soul, the city, the species"**
De Anima (On the Soul) — the soul as the form of the body, not a prisoner in a body
(the anti-Plato move). Aristotle's hylomorphic psychology: soul and body are one
composite, as shape and wax. The Politics: "man is by nature a political animal"
(1253a) — the polis as natural, not contractual; what it means to say human nature
requires a community. Natural slavery (Politics I.4–5, 1254a) and the subordination
of women (Politics I.5, 1254b / 1260a): named honestly, neither sanitized nor
lectured. The contrast with his women-guardians-are-possible Plato (who at least
opened the door): Aristotle closes it and locks it.

**Ch5 — "The unmoved mover"**
Metaphysics Lambda (Book XII): from the four causes to the question of what ultimately
moves things. Every motion requires a mover; infinite regress of movers is impossible;
therefore there is an unmoved mover. The unmoved mover's nature: pure actuality (no
potentiality), eternal, immaterial. What does it think about? Only itself —
*noesis noeseos* (thinking of thinking), 1074b. Why it moves everything: not by pushing
but by being the object of desire and thought — the Good that everything is drawn toward.
This is where Aristotle meets Aquinas six centuries later.

**Ch6 — "How to think: logic and science"**
The Organon — Aristotle founds formal LOGIC, the first fully worked-out system of valid
inference in Western thought. The syllogism: what it is, how it works, why it matters.
Barbara (AAA-1) as the textbook perfect syllogism. Prior Analytics (the form); Posterior
Analytics (demonstration — science as demonstrated knowledge from first principles).
The Poetics: what tragedy is FOR (catharsis; hamartia / tragic flaw not as moral failing
but as error of judgment). Rhetoric: the three modes of persuasion (ethos/logos/pathos —
not a manual for manipulation, a taxonomy of how real persuasion works).

**Ch7 — "What came after"**
Theophrastus and the Peripatetics. The Arabic transmission: the distinction that matters
here is NOT "translated" (the era-1/era-2 error already caught; see §8) — al-Fārābī,
Avicenna, and Averroes (the Commentator) STUDIED Aristotle in Arabic translation and
wrote their own commentaries; Averroes' Grand Commentary became the doorway for the
Latin West. Aquinas calls him "The Philosopher" (citing Averroes "The Commentator" for
the commentary tradition). The 1277 condemnation — 219 propositions condemned in Paris,
many Aristotelian. The revival of virtue ethics: Anscombe's 1958 "Modern Moral
Philosophy," MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981). The lineage edges for the data spine:
plato→aristotle, aristotle→theophrastus, aristotle→avicenna, aristotle→aquinas.

## 2. The corpus — lecture notes, not published books (LOAD-BEARING)

This distinction matters and is often missed. Keep it early in any chapter that quotes
Aristotle.

**What we have:** The surviving Aristotelian corpus consists overwhelmingly of what
scholars call "esoteric" works — working notes, lecture records, and research
compilations intended for use INSIDE the Lyceum, not for a general public audience. The
SEP describes them as texts that "read like what they very probably are: lecture notes,
drafts first written and then reworked, ongoing records of continuing investigations"
and as "in-house compilations intended not for a general audience but for an inner
circle of auditors." `DOCUMENTED, SEP`

**What we've lost:** Aristotle also wrote polished published dialogues ("exoteric" works)
in the manner of Plato, intended for a general educated audience. These won their own
literary reputation in antiquity — Cicero, writing after reading them, described
Aristotle's style as "a flowing river of gold." These dialogues are almost entirely
LOST, surviving only in fragments and secondhand summaries. `DOCUMENTED, SEP citing Cicero`

**How we got what we have:** According to accounts in Strabo and Plutarch (reported much
later and treated with scholarly caution), Aristotle willed his library to Theophrastus,
who left it to Neleus of Scepsis; Neleus's heirs hid the manuscripts in a cellar to
prevent confiscation; they were recovered by the book-collector Apellicon of Teos ~1st
century BC, brought to Rome after Sulla sacked Athens (86 BC), and eventually edited
by Andronicus of Rhodes (~60 BC). Andronicus organized the corpus into the form we
largely inherit. The term "metaphysics" (*ta meta ta physika* = the [books] after the
physics) is essentially Andronicus's editorial label, not Aristotle's own title.
`PARAPHRASE-ONLY from multiple sources; full reliability of the Strabo/Plutarch account
is debated by scholars — state it as "the ancient account" not as verified history`

**Why it matters for the reader:** When Aristotle writes "virtue, then, being of two
kinds…" it can sometimes feel abrupt or compressed. That is what working notes feel
like. The reader is not receiving an essay polished for publication; they are getting
access to the actual workshop.

**Major surviving works (approximate composition — no "publication dates"; explain why):**
Aristotle's works were not "published" in the modern sense. The surviving texts were
composed over his decades of teaching and research; dating is approximate and based on
internal cross-references and scholarly consensus.

| Work | Group | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Organon** (*Categories*, *On Interpretation*, *Prior Analytics*, *Posterior Analytics*, *Topics*, *Sophistical Refutations*) | Logic | The founding texts of formal logic; the syllogism lives in *Prior Analytics*. The word "Organon" (instrument) is a collective label applied by later editors, not Aristotle's. |

## 3. THE APOCRYPHAL QUOTE DEBUNK — load-bearing, must be in any chapter on ethics

### The fake quote
> "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit."

This is **NOT Aristotle**. It does not appear anywhere in his corpus.

### Its real source
**Will Durant**, *The Story of Philosophy* (Simon & Schuster, 1926). Durant's passage,
paraphrasing Aristotle's ethics, reads:

> "Excellence is an art won by training and habituation: we do not act rightly because
> we have virtue or excellence, but we rather have these because we have acted rightly;
> 'these virtues are formed in man by his doing the actions'; we are what we repeatedly
> do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit."

Durant is NARRATING, in his own words, his reading of Aristotle — the embedded single
quotation ("these virtues are formed in man by his doing the actions") is Durant's
paraphrase of the NE II.1 argument. The famous two-sentence summary IS Durant, not
Aristotle. Over decades of anthologizing the phrase detached from its context and was
re-attributed to Aristotle directly.

`DOCUMENTED: Snopes, Check Your Fact, Medium/Caelan Huntress, umb.edu/quoteunquote (2012);
corroborated by Northwestern professor Richard Kraut: "Those aren't Aristotle's words
translated into English. But it is an accurate and succinct way of conveying what
Aristotle says in a more complicated way." Source: checkyourfact.com/2019/06/26/`

### What Aristotle ACTUALLY says (the real textual basis)
**Source:** NE II.1, 1103a14–b2, W. D. Ross translation
**Online text:** https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html

Exact passage (Ross translation, VERIFIED against classics.mit.edu):

> "Virtue, then, being of two kinds, intellectual and moral, intellectual virtue in the
> main owes both its birth and its growth to teaching (for which reason it requires
> experience and time), while moral virtue comes about as a result of habit, whence
> also its name (*ethike*) is one that is formed by a slight variation from the word
> *ethos* (habit)."

And further in NE II.1 (Ross, VERIFIED):

> "the virtues we get by first exercising them, as also happens in the case of the
> arts"

> "we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by
> doing brave acts"

**Bekker reference:** NE II.1 begins at 1103a14. Bekker numbers appear in scholarly
editions but not in the classics.mit.edu text; the chapter structure confirms location.

**The Greek concept:** *Hexis* (ἕξις) — often translated "habit" but more precisely an
"active state" or "settled disposition," something one must actively maintain (not a
mere rut). Virtue as hexis means: a stable condition of character, formed through
repeated practice, that disposes one toward the appropriate response. It is acquired,
not innate; cultivated, not given. The IEP notes: "hexis is an active condition, a
state in which something must actively hold itself" — this nuance is worth a sentence
in the narrative (overclaiming "habit" = rut undersells Aristotle's actual argument).

**How to frame it in prose:** Durant's paraphrase is a fine summary (give him credit
for it); the real Aristotle says it in a more qualified and philosophically interesting
way — which is worth showing the reader directly, because the actual text is more
interesting than the bumper sticker.

## 4. Key passage candidates — born-verified, exact wording, named PD translation

### Passage A — NE I.7: The function argument / eudaimonia (RECOMMENDED as lead passage)
**Work:** *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book I, Chapter 7
**Bekker:** 1097b22–1098a20 (function argument spans this range)
**Key line (Ross translation, VERIFIED against classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html):**

> "human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there
> are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete."

**Full sentence for context (Ross, NE I.7, VERIFIED):**
> "If the function of man is an activity of soul which follows or implies a rational
> principle… human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue,
> and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete."

**sourceUrl (PD, resolves, right document):**
https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html
(Book I, all chapters on one page; Chapter 7 is the function argument)

**Why it's the right key passage:** This single sentence is Aristotle's answer to the
question that the whole NE is built on — what IS the good life? The function argument
is the move: find the distinctive activity (ergon) of a thing → its good is excelling
at that activity → the human ergon is rational activity of soul → therefore the human
good (eudaimonia) is excellent rational activity. Every subsequent chapter of the NE
flows from this line.

**Unpacking for the reader (house-voice gloss, to go in the narrative near this passage):**
Notice what "good" means here: not "nice" or "pleasant," but excellent performance of the
function that makes you the kind of thing you are. A good knife cuts well; a good flautist
plays well; a good human lives and acts well according to reason. Aristotle is asking what
a human DOES by nature — what is our characteristic activity? — and saying that doing that
excellently, over a whole life, is what the good life consists in. Not a feeling of
happiness. Not a reward at the end. The activity itself, done well, is the thing.

**EUDAIMONIA FRAMING TRAP:** The word is traditionally translated "happiness" but this
is anachronistic. Modern "happiness" connotes a subjective feeling-state. Aristotle's
eudaimonia is an objective, whole-life assessment of excellent activity — it is about
what you DO and how you live, not what you feel. The framing gate will flag any chapter
that uses "happiness" as if it means contentment or pleasure. Prefer "flourishing" or
"living well" with a note that the translation "happiness" doesn't carry it. IEP and
Britannica both note this; SEP is explicit: eudaimonia "is not a feeling."

### Passage B — Politics I.2: "Man is by nature a political animal"
**Work:** *Politics*, Book I, Chapter 2
**Bekker:** 1253a2–3
**Translation:** H. Rackham (Loeb/Perseus), PD

**Exact wording (Rackham, VERIFIED at perseus.tufts.edu):**

> "man is by nature a political animal"

**Longer passage for context (Rackham, VERIFIED):**
The surrounding passage reads: "the city-state is a natural growth, and that man is by
nature a political animal" — Rackham 1253a.

**sourceUrl (PD, resolves, right document):**
https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0058:book%3D1:section%3D1253a

**The Greek original:** *ζῷον πολιτικόν* (zōon politikon) — literally "political
animal" or "animal of the polis." Not "social animal" (which softens it) — the word is
*politikon*, from *polis* (city-state), meaning: a being whose nature is completed only
within a political community. A human living outside the polis is, Aristotle says,
"either a beast or a god."

**Why it matters:** This is Aristotle's direct push-back on Plato's Republic and on any
view of the state as purely conventional (a contract, a useful fiction). For Aristotle,
the polis is natural — it grows from the household, which grows from the male/female
pair, which is natural; the polis is the completion of a natural development toward
self-sufficiency. This will connect directly to Aquinas, Hobbes (who disputes it), and
the social-contract theorists.

### Passage C — NE II.6: Virtue as the mean (alternative/supplementary key passage)
**Work:** *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book II, Chapter 6
**Bekker:** 1106b36–1107a2
**Translation:** W. D. Ross

**Exact wording (Ross, VERIFIED at classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.vi.html):**

> "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e.
> the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that
> principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it."

**sourceUrl (PD, resolves, right document):**
https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.vi.html

**Why it's useful (secondary passage — the mean doctrine):**
This is the golden-mean claim in Aristotle's own words. Key nuances the author must
carry: (1) the mean is "relative to us," not an absolute midpoint — the right amount
of courage for a trained soldier differs from the right amount for a frightened child;
(2) it is determined by *phronesis* (practical wisdom, the "man of practical wisdom" in
Ross's translation) — wisdom is what calibrates the mean for a particular person in a
particular situation; (3) the mean is between excess and deficiency — but the extremes
are not equally bad in every case (Aristotle is clear that some acts have no mean, e.g.,
murder is never "done in the right amount").

## 5. The ideas — documented positions with sources (NOT caricatures)

### 5a. The four causes (Physics II.3) and teleology
**Source:** *Physics*, Book II, Chapter 3; SEP "Aristotle on Causality"
**Bekker:** Physics II.3, ~194b–195b

Four causes answer the question "Why?" for any entity or process:
1. **Material cause** (*hyle*): what something is made of — "the bronze of a statue."
2. **Formal cause** (*eidos/morphe*): what makes it the kind of thing it is — "the shape
   of the statue."
3. **Efficient cause** (*arche tes kineseos*): the primary source of the change or rest
   — "the craftsman who made the statue."
4. **Final cause** (*telos*): the end, purpose, or function for the sake of which it
   exists — "the statue's purpose (to honor a general, to beautify a temple)."

`DOCUMENTED: SEP "Aristotle on Causality"; four-cause framework in Physics II.3 is one
of the most reliably sourced claims in the corpus`

**Teleology:** Aristotle extends final causes beyond artifacts into nature itself. He
argues that the eye is FOR seeing not because a designer intended it, but because the
kind of thing that has an eye is the kind of thing whose nature it is to see. This is
natural teleology (ends immanent in things, not imposed from outside) — a doctrine that
will run into trouble under Descartes and Newtonian mechanism, and which is still live
in philosophy of biology.

**The push-back on Plato:** For Plato, the "form" of a thing is a separate entity (the
Form-in-itself, the original of which the thing is a copy). For Aristotle, the formal
cause is IN the thing, not floating in a separate realm. This is the core of the
empirical turn: explanations are given IN TERMS of the thing under examination, not by
pointing elsewhere.

### 5b. Hylomorphism: form and matter
**Source:** *Physics* I–II; *Metaphysics* Z (VII); *De Anima* II.1

Aristotle holds that every ordinary physical object is a compound of matter (*hyle*)
and form (*morphe* or *eidos*). Neither matter alone nor form alone is a complete
substance: matter without form is bare potentiality (no particular thing); form without
matter is pure actuality (not a physical entity at all). When you see a bronze sphere,
you are seeing a composite: the bronze is the matter, the spherical shape is the form,
and the SPHERE (this particular bronze sphere) is the substance.

`PARAPHRASE-ONLY — the exact scholastic summary "matter and form" is a reasonable
rendering; cite SEP "Aristotle's Metaphysics" or SEP "Hylomorphism" for depth`

**Actuality and potentiality (*energeia* / *dynamis*):** Closely linked. The acorn is a
potential oak; the mature oak is the actuality. Change, for Aristotle, is always the
transition of some matter from potentiality to actuality under the operation of a form.
This pair is central to everything in his system — ethics (virtue is the actuality of
human potential), psychology (soul is the actuality of the body), metaphysics (God is
pure actuality with no potentiality at all).

**Applied to the soul (De Anima II.1):** The soul is not a substance imprisoned in a
body (Plato's view). It is the *form* of the body — the organizing principle that makes
a body be alive, be capable of nutrition, sensation, and thought. J. A. Smith's PD
translation of De Anima II.1, 412b (VERIFIED at psychclassics.yorku.ca):

> "That is why the soul is the first grade of actuality of a natural body having life
> potentially in it."

**sourceUrl for De Anima:** https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima2.htm

**The anti-Platonic implication:** If the soul is the form of the body, it cannot
pre-exist the body (it has no body to organize before there is a body) and it cannot
survive the body's destruction (the form of a disassembled thing ceases to be the form
of that thing). The soul does not transmigrate. Aristotle does leave a small opening
for the *nous* (intellect) as potentially separable — scholars debate this and it will
preoccupy Averroes — but the hylomorphic soul in general is mortal. This is one of the
sharpest breaks from Plato in the entire corpus.

### 5c. The function argument, eudaimonia, and the golden mean
(Full material under §4 above; summarized here for the ideas section)

**Eudaimonia** = the highest good for a human; the NE's subject. Etymology from *eu-*
(well) + *daimōn* (a kind of spirit or fate) — living in accordance with one's daimōn.
Not a feeling. A whole-life judgment: is this person living and acting excellently
according to their human nature? A human who is chronically enslaved, severely ill,
bereft of friends, cannot achieve full eudaimonia — external goods matter as
preconditions, which is why the slave question and poverty are not merely political but
ethical in Aristotle.

**Golden mean / doctrine of the mean:** Every moral virtue is a *hexis* (stable
disposition) lying between excess and deficiency. Courage lies between cowardice and
recklessness. Generosity lies between miserliness and prodigality. BUT: the mean is
relative to the person and situation — not an arithmetic midpoint. And *phronesis*
(practical wisdom / *nous*) is what determines the mean in context. You cannot reduce
Aristotle's ethics to a formula; you must cultivate judgment.

**Virtue ethics vs. Plato:** For Plato, virtue flows from knowledge of the Forms; a
person who truly KNOWS the Good cannot fail to act well (the intellectualist
Socratic-Platonic position). For Aristotle, knowledge of the good is necessary but not
sufficient — you also need trained dispositions (*hexeis*), which requires time and
practice. This is why children cannot be virtuous (they lack mature rational agency and
trained habits) and why moral education is principally about forming good habits, not
teaching doctrines.

### 5d. Substance (ousia)
**Source:** *Metaphysics* VII (Z) and IV (Gamma)

"What is being?" — Aristotle frames this as the central question of "first philosophy"
(what we call metaphysics). His answer: being is said in many ways, but the primary
sense is *ousia* (substance) — the "what-it-is" of a thing, the individual entity that
bears properties but is not itself a property of anything else. This table is a
substance; the table's brownness is not a substance (it is a quality of the table).

The *Categories* (Organon Book I) distinguishes primary substance (this individual
table, THIS human being) from secondary substance (the species or genus — "human being"
in general). Primary substances are the primary reality; species and genera depend on
them.

`PARAPHRASE-ONLY: the doctrine is documented; no clean one-sentence verbatim quote
in PD English that is both accurate and brief enough to drop into prose`

### 5e. The unmoved mover (*to kinoun akineton*)
**Source:** *Metaphysics* Book XII (Lambda), especially ch. 6–9; also *Physics* VIII
**Bekker:** *Metaphysics* Lambda 1071b–1076a; key passage on *noesis noeseos* at 1074b

**The argument chain:**
1. Motion (change) exists in the world (empirically obvious).
2. Every motion requires a mover.
3. An infinite regress of movers is explanatorily useless (it would never get started).
4. Therefore there must be a first unmoved mover.
5. This mover must be purely actual (no potentiality), or it would itself need something
   to actualize it and would not be truly unmoved.
6. Being purely actual, it is eternal, immaterial, unchanging.
7. How does it move things if it does not itself move? By being the object of desire and
   thought — the Good toward which all motion is oriented. It moves as a final cause.

`DOCUMENTED: SEP, Wikipedia (Unmoved Mover), philosophy.institute/ancient-medieval`

**What does the unmoved mover think about?** Only itself — anything else would imply
something outside it superior to it, which would make it dependent. Aristotle's phrase
(Bekker 1074b35): *noesis noeseos noesis* — "thinking of thinking thinking" or,
in standard English, "thought thinking itself" / "thinking about thinking."

`VERIFIED (the Greek phrase): multiple scholarly sources; the exact English rendering
varies across translators — "thought of thought" (Ross), "thinking of thinking" (others).
The Bekker number 1074b35 is widely confirmed.`

**Caution for the author:** The unmoved mover is NOT the personal God of later
monotheism — it does not create the world, does not know or care about individual
humans, does not intervene. It is the eternal terminus of explanation, the condition for
all motion. Aquinas will borrow the argument and re-point it toward the Christian God —
but that is Aquinas's move, not Aristotle's. Flag this distinction in the narrative.

### 5f. The syllogism and the founding of formal logic
**Source:** *Prior Analytics* (Organon Book III); *Posterior Analytics* (Organon Book IV)

Aristotle's *Prior Analytics* contains the first fully worked-out formal system of
logical inference in Western thought. A syllogism is an argument in which, given
certain premises, a conclusion follows of necessity from their logical form alone.

**Classic example (Barbara, AAA-1 — the "perfect syllogism"):**
- All men are mortal (All A are B)
- Socrates is a man (All C are A)
- Therefore Socrates is mortal (All C are B)

`DOCUMENTED: SEP "Aristotle's Logic," IEP "Aristotle: Logic," Wikipedia "Prior Analytics"`

Kant (writing in 1781) famously said logic "has not had to go a single step backwards"
since Aristotle — though modern mathematical logic (Frege, Russell) would significantly
extend and revise it. Aristotle also identifies the three figures of the syllogism,
discusses modal logic (necessary/possible propositions), and in *Posterior Analytics*
builds a theory of scientific demonstration: genuine scientific knowledge is demonstrated
from first principles (known, true, and explanatory) through valid syllogistic argument.

**The Organon (collective label for 6 logical works):**
- *Categories* — the basic categories of being and predication
- *On Interpretation* — propositions, contradiction, future contingents
- *Prior Analytics* — the syllogism
- *Posterior Analytics* — scientific demonstration
- *Topics* — dialectical argument
- *Sophistical Refutations* — logical fallacies

The label "Organon" (instrument/tool) was applied by later editors; Aristotle did not
group them this way himself.

## 6. Honest framing — the warts (required; framing gate mandatory)

### Natural slavery (*Politics* I.4–5, 1254a–1255a)
**Source:** Perseus / Rackham translation, 1253a–1255a; 1000wordphilosophy.com

Aristotle argues that some human beings are "slaves by nature" — they lack the
full deliberative capacity for self-governance and are better off (and it is just)
that they be ruled. A natural slave is defined as "a human being belonging by nature
not to himself but to another" (Rackham, VERIFIED at Perseus 1254a). The natural slave
is like a tool: separable from its owner but functioning for the owner's purposes.

**His argument (paraphrase — the framing gate requires showing the real argument, not a
caricature):** The soul naturally rules the body; reason naturally rules appetite; in the
same way, those with fuller rational capacity naturally rule those with less. The
slave-by-nature has a body suited to labor and a limited capacity for deliberation —
sufficient to follow orders but not to direct a life. Being governed by someone more
rational is therefore GOOD for them.

**The honest assessment:** This is not an incidental application of his principles — it
is structurally embedded in how he explains natural hierarchy throughout the *Politics*.
Aristotle acknowledges the existence of conventional slavery (war captives) and says it
is sometimes unjust; natural slavery, he insists, is not. Modern scholarship is
unanimous that the "natural slave" argument has no empirical basis and was used to
rationalize existing Athenian practice.

**Key passages:**
- 1254a13 (Rackham, VERIFIED): "a human being belonging by nature not to himself but to another"
- 1254b13 (Rackham, VERIFIED): "the male is by nature superior, and the female inferior; and the one rules, and the other is ruled"
- 1255a (Rackham): "there exist certain persons who are essentially slaves everywhere and certain others who are so nowhere"

**sourceUrl:** https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0058:book%3D1:section%3D1254a

**Author's framing note:** Name it directly; do not sanitize it. Do not turn it into a
three-paragraph lecture either. One honest paragraph, stating the argument clearly and
noting that it is a documented position, not an eccentricity, is enough. The framing
gate will fail any chapter that silently omits it.

### Subordination of women (*Politics* I.5, 1254b / I.13, 1260a)
**Source:** Perseus / Rackham; Wikipedia "Aristotle's views on women"

Aristotle holds women to be naturally inferior in deliberative capacity:

**Key passages (Rackham, VERIFIED at Perseus):**
- 1254b13: "the male is by nature superior, and the female inferior; and the one rules, and the other is ruled"
- 1260a11: "The slave is wholly lacking the deliberative element; the female has it but it lacks authority; the child has it but it is incomplete"
- 1260a20–23: "the courage of a man is shown in commanding, of a woman in obeying"

**sourceUrl:** https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0058:book%3D1:section%3D1260a

**The contrast with Plato:** Plato (in the Republic) argued for women guardians on the
basis that soul-type, not sex, is the relevant criterion for ruling. Aristotle explicitly
rejects this. His position is consistent: if women have a deliberative faculty that
"lacks authority" (akuron — without authority/decisive force), they can follow orders
but cannot direct political life. The narrative should note this contrast — it is a
genuine and significant divergence between the two philosophers.

### The Arabic transmission (consistent with era 2 / "Faith meets reason")
**Source:** SEP "Influence of Arabic and Islamic Philosophy on the Latin West";
faith-reason-fact-pack.md (already built)

**THE ERROR TO AVOID:** Do not write that the Arabic scholars "translated" Aristotle.
The correct account (confirmed in era-1 r2 fact-check, greeks-fact-pack.md r2 note):
Arabic translations of Aristotle existed (made from Greek and Syriac); al-Fārābī,
Avicenna, and Averroes STUDIED those translations and wrote their OWN commentaries
and philosophical syntheses. They are not translators; they are philosophers who worked
in a tradition of Aristotle reception. `DOCUMENTED: SEP, era-2 pack`

**Al-Fārābī** (~872–950): Harmonized Aristotle with Plato and Islamic political thought;
his "Second Teacher" honorific (Aristotle was "First Teacher") signals the scope of his
engagement. `DOCUMENTED`

**Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā, 980–1037):** Extended Aristotle's metaphysics and natural
philosophy; his *Kitāb al-Shifāʾ* (Book of Healing) is a massive Aristotelian synthesis.
Key for the unmoved mover / Necessary Existent argument (which Aristotle's theology
influenced but which Avicenna reformulates for the Islamic tradition). `DOCUMENTED`

**Averroes (Ibn Rushd, 1126–1198):** Wrote three layers of commentary on Aristotle
(short, middle, long). His Long Commentaries became the gateway through which Latin
scholars accessed a full Aristotle. Called simply "The Commentator" by Aquinas (and
Aristotle called "The Philosopher") — a naming convention that signals how central both
were to scholastic thought. `DOCUMENTED: NewAdvent/Catholic Encyclopedia on Averroes`

**Thomas Aquinas:** Synthesized Aristotle with Christian theology in the *Summa
Theologiae* (begun 1265). The five ways of proving God's existence in the Summa owe
an explicit debt to Aristotle's unmoved mover. Aquinas's synthesis was so complete
that later critics of scholasticism would attack "Aristotle" when they meant the
whole Thomistic edifice. `DOCUMENTED: faith-reason-fact-pack.md`

**Data spine edges:**
- plato → aristotle (answered Plato, broke with Forms)
- aristotle → theophrastus (successor at Lyceum)
- aristotle → avicenna (Aristotle's natural philosophy and metaphysics)
- aristotle → averroes (commentaries)
- aristotle → aquinas (via Averroes; Aquinas directly cites Aristotle throughout)

## 8. Lineage — inherited and answered

### Inherited from
- **Plato / the Academy** (20 years). Aristotle takes: the importance of the question
  "what is the good life?"; the hylomorphic structure (forms matter — he reworks it);
  the teleological framework; the idea that reason should rule. Aristotle rejects: the
  theory of transcendent Forms; the doctrine of recollection / pre-existing soul; the
  philosophical ruler (he argues for constitutional government in the Politics); Plato's
  dismissal of the senses as unreliable.
- **Pre-Socratics** (studied intensively): the four-element tradition (he works with it),
  Heraclitus, Democritus (whose atomism he rejects but whose empirical spirit he shares).

### Immediately answered by / immediate successors
- **Theophrastus** (~371–287 BC): Aristotle's closest colleague and successor as head of
  the Lyceum; expanded the botanical and natural-history work; the Peripatetic school
  under him was the most active and productive period of the tradition.
- **Andronicus of Rhodes** (~60 BC, 4th head of the Peripatetics): organized and edited
  the surviving corpus into the form we inherit; the label "metaphysics" is his.

### Major downstream reception
- **Arabic commentators** (9th–12th century): al-Fārābī, Avicenna, Averroes — see §6.
- **Scholastic philosophy** (12th–15th century): Albert the Great (Albertus Magnus),
  Thomas Aquinas — Aristotle's *Physics*, *De Anima*, *Ethics*, *Politics*, and
  *Metaphysics* all assimilated into the university curriculum; Aquinas calls him
  "The Philosopher" without further specification.
- **The 1277 condemnation**: Bishop Étienne Tempier condemned 219 propositions in Paris,
  many of them Aristotelian (the eternity of the world, the denial of individual
  providence). This represents the high-water mark of Aristotle's influence also being
  perceived as a theological threat. `DOCUMENTED: faith-reason-fact-pack.md §9`
- **Modern virtue ethics**: Elizabeth Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) —
  reintroduced the Aristotelian framework of virtues and *eudaimonia* as an alternative
  to Kantian deontology and utilitarian consequentialism. Alasdair MacIntyre, *After
  Virtue* (1981) — the major neo-Aristotelian work of the 20th century. `DOCUMENTED:
  Wikipedia "After Virtue"`

## 9. Framing traps and potted-summary alerts (pre-loading for the critics)

1. **"Aristotle was the first logician"** — correct but requires one caveat: the
   Nyāya school in India developed formal logic independently; in the Western tradition
   Aristotle is unambiguously first. The scope is Western. `SHOULD-FIX if scope note is absent`

2. **"The golden mean" as a lazy formula** — Aristotle does not say "everything in
   moderation." The mean is relative to persons and contexts; some actions (adultery,
   murder) have no mean — they are intrinsically wrong regardless of amount. The
   "moderation in all things" bumper-sticker version is a potted-summary distortion.

3. **"Eudaimonia" = "happiness"** — the anachronism trap. See §4 above. Always gloss
   as flourishing / living well on first use.

4. **"Aristotle was Plato's student therefore his philosophy is derivative"** — the
   reverse is closer to the truth. Aristotle is the most independently original of
   all Plato's students; his entire system is a sustained, often sharp rebuttal of
   Platonism even as it inherits the central questions.

5. **"Aristotle's natural teleology = creationism"** — the unmoved mover is NOT the
   personal God who designed the world. Teleology in Aristotle is immanent in things,
   not imposed from outside. This is a framing error that conflates Aristotle with
   Aquinas's use of Aristotle.

6. **"Aristotle was medieval / Christian"** — he died 322 BC and had nothing to do with
   Christianity. His texts arrived in Western Europe via the Arabic scholars. The
   medieval Aristotle (through Aquinas) is a re-pointed Aristotle; the Aristotelian
   text is the source, not the medieval application.

7. **"Amicus Plato sed magis amica veritas" (a friend to Plato but a greater friend to
   truth)** — this famous Latin phrase is NOT a direct Aristotle quote. It is a medieval
   Latin paraphrase of an idea at NE I.6, 1096a (where Aristotle distinguishes his
   duty to truth from piety toward those who introduced the Forms). The exact Latin
   sentence appears in various medieval texts; Plato's actual *Phaedo* (91c) and the NE
   passage inform it, but no single ancient source has the exact Latin wording attributed
   to Aristotle. **Ship as paraphrase / medieval tradition; never as a direct quote.**
   `PARAPHRASE-ONLY`

8. **Plato's Politics of philosopher-kings vs. Aristotle's constitutionalism** — the
   contrast is real and important, but do not overstate Aristotle as a proto-democrat.
   His preferred constitution in the *Politics* is a "polity" (mixed constitution) for
   the middle class; he also defends oligarchy and kingship under certain conditions.
   He explicitly excludes slaves, women, and laborers from citizenship.

## 10. The break block (required: before/after)
**Before:** The really real is elsewhere — in a separate, timeless realm of Forms,
unchanging and perfect, of which the things you see are pale copies. Philosophy's job
is to climb out of the sensory world and grasp that realm through pure reason. The body
is a prison; the senses mislead; truth lives above.

**After:** The really real is HERE, in the things themselves. The form of a horse is in
the horse, not floating in Platonic heaven. Understanding anything means studying IT —
its matter, its form, its origins, its purpose — through careful observation and
systematic reasoning. Philosophy does not escape the world; it explains it.

**The move, stated concretely:** When Plato's Socrates wants to know what justice IS,
he builds an ideal city in imagination. When Aristotle wants to know what justice is,
he collects and analyzes 158 actual Greek constitutions. The gesture is everything.
Raphael painted it: Plato's finger raised toward heaven, Aristotle's palm turned down
toward the earth. The break is that turn of the hand.
