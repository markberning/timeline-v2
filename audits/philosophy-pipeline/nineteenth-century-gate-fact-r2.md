### Tag 2 — Ch 2 (Mill, anti-apriorist / inductivist thesis)

**R2 text (lines 163–167):**
> "Against the rationalist claim that some truths are known in advance of all experience, written into the structure of the mind, Mill held that even the laws of mathematics and logic are inductive generalizations, conclusions we have drawn so many times, so reliably, from what the world shows us that they feel necessary but are in fact learned. [VERIFY: Mill's *System of Logic* argues that even mathematical and logical truths are inductive generalizations from experience, an anti-apriorist empiricism.]"

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

**Sources:** SEP — John Stuart Mill (§2, "Epistemology and Philosophy of Science"); Wikipedia — *A System of Logic*; IEP — Mill, John Stuart.

The SEP entry is explicit: Mill "contends that arithmetic propositions assert genuine facts about the world, not mere definitional conventions" and holds that "there is in every step of arithmetical or algebraical calculation a real induction, a real inference of facts from facts." Geometric axioms are similarly treated as empirical idealizations, not a priori certainties. The SEP confirms Mill "denies that mathematics enjoys special necessity or a priori status" and that this is "the most thorough attempt to argue for empiricism in epistemology, logic, and mathematics before the twentieth century." The r2 description — "inductive generalizations ... they feel necessary but are in fact learned" — is a fair paraphrase of this position.

**One precision note (not a fix):** The r2 text says "even the laws of mathematics *and logic*"; Mill's treatment of logic's first principles as inductive is slightly more contested in the literature than his treatment of mathematics, but both are part of his empiricist program and the claim as written is defensible at era altitude. No change required.

**No change required.**

**R2 text (lines 369–373):**
> "A predecessor named Ludwig Feuerbach had already tried, in *The Essence of Christianity* (1841), reducing theology to anthropology: God, Feuerbach argued, is not a being above us but a projection of human nature, our own best qualities thrown onto the sky and worshipped back. **Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

**Sources:** Wikipedia — *The Essence of Christianity*; Wikipedia — Ludwig Feuerbach; Britannica — *The Essence of Christianity*; Project Gutenberg #47025.

Three sub-claims verify:

1. **Date 1841:** The book was first published in 1841 in German as *Das Wesen des Christentums*. Confirmed across all sources.
2. **Projection critique:** Wikipedia quotes Feuerbach directly: "Thus God is nothing else than man: he is, so to speak, the outward projection of man's inward nature." Confirmed as the book's central thesis.
3. **"Our own best qualities thrown onto the sky":** The r2 paraphrase captures the mechanism accurately. Feuerbach argues that every divine attribute (understanding, morality, love) corresponds to a human quality that humanity has alienated from itself by projecting it onto an imagined external being. The word "best" in the r2 paraphrase is a fair shorthand — Feuerbach specifically analyzes God's qualities as human excellences (love, justice, wisdom) projected outward.

**Species-being as Marx's springboard:** The r2 text in the lines immediately following (374–378) states "Marx took the move and said it did not go far enough" and (411–413) glosses species-being as "what Marx (borrowing the term from Feuerbach) calls our species-being." Both claims are accurate: species-being (*Gattungswesen*) is Feuerbach's term which Marx adopted and transformed; and Marx's critique of Feuerbach (the Theses, the 1844 Manuscripts) is precisely that Feuerbach identified the projection but failed to explain the material conditions that produce it.

### Tag 5 — Ch 5 (BGE §22 perspectivism locus, Zimmern trans.)

**R2 text (lines 543–549):**
> "In *Beyond Good and Evil* §22 he puts this against the physicists who think they are reading nature's own statutes, and insists they are reading their own grammar into it. [VERIFY: in *Beyond Good and Evil* §22 (Zimmern trans.) Nietzsche argues that what physicists call the 'conformity of nature to law' is interpretation, not text.]"

**Verdict: ✅ CONFIRMED**

**Sources:** Project Gutenberg #4363 (*Beyond Good and Evil*, trans. Helen Zimmern, 1907); Marxists.org — BGE Chapter 1 (Zimmern text); secondary analysis of §22.

The Gutenberg Zimmern text of §22 is confirmed to contain Nietzsche's critique of physicists. The section argues that "Nature's conformity to law" is not a text read off nature but an interpretation — "a naively humanitarian adjustment and perversion of meaning" — that reflects the interpreter's own democratic assumptions. Nietzsche explicitly frames the alternative: what is called natural law could equally be read as the "tyrannically inconsiderate and relentless enforcement of the claims of power" without any law at all. The section concludes by acknowledging that even this counter-reading is "only interpretation," a self-conscious perspectivist move. The r2 description — "what physicists call the 'conformity of nature to law' is interpretation, not text" — matches the Zimmern text's argument accurately. The phrase "conformity of nature to law" is the Zimmern rendering for the German *Gesetzmässigkeit der Natur*.

The r2 draft paraphrases this rather than quoting it directly, which is correct practice (the Kaufmann translation is in copyright; the Zimmern is PD but the r2 correctly describes rather than quotes from BGE §22). No quotation marks appear around the §22 content in the r2 text.

**No change required.**
