## MUST-FIX list

**MF-1 (Ch 1) — The cogito's philosophical content is walked with precision, but the rebuild-from-the-cogito step (God argument → Cartesian circle) is resolved too quickly, and the chapter's own biggest idea — dualism — gets its worked example cut short before the reader fully holds it.**

The cogito section is excellent: the method of doubt walked in three escalating levels, the demon as terminal doubt, the "nothing cannot be tricked" move, the philological care on where COGITO ERGO SUM actually appears. This is Gate 2 at its best. But the chapter then needs to deliver two more philosophical payloads and runs short on both.

**Dualism:** The mind-body distinction (res cogitans / res extensa) is defined, the pineal gland is named, and Elisabeth's objection is run powerfully. But the chapter never gives the reader the plain-language restatement that makes them feel the strangeness of the position itself — not just the objection to it. What does it actually mean that your decision to raise your arm and the physical event of the arm rising are events in two utterly different kinds of stuff that share no properties? The reader gets the interaction problem before they have sat with the problem's premises long enough to feel why Elisabeth's objection lands as hard as it does. One concrete illustration — even a single sentence asking the reader to think about any moment they changed their mind and then did something — would close this. The beeswax aside at the end of the chapter is charming and correct, but it teaches something slightly different (mind over senses, not the two-substance claim). The substance distinction needs its own worked moment.

**The God argument and the Cartesian circle:** These are dispatched in one paragraph. The argument from the idea of perfection is summarized but not walked — the reader is not given the step that would make them feel its force before hearing the Arnauld objection. The three-part test requires the steelman before the counter. As written, the reader gets: (a) "he argues his way to God" (summary), then (b) the Arnauld circle (objection), with no step in between where the reader is made to feel why the God argument seemed compelling to a seventeenth-century mind. One sentence is enough: what does it mean that a finite mind cannot be the source of the concept of infinity? That is the argument's actual grip, and it is not there. Without it, the Arnauld objection feels like kicking a straw man.

This is not a length problem — the chapter is already ~1,790 words (see the long-chapter ruling below). It is a sequencing and completeness problem that can be fixed within the existing length by tightening the philological detail on the cogito (several sentences on the three-book variant can compress to two) and using the recovered space for the two worked steps above.

**MF-2 (Ch 7) — Du Châtelet is placed correctly at the bridge to era 4 but her philosophical contribution is told as biography-and-summary rather than run as an argument. The chapter that closes the era cannot have its era-4 handoff running below the gate's standard.**

The du Châtelet section (final six paragraphs of Ch 7) does the right structural work: she is placed as a thinker, not a footnote; the incompleteness of Newton's physics on metaphysical grounds is named; the Leibniz-to-Newton synthesis direction is given. But the argument she makes — that Newton needs Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason to say WHY there are lawful reasons at all — is told as a claim, not run as a worked example. The three-part test requires:

1. **Worked example:** What does it mean for physics to be "metaphysically incomplete"? A single concrete move would land this: Newton tells you F = ma; du Châtelet asks what entitles us to assume there is a sufficient reason for F equaling ma rather than something else, at every instant, without exception. Leibniz's PSR is the answer she proposes. The reader should feel that question, not just hear that she asked it.

2. **Steelman:** The synthesis she proposes was genuinely contested — why isn't Newton's success its own sufficient reason? A sentence giving that pushback before naming her answer would do the steelmanning work.

3. **Plain restatement:** The chapter gives none. "She argued Newton needed Leibniz to say why there should be lawful, sufficient reasons at all" is close but passive — the reader cannot carry it to the dinner table as a felt position.

The du Châtelet section also needs to land the era-4 handoff more precisely. As written, the closing synthesis paragraph correctly names Hume → Kant and then names "du Châtelet's bridge." But the two threads (Hume's problem → Kant; du Châtelet's Newton-Leibniz synthesis → German thought) are run together without distinguishing how they join. Kant was reading BOTH: the Humean induction crisis AND the question of what grounds Newtonian mechanics. Du Châtelet's synthesis pointed exactly at the latter. The handoff would be cleaner if it said this explicitly — one sentence connecting du Châtelet's specific question (grounds for Newtonian lawfulness) to the problem Kant was already holding (what entitles our synthetic a priori knowledge of nature). This also makes the era-4 hand-off match the precision of the era-2 → era-3 hand-off (Descartes in the warm room), which is the draft's best structural moment.

## SHOULD-FIX list

**SF-1 (Ch 1) — Ch 1 runs ~1,790 words and earns roughly 1,400 of them. The philological detail on the three-book cogito variant is the draft's most compressible passage.**

The three-paragraph philological walk (Discourse / Principles / Meditations; French vs. Latin; "je pense, donc je suis" vs. COGITO ERGO SUM; the Molyneux and Veitch translations) is accurate and admirably precise, but at ~280 words it runs longer than the idea requires for a reader at this altitude. The essential point is: the punchy Latin lives in the Principles, not the Meditations people cite; the Meditations version arrives as bare first-person fact without "therefore." That is two sentences, not ten. The full philological trail is worth a thinker-level or work-level page; at an era read it costs space that MF-1 needs. A trim to ~120 words here would recover the space for the dualism and God-argument worked examples without adding length.

**SF-2 (Ch 3) — The contrast between Spinoza and Leibniz at the chapter's close is the era's best structural move, but it arrives as an authorial summary rather than a moment the reader is walked to.**

"Same inheritance, same method, opposite universes" is the correct conclusion. But the draft gets there by telling the reader that the contrast is the point ("set the two rationalist poles side by side, because the contrast is the point") rather than by placing the two conclusions next to each other and letting the reader feel the gulf. The contrast paragraph currently runs: Spinoza's conclusion → Leibniz's conclusion → authorial judgment. If it instead opened by asking the reader to hold both images simultaneously — one infinite God-or-Nature in which you are a determined ripple, one infinite colony of sealed windowless clocks synchronized by a loving God who picked the best option — the reader arrives at "same method, opposite universes" by their own perception rather than being told to notice it. The edit is a single reordering, not a rewrite.

**SF-3 (Ch 4) — The Locke-Jefferson footnote is accurate and good, but it interrupts the chapter's stride at the wrong moment.**

The note that Jefferson swapped "estate" for "pursuit of happiness" and that "consent of the governed" is Jefferson's rather than Locke's is exactly the kind of apocryphal-summary correction this vertical exists to make. The problem is placement: it arrives mid-paragraph, just as the chapter has built to the most important structural contrast (Hobbes sovereign makes no promise; Locke government is a trustee). The footnote deflates that contrast before it lands by moving the reader out of Locke's argument and into 1776. Moving the Jefferson note to a clean parenthetical after the paragraph's close, or compressing it to one sentence inside the Locke summary, would keep the contrast's momentum and retain the correction.

**SF-4 (Ch 6) — The missing shade of blue and the is/ought gap are the two Hume ideas most likely to feel like an appendix rather than a landing.**

Both ideas are handled correctly — the missing shade is noted as a genuine exception and Hume's shrug is named; the is/ought gap is given its proper structural importance. But they arrive after the causation and bundle-self arguments, which are the chapter's philosophical peaks, and the effect is slightly falling action rather than a plateau. The missing shade in particular is brief enough that a reader could mistake it for a fun aside. A single sentence establishing its stakes before the example — something like "Hume's most honest moment is when he notices his own system has a hole" — would give it enough weight to land as a real point rather than a coda. The is/ought gap is currently handled better (the "quietly demolished a whole way of doing ethics" wind-up is good) but "every moral system that claims to read its values straight off the facts of nature has, somewhere, made an illegal crossing" should be the close of the section, not buried in the paragraph's interior.

## Long-chapter ruling

**AUTHOR-FLAGGED: Ch 1 runs approximately 1,790 words. Is the overrun earned?**

**Ruling: PARTIALLY EARNED — with a specific required trim and a specific required addition.**

Ch 1 carries four distinct philosophical loads: the method of doubt (three levels), the cogito and its textual variants, the dualism (with the interaction problem and Elisabeth), and the God argument (with the Cartesian circle). Four loads for the era's foundational thinker is correct at this altitude — none can be silently dropped. The chapter's actual problem is not that it carries too much but that it distributes its space poorly: the philological detail on the cogito's three-book variant runs ~280 words, which is the era-read equivalent of a footnote expanded to a section. The two worked-example gaps named in MF-1 (dualism as a position, the God argument's steelman) require ~150 words of recovery space. The trim and the addition roughly balance; the net chapter length would hold near its current word count while fixing the gate failure.

**Specific ruling:** Ch 1 is PERMITTED at approximately current length PROVIDED that (a) the philological detail on the cogito variant is trimmed to ~120 words (two paragraphs to one), and (b) the recovered space is used for the two MF-1 worked examples. A chapter that is long for the wrong reasons is not the same as one that has earned its length — Ch 1 is currently the former. After the rebalancing it will be the latter.

**Ch 7 runs approximately standard length and earns it.** No ruling needed.

## Biggest concern

**The draft's most vulnerable chapter is Ch 7, not Ch 1 — but for a different reason than the spec's usual biography-without-thinking risk.** Ch 1's philosophy-without-worked-examples gap is real (MF-1) but targeted and fixable within the chapter's own frame. Ch 7's risk is structural: the chapter closes the era and carries the era-4 handoff, which means it has to do three things simultaneously — complete Hume's argument, evaluate the era's question honestly, and hand the knife forward. For Hume's argument and the era synthesis, Ch 7 is GOOD to STRONG. The vulnerability is entirely in the du Châtelet section (MF-2), which is the era-4 handoff's second thread and the chapter's weakest passage. A GOOD draft that closes with an underworked handoff is a draft that risks making the next era feel discontinuous. MF-2 is not optional.

The secondary concern is that the draft's overall strength invites a mistake the spec explicitly warns against: a passing draft is not a gated draft. Every STRONG chapter here passes because its worked examples work. The two MUST-FIX items are precisely in the chapters that run close to the gate — Ch 1 passes on voice and structure while failing the three-part test on two of its four philosophical loads; Ch 7 passes on Hume while failing it on du Châtelet. Both are correctable without structural revision. Both must be corrected before ship.
