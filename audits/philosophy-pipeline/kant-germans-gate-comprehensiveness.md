## MUST-ADD LIST (in priority order)

**MA-1 — The third Critique's content needs one concrete sentence beyond its title**
The *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790) is named in the draft and its domain correctly stated ("beauty and purpose, how aesthetic and natural judgments work"). But the naming does no work: a reader finishes Ch 3 knowing there is a third Critique about "beauty and purpose" with no handle to hold. At era altitude the essential move is: the third Critique bridges the gap between the phenomenal (nature, causation, necessity) and the practical (freedom, morality) by showing that aesthetic and teleological judgments — particularly the sublime and our sense that nature appears purposive — are what allow us to feel at home in a world Kant has otherwise split in two. That bridging function is the REASON the third Critique completes the system; without it, naming it is just a list. One sentence would close this. The sublime as an aesthetic experience that makes us feel the mind's superiority to nature is also the most vivid concept in the third Critique for a newcomer and is completely absent. **New verification needed: none — this is standard Kant scholarship; no new fact-finding required beyond what the pack already carries.**

**MA-2 — Kant's third formula of the categorical imperative (Kingdom of Ends) is absent, and its absence makes autonomy invisible**
The draft covers Formula 1 (universal law) and Formula 2 (humanity as end) correctly and well. Formula 3 — the Kingdom of Ends ("act as if you were always both a legislating member and a subject of a kingdom of ends") — is omitted. At bare minimum, the principle of AUTONOMY (the idea that the moral law is a law rational beings give to themselves, not one handed down from God or custom) needs one sentence. Kant himself considered autonomy — self-legislation — the centerpiece of his ethics and the ground from which both prior formulas follow. The omission means the draft implies Kant's ethics is fundamentally about rule-testing and people-respecting, but never names the deeper claim: that a rational being is its own moral legislator. That is the root idea from which Kierkegaard's revolt, Mill's utility objection, and Nietzsche's eventual "create your own values" all implicitly push off. A single sentence is enough: the point is that the moral law is not given from outside — rational beings legislate it to themselves, and that self-legislation is what makes it binding. **New verification needed: the fact pack does not explicitly carry the Kingdom of Ends / autonomy formula; the fact-checker should confirm the Abbott *Groundwork* wording before a sentence ships.**

**MA-3 — German Idealism as a named movement is latent but never surfaced**
The draft introduces Fichte and Schelling as "two thinkers" who tried to fix Kant's wall before Hegel. It never says these four figures (plus Kant) constitute a recognized philosophical movement called "German Idealism," what unifies them (the shared project of overcoming Kant's thing-in-itself while keeping the mind-is-active insight), or why that movement matters as a unit. The fact pack's scope description is "the era that reorganized everything," but the movement label that historians, readers, and every subsequent philosophical tradition uses to name what happened in 60 years of German thought goes unmentioned. At era altitude one sentence suffices: "The four of them — Kant, Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel — constitute the tradition called German Idealism, a movement of roughly 60 years (1780s to 1840s) that tried to complete Kant's revolution without accepting any of its limits." That frame makes the Schopenhauer counter-move legible as a counter to a movement, not just a personal grudge. **New verification needed: none — the dates and scope of German Idealism are standard.**

## SHOULD-CONSIDER LIST

**SC-1 — Kant's Antinomies: naming one gives the thing-in-itself wall a stronger "here's why" explanation**
The draft correctly establishes the thing-in-itself as a consequence of the Copernican move, but does not mention the Antinomies — the passages in the first Critique where Kant shows that pure reason, when applied beyond experience, generates contradictions (e.g., the universe has a beginning / the universe has no beginning — both provable by reason). The Antinomies are Kant's proof that unconstrained metaphysical speculation is not just unhelpful but self-destroying. Naming them in one sentence would give the wall a stronger "and here is exactly why pure reason cannot cross it" justification. At era altitude this is SHOULD-CONSIDER, not must-add, because the draft achieves the same conclusion by other means.

**SC-2 — Hegel's *Phenomenology* preface: the "night in which all cows are black" on Schelling**
The fact pack includes this as a soft spot with verified concept. The draft introduces Schelling as a bridge-builder and Hegel as someone who found him stopping short — but Hegel's famous put-down of Schelling-style Absolute philosophy (reducing all distinctions into an undifferentiated whole) would make the Fichte → Schelling → Hegel progression vivid rather than merely sequential. One sentence, framed as the pack instructs ("Hegel writes in the *Phenomenology*'s Preface..."), would earn the transition. This is personality-and-content gold; the draft is slightly thinner for its absence. **The fact-checker's soft spot §10 item 2 governs: confirm the exact Baillie wording before inserting.**

**SC-3 — Hegel's Napoleon anecdote (the "world-soul on horseback" letter)**
The fact pack flags this as documented (Hegel's letter of 13 Oct 1806) and notes it as "one of philosophy's great biographical scenes." The draft finishes the *Phenomenology* in Ch 4 without mentioning that Hegel finished the manuscript the night Napoleon rode past Jena. This is not a comprehensiveness gap — the letter is biographical color — but it is the kind of concrete moment (philosophy and world-history literally meeting on the same street at the same time) that earns the Hegelian claim that philosophy and history are connected. The storytelling critic should flag it; this gate surfaces it as a consistent should-consider.

**SC-4 — The third formula / Kingdom of Ends: if MA-2 is added, autonomy's political consequences deserve a single pointer**
If the draft gains one sentence on autonomy as self-legislation (MA-2), a second sentence noting that this is the root from which Kant's claim about human dignity — and by extension the political tradition of liberalism built on rational agency — grows would close the loop the ch 6 handoff opens. Without it, the Kant→Mill handoff in ch 6 looks like a technical debate about methods; with it, it looks like a political-moral debate about the grounds of human value. SHOULD-CONSIDER after MA-2 is addressed.
