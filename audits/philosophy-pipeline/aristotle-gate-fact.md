### F-1 — Durant debunk present and correctly handled

**Draft text (Ch4):** "You have certainly seen it, probably on a poster or a graduation speech: *'We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit.'* It is attributed to Aristotle everywhere. **Aristotle never wrote it.** ... The sentence is from **Will Durant**, in his 1926 popular book *The Story of Philosophy* ... Durant's actual passage paraphrases Aristotle and then adds, as Durant's own neat capstone: 'we are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit.'"

**Verification:** Web-confirmed. The Durant passage (from Chapter 7, subsection 2 of *The Story of Philosophy*, 1926) matches the pack's §3 text exactly. The embedded single-quote ('these virtues are formed in man by his doing the actions') is Durant's paraphrase; the two-sentence summary is Durant's capstone, not Aristotle. Snopes, CheckYourFact, and umb.edu/quoteunquote all confirm. The scholar-grants-it's-accurate note is verified.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED — appears ONLY as debunk, never asserted as Aristotle. The load-bearing requirement is fully met.

### F-10 — Politics 1260a11: deliberative element

**Draft text (Ch5):** "The slave is wholly lacking the deliberative element; the female has it but it lacks authority; the child has it but it is incomplete" — Rackham (1260a11)

**Problem:** The draft quotes this in quotation marks attributed to Rackham, but the wording is a paraphrase/condensation, not the actual translation. The differences are: "wholly lacking" ≠ "has not got… at all"; "lacks authority" ≠ "without full authority"; "is incomplete" ≠ "in an undeveloped form." This is a born-verified text violation — the quote marks imply verbatim Rackham.

**Status:** ❌ MUST-FIX — Either use the actual Rackham wording ("the slave has not got the deliberative part at all, and the female has it, but without full authority, while the child has it, but in an undeveloped form") or rephrase as paraphrase without quotation marks.

### F-15 — Aristotle's death: "digestive ailment" attribution ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX

**Draft text (Ch1):** "He died there the next year, in 322 BC, around sixty-two or sixty-three, of a digestive ailment."

**Verification:** Web-checked. DL Book V (penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer) gives the death as "drinking aconite, at the age of seventy" — citing Eumelus. A separate ancient source (Apollodorus) gives a natural death from a "languishment of stomach" (digestive/stomach ailment). The draft uses "digestive ailment" without any attribution hedge, implying it is a fact rather than a disputed ancient claim. The pack's ledger notes "death cause per DL framed softly ('of a digestive ailment')" — but this conflates DL (who says aconite-poison, citing Eumelus) with Apollodorus (who says stomach ailment). The actual DL account is more alarming (poison) and the pack's note is slightly misleading about which source says what.

**Problem:** The draft drops "of a digestive ailment" without any "according to one ancient account" or "the ancient sources disagree" hedge. Given that DL itself gives poison-by-aconite as one account, and another source gives stomach ailment, this should be framed as the ancient tradition is disputed. The current prose reads like a confirmed fact.

**Status:** ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX — Add a light hedge: "the ancient sources disagree about the cause — one gives a digestive ailment, another says poison — and none are reliable" or simply omit the cause if a clean hedge disrupts the flow.

### F-17 — Third Man argument: attribution and wording

**Draft text (brk):** "He had a concrete objection to drive it home… the argument later nicknamed the **Third Man**… If many large things are large by sharing in one Form, Largeness itself, then notice that Largeness itself is also, presumably, large…"

**Verification:** Web-searched. The Third Man argument is associated with Aristotle (from *Metaphysics* and *Sophistic Refutations*) but the name "Third Man" comes from Aristotle's own version using the example of "a man" — not "Largeness." The "Largeness" version is Plato's own in the *Parmenides* (what the SEP calls the "Large" or "Greatness" regress). The draft correctly links this to the *Parmenides* and says "the argument later nicknamed the Third Man," attributing it to Aristotle as the one who "made it the center of his break." This is accurate: the argument is in both the *Parmenides* and Aristotle's *Metaphysics*; the name "Third Man" is Aristotle's. The draft says "Plato had already staged a version of this objection against himself in his late dialogue the *Parmenides* … using the example of greatness; Aristotle took the crack his teacher had exposed." This is a fair and accurate account.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED — handling of TMA is accurate and the Parmenides precedent is correctly noted.

### FR-8 — The "not a proto-democrat" guardrail — gap ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX

**Draft text (Ch5):** The chapter covers natural slavery and women. It does not mention what Aristotle's preferred constitution actually was in the *Politics* — the "polity" (mixed constitution), his exclusion of slaves, women, and laborers from citizenship.

**Assessment:** The pack §9.8 warns: "do not overstate Aristotle as a proto-democrat. His preferred constitution in the *Politics* is a 'polity' (mixed constitution) for the middle class; he also defends oligarchy and kingship under certain conditions. He explicitly excludes slaves, women, and laborers from citizenship." The draft covers the honest-warts passages but does not mention the citizenship exclusion beyond those passages. The "political animal" framing in Ch5 implies a broad natural political vocation but doesn't note that Aristotle's *actual* polis excludes most of the population from political participation.

**Problem:** The gap is not a distortion but an incompleteness in the framing — a reader could finish Ch5 believing "political animal" means all humans are included in political life, when Aristotle explicitly excludes slaves, women, and manual laborers from citizenship. One sentence noting this would close the gap. This is a mild framing issue, not a factual error.

**Status:** ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX — Add a closing note in Ch5 that the polis Aristotle treats as natural was one that excluded slaves, women, and laborers from citizenship — the people whose natural inferiority he just argued for. The point closes the honest-warts loop neatly.
