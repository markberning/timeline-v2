## Preliminary: what the framing gate is testing in this era

Era 5 carries the corpus's heaviest framing load. Four thinkers, each with a distinct posthumous-distortion track record:

- Nietzsche: the hardest misuse case in the entire v1 arc — Nazi appropriation via Elisabeth's editorial construction; requires the honest-warts treatment at its fullest.
- Harriet Taylor Mill: the canonical erasure-by-omission case in 19th-century philosophy; the lone-genius axis turns entirely on her.
- Marx: 20th-century state-communism piled on top of a philosopher who died in 1883; the era-altitude discipline requires keeping those apart.
- Kierkegaard: the anachronism trap of reading 20th-century existentialism (Sartre's framework) backward onto him.

This gate checks all five axes. The flagship findings are Nietzsche-misuse and Harriet-Taylor-as-partner; both are confirmed passed below, with findings registered.

## MUST-FIX findings

### MUST-FIX 1 — Ch 5 / Ch 6, axis 5 (Posthumous-misuse): Elisabeth's editorial motive called "she shaped the published record" — fact pack requires nuancing the motivation claim

**Location:** Ch 5 (Nietzsche chapter), the Elisabeth paragraph:

> "She was married to a prominent antisemitic German nationalist, the very kind of man Nietzsche despised; he had broken with her over the marriage and mocked her husband in his letters. After his death she shaped the published record to fit her own nationalist, antisemitic views, and she assembled a book called *The Will to Power* out of scraps from his notebooks, selecting and ordering them to support that reading."

**What the problem is:** The phrase "to fit her own nationalist, antisemitic views" states deliberate falsification as established fact. The fact pack's §10, item 3 explicitly flags this: "Recent scholarship (notably Robert Holub) argues her primary motive was protecting Nietzsche's reputation rather than deliberate falsification, while still agreeing the results were distorting." The pack's PINNED section (§10 final block, item 3) specifies: "The narrative should say 'distorted' without claiming malicious intent as established fact — the motivation is contested, the effect is not."

The draft says more than the evidence warrants. The effect of Elisabeth's editing is documented and agreed upon; her internal motive is not. Stating that she "shaped the published record to fit her own nationalist, antisemitic views" implies she knew the result was false and chose it anyway. That is a claim the scholarship does not settle. The narrative is allowed to say the results served those views; it is not allowed to assign that as her conscious program.

**What the distortion does:** It makes the story simpler and more satisfying (villain acts out of villainy) than the record supports. It also, paradoxically, weakens the force of the historical point — the real horror is that distortion happens without obvious malice, through selective emphasis, family loyalty, and ideological sympathy, not only through calculated fraud.

**Required repair:** Replace the causal framing with a documented-effect framing:

Current: "she shaped the published record to fit her own nationalist, antisemitic views"
Revised: "she shaped the published record in ways that fit her own nationalist, antisemitic sympathies — whether by design or by the instincts of a true believer, the scholarly consensus is that the results distorted the work, not that the motivation is resolved"

Or more economically: "she shaped the published record in ways that aligned with her own nationalist, antisemitic sympathies; the effect is documented, the intent still debated."

The rest of the Elisabeth passage is well done and should be preserved. The distinction between effect and intent is one sentence; it does not require restructuring the paragraph.

**Severity:** MUST-FIX. The fact pack explicitly requires this nuance. Overclaiming intent is a framing error under axis 5 — not a sanitization in this case, but an overclaim in the other direction that goes beyond what the evidence supports.

### MUST-FIX 2 — Ch 2, axis 3 (Lone-genius / erasure): Harriet Taylor's role in *The Subjection of Women* is overstated relative to the fact pack's guidance

**Location:** Ch 2, near the end of the Harriet Taylor section:

> "Their joint thinking produced Mill's most radical book, *The Subjection of Women* (1869), and it matters that it is an argument and not a plea... it grew directly out of a partnership the dedication insists on naming."

And earlier: "the 1851 essay 'The Enfranchisement of Women,' published under Mill's name, is now generally taken to be primarily her work."

**What the problem is:** The draft correctly refuses to erase Harriet. It correctly names her as co-author of *On Liberty* and primary author of "The Enfranchisement of Women." The problem is in how it handles *The Subjection of Women*. The phrase "Their joint thinking produced Mill's most radical book" implies co-production of *The Subjection of Women* at the same level as *On Liberty*. The fact pack's §10, item 5 explicitly constrains this: "The SEP says the work 'draws heavily' on her 'Enfranchisement of Women' (1851) and was developed jointly; it does NOT attribute her as primary author of *Subjection* outright (Mill drafted it 1861, revised 1869). Ship as: 'developed from ideas and drafts Harriet had been shaping since at least the "Enfranchisement of Women" (1851).' Do not overclaim sole authorship for her; do not erase her contribution."

The draft's current framing, while not as strong as "Harriet wrote it," implies a joint production of *Subjection* that the evidence does not quite reach — especially given that Harriet died in 1858 and the book was not published until 1869 (Mill's revisions came after her death). The intellectual seeds were hers; the final book was Mill's.

**Required repair:** Change "Their joint thinking produced Mill's most radical book" to something closer to the pack's guidance: "That partnership shaped what became Mill's most radical book — *The Subjection of Women* (1869) grew from arguments Harriet had been developing since at least her 'Enfranchisement of Women' essay, published in 1851." This preserves full credit for her intellectual contribution without overclaiming joint authorship of a book she never saw completed.

This is the mirror-image problem to erasure: under-serving the partner is wrong, but overclaiming is also wrong, and both are framing failures.

**Severity:** MUST-FIX. The fact pack explicitly specifies the correct formulation and specifically warns against overclaiming. The gate requirement here is precision, not more credit.

### MUST-FIX 3 — Ch 5, axis 5 (Posthumous-misuse): The [VERIFY] tag on the March 2026 "On Liberty" dual-authorship recognition was not resolved — unresolved factual claim present in the draft

**Location:** Ch 2, final paragraph of the Harriet Taylor section:

> "[VERIFY: a March 2026 report that *On Liberty* has been formally recognized as having two authors; the fact-checker should confirm what this refers to before the narrative asserts it.]"

**What the problem is:** This [VERIFY] tag is explicitly unresolved in the draft. The pipeline rule is absolute: a [VERIFY] tag means the claim it guards cannot ship. If the claim is not resolved, it must either be confirmed (and the tag cleared) or dropped from the draft. The tag cannot travel into the gated draft — it signals that a factual claim was not born-verified at authoring time.

The fact pack's §10, item 6 flags this as a soft spot to resolve: "The fact-checker should verify what this refers to — whether a publisher or archival authority has formally recognized dual authorship. If confirmed, this is a clean 'framing gate satisfied' moment."

At gate time, this remains unresolved. The framing gate cannot clear a [VERIFY] tag; clearing it is the fact-checker's job. If the fact-checker (gate 1) has not resolved this, the tag must be stripped and the conditional claim dropped until confirmed.

**Required repair:** Either (a) the fact-checker (gate 1) resolves this and writes the confirmed claim into the pack, allowing the narrative to state it clearly, or (b) the sentence containing the [VERIFY] tag is removed from the narrative and the surrounding text adjusted so that Harriet's co-authorship rests only on what is already fully documented: Mill's own dedication language ("in part the author"), his *Autobiography* statement, the SEP confirmation. Those alone are sufficient; the 2026 formal recognition is a bonus that may or may not be verifiable.

**Severity:** MUST-FIX. An unresolved [VERIFY] tag is a structural ship-blocker regardless of which gate catches it. The framing gate catches it here because it is in a framing-sensitive passage.

## SHOULD-FIX findings

### SHOULD-FIX 1 — Era hook, axis 1 (Anachronism): Throughline groups Nietzsche's contribution in a way that front-loads the post-Kantian system as the common target — risks flattening Mill

**Location:** The throughline (front matter) and the "Why this is a break" block.

The throughline correctly names all four exits from the Hegelian room. But the "Why this is a break" block gives three of the four their full anti-Hegelian profile (Kierkegaard: the individual vs. system; Marx: materialism inverts idealism; Nietzsche: the death of God) and treats Mill's break as the one that "looks least like a revolt" (Ch 1). Mill's break is actually not primarily from Hegel at all — it is from Bentham's hedonic calculus, with the German tradition as background noise, not foreground target. The draft correctly narrates this in Ch 2. But the era-level framing ("four minds climbed out [of Hegel's system]") slightly distorts Mill's intellectual position by placing him within the post-Hegelian German frame. Mill was not running from Hegel; he was retooling Bentham.

This is a soft anachronism — not reading 20th-century frameworks back, but reading a German-idealist frame onto a thinker whose revolt was against English utilitarianism. The Ch 1 parenthetical ("and it ran through England rather than Germany") partially corrects it, but the era frame still implies all four are anti-Hegelian in the same way.

**Required repair:** One qualifying sentence in the "Why this is a break" block acknowledging that Mill's revolt has a different address: "Mill's break is not from Hegel but from Bentham's British utilitarianism — his entry ramp is English, not German, though the era's shared question (how should a single person live?) runs through all four." This does not undermine the throughline; it makes it more accurate.

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX. The framing is not distorted, only imprecise; the chapter-level treatment is correct.

### SHOULD-FIX 2 — Ch 3, axis 1 (Anachronism): "Kierkegaard — the first existentialist" is used as chapter title and as a description without the anachronism caveat the fact pack requires

**Location:** Ch 3 header: "Kierkegaard and the individual before God" — this title is fine. But the chapter opens with:

> "Kierkegaard (1813 to 1855) grew up in Copenhagen..."

and later:

> "His conviction was that the truths that matter most..."

The fact pack's §2 explicitly flags: "'the first existentialist' is a retrospective label — Kierkegaard never called himself this, and the word 'existentialism' comes later (Sartre). Use the label as the inherited description it is, with one honest sentence about its anachronism."

The draft does not call him "the first existentialist" in the prose — that language only appears in the fact pack's ch3 section description and is not reproduced in the draft. Good. But the chapter does not include the one honest sentence about the retrospective nature of the label, which the pack requires if the label is used at all. Since the pack's chapter title used the phrase "the first existentialist," the guard sentence is still owed. Even if the chapter avoids the phrase in prose, a reader familiar with "existentialism" will bring that frame to Kierkegaard; one sentence explicitly noting that he never used the word, and that Sartre came a century later, would inoculate against the anachronistic reading.

**Required repair:** Add, early in Ch 3: "His work would later be called existentialist — a label he never used and that belongs to Sartre, Camus, and the 20th century; better to say he was the first philosopher to put the individual, the one suffering, deciding person, at the center of philosophy."

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX. The draft does not commit the anachronism, but the prophylactic sentence the pack requires is missing.

### SHOULD-FIX 3 — Ch 4, axis 5 (Posthumous-misuse): The Marx-and-Engels collaboration is not named — Engels is reduced to the function of a posthumous distorter

**Location:** Ch 4, opening paragraph and the Thesis XI passage:

> "...the revolutions, the regimes, all came long after his death and leaned heavily on a curated, simplified Marx assembled by his collaborator Engels and later political movements."

> "The *Theses on Feuerbach* are eleven terse notes Marx jotted in 1845; the eleventh, as Engels later published it, reads..."

**What the problem is:** The draft correctly names Engels as the editor/shaper of the published record and correctly attributes Thesis XI to Engels' 1888 version. But the framing of Engels throughout Ch 4 is entirely as a downstream compiler and distorter ("curated, simplified Marx assembled by his collaborator Engels"). The fact pack's influence-chain table (§6) names Engels as completing the project "for better or worse." The phrase "for better or worse" acknowledges genuine collaboration — *Capital* volumes 2 and 3 were largely Engels' editorial work; the *Communist Manifesto* was co-authored by both men; Engels' own *Anti-Dühring* shaped what "Marxism" meant for a generation. The draft's framing reduces Engels to a figure who simplified and distorted, without acknowledging that the collaboration was real and the shared project was genuine.

This is not an erasure of a woman or a marginalized figure — it is a framing choice that tilts axis 3's "lone-genius" correction in an odd direction: Marx is presented as a pure original whom Engels distorted, when the historical relationship was more collaborative and more complicated.

**Required repair:** One sentence acknowledging the genuine collaboration: "Engels was not only a posthumous editor but a long collaborator — they wrote the *Manifesto* together; the later distortion ran through the collaboration, not past it." This does not require more detail about Engels; it simply avoids the framing that reduces him to a distortion machine.

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX. The distortion of the Marx-Engels relationship is not the era's primary framing concern, but it is a tilt that the pipeline's lone-genius axis would catch.

### SHOULD-FIX 4 — Ch 5, axis 2 (Strawman): Utilitarianism's position as Nietzsche's rival is not steelmanned before Nietzsche's attack on it

**Location:** The throughline and Ch 1 position Nietzsche as one exit from the system, with his critique of morality as his central move. Ch 5 presents Nietzsche's genealogy of morality (master/slave, ressentiment) and correctly steelmans the slave morality analysis (this is well done — see passing notes below). However, the utilitarianism that Mill spent Ch 2 building is not acknowledged as a live rival to Nietzsche's genealogical project at the era-level.

Nietzsche in the *Genealogy* was explicitly targeting utilitarian accounts of morality ("the utility hypothesis" — BGE §§201–203; *Genealogy* I §1–3 attacks what he calls the "genealogists" who argue morality evolved from utility). The draft presents Mill and Nietzsche as parallel exits from the Hegelian room but does not indicate that they are, on the question of morality, in direct disagreement — Nietzsche's genealogical method was partly aimed at the utilitarian project of explaining morality through consequences.

This is not a strawman of Nietzsche — his position is correctly and fully presented. It is a missed opportunity to show the era as internally contested, not just four parallel revolts against Hegel. The steelman requirement applies to showing what rival positions look like at their strongest before the critique lands.

**Required repair:** One sentence in the Ch 6 reckoning or in the Nietzsche chapter acknowledging that Mill and Nietzsche are not just parallel but rival responses to the same problem: what gives moral claims their authority? Mill's answer is consequences and human welfare; Nietzsche's is that moral claims do not have authority — they have genealogies and should be examined as such. This does not require new factual material; it is a framing observation that the narrative's current structure implies without quite stating.

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX. The omission is not a distortion; it is a missed clarity. The chapter-level treatments are correct.

### SHOULD-FIX 6 — Ch 6, axis 4 (Western overclaim): The arc-closing passage implies the entire philosophical tradition's God is dead — scoping not explicit

**Location:** Ch 6 (The reckoning), the long closing passage:

> "Thales tried to explain the world without gods at the start. Nietzsche announces, at the end, that the West's God is gone and nobody is ready for what that means. The snake has reached its own tail."

And:

> "Philosophy began when a man decided the world could be understood by human reason without appealing to a god. Twenty-five centuries later, the same project arrives at a man declaring that the god the West had reasoned its way into needing is dead..."

**What the problem is:** The closing frame is powerful and mostly well-scoped. The phrase "the West's God" correctly scopes the claim to Western Christianity. But the sentence "Philosophy began when a man decided..." echoes a Western overclaim that the pipeline brief explicitly guards: the scope is "Western philosophy begins" not "philosophy begins." The sentence "Philosophy began when a man decided..." could be read as claiming philosophy itself (globally) began with Thales, not Western philosophy.

The fact pack and the pipeline brief both specify that the v1 arc is Western philosophy. The Greeks chapter presumably opened with the scope explicitly stated; the reckoning chapter should not implicitly unclaim it by narrating "philosophy" as a universal project that Thales started.

**Required repair:** Change "Philosophy began when a man decided..." to "Western philosophy began when a man decided..." — one word. This is a minimal fix with no rhetorical cost; the parallelism with "the West's God is gone" actually improves when the opening is scoped to match.

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX. The overclaim is accidental and small; one word corrects it. The chapter otherwise handles scoping well.
