## Part 1: Forward Findings (new TL gaps)

### Chapter 1 — Two Capitals, Two Powers

1. **"the Battle of Dan-no-ura ... a sea fight in the narrow Shimonoseki Strait"** — The Genpei War and Dan-no-ura are covered in far more detail in the immediately preceding reference TL, including the tidal reversal that decided the battle, the defection of Taguchi Shigeyoshi who identified the Taira flagship, and the loss of the imperial sword. medieval-japan should add a terse cross-ref (e.g. "the Genpei War, told from the classical court's side in the Heian Japan narrative, Chapter 8") so the reader can pick up the detailed naval account. — Heian Japan, Ch 8.

2. **"drowned by his own grandmother rather than be captured, died the child-emperor Antoku, eight years old"** — Heian Ch 8 gives the fuller scene (Tokiko/Nii no Ama dressing him in court robes, telling him of a capital beneath the waves, stepping off with the imperial sword in her sash) and the detail that the sword was lost on the seabed. A pointer to that account would enrich without duplicating. — Heian Japan, Ch 8.

3. **"the deadliest weapon turned out to be Yoritomo's younger half-brother, Minamoto no Yoshitsune"** — Yoshitsune is "one of the most beloved characters in Japanese cultural memory" and the central figure of Heian Ch 8 (Ichinotani cliff descent, Yashima storm crossing). medieval-japan introduces him only to kill him; a cross-ref to the chapter that tells his campaigns in full is warranted. — Heian Japan, Ch 8.

4. **"a relationship not unlike the lord-and-vassal bond taking shape in Europe in the same centuries, though it grew from entirely separate roots"** — A genuine cross-civ comparison is gestured at but left abstract. No completed European-feudalism reference TL is in this audit's set, so this is flagged as an opportunity rather than a fixable gap; if a high-medieval-Europe narrative is available it should be named with a rough date anchor (the European fief-and-vassal system of the 11th–12th centuries).

5. **"It left the emperor exactly where he was ... It did not replace him with a king of a new line, the way Europe did over and over"** — The chapter's own framing invites a comparison the chapter then only sketches ("late Roman emperors propped up by their generals," "the Frankish kings reduced to 'do-nothing' puppets," "the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad reduced to a sacred figurehead"). These three are strong parallels but none is anchored to a date or a reference TL. The Abbasid case in particular is covered in the Islamic Golden Age narrative and could be cross-referenced with the approximate date the caliph became a figurehead under the Buyids/Seljuks (mid-10th to 11th c.).

### Chapter 4 — The Divine Wind

6. **"Kublai Khan ... ruler of the Mongol Empire, which by then was the largest land empire in the history of the world"** — This is exactly the subject of Mongol Empire Ch 6 ("Kublai and the Yuan"), which narrates the same two invasions (1274, 1281), gives the 1281 fleet as "roughly 4,400 ships and 140,000 men ... the largest naval operation in pre-modern history," and the kamikaze. medieval-japan should add a cross-ref so the reader sees the invader's side. — Mongol Empire, Ch 6.

7. **"where he would rule as emperor of a dynasty he named the Yuan"** — The naming, meaning, and Mandate-of-Heaven logic of the Yuan are detailed in Mongol Ch 6 (proclaimed 1271, name from the Yi Jing). A pointer would let the reader follow the China side. — Mongol Empire, Ch 6.

8. **"he had subjugated Korea ... sailed from Korea in the autumn of 1274"** — The invasions were launched from a Korea that the Mongols had ground into a vassal supplier of ships and men; the Korean side is the proximate context for both fleets. medieval-japan treats Korea only as a launch point. A "meanwhile" note that Korea bore the cost of building these fleets (and, three centuries later, would be invaded by Japan in Ch 13) would tie the maritime story together. — (Joseon/Ancient Korea TLs not in this audit set; flag as opportunity.)

9. **"the second invasion, the Kōan invasion ... carrying a combined force that the sources put at something like 140,000 men, very probably the largest seaborne invasion attempted anywhere in the world up to that time"** — This "largest seaborne invasion" superlative is asserted on the Japan side; Mongol Ch 6 makes the identical claim from the invader's side ("the largest naval operation in pre-modern history"). The two TLs should explicitly cross-reference each other on this shared superlative so a reader meeting it in either place is pointed to the other. — Mongol Empire, Ch 6.

10. **"the disciplined enemy formations ... they threw, for the first time in Japanese experience, exploding bombs, primitive gunpowder weapons"** — Mongol Ch 6 describes Yuan gunpowder weaponry (fire-lances at the battle of Yamen, 1279, "the immediate ancestors of firearms") in the same campaign window. The arrival of gunpowder in Japanese experience here is worth anchoring against the Mongols' broader gunpowder deployment, and against the matchlock that arrives in medieval-japan's own Ch 12 — i.e. note that gunpowder reached Japan first as a Mongol weapon in 1274, then again as a Portuguese gun in 1543. — Mongol Empire, Ch 6 (and internal link to medieval-japan Ch 12).

### Chapter 12 — The Black Ships and the Demon King

11. **"the first Europeans, as far as the surviving record shows, ever to set foot in Japan ... the matchlock musket"** — A "first" claim. Worth a brief cross-civ anchor: gunpowder firearms were already centuries old in China (the fire-lances of the Yuan, Mongol Ch 6) and the matchlock was already in use across Europe and the Ottoman/Safavid world — Edo Ch 1 explicitly compares Nagashino to "the Ottomans ... on the Safavids at Chaldiran in 1514" and "the Spanish on Aztec warriors at Tenochtitlan in 1521." medieval-japan should add a one-line global anchor that the gun arrived late at the eastern edge of a long Eurasian gunpowder story. — (Mongol Ch 6 for the Chinese fire-lance origin; the Chaldiran/Tenochtitlan parallels live in Edo Ch 1.)

12. **"Francis Xavier, a Spanish-born Jesuit priest ... came to Japan"** — Xavier's arrival in 1549 is also the opening of Edo Ch 3 ("Sakoku and the Christian Purge"), which carries the fuller Christian-century story (300,000–500,000 converts, the Christian daimyo, Nagasaki as a Christian city, the 1614 expulsion). medieval-japan sets up Christianity but the payoff is in Edo; a cross-ref is warranted. — Edo Japan, Ch 3.

13. **"This was the Nanban trade ... back went Japanese silver, which the warring lords were mining in unprecedented quantities"** — medieval-japan Ch 10 already established the Iwami silver boom as "comparable in its day to the silver flooding out of Spanish America on the other side of the planet." The Nanban-trade silver here is the same silver; a tie-back to Ch 10's global-silver comparison (and an external pointer that this silver fed the same world bullion network as Spanish-American silver) would strengthen the cross-civ thread already present in the document.

### Chapter 13 — The Peasant Who Ruled Japan

14. **"This is the war Koreans call the Imjin War, and it was a catastrophe"** — The narrative already does the right thing here, naming the Joseon Korea narrative and Admiral Yi Sun-sin's turtle ships ("among its proudest chapters"). This is a model cross-ref; flag only that the date anchor (1592 / 1597) and the Ming-China intervention could also be pointed at the Ming-dynasty narrative if one is completed, since the war is a three-party event.

15. **"had twenty-six Christians ... executed by crucifixion at Nagasaki. The Twenty-Six Martyrs"** — This is the seed of the systematic persecution that Edo Ch 3 develops at length (the 1614 edict, fumi-e tests, the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–38, 5,000–6,000 executed). medieval-japan should cross-ref forward to the Edo narrative so the reader sees where the 1597 killings led. — Edo Japan, Ch 3.

### Chapter 14 — The Gambler's Victory

16. **"a narrow valley in central Japan ... called Sekigahara ... Each side fielded somewhere around eighty thousand men"** — Sekigahara is retold in full in Edo Ch 1 ("The Road to Sekigahara"), with the same ~80,000-per-side figure, the same Kobayakawa Hideaki defection, the same noon arquebus-volley signal, and the same outcome. The two accounts are nearly verbatim parallels; medieval-japan must cross-ref the Edo narrative (which continues the story into the Tokugawa peace) so the two are explicitly linked rather than silently duplicative. — Edo Japan, Ch 1.

17. **"Kobayakawa Hideaki ... charged into the flank of the Western Army he was supposed to be fighting for"** — Edo Ch 1 adds a second defector (Wakizaka Yasuharu) and names the loyal ally whose troops Kobayakawa hit (Ōtani Yoshitsugu). medieval-japan's account is slightly thinner on the mechanics; a pointer to Edo Ch 1 would let the reader get the fuller defection cascade. — Edo Japan, Ch 1.

18. **"the world that the black ships had opened ... William Adams the shipwrecked Englishman whom Ieyasu kept as an advisor and even made a samurai"** — Edo Ch 3 gives Adams's arrival precisely (the Dutch ship Liefde wrecked on the Kyushu coast in 1600) and his role as Ieyasu's adviser on Western affairs. A cross-ref avoids two unconnected tellings of the same man. — Edo Japan, Ch 3.

19. **"by the 1630s they had largely sealed the country off from the outside world ... reducing European contact to a single tightly controlled Dutch trading post"** — This is sakoku, the entire subject of Edo Ch 3, which dates the closing edicts to 1612–1639 and names the Dutch post at Nagasaki (Dejima). medieval-japan closes by gesturing at this; it should cross-ref the Edo narrative where the closure is the central story. — Edo Japan, Ch 3.

20. **"the long, locked-down, astonishingly stable peace of Edo Japan ... told in its own narrative (Edo Japan)"** — The narrative already names the Edo Japan narrative as the continuation. This is correct and is the model the other Edo-facing findings (12, 15, 18, 19) should follow; flag only that the single hand-off at the very end could be supplemented by the more specific in-text pointers above so the reader is bridged at each shared event, not just once at the close.

21. **"Sekigahara, on a foggy morning decided by one man's betrayal at noon"** — Edo Ch 1 supplies a cross-civ temporal anchor that medieval-japan lacks: Sekigahara (1600) was contemporary with the founding of the English East India Company, with Akbar of Mughal India, Shah Abbas of Safavid Persia at his height, and the terminal decline of Ming China. medieval-japan's closing chapter would gain from one such "meanwhile, across Eurasia" anchor at the hinge moment, matching the framing Edo Ch 1 already uses. — Edo Japan, Ch 1.

## Part 2: Backward Findings (reference TL gaps)

### heian-japan.md — Chapter 8 — The Genpei War

1. **"This strategic decision — to rule from Kamakura rather than from Kyoto — turned out to be one of the most important institutional choices in Japanese history. The Kamakura shogunate, the Kamakura culture, the Kamakura warrior ethos, all flow from Yoritomo's 1180 decision"** — Heian Ch 8 ends exactly where medieval-japan begins and points forward to "the Kamakura shogunate" abstractly. It should now carry a terse cross-ref to the narrative that tells that whole story. Add a parenthetical such as "(the four-century warrior age that follows is the subject of the Medieval Japan narrative)". — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 1.

2. **"Everything after it belongs to the medieval era of warrior government from outside the Heian system. The chapter between those two eras is what this chapter covers."** — This is the explicit hand-off sentence; it names "the medieval era of warrior government" but does not point at the medieval-japan narrative now that it exists. Add the pointer here. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 1.

3. **"By basing his administration at Kamakura ... the new warrior government would not operate inside the Heian framework. It would be a parallel structure"** — medieval-japan Ch 1 develops this exact insight (the "two capitals, two powers" thesis; shugo/jitō; the bakufu as "tent government"). A cross-ref would let the Heian reader follow the institution forward. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 1.

4. **"He was Yoritomo's half-brother ... Yoshitsune ... one of the most beloved characters in Japanese cultural memory"** — Heian Ch 8 tells Yoshitsune's victories but stops at 1185; his betrayal and death (1189) are narrated in medieval-japan Ch 1 ("Yoshitsune ... in 1189 was cornered and forced to kill himself"). A forward cross-ref would complete the arc for the Heian reader. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 1.

5. **"he married into the Hojo family, a local Izu clan whose senior member, Hojo Tokimasa, would later become his most important supporter"** — Heian Ch 8 plants the Hōjō but does not say they will, within a generation, displace the Minamoto and rule as shikken regents. medieval-japan Ch 2 ("The Power Behind the Power") is the payoff. A terse pointer ("the Hōjō would go on to rule Japan as regents — Medieval Japan Ch 2") would reward the planted detail. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 2.

### mongol-empire.md — Chapter 6 — Kublai and the Yuan

6. **"Kublai sent fleets against Japan twice — in 1274 and 1281 — both of which were destroyed by typhoons that the Japanese called kamikaze ... the kamikaze concept entered Japanese national mythology where it would have, much later, a different and darker afterlife."** — Mongol Ch 6 disposes of both invasions in three sentences and explicitly defers the "darker afterlife" without saying where it is told. medieval-japan Ch 4 ("The Divine Wind") is now the full Japanese-side account — the Hakata Bay wall, the reward crisis, and the WWII kamikaze afterlife. Add a cross-ref. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 4.

7. **"The 1281 invasion was the largest naval operation in pre-modern history: roughly 4,400 ships and 140,000 men, sailing from Korea and southern China."** — This superlative is the same claim medieval-japan Ch 4 makes from the defenders' side ("very probably the largest seaborne invasion attempted anywhere in the world up to that time"). The two should cross-reference so the shared superlative is anchored in both. Note also a figure to reconcile: Mongol Ch 6 gives "roughly 4,400 ships"; medieval-japan gives "two enormous fleets" and the 140,000 men but no ship count — not a contradiction, but the editor may want the ship figure echoed. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 4.

8. **"A typhoon in August 1281 destroyed most of the fleet at sea, drowning tens of thousands."** — Mongol Ch 6 attributes the defeat almost entirely to the typhoon; medieval-japan Ch 4 deliberately corrects this, arguing the wall and the coordinated samurai defense stalled the fleet offshore first and that the 1274 storm in particular is "the part historians most doubt." The reference TL's clean "destroyed by typhoons" framing is exactly the legend medieval-japan pushes back on. A cross-ref to medieval-japan Ch 4 would let the Mongol reader see the defenders' more skeptical account. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 4.

9. **"Möngke Khan ... the Yassa's principle of tolerance ... no religion was systematically persecuted"** / **"Kublai ... concluded that the only sustainable way to govern China was as a Chinese-style emperor"** — Mongol Ch 6's portrait of Kublai as a Sinicizing, cosmopolitan ruler is the same Kublai who, in medieval-japan Ch 4, addresses Japan "from the 'Emperor of Great Mongolia' to the 'King of Japan'" demanding tributary submission. The "King of Japan" tribute logic resurfaces in medieval-japan Ch 7 when Yoshimitsu accepts that very title from Ming China. A pointer noting that the tributary demand Kublai made of Japan in 1268 was the same framework a Japanese shogun would later accept from China would enrich the Mongol account. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 4 (and Ch 7).

### edo-japan.md — Chapter 1 — The Road to Sekigahara

10. **"the period the Japanese call the Sengoku Jidai ... covered in the closing chapters of the Ancient Japan TL."** — STALE / WRONG cross-ref. The Sengoku unification is now covered in full by medieval-japan Chapters 9–14, not the "Ancient Japan TL" (which the corpus split into prehistoric/asuka-nara/heian). This pointer should be retargeted to the medieval-japan narrative. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 9–14.

11. **"Oda Nobunaga ... by the time of his assassination in 1582 controlled roughly half of Japan ... At the Battle of Nagashino in 1575, he stationed three thousand arquebusiers in rotating volley fire"** — Edo Ch 1 retells Nobunaga, Nagashino, and the Honnō-ji Incident in summary; medieval-japan Ch 12 now tells all three in full (and notably *qualifies* the "three thousand arquebusiers / rotating volley" picture as "partly later embroidery ... stylized rather than literal"). Edo Ch 1 states the three-thousand/three-rank figure flatly. A cross-ref to medieval-japan Ch 12 is warranted, and the editor should note the two TLs characterize the rotating-volley claim differently (Edo asserts it; medieval-japan cautions against it). — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 12.

12. **"Toyotomi Hideyoshi ... Born into a peasant family with no surname ... by 1590 had completed the unification of Japan ... took the older imperial-court title of kampaku ... confiscated swords ... in the Sword Hunt of 1588"** — The entire Hideyoshi paragraph in Edo Ch 1 is a compression of medieval-japan Ch 13 ("The Peasant Who Ruled Japan"). Add a cross-ref so the Edo reader can get the full ascent (Yamazaki, Osaka Castle, Sen no Rikyū, the land survey, the class freeze). — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 13.

13. **"Hideyoshi's Korean invasions (Imjin War, 1592–98) are covered in detail in Ancient Korea Ch 8 and the Joseon Korea TL."** — Edo Ch 1 routes the Korean-invasion cross-ref only to the Korea-side narratives. Now that medieval-japan Ch 13 tells the invasions from the Japanese side (Yi Sun-sin's turtle ships, the Ming intervention, the carrying-off of Korean potters), Edo Ch 1 should also point to medieval-japan Ch 13 for the Japanese perspective. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 13.

14. **"What decided it was treachery. Two western daimyo, Kobayakawa Hideaki and Wakizaka Yasuharu ... Around noon, Ieyasu fired arquebus volleys at Kobayakawa's stationary force"** — Edo Ch 1 and medieval-japan Ch 14 narrate the identical decisive moment at Sekigahara. Since medieval-japan Ch 14 is the closing chapter of the age and Edo Ch 1 is the opening of the sequel, they should cross-reference each other directly at this shared hinge. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 14.

15. **"the Ashikaga shogunate (ah-shee-KAH-gah, the dynasty that had held the office since 1336) had collapsed into civil war"** — Edo Ch 1 names the Ashikaga collapse in one clause; medieval-japan Chapters 6–9 are the full account of the Ashikaga rise, golden age, and collapse in the Ōnin War. A pointer would let the Edo reader follow the dynasty's whole story. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 6 (rise) and Ch 9 (collapse).

16. **"castles like Himeji (HEE-meh-jee) and Matsumoto"** — Edo Ch 1 name-drops Himeji; medieval-japan Ch 14 tells Himeji's rebuilding (~1601, the "White Heron") as the architectural monument that closes the age. A terse cross-ref would connect the castle's mention to its story. — Cross-ref target: medieval-japan Ch 14.
