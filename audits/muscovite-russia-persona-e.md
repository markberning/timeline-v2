## Part 1: Forward Findings (new TL gaps)

### Chapter 1 — The World the Mongols Broke

1. **"the most effective military machine the medieval world ever produced"** — The Mongol army is introduced here with no cross-reference to the dedicated Mongol Empire narrative, which covers the same force, its origins under Temüjin/Genghis, and its conquest method in full. A terse pointer ("the steppe army whose rise is the subject of the Mongol Empire narrative") would anchor the reader. — mongol-empire Ch 1–3.

2. **"the largest contiguous land empire in human history, stretching eventually from Korea to the gates of Europe"** — This is effectively a "first/biggest" claim that the Mongol Empire narrative develops at length, including the four-khanate split. The reader gets the claim with no signpost that the full story exists. — mongol-empire Ch 3–4 (the empire's extent and the ulus division).

3. **"a Franciscan friar named **Giovanni da Pian del Carpine**"** and **"He is the first western European to leave a detailed eyewitness account of the Mongols"** — The kievan-rus narrative cites the same envoy (there called "John of Plano Carpini") describing the same skull-littered ruins of Kyiv in 1246. Two completed narratives now quote the identical source on the identical scene; the muscovite text should note the Kievan side covers Kyiv's destruction directly. — kievan-rus Ch 12.

4. **"the brilliant river-civilization of Kievan Rus' (the subject of the previous book)"** — Good that it points back, but the "first stone fortress / brilliant culture" framing of what was lost (Saint Sophia, the Golden Gate, birchbark literacy, the Primary Chronicle) is never characterized concretely; a one-line nod to what Kyiv actually *was* at its height would let the reader feel the scale of the loss the chapter rests on. — kievan-rus Ch 7 and Ch 10.

5. **"the Mongols, for their own reasons we will come to, had no interest in destroying it and good reasons to protect it"** — The Church's tax exemption is presented as a Russian/Horde story, but the Mongol narrative frames it as the Yassa's general religious-tolerance principle applied to every conquered faith (Buddhist, Nestorian, Muslim, Orthodox alike). A comparison would show this was Mongol policy everywhere, not a special Russian deal. — mongol-empire Ch 7 ("the Yassa's religious-tolerance principle, applied in practice").

### Chapter 2 — Living Under the Yoke

6. **"This state is the one this book calls the **Golden Horde**"** and **"the word *horde* comes from the Mongol *ordu*"** — Both the Mongol narrative and this one independently gloss "Golden Horde / *Altan Ordu* / *ordu*." The Mongol narrative also explains the Horde was the *ulus* of Jochi, Genghis's eldest son — origin detail this chapter omits. Cross-reference so the reader knows the Horde's place inside the larger empire. — mongol-empire Ch 4 (Jochi's ulus) and Ch 7 (the "Golden Tent" etymology).

7. **"its rulers and its people converted to Islam"** — Stated as a vague drift. The Mongol narrative dates and mechanizes it precisely: personal conversion of Berke c. 1252, made state policy under Öz Beg (r. 1313–1341), who forced the noble class, with intermarriage as the mechanism. This chapter should anchor the conversion to those reigns/dates. — mongol-empire Ch 7–8.

8. **"the galling part... is that the Rus' princes were made to help *suppress* those revolts"** (the 1257 census) — The Mongol census-and-tax system is treated as a Russian phenomenon. The Mongol narrative ties the Russian census to the empire-wide administrative apparatus (census-based taxation, the post-relay system) borrowed from China and applied across all khanates. A "meanwhile" note that this was the same machinery running from China to Persia would be illuminating. — mongol-empire Ch 9 (Russian state borrowed "census-based taxation system and the use of post-relay messengers").

9. **"helped enforce the hated census against rebellious Russian towns, including his own Novgorod"** (Alexander Nevsky) — kievan-rus Ch 12 tells the identical Nevsky story (Neva 1240, Battle on the Ice 1242, submission to the Horde, enforcing the census) and explicitly frames the saint-vs-collaborator debate. The two narratives now cover the same man with the same paradox; muscovite should cross-reference that the Kievan side opens this debate. — kievan-rus Ch 12.

10. **"the Mongols, for their own reasons... a charter (a *yarlyk* of its own) exempting the Church's lands... from tribute"** (1267) — The Mongol narrative makes the same point about Church tax-exemption fueling Moscow's eventual rise; the muscovite version should note the conqueror's side covers this as deliberate Horde policy. — mongol-empire Ch 7.

11. **"the claim that the Mongols *invented* Russian autocracy is now treated with deep suspicion"** — This is a strong, debate-aware passage, but the Mongol narrative comes down somewhat differently — it states Russian administrative practice "owed at least as much to Sarai as to Constantinople" and that Russian words for treasury/customs/money are Turkic-Mongol. The two narratives are in mild tension on how much Russia borrowed; muscovite should acknowledge the loan-word/administrative-borrowing argument even while rejecting the "Asiatic despotism" caricature. — mongol-empire Ch 9.

### Chapter 3 — The Rise of the Moneybag

12. **"Ivan Kalita's response was to ride to Sarai... and then *lead a Mongol army back to devastate Tver*"** (1327) — Both kievan-rus Ch 12 AND mongol-empire Ch 7 narrate this exact episode (Tver kills the tax party; Ivan Kalita leads the punitive army; is rewarded the yarlyk and tribute-collection right) as the hinge of Moscow's rise. Three narratives now converge on 1327; muscovite should note the conqueror's-side and Kievan-side treatments. — mongol-empire Ch 7 and kievan-rus Ch 12.

13. **"the **Black Death**, the great plague pandemic that swept out of Asia... It reached the Russian lands by about 1352"** — The Mongol narrative tells the plague's origin and route in detail (Caffa 1346, the Janibeg siege, the Genoese galleys), and notes the Golden Horde's own territories were severely hit. The muscovite "carried up the same trade routes that carried silk and silver" begs for a cross-reference to that fuller account, including that the Horde was the corridor. — mongol-empire Ch 8.

14. **"the same pandemic that was emptying the cities of Italy and the towns of England"** — Good instinct toward a "meanwhile," but the most directly relevant parallel — the Golden Horde, Russia's own overlord, being ravaged by the same plague that came through Caffa — is the one the corpus already covers and this passage misses. — mongol-empire Ch 8.

### Chapter 4 — Kulikovo and the Idea of Resistance

15. **"the Golden Horde... had fallen into a long internal crisis the Russians called the *velikaya zamyatnya*, the 'great troubles'"** — The Mongol narrative names and dates this same crisis ("the period after 1359 — what Russian historians call the **Velikaya Zamyatnya**... twenty different Golden Horde khans in twenty years"). Two narratives gloss the identical term; a cross-reference would let the reader see the Horde's collapse from the steppe side. — mongol-empire Ch 8.

16. **"the Central Asian world-conqueror **Timur** (also called Tamerlane) would smash Tokhtamysh and devastate the Horde's cities"** — Timur is a major figure with his own narrative (and the Mongol narrative notes the Chagatai zone "produced new dynasties (Timurid, Moghul) that claimed Genghisid descent"). Introduced here with no signpost that his story is told elsewhere. — mongol-empire Ch 9 (Timurid successor states).

17. **"as Kiswahili distilled an African language out of borrowed Arabic words"** — A cross-civ comparison is *made* here (Rublev/Russian art remaking a Byzantine inheritance, likened to Swahili and to Russian architecture). This is exactly the kind of comparison the house style wants, and it is well done — but the Byzantine source of Russian icon-painting (Theophanes the Greek carrying the late-Byzantine style north) could also point at the Byzantine Empire narrative, which covers that artistic tradition at its source. — Byzantine Empire narrative (not in the supplied reference set; flag for whoever holds the cross-link map).

### Chapter 5 — The Gathering of the Lands

18. **"In 1395 the Central Asian conqueror **Timur**... fell on the Golden Horde and wrecked it"** — Same Timur cross-reference gap as Ch 4; the wrecking of the Horde and its fragmentation into Kazan/Astrakhan/Crimea/Great Horde is also covered by the Mongol narrative's account of the Horde's successor khanates. — mongol-empire Ch 9 ("the Golden Horde fragmented into successor khanates (Crimean, Kazan, Astrakhan, Sibir)").

19. **"In 1453 the Ottoman Turks took Constantinople"** — A pivotal "meanwhile" — the event that makes Moscow the last free Orthodox power — is told from the conqueror's side in the Ottoman Empire narrative and the victim's side in the Byzantine Empire narrative. No cross-reference to either. — Ottoman Empire / Byzantine Empire narratives (not in the supplied reference set; flag for the cross-link map).

20. **"**lateral** or **collateral succession** — the throne passed not from father to son but along a generation"** — This is the same Rurikid succession problem (the "ladder"/*rota* system) that kievan-rus Ch 8 ("The Ladder That Fell Down") explains as the structural flaw that fragmented Kievan Rus'. The muscovite chapter calls it "inherited from the Kievan world" but does not point the reader to the Kievan narrative that anatomizes it. — kievan-rus Ch 8.

### Chapter 6 — Ivan the Great and the Third Rome

21. **"In **1472 Ivan III married Sophia Palaiologina**... the niece of Constantine XI"** — russian-empire Ch 1 covers this exact marriage ("in 1472 the Moscow grand prince Ivan III had married Zoe (Sophia) Palaiologina, a niece of the last Byzantine emperor") as the dynastic prop of the Third Rome claim. The sequel narrative already cross-references muscovite ground here; muscovite is the fuller account and should be aware the link runs both ways. — russian-empire Ch 1.

22. **"the **Great Stand on the Ugra River**, is conventionally taken as the end of the 'Tatar yoke'"** (1480) — Both the Mongol narrative and russian-empire give 1480 as the end-date of the Yoke ("the Mongol period (1237 to roughly 1480...)"). The muscovite account is the only one that narrates the bloodless standoff itself; it should note that the conqueror's-side narrative tracks the Horde's disintegration that made the Ugra possible. — mongol-empire Ch 9; russian-empire Ch 1.

23. **"In **1497 Ivan issued the Sudebnik**... St George's Day... the first legal restriction on the freedom of the Russian peasant"** — russian-empire Ch 1 traces serfdom's legal ancestry back through exactly this chain ("the end of a process whose legal ancestors reach back to the *kholopy* (slaves) and tribute-owing peasants of Kievan Rus'"). kievan-rus Ch 10 documents the *kholopy*/*smerdy*/*zakupy* ranks. The muscovite serfdom thread should point back to the Kievan-era unfreedom it builds on. — kievan-rus Ch 10; russian-empire Ch 1/7.

24. **"a monk named **Philotheus of Pskov**... Moscow was the **third Rome**"** — The Third Rome doctrine is independently summarized in russian-empire Ch 1 and referenced in mongol-empire Ch 9. The muscovite treatment is by far the most skeptical and complete; it is the source-of-record for the corpus, and the sequel should defer to it (see Backward findings). — russian-empire Ch 1.

### Chapter 7 — The First Tsar

25. **"**Tsar** (zar) is the Russian form of 'Caesar'... carried east through Byzantium"** (1547 coronation) — russian-empire Ch 1 and mongol-empire Ch 9 both gloss the same "Tsar = Caesar, claiming the Byzantine inheritance" point. The muscovite narrative is where the coronation actually happens; the others reference it. Worth a note that Ivan IV's 1547 coronation is the moment the title becomes formal. — russian-empire Ch 1; mongol-empire Ch 9.

26. **"The targets were the **khanates of Kazan and Astrakhan**, two Muslim Tatar states on the Volga River that were direct successors of the old Golden Horde"** — The Mongol narrative explicitly lists these as the Horde's fragments (Crimean, Kazan, Astrakhan, Sibir). The muscovite "the descendants of the conquered had become the conquerors" reversal is a powerful cross-civ beat that should point at the Mongol narrative's account of how those khanates came to exist. — mongol-empire Ch 9.

27. **"In **1553 an English expedition** under the navigator **Richard Chancellor**... the English **Muscovy Company**"** — A genuine trade/contact event (Russia's first direct link to Western Europe). No completed reference TL covers the English side, so no cross-reference is owed — but flag that this is the kind of contact point a future England/Tudor narrative should pick up.

### Chapter 8 — The Terror

28. **"In **1571 the Crimean Tatars** — a Muslim khanate to the south, a fragment of the old Golden Horde and a client of the Ottoman Empire — ... **burned Moscow to the ground**"** — The Crimean Khanate is named by both the Mongol narrative (a Horde successor) and russian-empire Ch 2 (the Crimean Tatars raiding for slaves, "tens of thousands a year"). The muscovite burning-of-Moscow should cross-reference the Crimean slave-raiding economy that russian-empire develops and the Horde-fragment origin the Mongol narrative supplies. — mongol-empire Ch 9; russian-empire Ch 2.

29. **"In **1582**... a band of **Cossacks**... led by a chieftain named **Yermak**... defeated the **Khanate of Sibir**"** — The Khanate of Sibir is the same Horde successor the Mongol narrative lists. The conquest of Siberia is also picked up by russian-empire Ch 17 ("Siberia"). A pointer that the eastward expansion begun here is carried forward in the sequel, and that Sibir was a Horde fragment, would tie the threads. — mongol-empire Ch 9; russian-empire Ch 17.

30. **"his exhumed remains showed high mercury levels"** — A suspiciously specific modern-forensic detail (handle as a Persona-B matter too), but cross-culturally fine; no comparison owed.

### Chapter 9 — The Dynasty Burns Out

### Chapter 10 — The Time of Troubles

34. **"the **Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth**, the vast and aggressively Catholic state that bordered Russia to the west and had been its rival for the old lands of Kievan Rus' for centuries"** — kievan-rus Ch 12 explains how the western Rus' lands (Galicia-Volhynia, Kyiv itself) fell to Lithuania and then Poland, producing the Ukraine/Belarus divergence. The muscovite "rival for the old lands of Kievan Rus'" claim should point at that Kievan account of how Poland-Lithuania came to hold them. — kievan-rus Ch 12.

Add a pointer so the reader who wants the *how* of serfdom's creation is sent to the prequel. — cross-ref target: muscovite-russia Ch 11.

3. **"Russian churchmen developed the idea that Moscow was the 'Third Rome'... in 1472 the Moscow grand prince Ivan III had married Zoe (Sophia) Palaiologina"** — muscovite-russia Ch 6 now gives the most complete *and most skeptical* treatment of the Third Rome doctrine in the corpus (a couple of lines in a monk's letter, inflated by later writers). russian-empire states the Third Rome flatly without the skepticism. It should cross-reference muscovite-russia Ch 6 so the two are not in tension on how much weight the doctrine carried. — cross-ref target: muscovite-russia Ch 6.
