### The mass surrender figure — scholarly range
- **Primary figure cited:** 12,392 captured or missing (Wikipedia infobox; American Battlefield Trust)
- **Tennessee Encyclopedia:** "12,000–15,000 prisoners"
- **Britannica:** "approximately 15,000 men captured" (higher end; possibly includes materiel account conflation)

- **NPS (Tennessee Encyclopedia source):** Grant's own report cited "12,000–15,000 prisoners, 20,000 stands of arms, 48 artillery pieces, 17 heavy guns, ~3,000 horses, and large commissary stores" — Tennessee Encyclopedia
- **Scholarly consensus range: approximately 12,000–13,500 captured** (the 15,000 figure appears in some accounts and may reflect Grant's initial field estimate; the 12,392 figure is the most precisely sourced)
- **Context:** This was the largest mass surrender on the North American continent to that point in history — Wikipedia notes the Confederate losses here "more than [Johnston captured] in any previous battle"
- **Camp Douglas first shipment:** About 8,000 of the Fort Donelson prisoners were Camp Douglas's (Chicago) first arrivals — Tennessee State Library and Archives exhibit

## 3. What Came Before

### The western rivers strategy
- The Tennessee and Cumberland Rivers penetrate deep into the Confederate heartland, running roughly parallel, ~10 miles apart at the fort sites — they were natural invasion highways
- Gen. Henry W. Halleck (Department of Missouri) authorized Grant's campaign against the twin river forts
- Confederate Gen. Albert Sidney Johnston's western line ran Columbus (KY) → Bowling Green (KY), anchored by the river forts: Fort Henry on the Tennessee, Fort Donelson on the Cumberland
- Johnston had ~12,000 under Leonidas Polk at Columbus and ~14,000 under William J. Hardee at Bowling Green — Wikipedia

### Fort Henry falls (February 6, 1862)
- Ten days before Donelson, Foote's gunboat flotilla alone reduced Fort Henry on the Tennessee River; the Confederate garrison (approx. 3,400 men) mostly escaped before the army could invest — Wikipedia
- Fort Henry's fall proved the power of naval artillery on these rivers and shook Confederate confidence
- Approx. 2,500 Confederate troops from Fort Henry escaped overland to Fort Donelson, reinforcing its garrison — Wikipedia
- The Tennessee River was now open to Union gunboats all the way into Alabama

### Grant's overland march (February 11–13)
- Grant moved his force ~12 miles overland from Fort Henry to Fort Donelson — History.com
- February 12: Union forces encountered Confederate cavalry under Forrest in skirmishes — Wikipedia
- Light probing attacks occurred February 13 — Wikipedia

### The weather — a critical variable
- An unseasonably warm spell preceded the battle (men discarded overcoats, slowing resupply)
- The night of February 13–14 a severe snowstorm arrived, dropping temperatures to 10–12°F (–12°C) with ~3 inches of snow — Wikipedia
- Wounded men left on the field overnight froze; this is a documented horror of the battle
- Sources: Wikipedia; American Battlefield Trust

## 4. The Battle

### The fort's layout (relevant to battle sequence)
- Fort Donelson was an earthen fortification built summer 1861 — Tennessee Encyclopedia
- Two tiers of water batteries on the river bluff: **lower battery** (closest to waterline, contained a 10-inch Columbiad) and an **upper battery** — NPS river batteries page
- Upper battery armament: one 64-pounder rifled Columbiad and two 32-pounder carronades — NPS/American Civil War forums
- The batteries sat on elevated ground above the Cumberland's west bank, with a strong downstream current and flooding adding difficulty for the gunboats — NPS river batteries page
- Land defenses: semicircular earthwork trenches roughly 3 miles long, protecting the landward approaches; the town of Dover sat inside the perimeter — Wikipedia/ABT

### Foote's gunboat attack is REPULSED (February 14)
- Foote brought his four ironclads (*St. Louis*, *Carondelet*, *Louisville*, *Pittsburg*) plus two timberclads (*Tyler*, *Conestoga*) — NPS/Wikipedia
- Attack began approximately 3:00 p.m., lasted ~1.5 hours, ending by ~4:30 p.m. — Wikipedia
- Foote used the same close-in tactics that succeeded at Fort Henry, closing to within ~400 yards — NPS river batteries page
- This proved a fatal error: the elevated Confederate water batteries had plunging fire advantage; the current made maneuvering difficult — NPS
- Confederate artillery inflicted severe damage:
  - USS *St. Louis*: hit 59 times — Wikipedia
  - USS *Carondelet*: hit 54 times — Wikipedia
  - USS *Louisville*: hit 36 times — Wikipedia
  - USS *Pittsburg*: hit 20 times — Wikipedia
- Two gunboats became unmanageable and drifted away; the entire flotilla retreated downriver — NPS
- **Flag Officer Foote was wounded** (ankle wound from a rifle shot or shell fragment; accounts vary) — Wikipedia; ABT
- Confederate water batteries: **zero casualties** during the gunboat engagement — Wikipedia
- **Key reversal:** Fort Henry had fallen to the gunboats alone; Fort Donelson's water batteries beat the ironclads — this forced Grant to plan a pure ground assault
- Grant was absent during the gunboat attack, meeting with Foote downriver — Wikipedia

### Grant absent from the field (morning of February 15)
- When the Confederate breakout began, Grant was downriver meeting with the wounded Foote to coordinate resupply — Wikipedia
- He left no designated second-in-command; division commanders acted on their own — Wikipedia

### Confederate breakout attempt (February 15)
- **The previous evening (Feb 14):** Confederate council of war at ~11:00 p.m. agreed the fort was "probably untenable" — Wikipedia
- Plan: Pillow to lead main assault southward to seize Wynn's Ferry Road (escape route toward Nashville); Buckner to hold the left; Floyd to coordinate
- **~5 a.m.:** Forrest's cavalry created a diversionary skirmish to the north — Civil War Months
- **~7 a.m.:** Pillow launched full assault on McClernand's division (Union right/south flank) — Civil War Months
- By midday/early afternoon, Confederates had pushed McClernand back approximately 1–2 miles — Wikipedia; Civil War Months
- **The escape road was open.** Union right had been rolled up; Wynn's Ferry Road was accessible
- **~1:00–1:30 p.m.:** Pillow ordered the assault troops to withdraw back into the trenches — Wikipedia; Civil War Months
- Pillow's stated reasoning: troops needed to resupply and regroup before the breakout march; he believed the road would remain open — Civil War Months
- Floyd accepted this decision; Buckner protested but was overruled — Civil War Months
- **Pillow later claimed he made no error:** he responded to Floyd's questioning by stating he was "conscious of the commission of no errors" — Civil War Monitor

### Grant returns and counterattacks (February 15, afternoon)
- Grant returned to find his right broken and his commanders dispirited — Wikipedia
- Observing that captured Confederate soldiers carried full knapsacks (3 days' rations packed for marching), Grant deduced the enemy was attempting escape, not a sustained assault — Web search (Civil War Months/Emerging Civil War sources)
- Grant to Col. J.D. Webster of his staff: "The one who attacks first now will be victorious. The enemy will have to be in a hurry if he gets ahead of me." — Emerging Civil War / web search [VERIFY exact wording — multiple versions in circulation]
- Grant ordered simultaneous counterattacks:
  - **C.F. Smith** (left/north): assaulted Confederate outer earthworks — Wikipedia; NPS
  - **Lew Wallace** (center): moved south to support McClernand — Civil War Months
  - **McClernand** (right): rallied and retook morning-lost ground
- Grant's direction to Smith: "All has failed on our right — you must take Fort Donelson." Smith's division captured the outer entrenchments of the 30th Tennessee by evening — NPS; Wikipedia
- By ~5:30 p.m. Union forces had retaken most of the morning-lost ground — Wikipedia
- The trap was back in place; escape no longer viable

## 5. The Surrender

### Night of February 15–16: The council of war
- Confederate commanders conferred; Buckner estimated a 75% casualty rate if Smith's troops attacked the next morning with their newly won position — Wikipedia
- Three options discussed: continued fighting, breakout, or surrender — Civil War Monitor

### Floyd's escape
- Floyd (former U.S. Secretary of War under President Buchanan) feared criminal prosecution for treason and/or corruption in the North if captured — American Battlefield Trust; American Battlefield Trust long article
- Floyd transferred command to Pillow
- Floyd departed on the only available steamer, taking his two Virginia infantry regiments with him — Wikipedia; web search
- Floyd escaped early the morning of February 16 — Wikipedia

### Pillow's escape
- Pillow, reportedly fearing Northern reprisals, accepted command from Floyd — then also escaped
- Pillow passed command to Buckner
- Pillow escaped by small boat across the Cumberland River during the night — Wikipedia; web search

### Forrest's escape
- Col. Nathan Bedford Forrest refused to accept surrender: "I did not come here to surrender my command." — American Battlefield Trust (long article); web search
- Forrest led approximately 700 cavalrymen out through Lick Creek (icy, waist-deep water), skirting the Union left flank — Wikipedia; web search
- They encountered no enemy forces — confirmed escape routes remained open
- **Key implication:** Forrest's successful escape proved many more troops could have escaped had Pillow not ordered the retreat and had Buckner not posted guards to prevent further escape attempts — American Battlefield Trust

### Buckner's surrender request (February 16)
- Buckner, left as ranking officer, sent a note to Grant asking for an armistice and to discuss surrender terms — Wikipedia; ABT

### Grant's reply — the "unconditional surrender" message
**Full text (well-documented):**
> "No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender can be accepted. I propose to move immediately upon your works."
— Ulysses S. Grant, February 16, 1862

Source: American Battlefield Trust; Wikipedia; NPS; History.com — consistent across all sources

**Context of the message:**
- ABT notes Grant did not originate the phrase entirely: Flag Officer Foote had used similar language at Fort Henry; Gen. C.F. Smith told a Confederate emissary "No terms to the damned Rebels!" before Grant composed his reply — ABT "Myth of Unconditional Surrender" article [NOTE: the "Smith first" detail is from a single ABT article; treat as [VERIFY] for editorial use]
- The message was widely reprinted in Northern newspapers and became a sensation

### Buckner's reply
**Paraphrase (widely cited; exact text varies slightly by source):**
Buckner called Grant's terms "ungenerous and unchivalrous" but acknowledged the "overwhelming force" arrayed against him and accepted surrender, noting "the brilliant success of the Confederate arms yesterday" — ABT "Myth" article; Wikipedia

### The "Unconditional Surrender Grant" nickname
- The initials U.S. Grant + "unconditional surrender" aligned perfectly; Northern press ran with it — ABT; Wikipedia
- Grant was inundated with cigars from the Northern public after reports that he'd been seen chewing one during the battle; he reportedly became a regular cigar smoker afterward — Wikipedia

### The Grant–Buckner personal history — verified
- Both attended West Point in the 1840s; served together under Winfield Scott in Mexico, including an expedition up the volcano Popocatépetl in 1847 — Grant Cottage NHS blog
- **The loan:** In 1854, when Grant was destitute in New York City at the Astor House hotel after resigning from the army, Buckner vouched for him / covered his hotel bill until Grant's funds arrived — Grant Cottage NHS; ABT "Myth" article
- **At Dover Tavern, February 16:** After the surrender, Grant offered his personal purse to Buckner; accounts differ on whether Buckner accepted — ABT "Myth" article [VERIFY: whether Buckner accepted; Grant Cottage says "declined"; ABT says "accounts differ"]
- Grant refused to hold a formal surrender ceremony — NPS Grant at Donelson article
- Post-war: both men remained friends; Buckner served as a pallbearer at Grant's funeral in 1885 and provided Grant's widow a monthly stipend — Grant Cottage NHS

## 7. What It Meant

### Immediate military consequences
- **Nashville abandoned:** The Confederate government and military evacuated Nashville within days; Nashville fell February 23, 1862 — Wikipedia; Britannica; History.com
- **Nashville = first Confederate state capital to fall** in the war — standard in all sources
- **The Columbus–Bowling Green defensive line collapsed:** Polk evacuated Columbus March 2; the entire Confederate western cordon was gone — Wikipedia
- **Tennessee opened:** Union armies now had water highway access deep into the Confederate heartland
- **Johnston's reputation was gravely damaged** — Britannica
- **European recognition:** The Confederacy's bid for British/French recognition required demonstration of military viability; Fort Donelson was a serious blow to that case

### Grant's rise
- Promoted to Major General of Volunteers — became second in seniority only to Halleck in the West — Wikipedia
- Lincoln famously responded to calls to fire Grant after later controversies: "I can't spare this man; he fights" [VERIFY: not specifically a Fort Donelson quote — associated with Shiloh, not Donelson]
- Grant rocketed from relative obscurity to national fame — all sources

### Morale and the wider war
- First major Union victory of the war (first significant ground victory) — History.com; ABT; Britannica
- The North had been starved for good news; the victory was celebrated wildly
- **The slavery stakes:** The rivers strategy aimed to split, strangle, and defeat a Confederacy fighting to preserve slavery — the institution that Johnston's western line was defending. Fort Donelson cracked that line open.

### Prisoner scale — historical benchmark
- Largest mass surrender on the continent to that point — widely stated in sources
- Previous largest: approx. 5,000 Americans captured at Saratoga / comparable Revolutionary War events — [VERIFY exact prior record; standard claim, not yet pinned to a specific comparison source]

## 10. Contested / Uncertain Facts

### 1. Number of Confederates captured (CONTESTED — flag for author)
- Most precise figure: **12,392** captured or missing — Wikipedia infobox, ABT fact sheet
- Range in scholarship: **12,000–15,000**
- Britannica uses ~15,000; this may reflect Grant's own field report estimate or include some double-counting with those who escaped before the surrender
- Tennessee Encyclopedia cites Grant's report as giving 12,000–15,000
- The number 13,500 appears in some popular accounts as a midpoint
- **Recommend:** use "roughly 12,000–13,500" as the defensible range, noting ~12,400 as the most precisely sourced figure; acknowledge 15,000 appears in some sources

### 2. Floyd/Pillow command handoff — sequence (PARTIALLY CONTESTED)
- Wikipedia and most sources: Floyd transferred command to Pillow, who then transferred to Buckner; Floyd escaped first; Pillow escaped separately
- Civil War Monitor: Floyd transferred to Pillow who passed to Buckner "before escaping by riverboat"; Floyd "also escaped by boat with several regiments"
- The exact chronology of who formally handed off and in what order is murky — both men were trying to leave, and their actions overlapped
- Buckner was clearly left as final ranking officer and negotiated the surrender
- **Contested point:** Did Floyd formally transfer to Pillow who then transferred to Buckner in sequence, or was it more chaotic? Primary sources (both men's post-war accounts) are self-serving

### 3. Why Pillow ordered the retreat (DISPUTED)
- Pillow's stated reason: troops needed resupply and reorganization before the march; believed the road would stay open — Civil War Months
- Critics (including Buckner at the time): a catastrophic blunder that threw away a won escape
- Pillow maintained "I committed no errors" — Civil War Monitor
- Whether this was incompetence, miscommunication, or timidity remains debated by historians

### 4. Whether C.F. Smith "said first" what Grant later wrote (LOW CONFIDENCE)
- ABT "Myth" article claims Smith told a Confederate emissary "No terms to the damned Rebels!" before Grant wrote his message, and that Foote had used similar language at Fort Henry
- This detail is in one ABT article; not corroborated in Wikipedia or NPS sources reviewed
- **Flag as [VERIFY] for the author; do not assert without a primary source**

### 5. Whether Buckner accepted Grant's offered money at Dover Tavern (UNCERTAIN)
- Grant Cottage NHS: Buckner "declined" Grant's offered money
- ABT: "accounts differ"
- NPS Grant at Donelson: does not mention the money detail at all
- The 1854 loan itself (Buckner covering Grant's hotel bill at the Astor House) is consistent across multiple sources — treat as established

### 6. Grant's "first who attacks" quote — exact wording (VERIFY)
- The substance is documented (multiple secondary sources); the exact wording varies
- Should be checked against Grant's *Personal Memoirs* (1885) for the authoritative text before quoting directly in prose

### 7. "Largest mass surrender on the continent to that point" — prior record
- The claim itself is standard and consistent across sources
- The specific comparison event (what it surpassed) is not pinned in the web sources reviewed
