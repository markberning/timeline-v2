# FACT PACK — Siege of Savannah, September 16 – October 18, 1779 (assault October 9)

*American Revolution (the southern turn). ONLY source for the battle page. The southern-strategy opener: Britain pivots south betting on a Loyalist rising, a French fleet races the hurricane clock up from the Caribbean, and one of the most diverse armies of the entire war — French regulars, over 500 free men of color from Saint-Domingue, Continentals, militia, Pulaski's international legion — is slaughtered in under an hour at the Spring Hill redoubt. Confidence tags: HIGH / MED / DISPUTED; HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags inline.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **Siege of Savannah (also "Second Battle of Savannah"), September 16 – October 18, 1779; the grand assault October 9, 1779** — Savannah, Georgia (32.081,-81.091), on the bluff above the Savannah River. [HIGH]
- **Result: British victory.** The allies lifted the siege; Savannah stayed British until July 1782. [HIGH]
- **The framing [HIGH]:** This is the battle that locked in Britain's southern strategy. Ten months after a small British force took Savannah almost for free (Dec 1778), a Franco-American army of 7,000-8,000 — backed by more than 20 French ships of the line — tried to take it back and was bloodily repulsed. The October 9 assault cost the allies roughly five casualties for every British one; Clinton called the news "the greatest event that has happened the whole war," and within months he sailed south himself and took Charleston (May 1780), the worst American defeat of the war. The southern war the British wanted now had its theater.
- **Signature elements:** (1) **the most diverse allied army of the war** — French line regiments (including Dillon's Irish regiment in French service), the **Chasseurs-Volontaires de Saint-Domingue** (~545 free men of color and enslaved volunteers from the French colony that is now Haiti — by standard accounts the largest unit of soldiers of African descent to serve on either side in the war), South Carolina and Georgia Continentals and militia, Pulaski's legion of European volunteers, a Swedish count and a Polish general among the column leaders; (2) **the 24-hour stall** — d'Estaing summoned the town in the name of the King of France, Prevost asked for 24 hours "to consider," and in those hours Lt. Col. John Maitland brought ~800 men through the coastal creeks and marshes from Beaufort to make the garrison defensible; (3) **the Spring Hill redoubt** — a deserter betrayed the assault plan, the columns tangled in fog and swamp, and the attack died on the abatis: **Casimir Pulaski mortally wounded, Sergeant William Jasper mortally wounded with the colors, d'Estaing hit twice.** [HIGH on all three as events; care-notes below]
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — superlatives:** "one of the bloodiest hours of the war for the allies" is safe; do NOT rank it precisely ("second-bloodiest battle of the war" circulates without a stable basis). [Framing guidance]
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — the Haitian beats:** the Chasseurs' *documented* record and the *traditional* record differ (Sec. 4 & 6). Tell it dignified and precise: the documented floor is already remarkable; don't inflate it with the un-documented ceiling.

## 2. BACKGROUND
### Britain pivots south [HIGH]
- After Saratoga (Oct 1777) and France's entry (treaties Feb 1778, war from summer 1778), Britain's war went global — the Caribbean sugar islands, Gibraltar, India all competed with America for ships and troops. London's new plan: hold New York, shift the offensive to the South. Colonial Secretary **Lord George Germain** told Clinton the recovery of the southern colonies was "considered by [the King] as an object of great importance in the scale of the war." [HIGH; quote attested via the Capture-of-Savannah literature]
- The theory: the South was believed thick with Loyalists who would rise and hold reconquered territory with minimal British garrisons; southern exports (rice, indigo, tobacco) funded the rebellion's European purchases; taking Savannah and Charleston would roll the rebellion up from the bottom. The British also recruited enslaved African Americans with promises of freedom for service (formalized in Clinton's Philipsburg Proclamation, June 1779). [HIGH on the strategy; standard]
### The capture of Savannah, December 29, 1778 [HIGH]
- Lt. Col. **Archibald Campbell** sailed from New York (Nov 26, 1778) with ~3,500 men — 71st Highlanders, Hessians, Loyalist battalions — reached Tybee Island Dec 23, and on Dec 29 attacked Maj. Gen. **Robert Howe's** ~850 Continentals and militia south of Savannah. A local enslaved man showed Campbell a path through the swamp around the American right (the name **Quamino Dolly** appears throughout the secondary literature [MED on the name; HIGH that an enslaved guide showed the path]); 600 British troops emerged behind the line, and Howe's force was routed — ~83 killed, ~453 captured, ~30 drowned in Yamacraw Creek, against 7 British killed. Campbell reportedly took the city at a cost of barely two dozen casualties and quipped about ripping a star and stripe from the rebel flag [paraphrase only — the exact "first stripe" quote NOT verified this session; don't quote]. [HIGH on the action and figures]
- **Augustine Prevost** marched up from British East Florida, took overall command (Jan 1779), took Sunbury; Campbell took Augusta (Jan 31). Royal governor James Wright returned (July 1779) — **Georgia became the only rebel state where royal government was actually restored**. [HIGH]
- 1779 seesawed: Patriot win at Kettle Creek (Feb 14), British win at Briar Creek (Mar 3); in May Prevost raided to the gates of Charleston; on June 20 Lincoln attacked the British rear guard under **Maitland at Stono Ferry** and was repulsed — the same 71st Highlanders who would hold Spring Hill. [HIGH, context level]
### Why a French fleet was off Georgia [HIGH]
- **Charles Henri, comte d'Estaing** — a soldier-noble turned admiral — had had a frustrating American debut in 1778 (arrived too late off New York, the Newport operation wrecked by storm), then sailed for the Caribbean, where in summer 1779 he took St. Vincent and Grenada and fought Byron's fleet off Grenada (July 6) to a bloody draw that left the British mauled. [HIGH]
- Appeals from Charleston (Gen. Lincoln, South Carolina's government, the French consul) begged him to strike the British in Georgia before returning to France. He came with **20-25 ships of the line and roughly 4,000-5,000 troops**, appearing off the Georgia coast in the first days of September 1779 (news reached Charleston Sept 3; the fleet stood off Tybee ~Sept 8). [HIGH; troop totals vary 3,500-5,500 across sources — use "about 4,000"]
- **The clock [HIGH]:** it was hurricane season; the fleet lay exposed on an open coast; scurvy and dysentery were spreading aboard; supplies were short; and d'Estaing had orders to bring the fleet home. He initially budgeted only days ashore. Every decision that follows — the early summons, the impatience with siege approaches, the gamble on a frontal assault — runs on this clock.

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
### Strengths (dossier stats)
- **Allied: ~7,000-8,000** total — French ~4,000+ (including ~545 Chasseurs-Volontaires de Saint-Domingue) landed from the fleet; Americans ~2,000+ (roughly 600-1,000 Continentals plus militia; Pulaski's legion ~200 horse). Wikipedia's compiled figure: 7,722 ground troops + 42 ships. [MED-HIGH; round to "about 7,500"]
- **British: ~3,200-4,800** under Prevost — sources split between ~2,500-3,200 effectives (britishbattles) and 4,813 counting armed sailors, militia, and armed Black auxiliaries (Wikipedia). Over 100 guns in the works. Use "roughly 3,200, swelling toward 4,800 with sailors and militia." [MED — flag the range]
- **Garrison units [HIGH]:** 71st Highlanders (both battalions), grenadiers of the 60th, light infantry, marines and sailors landed from the ships, Hessian regiments (Trümbach/Wissenbach), Loyalist provincials — DeLancey's New York Volunteers, New Jersey Volunteers, South Carolina Royalists, North Carolina Loyalists, Thomas Brown's King's Rangers — and Georgia/Carolina Loyalist militia.
- **Fortification sprint [HIGH]:** Capt. **James Moncrief** (Royal Engineers) threw up a ~1,200-foot arc of earthworks and redoubts around the landward side in about three weeks, using **500-800 enslaved African Americans** working up to twelve hours a day — by the assault, ~13 redoubts and 15 gun batteries. HMS *Rose* was scuttled in the channel (Sept 20) to block the French ships from coming upriver. [HIGH]
### The allied army's diversity [HIGH — lean in, it's the story]
- French line regiments drawn from the Caribbean garrisons (Armagnac, Agénois, Gâtinais, Cambrésis, Foix, Hainaut and others), plus **Dillon's regiment** — Irish Catholics in the French service under Col. **Arthur Dillon**. [HIGH]
- **Chasseurs-Volontaires de Saint-Domingue [HIGH on the documented core]:** raised March-April 1779 in the French sugar colony of Saint-Domingue (modern Haiti) from *gens de couleur libres* (free men of color) and enslaved men promised freedom for service; ~545 served at Savannah. Standard accounts call them **the largest unit of soldiers of African descent to fight in the war on either side** [HIGH as the standard claim]. Officially they were assigned to labor and reserve duty — the French order of battle said they "were only to be used to dig trenches" — but they took casualties under fire, including in the British sortie of Sept 24 (1 killed, 7 wounded documented). See Sec. 4 + 6 for the retreat tradition and the Haitian-Revolution legacy. [HIGH]
- **Pulaski's Legion** (~200 cavalry + infantry): Poles, Frenchmen, Germans, Irishmen, Americans — the Continental Army's international cavalry corps. [HIGH]
- Column leaders included a Swedish count (Stedingk), an Irish colonel in French service (Dillon), a Polish general (Pulaski), and Lafayette's brother-in-law (Noailles). [HIGH]
### Allied commander bios (60-90 w material, verified)
- **Charles Henri, comte d'Estaing (1729-1794) [HIGH]:** Auvergne noble who began in the army (wounded and twice a prisoner in India in the Seven Years' War) and crossed to the navy, rising to vice admiral — sailors grumbled at serving under a converted general. Led France's first fleet to America (1778): missed his moment at New York, lost his Newport gamble to a storm, then won real prizes in the Caribbean (St. Vincent, Grenada). Brave to a fault at Savannah — he led the assault columns on foot and was shot twice (arm and leg) [HIGH on two wounds; arm/leg detail MED]. Guillotined in Paris April 28, 1794, after testifying in defense of Marie Antoinette. [HIGH]
- **Benjamin Lincoln (1733-1810) [HIGH]:** Massachusetts farmer and militia officer, made a Continental major general; helped pen Burgoyne at Saratoga, where a musket ball shattered his ankle. Sent south (Sept 1778) to command the Southern Department, he spent 1779 trading blows with Prevost across the Georgia-Carolina line (beaten at Stono Ferry in June). At Savannah he was the junior partner in practice — the fleet, most of the troops, and the clock were French. Seven months later he surrendered Charleston and ~5,000 men, the war's worst American defeat; at Yorktown, exchanged and restored, he formally received the British surrender as Washington's second-in-command.
- **Casimir Pulaski (1745-1779) [HIGH]:** Polish noble who fought Russia in the Bar Confederation uprising; implicated in a plot to abduct Poland's king, he was condemned in absentia and exiled. Benjamin Franklin sent him to Washington as "an officer famous throughout Europe for his bravery" [MED-HIGH on quote]. At Brandywine his charge covered the army's escape (he is credited with helping save Washington from capture — keep "credited with" [MED]); Congress made him a brigadier and "Commander of the Horse." He built the cavalry into a real arm — the "father of the American cavalry" — then raised his own legion. At Savannah, charging into the smoke between the redoubts, he was struck down by grapeshot. See Sec. 4 + 6.
- **John Laurens (1754-1782) [HIGH]:** South Carolinian, Washington's aide, already wounded at Germantown and Monmouth; led the American light troops/2nd South Carolina column against the Spring Hill abatis, where roughly half his column fell. (His proposal to raise regiments of enslaved men promised freedom — approved by Congress in 1779, killed by South Carolina's legislature — is a strong resonance beat next to the Chasseurs, [HIGH] as fact, use briefly if at all.)
- **Lachlan McIntosh (1727-1806) [HIGH]:** Georgia Continental brigadier leading the second American column — attacking his own home town, with his wife and children inside it [HIGH that his family was in Savannah during the bombardment]. (He had killed Button Gwinnett, a signer of the Declaration, in a 1777 duel — context only.)
- **Curt von Stedingk (1746-1837) [HIGH]:** Swedish count serving in the French army; led a French column to the top of the entrenchment ("I had the pleasure of planting the American flag on the last trench…"), was wounded, and came back with some 20 men, all wounded. Later Swedish field marshal and ambassador to Russia.
- **Arthur Dillon (1750-1794) [HIGH]:** Irish colonel of the Wild Geese regiment bearing his family's name in French service; his column lost its way in the Yamacraw swamp fog and surfaced in the open under the guns. Like d'Estaing, he died on the guillotine in 1794. [HIGH]
### British commander bios
- **Augustine Prevost (1723-1786) [HIGH]:** Geneva-born Swiss professional in the British 60th (Royal American) Regiment; badly wounded at Quebec 1759 (the nickname "Old Bullet Head" circulates — [MED], use with "his men called him" hedging or drop). Commanded East Florida, took over Georgia in early 1779, raided to Charleston's gates in May. At Savannah he played for time, got Maitland in, and answered the summons with defiance. Retired to England after the campaign.
- **John Maitland (1732-1779) [HIGH]:** Scottish professional, son of the Earl of Lauderdale, lieutenant colonel of the 71st Highlanders; sometime Member of Parliament. He had already beaten Lincoln at Stono Ferry (June 1779). In September, sick with fever, he brought ~800 men from Beaufort through the coastal creeks and marshes in roughly four days — arriving during the truce window and transforming the defense (Sec. 4). He died in Savannah on October 26, 1779, days after the siege lifted, of the fever he carried through the march. [HIGH; "neck-deep, alligator-infested" color comes from clan/popular accounts — use cautiously]
- **Thomas Brown (1750-1825) [HIGH, optional]:** Georgia Loyalist tarred, feathered, and scalped(-ish: burned and maimed) by Patriots in 1775; raised the King's Rangers. Among the Spring Hill sector's defenders — useful to show Loyalists really did rally in the South, exactly as the strategy predicted, just never in the numbers promised.
- **Capt. Thomas Tawse (d. 1779) [MED-HIGH]:** commanded dismounted Loyalist dragoons in the Spring Hill redoubt itself; killed defending it (accounts have him dying sword in hand at the moment the attackers crested the parapet — [MED], hedge with "by British accounts").
- **Capt. James Moncrief (1744-1793) [HIGH]:** the Royal Engineer whose three-week fortification sprint (with hundreds of enslaved laborers) made the town defensible at all — many British accounts make him, with Maitland, the real author of the victory.

## 4. THE SIEGE & THE ASSAULT
### Landing and the summons (Sept 8-16) [HIGH]
- The French crossed the bar, landed at **Beaulieu plantation on the Vernon River** (~13 miles south of town) starting Sept 12, and closed on Savannah. Lincoln marched from Charleston to join. On **Sept 16** d'Estaing — ahead of Lincoln's junction — summoned Prevost to surrender **"to the arms of the King of France."** [HIGH on phrase] The wording landed badly with the Americans (whose war it presumably was) and became a lasting grievance. [HIGH]
- **The 24-hour stall [HIGH]:** Prevost asked for 24 hours to consider. D'Estaing granted it. The British used every hour: Moncrief's crews dug, and — the truce's real prize — **Maitland's ~800 from Beaufort arrived** (Sept 16-17). His route: out of Port Royal, behind Daufuskie Island, through half-charted tidal creeks (Wall's Cut, Skull Creek) with local fishermen as guides, dodging French ships in the sounds and Lincoln's army inland; an intercepted dispatch boat in Skull Creek had even warned the Americans he was coming, but the allies left the inland water route unguarded — responsibility for watching it fell between the French navy and the American army. [HIGH on the arrival and the unguarded route; "fog covered the last leg" appears in accounts, MED] With Maitland in, Prevost sent his answer: no surrender — the evening gun would signal hostilities resumed. [HIGH; reply wording in Quotes]
- HANDLE-WITH-CARE: popular accounts make the truce a simple d'Estaing blunder. It was — but spread the failure honestly: the allies also failed to close the one water door they knew about, and d'Estaing had summoned the town before his siege guns or his American partner were in place. [Framing]
### The siege (Sept 17 – Oct 8) [HIGH]
- The allies dug approaches and hauled heavy guns and mortars ashore from the fleet (a slow, mule-starved process; the artillery wasn't positioned until early October). A British sortie on **Sept 24** hit the working parties — the Chasseurs-Volontaires were among those engaged (1 killed, 7 wounded documented). [HIGH]
- **Bombardment, Oct 3/4-8 [HIGH]:** ~53 cannon and 14 mortars opened on the town for about five days. It smashed houses and killed civilians without seriously damaging the earthworks. A British officer: "there was hardly a house that had not been shot through"; an American officer: "The poor women and children have suffered beyond description." Prevost asked leave to send the women and children out of town (his own wife and children among them, as were McIntosh's family); the allies refused, suspecting a play for time — standard siege practice, but grim. [HIGH on refusal; quotes MED — see Sec. 7]
- **The decision to assault [HIGH]:** d'Estaing's engineers told him regular approaches needed perhaps ten more days; his naval officers pressed him daily about the exposed fleet, the sick crews, and the season. Rather than lift the siege outright, he chose a pre-dawn storm of the works for **October 9** — main blow at the **Spring Hill redoubt** on the British right, where the defenders were believed to be mostly militia.
### The betrayal [HIGH that the plan was betrayed; name MED-HIGH]
- The plan leaked. By the standard account, **Sergeant Major James Curry of the Charleston Grenadiers**, who had overheard officers discussing the assault, deserted to the British and gave Prevost the point and timing of the main attack. Prevost stacked the Spring Hill sector accordingly — not militia, but a mix of Loyalist provincials (Tawse's dismounted dragoons, South Carolina Royalists, King's Rangers) backed by regulars including grenadiers of the 60th, marines, and the 71st under Maitland's command of the right. [HIGH that the redoubt was reinforced and NOT militia-only; defender detail MED on exact mix]
### October 9 — the assault [HIGH]
- The columns assembled around 1 a.m. to attack in the dark (~4 a.m.). Fog and the swamps wrecked the timetable: **Dillon's column lost its way in Yamacraw swamp** and emerged in the open under the guns; Huger's ~500-1,000 militia made a feint on the far left that was easily stopped; the main columns went in piecemeal at first light (~5:30) instead of together in darkness. [HIGH]
- At Spring Hill the storm broke and died: grapeshot and rifle fire from the works cut down the white-coated French columns (the first wave's dead and wounded filled the ditch before the second wave arrived); Laurens' Americans cut into the abatis (felled-tree obstacle belt) and reached the parapet — Lts. **Hume and Bush** of the 2nd South Carolina planted the regiment's colors on the rampart and were killed; Lt. Gray, holding them, was mortally hit; **Sergeant William Jasper** — famous since Fort Sullivan 1776 — was mortally wounded keeping a color up, and brought one off as the attack collapsed (Moultrie's account; see Sec. 7 and apocrypha). Stedingk's column momentarily planted an American flag on the entrenchment before being shot off it. **D'Estaing, leading on foot, was wounded twice.** Roughly half of Laurens' column became casualties. [HIGH]
- **Pulaski [HIGH]:** waiting with ~200 horse to charge through the expected breach, he rode forward into the smoke between the redoubts (by most accounts trying to rally the wavering infantry and find a gap) and was struck by canister/grapeshot and carried off the field. He was taken aboard the American privateer brig *Wasp* and died about two days later, generally dated **October 11, 1779** — see Sec. 6 for the burial dispute. [HIGH on wounding; "never regained consciousness" appears in some accounts and is contradicted in others — omit either claim]
- After about **an hour of carnage**, d'Estaing ordered the retreat. [HIGH]
- **The Chasseurs and the retreat [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — this is the pack's most delicate beat]:** The traditional account — carried by 19th-century memory, the Savannah monument, and most popular retellings — is that the Chasseurs-Volontaires, posted in reserve, **covered the allied retreat and blunted a British counterattack**, saving much of the army. What is *documented*: they were at the siege ~545 strong, were officially assigned to labor/reserve duty, served under fire, and took casualties (the Sept 24 sortie is the best-documented action). Contemporary French records of a dramatic rear-guard stand on Oct 9 are thin; scholars note the unit is "systematically occluded" by the colonial archive, which cuts both ways. SAFE FRAMING: "the Chasseurs stood in the reserve that covered the shattered columns' withdrawal; tradition credits them with blunting the British counterattack and saving lives in the retreat." Do NOT write the monument placard's "most brilliant feat of the day" as established fact; do NOT erase them either. [Reserve role HIGH; rear-guard stand = TRADITION/MED]
- A British counterattack/sortie out of the works pursued the broken columns briefly [MED — present in accounts at varying scale; keep small].

## 5. CASUALTIES (dossier ranges)
- **Allied [HIGH on range]:** ~**244 killed, ~584-600 wounded, ~120 captured/missing** (Marley's figures; britishbattles concurs at 16 officers + 228 men killed, 63 officers + 521 men wounded). The French bore the bulk (~650 of ~800 killed and wounded). American Battlefield Trust's rounded total: 948. Prevost claimed 1,000-1,200 — treat as the inflated victor's count. USE: "around 800 killed and wounded, plus over a hundred prisoners — the great majority French, in less than an hour of fighting."
- **British [HIGH]:** ~**40 killed, 63 wounded, 52 missing** (~155 total; ABT concurs). Capt. Tawse among the dead.
- Chasseurs-Volontaires' own losses: not reliably itemized beyond the Sept 24 sortie (1 killed, 7 wounded) — do not invent a figure. [Flag]

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **The siege lifts [HIGH]:** d'Estaing, wounded, re-embarked his guns and men; the siege was abandoned Oct 17-18, the French sailed (the fleet dispersing — d'Estaing home to France), and Lincoln marched back to Charleston. Franco-American relations hit their wartime low — the second straight joint operation (after Newport 1778) to end in recrimination. [HIGH]
- **The South stays British — and gets worse [HIGH]:** Clinton: "I think that this is the greatest event that has happened the whole war." With the French fleet gone, he sailed from New York (Dec 26, 1779) with 8,700 men, besieged Charleston, and took it on **May 12, 1780** — Lincoln surrendered the city and ~5,000 men, the largest American capitulation of the war. The full southern war (Camden, Kings Mountain, Cowpens, Guilford Courthouse) followed. Savannah itself stayed British until **July 1782**.
- **Maitland [HIGH]:** died in Savannah Oct 26, 1779, of the fever he had carried since Beaufort.
- **Pulaski's death, burial, and bones [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — three layers]:**
  1. **The dispute [HIGH that it exists]:** contemporaries split — shipboard accounts say he died aboard the *Wasp* and was **buried at sea** near the mouth of the Savannah (off Tybee); a parallel tradition says he was taken ashore and died at (or his body was landed and buried at) **Greenwich plantation** near Thunderbolt, just outside Savannah. In 1854 remains from Greenwich were placed under the new **Pulaski Monument in Monterey Square**.
  2. **The bones [HIGH]:** the monument remains were exhumed in 1996; identification stalled for years; they were reinterred with full military honors in 2005.
  3. **The 2019 study [HIGH on what was claimed; tag the epistemics]:** a Georgia Southern-led team (anthropologist Virginia Hutton Estabrook and colleagues; publicized via the Smithsonian Channel documentary *America's Hidden Stories: The General Was Female?*, 2019) reported the skeleton consistent with Pulaski's known injuries and a **mitochondrial DNA match with a grandniece** (tested at Lakehead University; mtDNA is weaker than nuclear DNA, but the analysts put the shared profile at ~0.02% of the population), concluding the Monterey Square remains are **likely Pulaski's** — which would settle burial-at-Greenwich over burial-at-sea. The skeleton also shows **female-typical features**, leading the team to hypothesize Pulaski may have been **intersex** — explicitly a hypothesis, not a proven finding, and a 2022 scholarly article challenged the identification itself. SAFE FRAMING: "a 2019 study concluded the remains are most likely Pulaski's — and, startlingly, that the skeleton bears female-typical features, leading researchers to suggest he may have been intersex; both the identification and the hypothesis still have scholarly challengers." [DISPUTED at the edges — keep all hedges]
  - **Pulaski memory [HIGH]:** Congress's memorial resolutions, Fort Pulaski, the annual presidential **General Pulaski Memorial Day** (Oct 11), the 1854 Savannah monument, and (2009) honorary U.S. citizenship — one of only eight ever granted [the honorary-citizenship fact is HIGH but verify count if used; "2009 honorary citizen" itself HIGH].
- **The Haitian legacy [the pack's long echo — handle precisely]:**
  - Veterans of the Chasseurs-Volontaires later appear among the leaders of the **Haitian Revolution** (1791-1804) — the literature names men like André Rigaud, Jean-Baptiste Chavannes, Louis-Jacques Beauvais, and Martial Besse as Savannah veterans, and the unit's recruiting ground (the North around Cap-Français) supplied much of the revolution's officer class. [MED — the general veterans-to-revolution pipeline is standard scholarship; *individual* service records vary in documentation; name at most 1-2 with "are said to have served" hedging]
  - **Henri Christophe** — later King Henry I of Haiti (r. 1811-1820) — is traditionally said to have served at Savannah as a **twelve-year-old drummer/servant** with the unit. This is TRADITION: widely repeated (it's on the monument — the drummer-boy figure added in 2009 represents young Christophe), but no contemporary muster roll proves it; Wikipedia itself tags it "possibly." Write: "tradition puts the future king of Haiti, Henri Christophe, in the ranks as a boy." [TRADITION/MED — never as plain fact]
  - **The 2007 monument [HIGH]:** the Haitian Monument in Franklin Square, Savannah — sculptor James Mastin, dedicated October 2007, completed 2009 — honors the Chasseurs-Volontaires; its placard calls their stand "the most brilliant feat of the day" (quote the placard AS the monument's words, not as history's verdict).
- **Archaeology [HIGH]:** the Spring Hill fortifications were located in 2005 (Coastal Heritage Society/LAMAR Institute) — first physical remains of the battlefield; the city's Battlefield Memorial Park now marks the sector. Three modern Army National Guard units trace lineage to siege participants.
- **Lasting oddity [HIGH, optional]:** Charles Dickens gave Joe Willet of *Barnaby Rudge* an arm lost "defending the Salwanners" — the siege had a long cultural shadow.

## 11. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)
- **Date:** September 16 – October 18, 1779; grand assault October 9 · **Where:** Savannah, Georgia
- **Forces:** ~7,500 allied (about 4,000 French — including ~545 Chasseurs-Volontaires de Saint-Domingue — plus ~2,000+ Americans and Pulaski's ~200 horse; 20+ French ships of the line offshore) vs ~3,200 British, Loyalist, and Hessian defenders (swelling toward 4,800 with armed sailors and militia) behind ~13 redoubts and 100+ guns
- **Duration:** a month of siege; the assault itself about one hour
- **Casualties:** Allied ~800 killed and wounded + ~120 prisoners (the great majority French); British ~155 (40 killed)
- **Winner line material:** British victory — and the southern strategy's green light. A French fleet racing the hurricane season, a garrison saved by a 24-hour stall and Maitland's march through the creeks, and a betrayed dawn assault that cost the allies five men for every defender lost: Pulaski dead, Jasper dead, d'Estaing twice wounded, and the most diverse army of the war broken at the abatis. The French sailed, the South stayed British, and Charleston fell next spring.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. **British strength:** ~2,500-3,200 effectives vs 4,813 all-in — use the range, never one bare number.
2. **French troop total:** 3,500-5,500 across sources — "about 4,000."
3. **Allied casualties:** ~800 k+w (+120 prisoners) is the modern consolidated figure; ABT says 948 total; Prevost claimed 1,000-1,200 (inflated). Use "around 800… some counts run higher."
4. **The deserter:** betrayal itself is standard in the literature [HIGH]; "Sgt. Maj. James Curry, Charleston Grenadiers" is the standard name [MED-HIGH]; nothing beyond that.
5. **Spring Hill garrison composition:** mixed Loyalist provincials (Tawse's dragoons, SC Royalists, King's Rangers) + regulars (60th grenadiers, marines, 71st in support) — exact mix varies by account; never "militia only," never "71st only."
6. **Chasseurs' Oct 9 role:** reserve documented; rear-guard stand = tradition (Sec. 4 safe framing locked).
7. **Christophe at Savannah:** tradition only — always hedged.
8. **Pulaski:** wounded Oct 9 [HIGH]; died aboard the *Wasp* ~Oct 11 [HIGH-ish; some accounts Oct 15 — use "two days later"]; burial at sea vs Greenwich plantation genuinely DISPUTED; 2019 study favors Greenwich/monument remains but is itself challenged (2022). Consciousness at death: conflicting — omit.
9. **Jasper:** Moultrie's account is the documented core; the deathbed speech and the heroic flag-planting print are legend-layer.
10. **D'Estaing's wounds:** two [HIGH]; arm-and-leg specifics [MED].
11. **Maitland's route color** ("neck-deep, alligator-infested") comes from clan/popular accounts — keep the verified spine (Beaufort, ~800 men, inside passage behind Daufuskie via Wall's Cut/Skull Creek, fishermen guides, ~4 days, arrived in the truce window).
12. **Quote chains:** Stedingk and the two bombardment quotes are translated/secondhand — attribute generically where flagged; Prevost's "evening gun" reply is single-sourced this session.
13. **Haitian monument photo** = US freedom-of-panorama risk (Sec. 9 item 16) — prose beat preferred, or user sign-off.
14. **Bombardment start:** night of Oct 3-4 (sources split on which date to print — "the night of October 3rd-4th" is safe).
