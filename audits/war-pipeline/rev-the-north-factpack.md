# FACT PACK — "The War for the North" (1776 → January 1777)

*American Revolution vertical, WAR-STORY SPINE, phase 2 (route slug `the-north`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against live-web sources; confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges and "flag" guidance for contested specifics.*

**THIS IS THE SIMULTANEITY SHOWCASE CHAPTER (locked).** The summer of 1776 must read as ONE pulse, not sequenced lanes: the Declaration adopted and read to the army, Loyalists hunted while Loyalist regiments form, Congress's secret envoy already in Paris, and the largest invasion fleet in British history anchoring in the same harbor — all inside roughly six weeks. Section 3 arms that pulse; the author should braid, not sequence.

**Orientation.** The spine's phase 1 ended at the siege lines around Boston: a New England army penned the British in, and the war was still officially a fight by aggrieved Britons for redress. This chapter is the year everything became continental. Knox's guns end the Boston siege in March; Britain answers with the largest expeditionary force it had ever sent anywhere, aimed at New York — the harbor, the Hudson corridor, the Loyalist base. Through summer and fall Washington loses nearly every battle and the city itself; the cause shrinks to a freezing remnant on the Pennsylvania bank of the Delaware; and then ten days (Trenton, December 26 → Princeton, January 3) psychologically reset the war. Braided through it all: the Loyalist civil war igniting in the occupied zone (the civil-war theme owns the machinery — one beat each here) and the first secret reach toward France (the french-alliance theme owns the arc). Close on January 1777: the cause alive by roughly ten days' margin.

## 1. THE BOSTON ENDGAME (January–March 1776)

- **Knox's "noble train of artillery."** Henry Knox (a 25-year-old Boston bookseller turned artillery colonel) left for Fort Ticonderoga in November 1775 and hauled roughly **60 tons of cannon and armaments** (~59 guns) some 300 miles by boat, horse, and ox-drawn sledge across two semi-frozen rivers and the Berkshire hills, arriving back at the Boston lines by **late January 1776**. (HIGH on the trek/tonnage; the gun count varies slightly by source — say "some sixty guns" or "60 tons.")
- **Dorchester Heights, the overnight fortification (night of March 4–5, 1776).** Washington put the Ticonderoga guns on Dorchester Heights, the hills commanding Boston and its harbor from the south, **in a single night**. The trick: **Rufus Putnam's prefabricated fortifications** — "chandeliers" (heavy 10-foot timber frames) and fascines (bundled sticks) built out of sight in advance, then carried up and assembled, because the frozen ground couldn't be dug. About **2,500 troops under General John Thomas** did the work; some **1,200 additional soldiers/volunteers and 300 oxcarts** hauled materials; hay bales screened the road; an artillery bombardment elsewhere masked the noise. The British woke on March 5 to finished works bristling with guns. (HIGH)
- **Howe's reaction.** General **William Howe** (British commander in Boston since October 1775) planned an assault on the Heights; a **violent storm** prevented it, and he chose evacuation instead. (HIGH)
- **The evacuation, March 17, 1776.** Roughly **8,000–9,000 British troops and about 1,100 Loyalist civilians** sailed out of Boston Harbor for **Halifax, Nova Scotia**. (HIGH on March 17 / Halifax / ~1,100 Loyalists.) The Loyalist exodus is **one beat** — families abandoning homes, property, and a country that now hunted them — the `civil-war-among-themselves` theme owns the arc.
- **Everyone knew New York was next, and why:** the best deep-water harbor on the coast (a navy's war-winning asset), the mouth of the **Hudson River** corridor that could split New England from the rest of the colonies, and a region (New York City, Long Island, the lower Hudson) with the strongest **Loyalist base** in America. Washington began shifting the army to New York within weeks. (HIGH — standard strategic framing.)

## 2. THE ARMADA (June–August 1776)

- **Scale — verify-tagged ranges.** Through summer 1776 Britain assembled at New York **the largest expeditionary force it had ever sent anywhere**: by August roughly **32,000 troops** (including ~8,000 German auxiliaries, the "Hessians" — hired principally from Hesse-Kassel) and a fleet commonly given as **~400+ ships** (transports plus dozens of warships, with ~10,000 sailors). Use "some 400 ships" and "roughly 32,000 men"; precise counts vary by source and date. (HIGH on "largest ever sent" + ~32,000; MED on any exact ship count — give the range.)
- **Timeline of arrival:** General William Howe's advance force arrived off Sandy Hook in late June (lookouts described the harbor filling with masts "like a forest"); troops landed unopposed on **Staten Island in early July** (its population heavily Loyalist, welcoming); Admiral **Lord Richard Howe** arrived **July 12**; Hessian contingents and the force repulsed at Charleston joined through August. (HIGH on the spine; MED on any single day-date except Richard Howe's July 12.)
- **The Howe brothers' strange both-hats mandate.** Richard Howe commanded the navy, his younger brother William the army — and the ministry ALSO commissioned them as **peace commissioners**, empowered to grant pardons and negotiate. But the instructions were narrow and inflexible: they could effectively treat only with colonists who **submitted to the Crown first**, and they had no authority to recognize independence or Congress. The brothers were personally sympathetic to America (their elder brother George died fighting alongside Americans in 1758, and Massachusetts had paid for his memorial); they genuinely hoped a show of overwhelming force plus pardons would end the rebellion without a bloodbath. Both hats at once: invade and reconcile. (HIGH on the dual commission + its submit-first limits; HIGH on the family's American connection.)
- **Washington's army at New York:** roughly **19,000–23,000 on paper**, heavily militia, many sick, almost none with battlefield experience; he had to defend an archipelago against an enemy with total naval command. Congress expected the city defended; militarily it was close to indefensible. (HIGH on the predicament; MED on exact strength — use a range.)

## 3. THE PULSE (late June–mid-July 1776) — THE CHAPTER'S HEART

*All of the following happened within about three weeks, in and around one city. Tell it as one pulse.*

- **June 28 — the Hickey hanging.** **Thomas Hickey**, an Irish-born private of Washington's own Life Guard, was hanged before a crowd reported at **~20,000** (a number to soften: "an enormous crowd, reported at twenty thousand") at present-day Chrystie and Grand Streets, New York — the **first Continental soldier executed** by the army. He was convicted by court-martial of **mutiny and sedition**: while jailed for passing counterfeit money he had revealed a conspiracy, fed by the royal governor **William Tryon** (operating from a ship in the harbor) and Loyalist mayor **David Matthews**, to ready soldiers to defect when the British landed. **What is documented vs. panic:** the court-martial record supports a defection/recruitment plot; the lurid versions (assassinate Washington; the folkloric poisoned-peas story) are **NOT documented** — Matthews himself later claimed a plan "for the taking of Mr. Washington and his Guard prisoners… which was not effected" (kidnap, not murder). Hickey was the only person tried. (HIGH on the execution/date/charges; DISPUTED on assassination — see HANDLE-WITH-CARE #6.) The fleet's first ships appeared off the harbor **within days** of the hanging.
- **July 2/4 — independence.** Congress voted independence July 2 and adopted the Declaration July 4 — **one beat only; the `idea-of-independence` theme owns it.** What this chapter carries is what it did to the war: every man in the army was now, in British law, a traitor in arms.
- **July 8–11 — the Paris thread, simultaneously.** **Silas Deane**, Congress's secret envoy (commissioned by the Committee of Secrecy in March 1776), **arrived in Paris on July 8, 1776** — days after the Declaration he didn't yet know about — and within days was meeting the French foreign minister **Vergennes**, who authorized arms and put Deane together with **Beaumarchais** (playwright of *The Barber of Seville*, now running a fake trading firm, **Roderigue Hortalez & Co.**, capitalized with a million livres from the French treasury, later matched by Spain, to funnel arms covertly). (HIGH on Deane's July 8 arrival + the Hortalez front; MED on the exact day he first met Vergennes — say "within days.") **One beat; `french-alliance` owns the arc.** The simultaneity is the point: while the armada anchored, the cause's first lifeline to Europe was already being knotted.
- **July 9 — the Declaration read to the army.** Washington had the Declaration **read aloud to the brigades in New York at 6 p.m. on July 9**. That evening soldiers and civilians pulled down the **gilded lead equestrian statue of George III at Bowling Green** (erected 1770, ~4,000 pounds of lead); the head was cut off; most of the lead went to Connecticut to be cast into **musket balls — the famous figure is 42,088 bullets** (a documented foundry tally, but phrase as "tens of thousands"; the "40,000+" figure is the standard one). Washington officially disapproved of the disorder while welcoming the spirit. (HIGH on July 9 reading + statue; MED on the exact bullet count — round it.)
- **July 12 — the river answer.** The same week, two British warships (the *Phoenix* and *Rose*) ran the Hudson batteries with ease, shelling the city and proving the navy could go wherever it pleased; Lord Howe's flagship arrived the same day. (HIGH; one beat — the harbor owned by Britain.)
- **Loyalists hunted AND enlisting — at once.** Through summer, committees hunted, disarmed, and jailed suspected Loyalists in and around New York; after the British landed, Loyalist regiments began forming under British protection — **Oliver De Lancey's brigade raised on Long Island from September 1776** (three battalions of 500) and **Cortlandt Skinner's New Jersey Volunteers from late 1776** (eventually six battalions). One beat each; `civil-war-among-themselves` owns the machinery. (HIGH)
- **The peace gesture inside the pulse:** Lord Howe opened with conciliatory letters — famously addressed to "George Washington, Esq.," which Washington's staff refused to receive (no rank, no recognition). (HIGH; light touch, sets up §5's Staten Island conference.)

## 4. LONG ISLAND & THE ESCAPE (August 22–30, 1776)

- **The landing.** August 22: Howe began landing troops from Staten Island onto Long Island (Gravesend Bay, Brooklyn); by the 26th some **20,000+ British and Hessian troops** were ashore facing American forward lines on the wooded Heights of Guan, in front of the fortified Brooklyn Heights camp. (HIGH on the spine; MED on exact landed strength.)
- **Jamaica Pass — the flanking (night of August 26–27).** Four passes cut the wooded ridge; the easternmost, **Jamaica Pass, was watched by just five mounted militia officers**. Local Loyalists told **General Henry Clinton** it was open. That night Clinton, **Cornwallis**, and Howe led a column of **~10,000 men** silently through it and appeared at dawn **behind the American left**. The American forward line collapsed into rout; **Generals John Sullivan and Lord Stirling (William Alexander) were both captured**. (HIGH)
- **The Maryland 400 — honest numbers.** Covering the retreat at the Old Stone House (Gowanus), Stirling led repeated charges by the Maryland battalion's rear guard — called "the Maryland 400," though modern research puts the contingent at **about 260–270 men**, of whom roughly **256 were killed or captured** (a minority cut their way back). Washington's anguished line watching from the heights — "Good God, what brave fellows I must this day lose!" — is **traditional, not contemporaneous; attribute as "Washington is said to have cried."** (HIGH on the stand; HIGH that "400" overstates the unit; MED on the precise loss figure — phrase "most of them"; the quote is MED/tradition — see HANDLE-WITH-CARE #4.)
- **Total cost — ranges.** American losses are commonly given as **~300 killed and over 1,000 captured** (Howe reported 1,000+ prisoners); British/Hessian casualties **~370–400**. Use ranges. (MED — counts vary by source.)
- **Howe stopped short.** Rather than storm the Brooklyn Heights lines, Howe halted and began siege approaches — remembering Bunker Hill's cost; it remains one of the war's great what-ifs. (HIGH on the halt; frame the "why" as judgment, not certainty.)
- **THE ESCAPE (night of August 29–30).** With the army trapped against the East River and a storm keeping the Royal Navy out of the river, Washington evacuated **about 9,000 men, with field guns, horses, and baggage, across the mile-wide East River in one night** — rowed in near-total silence by **Colonel John Glover's Marblehead regiment** (Massachusetts fishermen and sailors). Toward dawn a **providential fog** settled over Brooklyn and covered the final boatloads; Washington crossed with the last of them. The British found empty trenches. Losses in the evacuation: essentially none. (HIGH — the fog, Glover, ~9,000, Washington-last are all well attested; the whole operation took under 13 hours.)

## 5. THE FALL OF NEW YORK (September–November 1776)

- **Staten Island peace conference, September 11.** Lord Howe met a congressional committee — **Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, Edward Rutledge** — at the Billopp House on Staten Island. It failed in three hours on the built-in flaw: Howe could not treat with "ambassadors" of an independent state; Congress would not return to allegiance. The brotherly exchange (see VERIFIED QUOTES) belongs here. (HIGH)
- **Kips Bay, September 15.** British landing on Manhattan's east side under a naval barrage; the Connecticut militia **broke and ran without a fight**. Washington galloped into the rout and tried to rally them — by multiple accounts in a rage, striking at fleeing men and exposing himself to capture — and failed; the army abandoned the city to Harlem Heights. One of the most humiliating American episodes of the war. (HIGH on the panic + abandonment; the Washington-in-a-fury details are well attested but vary — keep concrete claims soft.)
- **Harlem Heights, September 16 — the small redemption.** A dawn scout by **Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Knowlton's Rangers** (~120 men) collided with British light infantry; the British pursued contemptuously (their buglers reportedly sounding a fox-hunt call); Washington turned it into a planned envelopment that, though the flanking move sprang early, **drove British regulars back in open field** — the first time this army had seen redcoats retreat before it. **Knowlton was killed.** The Virginia militia who had run at Kips Bay fought well — that's the beat. (HIGH; the fox-hunt bugle detail is from American accounts — MED, phrase "the Americans heard it as.")
- **The Great Fire, night of September 20–21.** Fire broke out in lower Manhattan days after the British occupied the city and burned **roughly 400–1,000 buildings — commonly stated as 10–25% of the city** (one careful count: ~493 buildings, about a quarter of the built city). **Arson is genuinely unresolved**: the British and many Loyalists were sure rebels fired the city (Washington had been forbidden by Congress to burn it, and wrote privately that "Providence, or some good honest fellow, has done more for us than we were disposed to do for ourselves"); evidence cited for arson includes multiple apparent ignition points and sabotaged alarms/equipment, and recent scholarship (Benjamin Carp's 2023 study; the contemporaneous Carleton Commission testimony) takes deliberate arson seriously — while other historians (e.g., Barnet Schecter) hold that no specific accusation has withstood scrutiny. **No one was ever convicted; present it as unresolved.** (HIGH on date/scale-range; DISPUTED on cause — see HANDLE-WITH-CARE #3.)
- **Nathan Hale, September 22.** A 21-year-old Connecticut captain of Knowlton's Rangers, caught behind British lines as a spy (in civilian clothes, with notes), **hanged without trial on September 22, 1776**. **The famous line's documentation chain (badge it):** "I only regret that I have but one life to lose for my country" comes via **Captain William Hull**, who was NOT present — he reported what British engineer **Captain John Montresor** (who was present) told him under flag of truce the next day; Hull's account was published in his memoir **decades later (1848, posthumously)**. The contemporaneous **diary of British officer Frederick MacKenzie** (Sept 22, 1776) records Hale dying with composure and saying it was "the duty of every good officer to obey any orders given him by his commander in chief" — brave words, but not THE words. The line also echoes Addison's *Cato*, the era's favorite play. Safe framing: he died with extraordinary composure; the immortal sentence is **secondhand and late, possibly polished, plausibly rooted in something he said.** (HIGH on the execution + chain; the verbatim line itself is MED-as-attributed — never assert it flatly.)
- **White Plains, October 28.** Howe, instead of storming Harlem Heights, moved by water up to Throg's Neck/Pell's Point (where **Glover's brigade** fought a sharp delaying action, October 18 — one beat) forcing Washington north to White Plains; the British took **Chatterton's Hill** on the American right after hard fighting; Washington withdrew again; Howe turned back toward Manhattan. (HIGH; keep at one-paragraph scale.)
- **Fort Washington, November 16 — the war's worst American defeat to date.** The last American post on Manhattan, a hilltop earthwork holding ~3,000 men under **Colonel Robert Magaw**, fell to a converging assault (Hessians under **Knyphausen** bore the bloodiest sector). Loss: **~59 killed, ~96 wounded, and 2,837 captured**, plus guns and stores. **Command responsibility — handle honestly:** Washington had inclined to evacuate (he wrote so on November 8 to Greene, leaving it to his discretion); **General Nathanael Greene argued the fort could hold and should**; Magaw believed it too; **Washington, on the scene in the final days, did not overrule them — the failure is his as much as theirs.** Most of the 2,800 prisoners went into New York's prison hulks and sugar-house prisons, where the majority died — a beat that lands later in the war's prisoner story. **Margaret Corbin** — who took her husband John's place at his cannon when he was killed and was herself terribly wounded — is **one cross-beat here; `women-of-the-revolution` owns her** (first woman to receive a U.S. military pension, 1779). (HIGH throughout; the prisoner-mortality specifics MED — "most died" is safe.)
- **Fort Lee, November 20, and the retreat.** Cornwallis crossed the Hudson with ~5,000 men and scaled the Palisades; **Greene evacuated Fort Lee** barely ahead of him, abandoning cannon, tents, and stores. Then the long retreat southwest across New Jersey — Newark, New Brunswick, Princeton, Trenton — Washington's force melting by expiring enlistments and desertion from thousands to **roughly 3,000** by the time it crossed the **Delaware into Pennsylvania (completed December 7–8)**, seizing or burning every boat for ~70 miles of river so the British couldn't follow. Howe spread winter garrisons across New Jersey (a chain of posts including Trenton and Bordentown) and went into winter quarters. (HIGH on the spine + ~3,000; MED on exact garrison/post details.)
- **Thomas Paine marched in that retreat** — drafting on the way what became *The American Crisis* (§7). (HIGH that he served with the army in the retreat and wrote the essay during/just after it.)

## 6. THE OCCUPIED ZONE — THE CIVIL WAR IGNITES (November 1776–January 1777)

*One beat each — the `civil-war-among-themselves` theme owns the machinery.*

- **The Howes' November 30 proclamation.** With New Jersey overrun, the Howe brothers proclaimed a **free pardon and "protection" to anyone who, within 60 days, swore allegiance** to the King. Takers received certificates entitling "himself, his family and property" to protection of His Majesty's forces. (HIGH)
- **How many took it — ranges.** By the Howes' own reporting, about **4,000 by the end of December** (some **2,700 in New Jersey alone**); by spring the cumulative claim was **~4,800 total**. The product brief's "3,000–5,000" is the right band for New Jersey-centered phrasing: say **"thousands — perhaps three to five thousand"** or use the documented ~2,700-in-New-Jersey figure. Signers included men of standing — even **Richard Stockton, a signer of the Declaration**, gave his parole under duress after capture (his case is contested in its details; if used, keep to one cautious clause). (HIGH on the proclamation + Howe-reported figures; MED on any single total; Stockton specifics MED/contested.)
- **Plunder unmade the conquest — the standard scholarship.** Both armies stripped the countryside, but British and especially **Hessian plundering of "protected" Loyalist and neutral farms alike** became notorious — a British officer wrote (November 1776) "the Country all this time unmercifully Pillaged by our Troops, Hessians in particular, no wonder if the Country People refuse to join us." Protection papers that didn't protect drove the undecided toward the rebels; by late December New Jersey militia were rising and ambushing patrols. The plunder-to-allegiance mechanism is standard modern scholarship (Fischer's *Washington's Crossing* makes it central). Be even-handed: **American troops and militia plundered too**; the British/Hessian version mattered more because they were the occupiers promising protection. (HIGH)
- **The "neutral ground."** Around occupied New York — Westchester County between the lines, and contested New Jersey — a lawless in-between zone formed, raided by both sides' irregulars for years (De Lancey's "Cowboys" among them). **Name it once as a thing now beginning**; the theme owns it. (HIGH)

## 7. THE CRISIS OF THE CAUSE (December 1776)

- **The arithmetic of extinction.** Nearly all Continental **enlistments expired December 31, 1776** — the army was a one-year army, and the year was up. Washington's effectives on the Delaware: a few thousand fit for duty, many shoeless. (`army-from-nothing` owns the enlistment mechanism — this chapter carries the cliff-edge moment.) (HIGH)
- **Congress flees.** On **December 12** Congress adjourned from Philadelphia to **Baltimore** (reconvening December 20 at the Henry Fite House), first resolving that Washington "be possessed of full power to order and direct all things relative" to the war effort; on December 27 it voted him sweeping six-month emergency powers — newspapers and later writers called them "dictatorial," a word to use only in quotes. (HIGH)
- **Lee captured, December 13 — the strange relief.** Major General **Charles Lee** — the army's most experienced professional, openly contemptuous of Washington and dawdling for weeks instead of bringing his division to the Delaware — was seized in his nightclothes-and-slippers morning at **Widow White's Tavern, Basking Ridge, N.J.**, by a British cavalry patrol under **Lt. Col. William Harcourt** (a young cornet named **Banastre Tarleton** among them), having lodged a careless three miles from his troops. That very morning he had written Horatio Gates that "a certain great man is most damnably deficient." **The historiographical irony (touch lightly):** contemporaries thought his loss a catastrophe; most historians since have judged it a quiet blessing — his division finally marched to Washington, and the rival voice was gone. Don't overclaim what Lee "would have" done. (HIGH on the capture/date/letter; the "relief" framing is interpretive — present as the standard later judgment, MED.)
- ***The American Crisis*, December 19.** Paine's first *Crisis* essay appeared in Philadelphia on **December 19, 1776** (as an eight-page pamphlet by Styner & Cist; the oft-cited *Pennsylvania Journal* printing is a myth — that paper had suspended publication). Opening verbatim in VERIFIED QUOTES. **The read-to-the-troops tradition:** it is widely stated that officers read it aloud to the regiments before the crossing on Washington's orders — **but there is no contemporary account of this**; the story is early tradition. Safe phrasing: "the words reached the camps within days; tradition holds they were read aloud to the regiments before the crossing." (HIGH on publication date/text; MED/tradition on the reading — badge it.)
- **Washington's own verdict, December 18:** "I think the game is pretty near up" — written to his brothers (see VERIFIED QUOTES); he also told his cousin Lund to be ready to move his papers from Mount Vernon. (HIGH)

## 8. TEN DAYS (December 25, 1776 – January 3, 1777)

- **The crossing, Christmas night.** Washington recrossed the Delaware at **McConkey's Ferry**, ~9 miles above Trenton, with **about 2,400 men, 18 guns, and horses**, in big black **Durham boats** (cargo boats of the iron trade) and ferries — **crewed again largely by Glover's Marblehead mariners** — through a **nor'easter of snow, sleet, and river ice**. The crossing finished around 3 a.m. (hours behind plan); the two supporting crossings downstream (**Cadwalader's and Ewing's columns**) were defeated by the ice and never made it, leaving Washington's column alone. The countersign was **"Victory or Death"** — documented in the operation's written orders (Fischer, *Washington's Crossing*; Benjamin Rush also recalled seeing the phrase on a scrap of paper Washington had been writing). Two soldiers are commonly said to have frozen to death on the night march — MED, phrase softly if used. (HIGH on the spine, numbers, password, failed supporting crossings; MED on the frozen-men detail.)
- **Trenton, morning of December 26.** Two columns (Greene and Sullivan, Knox's guns at the street-heads) hit the ~**1,400-man Hessian brigade** of **Colonel Johann Rall** in a driving storm. The fight took about **45 minutes—an hour**: artillery swept the two main streets, the Hessians' counterattack into the open failed, **Rall was mortally wounded**, and the brigade surrendered in an orchard. Tally commonly given: **~22 Hessians killed, ~80–90 wounded, ~900 captured** (a few hundred escaped south); American battle casualties: a handful of wounded (future president Lieutenant **James Monroe** among them). (HIGH; use "about 900 prisoners.")
- **THE HESSIAN MYTH — kill it.** The drunk-Hessians-sleeping-off-Christmas story is **false**. Modern scholarship (Fischer, *Washington's Crossing*, 2004, is the standard treatment; Washington Crossing Historic Park's own historians concur): **no evidence the garrison was drunk**; they were **exhausted by weeks of constant militia alarms and raids** — including a skirmish on Christmas night itself — and stood to arms repeatedly. **Rall's real failures:** he refused to fortify the town despite orders/advice (no redoubts, no works — he reportedly scoffed "Let them come… we will go at them with the bayonet"), he treated repeated specific warnings of an attack with public contempt (while privately writing his superiors that his post was exposed), and his pickets were ground down by the alarm fatigue that made one more storm-night alarm seem routine. The famous detail that he died with a **Loyalist's unread warning note in his pocket** comes from later accounts — colorful, **MED — badge as "the story goes."** (HIGH on the myth-kill + real failures; MED on the note anecdote.)
- **The week between.** Washington recrossed to Pennsylvania with the prisoners, then crossed a THIRD time and stood at Trenton. On **December 30–31**, with enlistments expiring at midnight, he appealed personally to the regiments to stay six more weeks for a **$10 bounty** — the famous account has the drums rolling, no one stepping forward, Washington wheeling his horse to speak again, and then the men stepping out one by one. That account is from a soldier's recollection ("Sergeant R—", published decades later) — **MED, badge as recollection**; the bounty and the mass re-engagement are documented. (`army-from-nothing` carries the mechanism.) (HIGH on bounty/re-enlistment; MED on the theatrical scene.)
- **Second Trenton (Assunpink Creek), January 2.** Cornwallis marched ~8,000 men from Princeton, fought through delaying actions all day, and at dusk failed three times to force the Assunpink bridge. That night Washington left his campfires burning, muffled his wheels, and **slipped around Cornwallis's flank by back roads toward Princeton**. (HIGH)
- **Princeton, January 3.** Washington's column collided with **Lt. Col. Charles Mawhood's** brigades leaving Princeton. **Brigadier General Hugh Mercer's** vanguard was overrun — Mercer, unhorsed and refusing to surrender, was **bayoneted seven times** (he died January 12; Trumbull's painting in CANDIDATE IMAGES). Washington personally rode into the wavering line to rally it — the report that he called "It is a fine fox chase, my boys!" as the British broke is **tradition, MED** — and the British line collapsed; the last defenders in Nassau Hall surrendered. (HIGH on the battle/Mercer; MED on the quote.)
- **What the ten days changed.** Cornwallis abandoned most of New Jersey; the British grip collapsed back to a corridor around **New Brunswick and Perth Amboy**; Washington went into winter quarters at **Morristown** (January 6). Through the winter the **"Forage War"** — dozens of militia-and-Continental ambushes on British foraging parties — bled the garrisons and kept New Jersey hostile ground (one beat). Psychologically the war reset: the army had not dissolved on December 31; the Hessian terror was broken; recruits came in under the new three-year Continental establishment. Howe's pardon-takers were left exposed holding worthless protections. (HIGH)

## 9. THE FRENCH BRAID (running thread — beats only; `french-alliance` owns the arc)

- **Secret aid in motion through late 1776:** Vergennes had Hortalez & Co. buying and loading muskets, cannon, tents, clothing, and gunpowder from royal arsenals through the fall; **the first Hortalez ships did not land until spring 1777** (*Mercure* into Portsmouth, N.H., March 1777; *Amphitrite* April 1777 — together carrying on the order of 20,000+ muskets, dozens of brass field guns, ~1,000+ barrels of powder), in time to arm the Saratoga campaign. Meanwhile gunpowder had been reaching America all through 1775–76 by covert overseas trade (Caribbean entrepôts like St. Eustatius): the standard scholarly figure is that **over 90% of American gunpowder in the war's first two and a half years came from abroad**, overwhelmingly French-sourced. **Do NOT have Hortalez cargo arriving during 1776** — in this chapter the aid is being *loaded*, not landed. (HIGH on the timeline + 90% figure (Stephenson, *AHR* 1925, still standard); HIGH that no Hortalez ship arrived in 1776.)
- **Franklin sails, October 26, 1776 — the simultaneity beat.** Seventy-year-old Benjamin Franklin, named a commissioner to France by Congress in late September, boarded the armed sloop **Reprisal** with two grandsons on **October 26, 1776** — while Washington's army was being maneuvered out of White Plains — crossed in about a month through gale and British cruisers, **landed at Auray, Brittany, in early December**, and reached Paris to a sensation just as the cause's stock hit bottom. ## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **The drunk-Hessian myth — kill it, by name.** No evidence the Trenton garrison was drunk on December 26; modern scholarship (Fischer's *Washington's Crossing* is the standard) replaces it with exhaustion from weeks of militia alarms plus Rall's real command failures (no fortifications; warnings publicly scorned). The unread-note-in-the-pocket anecdote is later and colorful — "the story goes," never flat assertion. Keep the Hessians professional and worn down, not comic.
2. **Nathan Hale's line is secondhand and late.** Via Hull (not present) from Montresor, published 1848; MacKenzie's same-day diary gives different (still brave) words; the famous sentence echoes Addison's *Cato*. The app must badge the chain — the composure is documented, the sentence is tradition.
3. **The Great Fire's arson question is genuinely unresolved.** Multiple ignition points and sabotage claims vs. no conviction and no confession; recent scholarship (Carp) re-arms the arson case, older scholarship (Schecter) is skeptical. Present both, conclude with "no one ever proved it." Washington's "some good honest fellow" line is a wink, not a confession — frame it exactly that way. Range the damage (10–25% of the city / ~400–1,000 buildings, ~493 in one careful count).
4. **The Maryland 400 — number inflation.** The rear guard was ~260–270 men, not 400; "most killed or captured" is safe; the "Good God, what brave fellows" cry is tradition ("is said to have"). Honor the stand without parade-float numbers.
5. **No Washington-the-infallible.** Long Island's dispositions (an open Jamaica Pass, divided forces) and Fort Washington (he had the inclination AND the authority to evacuate, deferred to Greene/Magaw, and was present in the final days) were HIS failures as much as anyone's; Kips Bay was his army breaking in front of him. The arc of 1776 is a commander learning — let him be wrong on the page. Equally: no Howe-the-fool — his Long Island halt and his pardons strategy were reasoned (Bunker Hill memory; reconciliation mandate).
6. **The Hickey "assassination plot" — panic vs. record.** Court-martial documented mutiny/sedition/defection recruiting; assassination is NOT in the record; Matthews later described a kidnap plan that "was not effected"; the poisoned-peas tale is folklore. Tell it as a city at war with itself amid invasion fever, not a thwarted murder thriller.
7. **"Victory or Death" is documented** (in the operation's orders; Rush anecdote corroborates) — usable straight, but don't embroider beyond countersign.
8. **The Crisis read-aloud-before-the-crossing scene has no contemporary account** — publication Dec 19 is solid; "tradition holds it was read to the regiments" is the honest phrasing. Also: first printing was a pamphlet, NOT the *Pennsylvania Journal* (suspended at the time).
9. **Oath-taker counts are British self-reporting** (~4,000 by end of December, ~2,700 of them New Jersey; ~4,800 by spring) — phrase as "the Howes reported…" / "thousands, perhaps three to five thousand." Don't present occupiers' success metrics as neutral counts.
10. **Lee's capture — keep the historiography light.** Contemporaries called it a disaster; the standard later judgment is quiet relief. Report both; never claim what Lee "would have" done.
11. **Armada and army totals are ranges, not census** (~32,000 troops, "some 400 ships," ~10,000 sailors; Washington ~19,000–23,000 paper strength). Same for Long Island losses (~300 killed / 1,000+ captured American; ~370–400 British-Hessian).
12. **Simultaneity discipline (the chapter's own rule):** the Declaration, Hickey, Deane-in-Paris, the statue, and the anchoring fleet are ONE three-week pulse — but each themed arc gets ONE beat here (the pills carry readers to the theme pages). Don't let the pulse section swell into four mini-chapters.
13. **Plunder cut both ways.** British/Hessian plunder is the allegiance-flipping engine (standard scholarship), AND American forces plundered too; the asymmetry is that the occupier had promised protection. Keep the even hand.
