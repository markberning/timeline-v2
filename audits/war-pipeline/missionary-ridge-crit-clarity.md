# Missionary Ridge — NEWCOMER / CLARITY critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

> Read COLD as a sharp 15-year-old with ZERO Civil War knowledge. Flag every undefined
> term/jargon, unexplained leap, named actor without side-tag/scene-set, overwhelming passage.
> Verdict per item: CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST. No edits, no commit.

**OVERALL VERDICT: NEEDS-GLOSS.** The narrative arc is genuinely readable cold — the
siege-trap, the muddled order, the unauthorized charge, and the "who ordered those men up
the ridge?" payoff all land for a newcomer without prior knowledge. The voice is strong and
the stakes are set well. BUT a cluster of military-organization terms (corps/division/brigade,
army-names-as-proper-nouns, "rifle pits," "military crest," "rear guard") are used as if the
reader already knows them. Most are quick fixes. Two MUST-FIX items would genuinely stop a
cold reader: the unglossed **corps/division/brigade** ladder behind every general's rank, and
the casualty-breakdown paragraph that asks the reader to "read the breakdown" without ever
telling them what "killed / wounded / missing / captured" buckets mean or why captured is the
tell. Nothing is fully LOST; the spine survives. Fix the glosses and this clears.

## MUST-FIX (a cold reader stalls or misreads here)

### M1 — Army organization ladder: "corps / division / brigade" never defined — NEEDS-GLOSS
The draft is built on a chain of command the reader is never handed:
- "**Brig. Gen.**" / "**Maj. Gen.**" / "**Lt. Gen.**" — four different rank abbreviations
  appear (Brig., Maj., Lt. Gen., Gen.) and a newcomer can't rank them. Which is bigger? The
  story leans on Cleburne being a *division* commander, Granger a *corps* commander, Bragg an
  *army* commander — the size hierarchy IS the drama (4,000 men holds off 16,600) but the
  reader has no scale to feel it.
- "**division**" — "one of the finest division commanders" (§the-plan), "the divisions of
  Sheridan and Wood" (§the-charge). Never sized. A newcomer doesn't know a division is
  ~thousands of men.
- "**IV Corps**" — "Granger, his IV Corps commander" (§the-charge). "Corps" undefined, and the
  Roman numeral "IV" will read as cryptic.
- "**brigade**" — implied by all the Brig. Gens. but the unit itself never appears, so the rank
  abbreviation floats free.
**Why MUST-FIX:** the whole "outnumbered division holds the hill" and "the diversion was the
smallest of the three jobs" tension depends on the reader feeling unit *sizes*. One short
gloss early — e.g. on first use, "a division (several thousand men), part of a corps (bigger
still), part of an army (the whole force)" — fixes nearly all of it at once. SHOULD also gloss
the rank ladder once (general ranks, biggest to smallest).

### M2 — The casualty-breakdown paragraph (§the-charge) asks the reader to "read the breakdown" with no key — NEEDS-GLOSS
"about 5,824 casualties (753 killed, 4,722 wounded, 349 missing)… about 6,667 — but read the
breakdown… Only 361 killed and 2,160 wounded. And **4,146 captured**."
Problems for a cold reader:
- "**casualties**" is never defined. A newcomer often thinks "casualties = deaths." Here it
  means killed + wounded + missing + captured combined — and the entire point of the paragraph
  (an army that *broke and ran* rather than was *ground down*) collapses if the reader thinks
  casualties means deaths.
- "**captured**" vs "**missing**" — the argument hinges on 4,146 *captured* being the
  "signature of an army that broke and ran," but the reader is never told why captured = a
  collapse rather than a fair-fight loss. The prose asserts "the whole story jumps out" — it
  does NOT jump out for someone who doesn't already read casualty tables.
**Why MUST-FIX:** this is a load-bearing analytical beat (it's how the draft proves the rout),
and it's currently legible only to readers who already know the vocabulary. One sentence
glossing "casualties" (killed + wounded + missing + captured, all the men lost to the fight,
not just the dead) and one clause explaining why mass *captures* mean a collapse would fix it.

### M3 — "rifle pits" introduced, but the two-line / base-vs-crest geography is the crux and is hard to hold — NEEDS-GLOSS
"rifle pits" IS glossed on first use ("shallow trenches for infantry") — good. But the spatial
setup the entire battle turns on is dense:
- There are **two Confederate lines** — one at the **base** of the ridge, one at the **crest** —
  and the reader must track which is which through three sections. The draft says it, but a
  newcomer juggling "base rifle pits," "the first line," "the second Confederate line," "the
  upper works," "the crest" can lose the thread of which trench is where.
**Why MUST-FIX (borderline — strong SHOULD):** the "pinned at the bottom, fire coming from the
top, only way out is up" logic is the emotional engine of the charge. It mostly works, but the
proliferation of names for the same two lines risks blurring. Recommend: name them once,
plainly and stick to it — "the lower trenches (at the foot)" and "the top line (on the crest)."

## SHOULD-FIX (a cold reader gets it, but stumbles or loses richness)

### S1 — "military crest" vs "topographic/physical crest" — NEEDS-GLOSS (but already half-handled)
§the-order glosses it inline: "the **military crest** — the line a little below the true top,
the place from which defenders can actually see and shoot down the whole face of the slope."
That gloss is GOOD and CLEAR. The stumble: the terms "topographic crest," "physical crest," and
"true crest" are all used for the SAME thing within a few lines, and a cold reader may not
immediately register they're synonyms. Pick one term ("the true top") and stay with it.

### S2 — Army proper-names used as characters before the reader knows they're units — NEEDS-GLOSS
The draft uses standing-army proper nouns as if they're known characters:
- "**the Army of the Cumberland**" — eventually contextualized ("the army that had been beaten
  at Chickamauga"), so it lands, but the FIRST appearance (§the-siege line 13) assumes the
  reader knows it's a named Union field army, not a place or a generic phrase.
- "**the Army of the Tennessee**" (§the-plan) and "**the Army of the Potomac (the main Union
  army back in Virginia)**" — the Potomac one IS glossed parenthetically (good). Cumberland and
  Tennessee are not, and "Army of the Tennessee" (Union) vs "Army of Tennessee" (Confederate,
  appears in §the-charge/§the-meaning) are nearly identical names for OPPOSITE sides — a real
  trap for a newcomer. The draft never flags that these two near-identical names are enemies.
**Fix:** gloss each field army once as "(a named Union/Confederate field army)" on first use,
and explicitly flag the Army of the Tennessee (Union) ≠ Army of Tennessee (Confederate) collision.

### S3 — "rear guard" — NEEDS-GLOSS
§the-plan and §the-charge: Cleburne "became the rear guard," "peeling off to form the rear
guard," "save the army's wagons." A newcomer may not know "rear guard" = the force left behind
to fight off pursuers and cover a retreat. One clause fixes it.

### S4 — "color-bearers" — NEEDS-GLOSS
§the-charge: "first a few **color-bearers** and knots of men scrambling up." Undefined; a
newcomer won't know these are the soldiers carrying the unit's flag (and why they're first up
matters — the flag leads the charge). Brief gloss.

### S5 — "piecemeal" — minor, NEEDS-GLOSS
§the-plan: "The attacks went in **piecemeal**." Comprehensible from context but the military
sense (sent in a few at a time instead of all at once, so each wave is beaten separately) is
the actual point and is worth a two-word gloss.

### S6 — "Vicksburg" used as a known prior victory — NEEDS-GLOSS
"fresh off **splitting the Confederacy at Vicksburg** that summer" (§the-siege); "the troops
Sherman and Grant had won with at Vicksburg" (§the-plan). The phrase "splitting the
Confederacy" gestures at significance but a cold reader doesn't know what Vicksburg was. It's a
cross-reference to another battle (like Chickamauga and Lookout Mountain, which use **(see X)**
tags) — but Vicksburg gets NO "(see…)" tag and no gloss. Either tag it or add a half-line gloss.

### S7 — "Chickamauga" leans on a cross-ref the cold reader may not have read — CLEAR-ish but watch
The draft handles Chickamauga well: it's glossed in-prose ("beaten at Chickamauga… driven back
into Chattanooga in a near-rout"), so even without the **(see Chickamauga)** tag a newcomer
understands it was a prior Union defeat at this same place. The emotional payoff ("Chickamauga!
Chickamauga!" as a revenge cry) lands. CLEAR — just confirm the (see Chickamauga) link is live
so a curious reader can follow it.

### S8 — "the Cracker Line" — CLEAR (handled)
§the-siege glosses it adequately in context ("cracked open a supply route — the 'Cracker
Line' — and got food and men flowing back in"). A newcomer gets it. No action needed.

### S9 — "lieutenant general / general-in-chief / general in chief" — NEEDS-GLOSS
§the-meaning: "promoted to **lieutenant general** and given command of *all* the Union armies —
**general-in-chief**." The draft does gloss "command of all the Union armies," which carries the
meaning — but "lieutenant general" sounds (to a newcomer) like a *lower* rank than the "Maj.
Gen." used all battle, when it's actually higher. Worth one clause: "lieutenant general (a rank
above any he'd held, the top of the army)."

### S10 — "secede / secession / the rebellion" — CLEAR (handled)
§the-siege: "The Confederacy had **seceded**… to protect and extend the enslavement of four
million Black people." Secession's meaning (the South splitting off) is carried by context and
the slavery-cause framing. A cold reader gets the gist. CLEAR.

### S11 — "the dossier card" / "[VERIFY]" / fact-ledger apparatus — NOT reader-facing
The §OUTCOME block, the [VERIFY] flags, the placement notes, and the entire "Fact ledger"
section are pipeline scaffolding, not shipped prose. Excluded from the cold-read (a reader will
never see them). Noting only so the integrator strips them — they are NOT clarity defects.

## SIDE-TAGS / SCENE-SET CHECK (named actors)

Side-tags are applied consistently on first mention per section — Thomas (North), Bragg (South),
Grant (North), Sherman (North), Hooker (North), Cleburne (South), Wood (North), Granger (North),
Sheridan (North), Willich (North), Hazen (North), Breckinridge (South), Hardee (South). All
tagged. CLEAR on the side-tag requirement.

Scene-setting for marquee actors is strong: Thomas ("the Rock of Chickamauga"), Grant (Vicksburg
+ "came to break the siege"), Cleburne ("one of the finest division commanders"), Breckinridge
("had been Vice President of the United States only a few years earlier" — excellent cold-reader
hook). Two thinner intros worth a beat of scene-set:
- **Willich and Hazen** (§the-charge) appear only as names rolling up the line — fine as
  spear-carriers, no fix needed.
- **Granger** (§the-charge) is introduced one clause before delivering the battle's signature
  line; "his IV Corps commander" is the only context. Tie to M1 (gloss "corps") and he's fine.
