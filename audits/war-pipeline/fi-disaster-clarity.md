## MUST-FIX (Comprehension Breakers)

**Count: 3**

### 1. "honors of war" — never defined, but the entire FWH massacre section depends on it [LOST]

The Fort William Henry section explains that warriors "had not crossed half a continent for a European general's idea of an honorable surrender," and that "the surrender terms handed all of that to the British and gave the warriors nothing." This is the narrative crux. But a zero-knowledge reader has no idea what "honors of war" means — that the defenders get to march out armed and free, unplundered. Without that, the warriors' grievance is incomprehensible: the reader can't understand what was *taken from them* by the terms. Add a one-sentence inline gloss on first use (Oswego): something like "the full honors of war — the right to march out with weapons, flags, and dignity intact, as opposed to surrendering as prisoners to be stripped and held."

### 2. "regulars" vs. "provincials" — distinction is structural but never defined [NEEDS-GLOSS]

The friction between British regular soldiers and colonial (provincial) troops is a constant drumbeat: regulars fire on provincials at the Monongahela, Shirley can't make "the colonies and the regulars work together," the whole chapter hinges on this fault line. Neither term is defined. The linked sidebar [Regulars & Provincials] exists but is embedded mid-section; a reader skipping it (or reading a plain-text version) will never get the definition. One sentence on first use in the intro paragraph or the Braddock section would fix it.

### 3. "parole" — the FWH surrender terms read as contradictory without it [NEEDS-GLOSS]

"The garrison would march to Fort Edward with the full honors of war, keeping their colors, their muskets, and one symbolic cannon, *on parole not to fight France for eighteen months*." A 15-year-old will read "parole" as early-release-from-prison and wonder why freed soldiers can't fight. The word means a formal gentleman's oath — a surrendered soldier's promise not to re-engage for a set period in exchange for freedom rather than captivity. One parenthetical phrase fixes it: "on parole (a formal oath not to re-engage) not to fight France for eighteen months."

## SHOULD-FIX (Clarity Improvements)

### 4. "sortie" — minor jargon, easy inline fix
"The French sortie collided with the British advance guard" → something like "the French attack party (a sortie, a rush out from the fort to meet the enemy)." Low stakes but real jargon.

### 5. "aide-de-camp" — minor, one-word fix
"serving as an unpaid aide-de-camp" → "serving as an unpaid aide-de-camp (a general's personal military assistant)." It's a throwaway line so the bar is low, but it's genuine jargon.

### 6. "grapeshot" — easy parenthetical
"cannon, loaded with grapeshot" → "cannon, loaded with grapeshot (clusters of iron balls that sprayed like buckshot at close range)." The scene already reads well; this just lets a zero-knowledge reader picture it.

### 7. "grenadiers" — one-word context fix
"regular grenadiers" could read as "soldiers who throw grenades." A brief tag like "grenadiers (elite front-line soldiers)" avoids the mismatch.

### 8. "35th Foot" — low stakes, easy drop
"career officer of the 35th Foot" → "career officer in the 35th Infantry Regiment" (or just cut "of the 35th Foot" — it adds nothing to the narrative).

## Overall Verdict

The draft reads well for a knowledgeable adult. For a zero-knowledge 15-year-old, the structural vocabulary ("regulars," "provincials," "honors of war," "parole") is the problem — those four terms carry the weight of entire sections, and three of them are undefined. The prose is NOT overwhelming anywhere; sentence lengths are controlled, scenes are set before people enter them, and the Acadian section in particular is a model of clear, humane storytelling. Fix the three MUST-FIX items and this clears the gate.
