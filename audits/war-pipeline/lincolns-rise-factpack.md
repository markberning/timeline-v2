## BEAT 1: Lincoln's Biography in Brief

- **Birth:** February 12, 1809, in a one-room log cabin on Sinking Spring Farm, near Hodgenville, Hardin County, Kentucky.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln" (citing standard biographical record)

- **Formal education:** Less than 12 months total, accumulated by age 15. Self-educated thereafter. Taught himself law by reading Blackstone's *Commentaries on the Laws of England* without formal law-school training.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"; Library of Congress, "Abraham Lincoln: Rise to National Prominence"

- **Illinois state legislature:** Served four terms representing Sangamon County, December 1, 1834 – December 4, 1842. Championed canal construction; successfully advocated moving the state capital from Vandalia to Springfield (completed 1837).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"

- **U.S. House of Representatives:** Represented Illinois's 7th Congressional District, March 4, 1847 – March 3, 1849. One term (had pledged in 1846 to serve only one). A Whig.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"; American Battlefield Trust, "Abraham Lincoln"

- **"Spot Resolutions" (December 1847):** During the Mexican-American War, Lincoln challenged President Polk to name the exact "spot" where American blood had first been shed, arguing it may have been on Mexican soil. This was politically unpopular.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"

- **Post-Congress return to law:** Lincoln declined appointment as secretary of the Oregon Territory, fearing it would disrupt his Illinois legal and political career. Returned to Springfield law practice ~1849–1854.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"

- **Whig Party collapse:** The Whigs effectively disintegrated after the bruising 1852 presidential election (candidate Winfield Scott lost to Democrat Franklin Pierce). Key Whig leaders Henry Clay and Daniel Webster both died in 1852. Most Northern Whigs migrated to the new anti-slavery Republican Party.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Whig Party (United States)"; History.com, "Why the Whig Party Collapsed"

- **Return to politics galvanized by Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854):** The act — which opened previously closed western territories to the possibility of slavery via popular sovereignty — provoked Lincoln's public re-entry into politics. He delivered a major anti-Kansas-Nebraska speech at Peoria, Illinois, October 16, 1854, considered the turning point of his return to political life.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"; Teaching American History, "Speech on the Repeal of the Missouri Compromise (Peoria Address)"

- **Joined Republican Party:** After the Whigs collapsed in the wake of Kansas-Nebraska, Lincoln joined the newly formed Republican Party. The first Illinois Republican organizing convention was held at Bloomington, May 29, 1854 (also called the "Anti-Nebraska" convention). Lincoln formally aligned with the Republicans by 1856.
  — Source: Northern Illinois University Digital Library, "The Kansas-Nebraska Act and the Rise of the Republican Party, 1854–1856"; Wikipedia, "Political career of Abraham Lincoln (1849–1861)"

- **"Honest Abe" nickname:** One of Lincoln's earliest campaign monikers, earned through his reputation for personal integrity (associated in popular lore with returning borrowed books and scrupulous dealings as a young store clerk and surveyor). Its precise first-use date in print is not established in available sources — treat as organic, early-career reputation rather than a coined slogan.
  — Source: Various (nickname origin sources are imprecise; flag as contested — see CONTESTED section)

- **"Rail-Splitter" / "The Rail Candidate" nickname:** Originated at the Illinois State Republican Convention at Decatur, May 9, 1860. Lincoln's supporter Richard J. Oglesby arranged for Lincoln's cousin John Hanks to march into the convention hall carrying two fence rails, placarded "Abraham Lincoln, The Rail Candidate for President in 1860." The fence rails were said to have been split by Lincoln circa 1830. Lincoln himself said he could not be certain they were the exact rails, but that he "had mauled many and many better ones since he had grown to manhood." The nickname spread to the national convention in Chicago and became a major campaign asset capitalizing on his frontier/working-class origins.
  — Source: Smithsonian Magazine, "The Legend of Lincoln's Fence Rail"; Encyclopedia.com, "Rail Splitter"

## BEAT 2: The 1858 Illinois Senate Race vs. Stephen A. Douglas

- **Douglas background:** Stephen Arnold Douglas, born April 23, 1813, Brandon, Vermont. Democrat. Had been an Illinois senator since 1847. Height: 5 feet 4 inches — hence the nickname "the Little Giant," coined because his small physical stature was wildly at odds with his forceful, dominant political presence.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Stephen A. Douglas"; U.S. Senate, "Stephen A. Douglas: A Featured Biography"

- **Douglas authored the Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854):** As chairman of the Senate Committee on Territories, Douglas proposed organizing Nebraska and Kansas territories under "popular sovereignty" — letting territorial settlers decide slavery's status — which required repealing the Missouri Compromise line (36°30'). Lincoln considered this a moral and political catastrophe and it became the central issue of their 1858 contest.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Stephen A. Douglas"

- **Douglas was the Democratic incumbent** seeking a third Senate term in 1858. He had a split with President James Buchanan over the Lecompton Constitution for Kansas (Buchanan wanted to impose a pro-slavery Kansas constitution over the will of Kansas voters; Douglas opposed it on popular-sovereignty grounds). This put Douglas at odds with the Southern wing of his own party going into 1858.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Stephen A. Douglas"

- **The race:** Illinois Republicans nominated Lincoln as their Senate candidate on June 16, 1858, at the Republican State Convention in Springfield (the same day he delivered the "House Divided" speech — see Beat 3).

- **Popular-vote outcome:** Republican candidates for the Illinois legislature collectively received MORE votes statewide than Democrats — approximately 125,000 Republican votes vs. 121,000 Democratic votes; the Republican margin was roughly 3,402 votes, or 50.6% of the statewide legislative vote. Republicans also outpolled Douglas-aligned candidates by 24,094 votes in legislative races.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"; various Illinois election records

- **Legislature vote outcome (January 5, 1859):** Under the system then in place (U.S. senators were elected by state legislatures, not direct popular vote — the 17th Amendment direct-election provision was not ratified until 1913), the Illinois General Assembly met in joint session January 5, 1859, and re-elected Douglas 54–46. The Democratic advantage reflected district-boundary apportionment that favored Democrats despite Republican popular-vote superiority.
  — Source: Illinois State Archives, "House Journal Entry on the Election of Stephen Douglas to the Senate (1859)"; Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"

- **Lincoln lost, but his national profile soared:** The debates were reported and reprinted nationwide. Lincoln's performance established him as a serious national figure in the Republican Party.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"; Britannica, "Lincoln-Douglas debates"

## BEAT 3: The "House Divided" Speech (June 16, 1858)

- **Context:** Delivered June 16, 1858, at the Illinois State Capitol (now the Old State Capitol), Springfield, Illinois, upon Lincoln's acceptance of the Republican nomination for the U.S. Senate.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln's House Divided Speech"; Miller Center, "June 16, 1858: 'A House Divided' Speech"

- **Venue and audience:** The Illinois Republican State Convention, approximately 1,000 delegates and observers in attendance.
  — Source: Various (crowd estimate ~1,000 from context; flag as imprecise — see CONTESTED)

- **The famous opening passage (VERBATIM, from *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln*, ed. Roy P. Basler et al.):**

  > "If we could first know where we are, and whither we are tending, we could then better judge what to do, and how to do it.
  >
  > We are now far into the fifth year, since a policy was initiated, with the avowed object, and confident promise, of putting an end to slavery agitation. Under the operation of that policy, that agitation has not only, not ceased, but has constantly augmented. In my opinion, it will not cease, until a crisis shall have been reached, and passed.
  >
  > 'A house divided against itself cannot stand.' I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free. I do not expect the Union to be dissolved — I do not expect the house to fall — but I do expect it will cease to be divided. It will become all one thing, or all the other. Either the opponents of slavery will arrest the further spread of it, and place it where the public mind shall rest in the belief that it is in the course of ultimate extinction; or its advocates will push it forward, till it shall become alike lawful in all the States, old as well as new — North as well as South."

  — Source: Abraham Lincoln Online, "House Divided Speech" (text from *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln*, Roy P. Basler ed.); Miller Center presidential speeches archive; Teaching American History

- **Biblical source of "a house divided":** The phrase is drawn from Mark 3:25 (King James Bible: "And if a house be divided against itself, that house cannot stand."). Lincoln cited the biblical phrase, then applied it as political metaphor.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln's House Divided Speech"

- **Reception:** Some Republican allies, including allies in Illinois, privately worried the speech was too radical and would hurt Lincoln in the general campaign. Lincoln defended the speech, saying it expressed what he believed.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln's House Divided Speech"

## BEAT 4: The Seven Lincoln-Douglas Debates (August–October 1858)

- **Total debates:** Seven, all in Illinois.

- **Dates and locations (all 1858):**
  1. Ottawa — August 21
  2. Freeport — August 27
  3. Jonesboro — September 15
  4. Charleston — September 18
  5. Galesburg — October 7
  6. Quincy — October 13
  7. Alton — October 15
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"

- **Format:** Each debate ran approximately three hours, with no moderator. Structure: Opening speaker (60 minutes) → response by second speaker (90 minutes) → rejoinder by opening speaker (30 minutes). Douglas and Lincoln alternated who opened; as incumbent, Douglas opened four of the seven debates. Held outdoors, typically afternoon.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"

- **Crowd estimates (reported by newspapers of the time — treat as estimates):**
  - Ottawa: ~12,000
  - Freeport: ~15,000
  - Jonesboro: not reliably reported
  - Charleston: not reliably reported
  - Galesburg: ~16,000–18,000
  - Quincy: ~12,000
  - Alton: ~5,000–10,000
  Debates near state borders (Freeport, Quincy, Alton) drew large numbers from neighboring states.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"; Lincoln-Douglas Society of Freeport

- **Central subject:** Whether slavery should be allowed to expand into the western territories. Lincoln argued that popular sovereignty was a moral sham that allowed slavery's spread; Douglas argued that popular sovereignty was democratic self-determination.

- **The Freeport Question and Doctrine:**
  At the second debate, Freeport (August 27, 1858), Lincoln posed the question to Douglas:
  > "Can the people of a United States Territory, in any lawful way, against the wish of any citizen of the United States, exclude slavery from its limits prior to the formation of a State Constitution?"

  Douglas's answer (the Freeport Doctrine): "The people have the lawful means to introduce it, or exclude it as they please, for the reason that slavery cannot exist a day or an hour anywhere, unless it is supported by local police regulations."

  Douglas elaborated in the same debate, saying territories could exclude slavery by "unfriendly legislation" — by simply refusing to pass the slave codes and local laws that made slavery viable in practice, even if the Dred Scott decision (1857) said Congress could not prohibit slavery in territories. The key phrase from Douglas's Freeport debate remarks: "Those police regulations can only be established by the local legislature; and if the people are opposed to slavery, they will elect representatives to that body who will by unfriendly legislation effectually prevent the introduction of it into their midst."
  — Source: NPS Lincoln Home, "Freeport Doctrine"; Wikipedia, "Freeport Doctrine"; Lincoln-Douglas debates primary text, Teaching American History

- **Why the Freeport Doctrine cost Douglas the South in 1860:** Southern slaveholders heard Douglas say slavery's survival depended on the goodwill of territorial legislatures — not on a constitutional guarantee. They demanded a federal slave code that would protect slavery in all territories regardless of what local legislatures decided. Douglas refused to endorse such a code. This gulf between Douglas and Southern Democrats was a primary driver of the party's split in 1860 (see Beat 8).
  — Source: NPS Lincoln Home, "Freeport Doctrine"; Wikipedia, "Freeport Doctrine"

## BEAT 5: Outcome of the 1858 Senate Race — Lincoln Loses, Becomes National Figure

- **Legislature vote:** January 5, 1859 — Douglas re-elected senator by the Illinois General Assembly, 54–46 (see Beat 2 for full detail).
  — Source: Illinois State Archives; Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"

- **Republican popular-vote margin:** Republicans outpolled Democrats by roughly 3,400 votes statewide (50.6%); Republican legislative candidates received 24,094 more votes than Douglas-aligned candidates. Lincoln effectively lost despite winning the popular vote, due to malapportioned legislative districts.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"

- **Debates published as a book:** Lincoln edited and compiled the debate transcripts; they were published in 1860 by Follett, Foster & Company of Columbus, Ohio, under the title *Political Debates Between Hon. Abraham Lincoln and Hon. Stephen A. Douglas*. The book had at least four printings; one printing alone sold ~16,000 copies. Total sales estimates vary: some sources cite 30,000+ copies sold, others up to 50,000 across editions. It became a de facto campaign document introducing Lincoln to Northern voters nationwide. [CONTESTED — see note below on sales figures]
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"; RR Auction; Shapell Foundation

- **Lincoln's comment after losing:** Lincoln reportedly wrote to a friend shortly after the loss: "I am glad I made the late race. It gave me a hearing on the great and durable question of the age, which I could have had in no other way; and though I now sink out of view, and shall be forgotten, I believe I have made some marks which will tell for the cause of civil liberty long after I am gone." [Treat as UNCERTAIN — sourcing varies; flag as paraphrase unless verified from *Collected Works*]

## BEAT 6: The Cooper Union Address (February 27, 1860)

- **Date and venue:** February 27, 1860. Cooper Union (Cooper Institute) Great Hall, New York City.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"; NPS Lincoln Home, "Cooper Union Address"

- **Who invited him:** Henry Ward Beecher initially invited Lincoln to speak at Plymouth Church in Brooklyn; the Young Men's Central Republican Union sponsored the event at Cooper Union instead.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"

- **Introduced by:** William Cullen Bryant, editor of the *New York Evening Post*.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"

- **Mathew Brady photograph:** Brady photographed Lincoln on the afternoon of February 27, 1860 — the same day as the speech. Lincoln later said: "Brady and the Cooper Institute made me President." [NOTE: This attribution is widely cited but treat as POSSIBLY APOCRYPHAL — verify from *Collected Works* before using. Standard practice is to note it as a quote "attributed to Lincoln." See CONTESTED section.]
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech" (Brady connection); NPS Lincoln Home

- **Crowd size:** Approximately 1,500 people on "a snowy night."
  — Source: NPS Lincoln Home, "The Cooper Union Address: The Making of a Candidate"

- **Central argument:** Lincoln conducted extensive historical research in Springfield (using Jonathan Elliott's *Debates in the Several State Conventions on the Adoption of the Federal Constitution* and other founding-era sources) to argue that a clear majority of the Constitution's 39 signers had previously acted in ways that demonstrated Congress had the power — and according to founding precedent, the duty — to restrict slavery's expansion into territories. Lincoln's count: 21 of 39 signers had directly voted or acted to restrict slavery in territories; he argued 15 more of the remaining 16 were anti-slavery in their general views; final tally presented: 36-to-3 among the framers that Congress could regulate slavery in territories.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"; NPS Lincoln Home, "Summary of Lincoln's Arguments at Cooper Union"

- **Closing lines (VERBATIM, from *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln*, ed. Basler):**

  > "Neither let us be slandered from our duty by false accusations against us, nor frightened from it by menaces of destruction to the Government nor of dungeons to ourselves. LET US HAVE FAITH THAT RIGHT MAKES MIGHT, AND IN THAT FAITH, LET US, TO THE END, DARE TO DO OUR DUTY AS WE UNDERSTAND IT."

  — Source: Abraham Lincoln Online, "Cooper Union Address" (text from *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln*); Voices of Democracy, University of Maryland

- **Press reception:**
  - *New-York Tribune* (Horace Greeley): "One of the most happiest and most convincing political arguments ever made in this City. ... Since the days of Clay and Webster, no man has spoken to a larger assemblage of the intellect and mental culture of our City. Mr. Lincoln is one of Nature's orators..."
  - Greeley also wrote: "No man ever before made such an impression on his first appeal to a New-York audience."
  - The *New York Times*, *Evening Post*, and three other New York papers all printed the full speech text. Chicago, Detroit, and Albany papers reprinted it.
  — Source: NPS Lincoln Home, "The Cooper Union Address: The Making of a Candidate"; Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"

- **New England speaking tour:** After Cooper Union, New England Republicans invited Lincoln to speak across the region. He spoke in eleven cities in twelve days (including appearances in New Hampshire, Connecticut, and Rhode Island).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"; NPS Lincoln Home

- **Impact on candidacy:** Historians — notably Harold Holzer in his book *Lincoln at Cooper Union: The Speech That Made Abraham Lincoln President* (2004) — argue the speech transformed Lincoln from a regional figure into a genuine national contender. "Lincoln's watershed, the event that transformed him from a regional leader into a national phenomenon" (Holzer).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"; University of Michigan/JALA, review of Holzer's book

## BEAT 7: The 1860 Republican National Convention

- **Dates:** May 16–18, 1860.
- **Location:** Chicago, Illinois.
- **Venue:** "The Wigwam" — a temporary wood-frame structure built specifically for the convention in about one month. Capacity: approximately 10,000–12,000 delegates, guests, and observers.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"; Wikipedia, "Wigwam (Chicago)"; Encyclopedia of Chicago History

- **Front-runner:** Senator William H. Seward of New York, former governor, considered the most prominent Republican nationally. Had strong Whig credentials. His weakness: perceived as too radical (his "higher law" and "irrepressible conflict" speeches alarmed moderates), opposed nativism (cost him in German-American communities), and had made enemies including influential editor Horace Greeley.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"

- **Other major candidates:** Gov. Salmon P. Chase of Ohio; former congressman/judge Edward Bates of Missouri; Sen. Simon Cameron of Pennsylvania. All had significant delegate support before balloting.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"

- **Why Lincoln won:**
  - Moderate position on slavery (opposed its expansion but did not call for immediate abolition where it existed)
  - Illinois was a critical swing state; Lincoln was its favorite son
  - Lincoln's team — including campaign manager David Davis, Leonard Swett, and Ward Hill Lamon — worked through the night of May 17–18 to win anti-Seward delegates; they reportedly distributed counterfeit admission tickets to pack the Wigwam gallery with Lincoln supporters
  - Lincoln had positioned himself as "the second choice of most delegates," correctly predicting no first-ballot winner would emerge
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"; American Battlefield Trust, "Inside the Wigwam"

- **Lincoln did not attend the convention.** He remained in Springfield.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"

- **Ballot results:**

  | Candidate | 1st Ballot | 2nd Ballot | 3rd Ballot (final) |
  |-----------|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
  | Seward    | 173.5     | 184.5     | 111.5             |
  | Lincoln   | 102       | 181       | 350 (nominated)   |

  Lincoln won with 350 of 466 votes cast on the third ballot (needed ~234 for majority).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"

- **Vice-presidential nominee:** Sen. Hannibal Hamlin of Maine, 367 votes on the second VP ballot.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"

## BEAT 8: The Four-Way Election of 1860

### The Democratic Party's Self-Destruction

- **Charleston Convention (April 23 – May 3, 1860):** First Democratic convention, held at South Carolina Institute Hall, Charleston, South Carolina. Douglas was the frontrunner but never reached the required two-thirds supermajority. Convention chairman Caleb Cushing ruled that two-thirds of the *whole* convention membership (202 of 253 delegates) was required — not two-thirds of those present — making Douglas's path nearly impossible. Douglas maxed out at 152.5 votes across 57 ballots.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Democratic National Conventions"

- **The walkout:** Between April 30 and May 1, 51 Southern delegates walked out — full delegations from Alabama, Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina, and Texas, plus partial delegations from Arkansas and Delaware. Trigger: Southern fire-eaters demanded a platform explicitly endorsing the Dred Scott decision and a federal slave code protecting slavery in all territories; Northern delegates refused.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Democratic National Conventions"

- **Convention adjourned without nominee** on May 3, 1860.

- **Baltimore Convention (June 18–23, 1860):** Reconvened in Baltimore. Again ~110 Southern delegates walked out. Remaining delegates nominated **Stephen A. Douglas** (Northern Democrat) on the second Baltimore ballot (59th ballot overall), with **Herschel V. Johnson** (Georgia) as his vice-presidential running mate (after Benjamin Fitzpatrick of Alabama declined).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Democratic National Conventions"

- **Southern Democratic Convention (also Baltimore, June 1860):** Breakaway Southern Democrats nominated **John C. Breckinridge** of Kentucky (then incumbent Vice President of the United States) for president, with Sen. **Joseph Lane** of Oregon as his running mate. Platform: explicit federal protection of slavery in all territories.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Democratic National Conventions"

### The Four Candidates

| Candidate | Party | Base |
|-----------|-------|------|
| Abraham Lincoln | Republican | Northern free states only |
| Stephen A. Douglas | Northern Democrat | National (but strong only in North) |
| John C. Breckinridge | Southern Democrat | Slave states |
| John Bell | Constitutional Union | Border states (sought to avoid the slavery question entirely) |

- **Constitutional Union Party:** Formed in 1860 by remnants of the old Whig and Know-Nothing parties. Nominated **John Bell** of Tennessee. Platform: support the Constitution, the laws, and the Union — deliberately silent on slavery. Aimed at border states that feared disunion.
  — Source: Britannica, "United States presidential election of 1860"

### Lincoln Not on the Ballot in the South

- Lincoln received **no votes** in ten Southern states where the Republican Party had not organized and printed electors: Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, and Texas.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1860 United States presidential election"; Emerging Civil War, "Dude, Where's My Candidate?: Lincoln, the Ballot, and the Election of 1860"

## BEAT 10: Secession Winter (December 1860 – February 1861)

### Sequence of Secessions

- **South Carolina:** Voted to secede from the United States, **December 20, 1860**. Vote was unanimous: **169–0** by the state convention.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Secession" (South Carolina); Charleston Daily, original Charleston Mercury transcript

- **South Carolina's "Declaration of the Immediate Causes Which Induce and Justify the Secession of South Carolina"** (December 24, 1860): Cited "increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the Institution of Slavery" and the election of a president (Lincoln) "whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery."
  — Source: Teaching American History, "Declaration of the Causes of Secession"

- **Deep South secessions (all by February 1, 1861):**
  | State | Secession Date |
  |-------|---------------|
  | Mississippi | January 9, 1861 |
  | Florida | January 10, 1861 |
  | Alabama | January 11, 1861 |
  | Georgia | January 19, 1861 |
  | Louisiana | January 26, 1861 |
  | Texas | February 1, 1861 |
  — Source: Wikipedia / AHA Chronology / ushistory.org, "The South Secedes"

- All seven seceded BEFORE Lincoln was inaugurated (March 4, 1861).

### Buchanan's Lame-Duck Paralysis

- **James Buchanan's Fourth Annual Message (State of the Union), December 3, 1860:** Buchanan denied that secession was constitutionally legitimate, but also denied that the federal government — specifically the president or Congress — had any power to prevent it or coerce a state to remain. His position: secession is illegal, but I cannot stop it. He stated "the sword was not placed in [Congress's] hand to preserve it by force."
  — Source: American Presidency Project, "Fourth Annual Message to Congress on the State of the Union"; AHA, "The Right to Secede"

- **Buchanan's posture** became the defining image of lame-duck paralysis: acknowledged the crisis, endorsed the Crittenden Compromise on January 8, 1861, as a resolution, but took no military or executive action to prevent Southern seizure of federal property.
  — Source: AHA Chronology of Major Events Leading to the Secession Crisis; ushistory.org

### The Crittenden Compromise (FAILED)

- **Proposed by:** Senator John J. Crittenden, Constitutional Unionist, Kentucky. Introduced **December 18, 1860**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Crittenden Compromise"

- **What it proposed:** Six constitutional amendments, the most important being: extend the old Missouri Compromise line (36°30') westward to the Pacific Ocean; guarantee slavery the protection of federal law in all territories south of that line, both "now held, or hereafter acquired"; prohibit Congress from ever abolishing slavery in the slave states.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Crittenden Compromise"

- **Lincoln's rejection:** President-elect Lincoln instructed Republican congressional leaders to oppose the compromise. His stated position: "Entertain no proposition for a compromise in regard to the extension of slavery," fearing it would "lose us everything we gained by the election." He fundamentally refused to accept any policy that permitted the continued expansion of slavery.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Crittenden Compromise"

- **Senate vote:** The Crittenden Compromise was killed in the Senate on **January 16, 1861**. Vote: **25 against, 23 in favor**. All 25 "nay" votes were cast by Republicans. Six senators from states already in secession abstained.
  — Source: History.com, "Crittenden Compromise is killed in Senate"; U.S. Senate History

- **Peace Conference of 1861:** A follow-on effort convened February 8–27, 1861 in Washington, D.C., with delegates from 21 states. It also failed to produce an acceptable compromise.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Crittenden Compromise"

### The 4-Month Gap

- Lincoln was elected November 6, 1860 but was not inaugurated until **March 4, 1861** — a constitutional gap of nearly four months during which he was president-elect with no executive power.
  — Source: NPS, "Lincoln's First Inauguration"; White House Historical Association, "Inauguration of 1861"

- During these four months: all seven Deep South states seceded; the Confederate government was organized in Montgomery (Feb 4–9, 1861 — belongs to "Two Governments" section); and Buchanan presided over the dissolution without effective action.

- Lincoln was "powerless to take any official action" as president-elect, and he deliberately avoided committing publicly to a detailed course of action before taking office.
  — Source: NPS, "Lincoln's First Inauguration"

### Star of the West Incident (January 9, 1861)

- **Date:** January 9, 1861.
- **What happened:** President Buchanan authorized sending the civilian merchant ship *Star of the West* to resupply Major Robert Anderson's Union garrison at Fort Sumter, Charleston Harbor (after South Carolina had seized other federal property following secession). The ship was chosen instead of a military vessel to reduce the appearance of provocation.
- **The firing:** As the *Star of the West* attempted to enter Charleston Harbor, South Carolina artillery batteries on Morris Island — manned in part by cadets from The Citadel — opened fire. The gunner who fired the first shots was George E. Haynsworth, a Citadel cadet. The ship was hit three times (no major damage).
- **Outcome:** Captain John McGowan turned the ship around and retreated to New York Harbor. The resupply mission was abandoned. Fort Sumter remained under Anderson's command, but without fresh supplies.
- **Significance:** Sometimes called the "first shots" of the Civil War — the first time South Carolina forces fired on a U.S. government vessel. It did not trigger open war; that standoff continued until the Confederate bombardment of Fort Sumter in April 1861 (next section's territory).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Star of the West"; History.com, "Star of the West is fired upon, January 9, 1861"; American Battlefield Trust

## CONTESTED / FLAGGED

### 1. Lincoln's Popular Vote Total (HIGH PRIORITY — USE RANGE)
- **The discrepancy:** UCSB American Presidency Project gives Lincoln 1,867,198 (39.8%); Wikipedia gives 1,855,276 (39.67%). Douglas's total varies wildly between sources: UCSB gives 1,379,434 (29.4%) vs. Wikipedia's 1,004,042 (21.47%).
- **Reason:** 19th-century vote counting was inconsistent; some states' returns were partially rejected or double-counted in different tallies. The UCSB figures are generally considered more reliable in the historiographic literature.
- **Author guidance:** Use "roughly 1.86–1.87 million votes, just under 40% of the popular vote" for Lincoln. Do not give a single definitive figure. The electoral total (180) is undisputed.

### 2. "Honest Abe" Nickname — Origin Uncertain
- Multiple sources attribute it vaguely to Lincoln's personal integrity as a young man (store clerk, postmaster, lawyer). No single documented first use has been found in available sources.
- **Author guidance:** Treat as an organic reputation rather than a coined slogan. Do not attribute a specific origin story without further research in primary sources (e.g., Lincoln newspapers from the 1830s–40s).

### 3. The Brady Quote ("Brady and the Cooper Institute made me President")
- Widely cited (Lincoln supposedly said this after winning the presidency). However, it is not confirmed in the *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln* in the searches performed.
- **Author guidance:** Attribute as "Lincoln reportedly said" or "attributed to Lincoln" — do not present as a verified quote. Check *Collected Works* (vol. 3–4) before use.

### 4. Lincoln-Douglas Debate Book — Sales Figures
- Sources disagree: one figure is ~30,000 copies; another cites up to 50,000 across editions. The fourth printing alone sold ~16,000.
- **Author guidance:** "Tens of thousands of copies" or "more than 30,000 copies" is supportable. Avoid a single round number without noting the range.

### 5. House Divided Speech Audience Size
- Available sources give ~1,000 for the Republican State Convention audience. This is softer than the debate crowd figures.
- **Author guidance:** "About 1,000 Republican convention delegates" is reasonable but flag as imprecise.

### 6. Crittenden Compromise — Lincoln's Exact Private Communications
- The quote "Entertain no proposition for a compromise in regard to the extension of slavery" appears in standard secondary sources but should be verified in the *Collected Works* (likely a letter to Lyman Trumbull or similar, late 1860) before quoting verbatim.
- **Author guidance:** The substance is accurate and well-sourced; the exact wording should be confirmed from *Collected Works* vol. 4 before using in quotation marks.

### 7. Douglas's Popular Vote — Major Source Discrepancy
- UCSB gives Douglas 1,379,434 (29.4%); Wikipedia gives 1,004,042 (21.47%). The difference is enormous (~375,000 votes). This likely reflects different handling of fusion tickets (Douglas + Bell fusion slates in some border states). UCSB is the preferred source, but flag for author.
