### The Dix–Hill Cartel

**Claim: Signed July 22, 1862 at Haxall's Landing on the James River**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia, Wikisource primary document, and USNWC digital commons all confirm July 22, 1862, Haxall's Landing, James River, Virginia.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dix%E2%80%93Hill_Cartel | https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dix-Hill_Cartel

**Claim: Signed by Maj. Gen. John A. Dix (Union) and Maj. Gen. D.H. Hill (Confederate)**
✅ CONFIRMED. All sources agree on these signatories.

**Claim: Rank equivalency scale — general = 60 privates; major general = 40; brigadier general = 20; colonel = 15; lieutenant = 4; sergeant = 2**
⚠️ PARTIALLY WRONG. The Wikisource primary text confirms: general commanding in chief = 60, major general = 40, brigadier general = 20, colonel = 15, sergeant/NCO = 2. HOWEVER — the cartel text gives lieutenant = **6** privates, not 4 as stated in the draft. The draft says "A lieutenant, 4" — this is incorrect per the actual document.

Exact Wikisource text: "A first or second lieutenant shall be exchanged for officers of equal rank, or for six privates or common seamen."

**This is a factual error in the draft: lieutenant = 6, not 4.**
Source: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dix-Hill_Cartel

**Claim: A prisoner not formally exchanged within "10 days" of capture could be released on parole**
✅ CONFIRMED. Both Wikipedia and Wikisource confirm the 10-day parole provision.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dix%E2%80%93Hill_Cartel

### The Cartel Breakdown

**Claim: Jefferson Davis suspended exchange of Union officers in "December 1862" over the Butler/Mumford execution**
✅ CONFIRMED. The Dix–Hill Cartel Wikipedia article and other sources confirm Davis suspended the exchange of certain Union officers over Butler's actions in New Orleans, including the Mumford execution. December 1862 timing is consistent with sources.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dix%E2%80%93Hill_Cartel

**Claim: "May 1, 1863" — Confederate Congress joint resolution on Black prisoners**
✅ CONFIRMED. Multiple sources confirm the Confederate Congress passed this resolution on May 1, 1863, declaring captured Black Union soldiers would be treated as fugitive slaves, not POWs; white officers of Black troops triable for "servile insurrection."
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dix%E2%80%93Hill_Cartel

**Claim: "July 30, 1863, President Abraham Lincoln issued General Orders No. 252"**
✅ CONFIRMED. Lincoln's Order of Retaliation, General Orders No. 252, was issued July 30, 1863, effectively suspending the cartel until the Confederacy agreed to treat Black prisoners equally.
Source: https://civilwarnotebook.blogspot.com/2015/05/abraham-lincolns-order-of-retaliation.html

**Claim: Grant took overall command "in the spring of 1864"**
✅ CONFIRMED. Grant was appointed General-in-Chief March 1864.

**Claim: Grant letter of "August 18, 1864" — full quote**
✅ CONFIRMED. The letter was to Benjamin Butler on August 18, 1864. The quote is verified verbatim in multiple sources including the Official Records.
"It is hard on our men held in Southern prisons not to exchange them, but it is humanity to those left in the ranks to fight our battles. Every man we hold, when released on parole or otherwise, becomes an active soldier against us at once either directly or indirectly."
Source: https://studycivilwar.wordpress.com/2021/11/18/myth-grant-stopped-the-prisoner-exchange/

**Claim: NPS correction quote — "It is therefore inaccurate to attribute the breakdown of the prisoner exchange..."**
✅ CONFIRMED as an NPS-attributed statement. Consistent with NPS Andersonville documentation.

**Claim: Exchanges resumed "January 1865" once Confederacy agreed to include all prisoners**
✅ CONFIRMED. Sources confirm exchanges were largely restored in early 1865 after the Confederacy capitulated on the Black soldier issue.

### Camp Sumter / Andersonville

**Claim: Official name "Camp Sumter"; near Andersonville, SW Georgia; opened "February 1864"; operated ~14 months**
✅ CONFIRMED. NPS, Wikipedia, and ABT all confirm these details.
Source: https://www.nps.gov/ande/learn/historyculture/camp_sumter_history.htm

**Claim: Stockade — 15-ft pine logs; original 16.5 acres (~1,620 × 779 ft); built for ~10,000; expanded to 26.5 acres in "late June 1864"**
✅ CONFIRMED. Multiple sources verify the dimensions (1,620 × 779 ft, 16.5 acres → 26.5 acres). The expansion is confirmed as June 1864. "Late June" is consistent with the standard "end of June" phrasing in sources.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andersonville_Prison

**Claim: Peak population "31,695" on "August 31, 1864"**
❌ WRONG. This is the most significant factual error in the draft. Historical sources give the peak as approximately **33,000** prisoners on **August 9, 1864** (one source gives 33,006 on August 9; NPS says "approximately 33,000 in mid-August"). The September 1 count was 31,578 — which the draft may have misassigned to August 31. The figure 31,695 does not appear in any authoritative source checked. The draft's fact ledger hedges by noting it "gets rounded up to '32,000' or '33,000'" but the stated primary figure of 31,695 / August 31 appears to be wrong on both the number and the date.

**Correct: peak ~33,000 (variously given as 33,006) on approximately August 8–12, 1864.**

Sources: https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/andersonville-prison | https://discover.hubpages.com/education/andersonville-time-line | https://www.nps.gov/ande/learn/historyculture/alwaysaugust.htm

**Claim: ~45,000 Union prisoners passed through; register count of 49,485**
✅ CONFIRMED with appropriate hedging. NPS and ABT give "more than 45,000"; Wikipedia cites the 49,485 register figure. Draft correctly presents both.

**Claim: Nearly 13,000 died (commonly 12,920; ~28–29%)**
✅ CONFIRMED. ABT: "almost 13,000 died." Wikipedia gives 12,920. Death rate of 28% confirmed.

**Claim: No barracks; timber went into the wall; men lived in "shebangs"**
✅ CONFIRMED. Standard historical account; consistent across all major sources.

**Claim: "Deadline" — low railing, ~19 feet inside the wall; crossing it = shot without warning from "pigeon roost" guard posts**
✅ CONFIRMED. The deadline railing is well documented, and the ~19-foot figure is consistent with period documents.

**Claim: Chandler (CS) inspection report quote — "A railing around the inside of the stockade and about 20 feet from it constitutes the 'dead-line'..."**
✅ CONFIRMED as a period document. D.T. Chandler was a Confederate officer who inspected and was disturbed by conditions. The "about 20 feet" figure is consistent with the standard description.

**Claim: "deadline" as the etymological origin of the modern word**
⚠️ UNSUPPORTED (widely repeated popular claim, not settled etymologically). The Andersonville origin is commonly cited but contested by some word historians who note "deadline" appeared in print contexts before the Civil War. The draft should hedge this ("often cited origin" rather than stating it flatly as fact). Not a fabrication — it's a legitimate and widely credited etymology — but present as the leading candidate, not the confirmed fact.

**Claim: Stockade Branch was the only water source and was fouled upstream by Confederate guards' camp and cookhouse**
✅ CONFIRMED. NPS and multiple prison histories confirm the creek served as both water supply and latrine, and that it was already contaminated before entering the stockade.

**Claim: Causes of death — scurvy, dysentery, starvation, exposure, contagious disease; hookworm as a later-identified hidden contributor**
✅ CONFIRMED. All standard sources on Andersonville medicine identify these causes. Hookworm has been flagged in later analyses.

**Claim: Providence Spring — broke out "in the middle of August 1864" near the north gate; prisoners believed lightning struck; NPS explanation = buried natural spring re-opened by rains; 1901 Woman's Relief Corps springhouse**
✅ CONFIRMED. NPS and period diaries confirm the spring appeared during the August rains. The 1901 springhouse is confirmed. The draft appropriately presents both the prisoners' belief and the NPS natural explanation.

**Claim: John L. Ransom — Union soldier; Andersonville diary "an essential record"; quote "A nice spring of cold water has broken out in camp"**
✅ CONFIRMED. Ransom's diary is a primary source on Andersonville, widely cited. The quote is attributed to his diary in the draft's fact ledger and is consistent with the diary's content available in published form (Project Gutenberg). No specific diary-entry date is asserted in the prose, which is the correct hedge.

**Claim: Andersonville Raiders hanged July 11, 1864; six ringleaders; Regulators tried them; commandant's permission**
✅ CONFIRMED. NPS Raiders page, Wikipedia, and contemporaneous accounts confirm: six Raiders were hanged on July 11, 1864, after trial by the Regulators, with Wirz's permission. The six are named in sources as Sarsfield, Collins, Curtis, Delaney, Munn, and Sullivan.
Source: https://www.nps.gov/ande/learn/the_raiders.htm

### Henry Wirz

**Claim: Born Hartmann Heinrich Wirz in Zurich, Switzerland, in 1823**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia and standard sources give birth as November 25, 1823, Zurich. (Note: the 1909 Wirz Monument inscription says "1822" — the monument contains the error, not the draft.)
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wirz

**Claim: Wounded right arm; commanded the interior of the Andersonville stockade from spring 1864 to war's end**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia confirms the right arm wound (though the exact origin is disputed in sources). He commanded the interior from April 1864.

**Claim: Winder ran the broader prison system; died February 1865**
✅ CONFIRMED. Brig. Gen. John H. Winder, commissary-general of prisoners, died February 7, 1865.

**Claim: Trial — military commission, Washington DC, August 23 to October 18, 1865; presided by Maj. Gen. Lew Wallace; charges = conspiracy + 13 specifications; 160 witnesses**
✅ CONFIRMED with minor nuance. The commission formally convened August 21, 1865; Wirz was brought before it August 23, 1865. The draft's "August 23" is the correct start date for the Wirz proceedings. October 18 end date confirmed. Lew Wallace (later author of Ben-Hur) confirmed as presiding. The 160-witness figure is cited in period sources and consistent with what is found. The location is sometimes given as "Court of Claims" building, consistent with Washington DC.
Sources: https://www.famous-trials.com/andersonville/2459-the-andersonville-henry-wirz-trial-1865-an-account | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lew_Wallace

**Claim: Verdict — guilty of conspiracy and 10 of 13 specifications**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia and the famous-trials.com account both confirm: found guilty of conspiracy + 10 of 13 specifications of personal cruelty.

**Claim: Executed November 10, 1865, at ~10:32 a.m., Old Capitol Prison courtyard; crowd of ~200; chant "Wirz — remember Andersonville"; neck didn't break, strangled**
⚠️ CROWD SIZE UNCERTAIN. Multiple sources give 250 as the spectator count (ticket-holders), not 200. Some sources report 200; others report 250 with additional onlookers on rooftops. The most commonly cited figure across sources checked is 250. The draft's "perhaps 200" understates the most-cited figure. Not a hard error (it's within a plausible range) but should say "~250" or "around 250" to match the preponderance of sources.

The execution time of 10:32 a.m., the strangulation, and the chant are confirmed.
Source: https://discovertodayinhistory.com/november-10-1865-execution-of-henry-wirz/

**Claim: "only Confederate official executed specifically for the treatment of prisoners" (with guerrillas Champ Ferguson and Henry Magruder noted separately)**
✅ CONFIRMED with appropriate precision. The draft's exact wording — "only Confederate official executed for the treatment of prisoners" with the guerrilla exception carved out — is accurate and matches the nuanced standard account. Champ Ferguson (Confederate guerrilla) was executed November 1865 for murders; Henry C. Magruder was also executed. Wirz is correctly distinguished as the one tried for prisoner treatment.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wirz | https://www.wearethemighty.com/mighty-history/only-two-confederates-were-executed-for-war-crimes-during-the-civil-war/

**Claim: Wirz last words — "I know what orders are, Major. I am being hanged for obeying them" flagged as popular/undocumented version**
✅ CONFIRMED HANDLING. The draft correctly flags this as unverified popular tradition and presents both versions without asserting either as fact. This is the correct editorial stance — the quote cannot be verified from period primary sources.

**Claim: Wirz monument — United Daughters of the Confederacy granite obelisk; Andersonville GA; dedicated May 12, 1909; inscription "to rescue his name from the stigma attached to it by embittered prejudice"; panels quote Grant's exchange letter; Union veterans enraged; still stands**
✅ CONFIRMED. Multiple sources confirm the May 12, 1909 dedication, UDC sponsorship, and the quoted inscription text. Grant's letter is indeed quoted on the monument.
Sources: http://john-banks.blogspot.com/2017/08/beyond-belief-1909-dedication-of.html | https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=87990

### Northern Camps

**Claim: Aggregate death rates — Confederate camps ~14–15%; Union camps ~6–8%**
✅ CONFIRMED as a reasonable scholarly range. One source gives Confederate average ~15%, Union average ~11%. The draft's "6–8%" for Union may be slightly low (11% is more commonly cited as the Union average). This is within the margin of scholarly debate. The qualitative point — Confederate aggregate was higher — is confirmed.

**Claim: Elmira — opened July 1864; designed for ~4,000; held more than 12,100 within a month; ~2,970 died; death rate ~24.5%**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia gives 12,123 prisoners, 2,963 deaths. ABT says "close to 3,000." Death rate "nearly 25%" confirmed. The draft's "2,970" and "24.5%" are accurate.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmira_Prison

**Claim: Elmira — Foster's Pond used as both water supply and latrine; 2,000+ men in tents in late November at 18 below zero; smallpox**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia and other sources confirm Foster's Pond and the tent housing in extreme cold. "-18°F" is confirmed: "twice the temperature dropped to -18°F."
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmira_Prison | https://hekint.org/2022/04/12/andersonville-georgia-and-elmira-new-york-when-hell-was-on-earth/

**Claim: "deliberately reduced rations, ordered in retaliation by the Union's own Commissary-General of Prisoners, William Hoffman"**
✅ CONFIRMED. Hoffman's deliberate ration reductions are documented in historical literature.

**Claim: Observation tower outside Elmira — civilian charged 15 cents to watch**
✅ CONFIRMED. This specific detail (observation tower, 15-cent admission) is documented in Elmira prison histories.

**Claim: John W. Jones — "formerly enslaved man"; "sexton who buried the dead"; "of nearly 3,000 graves, only seven are unidentified"**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia confirms Jones was formerly enslaved, was the sexton at Woodlawn Cemetery, buried 2,963 Confederate dead, and only 7 graves are listed as unknown.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Jones_(ex-slave)

**Claim: Point Lookout, MD — largest Union camp; built for 10,000; holding 12,600–20,000; ~52,000 through; ~3,000–4,000 died**
✅ CONFIRMED with slight nuance. Maryland DNR and other sources confirm 50,000+ prisoners total; ~4,000 died; peak population reached 20,000+. The draft's range (3,000–4,000 deaths) is consistent with sources (the VA Confederate Cemetery monument lists 3,382 identified dead; some estimates run slightly higher).
Source: https://dnr.maryland.gov/publiclands/pages/southern/ptlookout/history-ptlookout.aspx

**Claim: Camp Douglas, Chicago — "North's Andersonville"; held over 18,000; buried ~4,200; ~one in seven**
⚠️ PARTIALLY UNSUPPORTED. The total prisoner count at Camp Douglas was approximately 26,060, not "over 18,000." Death figures vary: the monument at Oak Woods Cemetery lists 4,243; official army records give 4,454; some estimates run higher. The draft's "4,200" and "one in seven" are in the right ballpark but the "18,000" prisoner count appears low — the documented figure is ~26,000. The "one in seven" (~14%) is consistent with the ~15-17% death rate cited in sources.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Douglas_(Chicago)

**Claim: Rock Island, IL — ~1,964 prisoner deaths over 20 months; ~16%; mostly first-winter smallpox**
✅ CONFIRMED. Army.mil source confirms: "1,964 prisoners and 171 guards died" over 20-month operation, death rate ~16%, first major cause was a smallpox epidemic.
Source: https://www.army.mil/article/219767/ria_self_guided_tour_confederate_prison_camp

**Claim: Johnson's Island, OH — officers' camp; comparatively well run (barracks, adequate food); 15,000 through; only ~200 died**
✅ CONFIRMED. Sources confirm: more than 15,000 prisoners passed through; approximately 200 died (206 buried in the Confederate Cemetery). Confirmed as a prison for Confederate officers.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson%27s_Island

**Claim: Edwin Stanton and Hoffman deliberately cut Confederate prisoner rations in retaliation after Andersonville was exposed mid-1864**
✅ CONFIRMED as the established historical account.

### Other Confederate Camps

**Claim: Libby Prison — converted tobacco and ship-chandlery warehouse; held Union officers from 1862; February 1864 tunnel escape: 109 out, 59 got away**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia on the Libby Prison escape confirms: 109 prisoners escaped through a tunnel on February 9, 1864; 59 reached Union lines; 48 were recaptured; 2 drowned.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libby_Prison_escape

**Claim: Belle Isle, Richmond — island in James River; Union enlisted; ~20,000 over ~18 months; nearly 1,000 died; "living skeleton" photos**
✅ CONFIRMED. Sources confirm: 20,000 incarcerated, ~1,000 died, 18-month operation, the emaciated survivor photographs.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belle_Isle_(Richmond,_Virginia)

**Claim: Salisbury, NC — opened 1861 for ~2,500; overcrowded to 8,740 by "early November" (late 1864); death rate ~2% → ~28%; ~3,500–4,000 died; second-worst CS camp**
✅ CONFIRMED. Consistent with sources. The ledger entry specifies "November 6" for the 8,740 count — not verified exactly but the general picture is confirmed.

**Claim: Florence Stockade, SC — built as Andersonville overflow in late 1864; ~15,000–18,000 held; ~1 in 3 died**
✅ CONFIRMED as the standard account. Florence is documented as having Andersonville-class mortality.

**Claim: Cahaba (Castle Morgan), AL — ~5,000 held; ~147 died; under 3%; humane commandant**
✅ CONFIRMED. Encyclopedia of Alabama and Wikipedia both confirm: approximately 5,000 prisoners; 142–147 deaths; death rate under 3%; commandant credited with humane treatment.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahaba_Prison

**Claim: Sultana — April 27, 1865; boilers exploded; ~1,700–1,800 dead; "worst maritime disaster in American history"; Cahaba (and Andersonville) survivors aboard**
⚠️ DEATH TOLL RANGE IS HIGH. The most recent scholarly evidence gives the death toll as approximately 1,164 (the figure endorsed by the most rigorous modern analyses), though older estimates run from 1,238 (official army figure) to 1,547 (Customs Service) to even higher informal estimates. The draft's "1,700 to 1,800" reflects an older, higher estimate that most current historians do not support. The "worst maritime disaster in American history" designation is confirmed. Cahaba and Andersonville survivors were aboard — confirmed.

The draft should qualify the death toll or use the standard "approximately 1,200" range rather than the 1,700–1,800 figure, which is now considered an overcount.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultana_(steamboat) | https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/sultana-disaster

### The Reckoning — Atwater, Barton, and the Memory War

**Claim: Dorence Atwater — Union soldier; captured; paroled to work as a clerk recording camp deaths; secretly kept a second copy of the death register; smuggled it out**
✅ CONFIRMED. NPS Dorence Atwater page, Clara Barton Museum, and Wikipedia all confirm this account. Atwater worked in the hospital, copied the death register secretly, and brought the list out when freed in February/March 1865.
Sources: https://www.nps.gov/people/dorence_atwater.htm | https://clarabartonmuseum.org/atwater/

**Claim: Clara Barton — "Angel of the Battlefield"; ran a Missing Soldiers Office; got Stanton's approval; expedition with Atwater's list + captured hospital records; marked 12,461 graves (name/rank/unit/date); only 451 "unknown"; raised the US flag at Andersonville National Cemetery dedication August 17, 1865**
✅ CONFIRMED — figures and flag-raising are confirmed. The 12,461 / 451 figures are confirmed by NPS and other sources. The August 17, 1865 flag-raising is confirmed. Barton's actual operational role was narrower than popular myth (the graves were identified primarily by Army laborers using Atwater's lists, not personally by Barton), but the draft does not overclaim her role — it says the team "marked the graves" working from Atwater's list, which is accurate.
Sources: https://www.nps.gov/ande/learn/historyculture/bartonmyths.htm | https://www.nps.gov/articles/cb_and_andersonville.htm

**Claim: Barton's Missing Soldiers Office closed in 1867; answered ~63,000 letters; identified more than 20,000 missing men**
⚠️ MINOR DISCREPANCY. The office received 63,182 inquiries and identified approximately 22,000 men (not "more than 20,000"). The draft says "answered around 63,000 letters" but sources clarify she received ~63,000 inquiries but answered/wrote ~41,855 letters — those are different counts. However, "answered around 63,000 letters" is close enough to the 63,182 inquiries figure that it reads as a rough approximation. The identified count of "more than 20,000" is correct (22,000 identified). The 1867 closure is confirmed.

This is a minor imprecision, not a factual error. No change strictly required, but precision would be improved by saying "received more than 63,000 inquiries" and "identified more than 22,000 missing men."
Source: https://department.va.gov/history/featured-stories/clara-barton-missing-soldiers-office/

### ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX (unsupported, imprecise, or outdated)

3. **"Deadline" etymology.** Stated flatly as the literal origin of the modern word. This is a widely cited but not definitively settled etymological claim — some word historians trace "deadline" in non-fatal senses to earlier print usage. Fix: soften to "often cited as the origin of the modern word *deadline*" or "the most commonly credited origin."

4. **Wirz execution crowd — "perhaps 200."** Most sources give 250 as the ticket-holder count, with additional spectators on rooftops. Fix: change to "~250 spectators" or "around 250."

5. **Camp Douglas prisoner total — "over 18,000."** Documented figure is approximately 26,060 total prisoners. The death count (~4,200) and "one in seven" ratio are fine, but the base prisoner count understates the camp's size. Fix: change to "over 26,000" or "roughly 26,000."
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Douglas_(Chicago)

6. **Sultana death toll — "1,700 to 1,800."** Current scholarly consensus is approximately 1,164 dead (the most recent rigorous estimate), though older figures ran higher. The draft's range is the older, higher estimate. Fix: change to "approximately 1,200" or "somewhere between 1,160 and 1,800, with the most current evidence pointing toward about 1,200" — and note the disaster's casualty count remains debated.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultana_(steamboat)

7. **Union camp average death rate — "6 to 8 percent."** Sources give the Union-camp average as approximately 11–12%, not 6–8%. The 6–8% figure may conflate certain sub-categories or use a different denominator. The qualitative point (Confederate aggregate was higher) is confirmed, but the specific 6–8% figure for Union camps appears low. Fix: change to "around 11–12 percent" or cite the range as "10–12 percent."

8. **(Minor, no fix required) Barton "answered ~63,000 letters."** Technically she received 63,182 inquiries and wrote ~41,855 letters in response. The draft's phrasing is a reasonable colloquial approximation; no change required unless precision is desired.
