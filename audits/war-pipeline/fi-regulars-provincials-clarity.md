## Findings

### LOST (comprehension-breakers) — NONE
No term, name, or leap rises to a genuine wall for a zero-knowledge reader. Verdict is PASS on that basis.

### NEEDS-GLOSS (SHOULD-FIX, not blocking)
1. **"flying column" (line 95)** — genuine military jargon, unglossed, and it sits inside the densest paragraph. A 15-year-old will not know it means a fast, lightly-burdened detachment moving ahead of the main force. A 3–4 word inline gloss would close it (e.g. "Braddock's flying column — a fast-moving advance force — of roughly 1,300…"). Currently the only unglossed genuine-jargon term in the piece.
2. **"quartering" / "take quarters by force" (line 42)** — the word *quarters* (= housing/lodging soldiers in a town's homes and buildings) is doing load-bearing work in the Loudoun beat and recurs as a Revolution-era flashpoint. The sentence "refused to house his troops properly" mostly carries it, so this is borderline, but "take quarters by force" assumes the reader maps *quarters → housing*. A light touch (e.g. "quarters — lodging for his troops") would make it airtight.

### SHOULD-FIX (clarity / pacing, not gloss)
3. **Monongahela paragraph density (line 95)** — see wall-of-text note. Consider one sentence break so the casualty contrast (457 vs 39 killed) isn't buried mid-paragraph. Comprehension is intact; this is pacing.
4. **"the King's regular commission" vs "the King's commission" (lines 23, 29, 80)** — the phrase is used several times and is clear in aggregate, but the very first use ("any officer who held the King's regular commission," line 23) lands a half-beat before the reader has fully absorbed that a *commission* = the formal royal appointment that makes someone an officer. It resolves immediately from context, so not NEEDS-GLOSS, but a one-word touch on first use would remove the half-beat.
