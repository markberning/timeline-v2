## SHIP-BLOCKING CHECK: Is slavery named as the cause?

**PASS — and it lands hard.**

Section 1, paragraph 1, sentence 4–6 (the-surprise):

> "The Confederacy he was trying to split apart was a new country that had broken away from the United States barely a year earlier for one overriding reason: to protect and preserve the enslavement of four million Black people. The South had built its economy and its social order on slavery, seceded rather than see it limited, and now fielded armies to keep it. Grant's campaign down the rivers was, at bottom, a campaign to strangle a slaveholding republic."

"Slavery" appears explicitly. "Enslavement of four million Black people" names the system and its victims. "Slaveholding republic" reframes the Confederacy precisely. No euphemism. The section does not bury this — it is the third and fourth sentences of the entire piece. The-reckoning closes: "the South beaten to the ground and slavery destroyed with it." Cause is bookended: first paragraph and last meaningful paragraph.

**Automatic must-fix threshold: NOT triggered.**

## Finding-by-Finding Analysis

### F1 — Johnston death section: FAIR (with one mild caution noted)

**Quote under review:**
> "Johnston rode forward to personally lead an attack near the Peach Orchard, out in front of his own men, the way he believed a commander should."

**Verdict: FAIR — SHOULD-FIX (very minor)**

The phrase "the way he believed a commander should" is accurate as biography — Johnston had a documented command philosophy of leading from the front — but in isolation it reads as gently approving characterization. It does not tip into romanticization because:
1. The very next sentences describe him failing to apply a tourniquet, bleeding into his boot, and dying of an avoidable wound — the passage undercuts heroism with darkly ironic incompetence.
2. The friendly-fire hedge immediately follows, introducing the possibility he was shot by his own men — a decidedly unheroic note.
3. Davis's "irreparable loss" quote is presented without editorial endorsement; it is sourced attribution, not authorial agreement.

The section does NOT deliver the classic Lost Cause Johnston elegies: no "greatest general the South had," no "the South's last best hope died here," no counterfactual "if Johnston had lived." The narrative moves on to Beauregard taking command and doing poorly.

**SHOULD-FIX (low priority):** The phrase "the way he believed a commander should" could be tightened to simply "riding in front of his own lines" or "leading from the front" without the approving "he believed" wrapper, to remove any faint whiff of validation. Not ship-blocking.

---

### F2 — "Both-sides" / valor framing of the carnage: FAIR

The bloodbath framing in the-reckoning does NOT drift into both-sides elegy. Specific checks:

- The section opens with the casualty figure and calls it "the whole story" — framing the battle through its cost, not its glory.
- The "short war illusion" passage is built around Grant's disillusionment, not around Confederate bravery. The pivot is: this is what total war looks like.
- The Grant quote — "I gave up all idea of saving the Union except by complete conquest" — is the interpretive anchor. It points forward toward Union victory and the destruction of slavery, not toward mourning the fallen on both sides equally.
- "The road from Shiloh ran south... through Corinth and on to Vicksburg, where Grant would split the Confederacy in two for good." The Confederate cause is framed as something that was going to be crushed, not something that deserved to fight on.
- Samuel Todd (Mary Todd Lincoln's brother, Union) is the casualty named — a Northern family, not a Confederate martyr.

There is no "both sides fought magnificently" elegy. The section is Grant's section. The Confederate dead are not eulogized.

**Verdict: FAIR**

### F3 — Hornet's Nest: Confederate assault framing: FAIR

The Confederate charges at the Hornet's Nest are described functionally — Bragg "fed brigades into the meat grinder" — which is accurate and does NOT glorify Confederate valor. "Meat grinder" is a Union-sympathetic image: the attackers are being wasted. Ruggles's artillery concentration is described as a tactical innovation but with no romantic overlay.

**Verdict: FAIR**

### F4 — Sherman rehabilitation: FAIR

Sherman's stand is praised, but it is contextualized as a fighting retreat that bought time — a Union officer's competence serving the Union cause. No romanticization of Confederate opponents in this section. Sherman's prior reputation crisis ("whispered about as having lost his nerve, even his sanity") is included but neutrally — it sharpens the arc, not the mythology.

**Verdict: FAIR**

### F5 — Beauregard's "complete victory" telegram: FAIR

The framing here is unflattering to the Confederacy: "Few telegrams in the war aged worse." The Confederate high-water-mark moment is set up to be knocked down immediately. No wistfulness for the lost Confederate cause, no "the South so nearly won it." The Richmond aside is a structural irony beat that makes the Confederacy look credulous, not heroic.

**Verdict: FAIR**

### F6 — Johnston's "I would fight them if they were a million": FAIR

The draft handles this exactly right — it includes the quote, then immediately flags it as "post-hoc recollection, not a contemporary record" and instructs the reader to "treat it as likely rather than gospel." The romantic bravado is undercut in the same breath.

**Verdict: FAIR**

### F7 — Forrest: FAIR with one note

Nathan Bedford Forrest is side-tagged "(South)" and described as a cavalry scout and then as the leader of a rearguard ambush. The human-shield escape ("he reportedly hauled a Union soldier up onto his horse as a human shield and galloped out") is presented as a fact with "reportedly" hedge — not glorified. There is no romanticization of Forrest, no "brilliant cavalryman" language that would touch on his postwar KKK role (which is not in scope for this section). The framing is neutral-to-cold.

**Note (not a finding):** Forrest's postwar history is not material to a Shiloh narrative. No flag here, just noting it was considered.

**Verdict: FAIR**

---

### F8 — "Slaveholding republic" framing sustained or dropped midway?: FAIR

The piece opens with "slaveholding republic" and closes with "slavery destroyed." The middle sections (the battle narrative) are appropriately focused on military action without needing to repeat the cause framing. The cause is not forgotten — it is named at the top and used as the interpretive endpoint at the bottom. This is the correct structural choice: the battle narrative does not need to pause every section to re-name slavery, as long as the frame holds at the entry and exit points. It holds.

**Verdict: FAIR**

---

### F9 — Presentism check: FAIR

The draft does not impose 21st-century moral vocabulary on 1862 actors. Grant's decision to not entrench is described in period-accurate terms: "sounds reckless now and sounded fine then." The shock at casualties is described in period-accurate terms ("a scale of loss the country had simply never imagined"). No anachronistic condemnation of individuals for period-typical views. The one modern moral anchor — slavery as the cause — is historically correct and was argued by contemporaries (Lincoln, Frederick Douglass), not imposed retroactively.

**Verdict: FAIR**

---

### F10 — Human cost: felt, not sanitized, not gratuitous: FAIR

The carnage is made concrete in three ways:
1. The raw numbers, contextualized against all prior Civil War battles combined.
2. The Samuel Todd detail — a named person in the Lincoln household, a real family torn by the war.
3. Grant's personal reckoning ("walking a field carpeted with the dead").

It is not gratuitous — there is no wound-by-wound description or gore for its own sake. It is not sanitized — the numbers are foregrounded and the phrase "carpeted with the dead" lands without flinching. The "short war dies" framing ensures the cost is not aestheticized but is given historical weight.

**Verdict: FAIR**

## Overall Verdict

**CLEAR TO SHIP on framing grounds.**

The draft is one of the cleaner Lost Cause / framing performances in the pipeline. Slavery is named bluntly in the first paragraph, sustained as a frame through the close, and used as the interpretive endpoint of the entire piece. Johnston's death is not romanticized — it is laced with irony (the avoidable wound, the absent physician, the possible friendly fire). The carnage framing does not drift into both-sides elegy: it is Grant's disillusionment and the death of the short-war fantasy, pointing toward Union total war and the destruction of slavery. No Confederate martyrdom, no Lost Cause counterfactuals, no soft-pedaling of the slaveholding republic framing.

The one SHOULD-FIX (F1) is genuinely minor — a phrase that approves of Johnston's tactics in a neutral voice. It can be addressed in the revision pass with a single word swap.
