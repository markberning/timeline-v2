## the-crossroads · Corinth · The crossroads of the Confederacy
### Why two armies wanted one small Mississippi town

There was nothing much to Corinth, Mississippi — a small town in the far northeast corner of the state, near the Tennessee line — except the one thing that made it priceless. Two of the most important railroads in the South crossed there, right at the town depot. The **Mobile & Ohio** ran north and south, tying the Gulf coast at Mobile all the way up to the Ohio River. The **Memphis & Charleston** ran east and west, and it was the only continuous rail line in the whole Confederacy linking the Atlantic coast to the Mississippi River. The two lines met and crossed at Corinth. Whoever held that crossing held the ability to move troops and supplies across the western half of the Confederacy — which is why the period gave the town a nickname far grander than its size: the "crossroads of the Confederacy." [VERIFY: epithet wording]

It is worth being clear, up front, about what those two armies were really fighting over, because a railroad junction is the *object* and not the *reason*. The Confederacy existed for one purpose: to preserve and extend slavery. Eleven states had broken away from the United States rather than accept any limit on the enslavement of Black people, built their economy and their society on it, and now fielded armies to defend it. The railroad at Corinth mattered because it fed that war effort — it carried the soldiers and the supplies of a slaveholding republic. The Union wanted Corinth because taking it was one more cut into the body of that republic. Hold the thought; by the end of this story it will be standing on the edge of the battlefield, in human form, and impossible to miss.

The Union already had the town. After the bloodbath at Shiloh in April 1862 (about 20 miles to the north), the Union commander Maj. Gen. Henry Halleck (North) had crept down on Corinth in a slow, dug-in siege, and the Confederates under Gen. P.G.T. Beauregard (South) had simply evacuated it in late May rather than be trapped — the "Siege of Corinth," a near-bloodless affair, not to be confused with the two-day battle this story is about. Corinth had been in Union hands ever since.

By the fall of 1862 the Confederacy wanted it back. The man who decided to take it was Maj. Gen. Earl Van Dorn (South), a cavalier of a general with a reputation for boldness. His logic was a gambler's. Grant's forces in the region were scattered — roughly 12,000 at Bolivar, about 7,000 under Sherman at Memphis, some 6,000 in reserve at Jackson, and Maj. Gen. William S. Rosecrans (North) holding Corinth itself with around 23,000. If Van Dorn could fall on the Corinth garrison fast, before all those pieces concentrated, he might overwhelm it, retake the junction, and — not incidentally — relieve the pressure on Gen. Braxton Bragg's (South) ongoing invasion of Kentucky by tying Grant's western armies down. He gathered roughly 22,000 men, gave the combined force the grand name of the Army of West Tennessee, and marched on the crossroads.

> **MEANWHILE — Iuka, two weeks earlier.** The Corinth attack did not come out of nowhere. On September 19, about 20 miles east at Iuka, Rosecrans (North) had already tangled with Maj. Gen. Sterling Price's (South) Army of the West in the opening move of the same campaign. The fight at Iuka was inconclusive and Price slipped away, but the larger result was that Price now joined forces with Van Dorn. The two Confederate armies that had been chasing around northern Mississippi became one, pointed at one target. Iuka was the windup; Corinth was the punch.

## october-third · October 3 · Driving the Federals back through the old works
### Van Dorn's good day

Van Dorn (South) attacked around ten in the morning on October 3, coming down out of the northwest along the **Chewalla Road**, the route that led toward town. He struck the **old outer works** — the entrenchments Beauregard's men had thrown up back in the spring, several miles north and northwest of the depot. Those old lines made a useful tripwire, but they ran too long a front for Rosecrans (North) to truly hold; the real defense lay closer in.

Rosecrans had his garrison sorted into the standard ladder of a Civil War army — divisions of brigades of regiments — with three of his divisions pushed forward to meet the blow. Brig. Gen. Thomas McKean (North) held the left, Brig. Gen. Thomas Davies (North) the center, and Brig. Gen. Charles Hamilton (North) the right, with Brig. Gen. David Stanley (North) held back in reserve south of town. Van Dorn tried for a *double envelopment* — hitting both ends of the Union line at once to fold it inward. Maj. Gen. Mansfield Lovell's (South) division came in against the Union left; Brig. Gen. Dabney Maury (South) pressed on Davies's flank in the center.

For most of the day it worked. Around 1:30 in the afternoon a gap tore open between Davies's center and McKean's left, and a gap in a battle line is a doorway. The pressure on either side of it forced the whole Union line to peel back, falling away from the old outer works to within about half a mile of the inner defenses. The first day went, plainly, to Van Dorn. He had pushed the Federals out of their forward line and driven them in on their last one.

It cost the Union army two of its generals in a single afternoon. Brig. Gen. Pleasant Hackleman (North) was killed — one of the relatively few Union generals to die in action in the entire war — and Brig. Gen. Richard Oglesby (North) was severely wounded (he survived, and would later be Governor of Illinois). [VERIFY: Oglesby governorship as included context] The Confederate momentum was real, and growing.

And then the sun did the Union a favor the Union could not have done for itself. Late in the afternoon, around three o'clock, Hamilton (North) was ordered to swing wide and flank the attacking Confederates. The order was garbled. As Wikipedia's account puts it, "through a misunderstanding of the order ... so much time was lost that it was sunset before the division was in position." [Source narration — paraphrase] By the time anyone was ready to deliver the killing stroke, the daylight was gone. The Confederates had pushed the Federals to the edge of breaking — and run out of day before they could finish it. Van Dorn would later put his frustration into a single line, mourning the hour he didn't have:

> "One hour more of daylight and victory would have soothed our grief." — attributed to Van Dorn (South) [VERIFY verbatim/attribution]

*If the burial words cannot be confirmed as Van Dorn's own, render indirectly: Van Dorn lamented that one more hour of daylight would have brought the victory.*

> **MEANWHILE — inside the inner ring.** As the Federals fell back through the dark, they were falling back onto something. Rosecrans (North) had inherited a three-tiered defense and spent his weeks of occupation strengthening the innermost ring: the close-in "College Hill Line" of batteries immediately outside town, anchored by five earthwork forts — among them Battery Robinett and Battery Powell. The outer works Van Dorn had just overrun were a tripwire. The Halleck line, about a mile and a half out, was another span too long to truly hold. But the inner battery line was short, close, and bristling with cannon, and it was waiting. Van Dorn had won the ground that didn't matter most. The ground that mattered most was still in front of him.

## battery-robinett · October 4 · The ditch at Battery Robinett
### Where Van Dorn's gamble bled out

The second day began in the dark with noise. Around 4:30 in the morning the Confederate artillery opened a bombardment on the town. Then the attack stalled before it started: about seven o'clock, Brig. Gen. Louis Hébert (South) — slated to lead a key part of the assault — reported himself too ill to command, and Brig. Gen. Martin Green (South) had to take over his troops. The reshuffle cost the morning's momentum, the second day in a row that Van Dorn's timing slipped. The infantry assault on the inner line didn't roll forward until around nine.

When it came, it came hardest at two places. To the north, Green's (South) brigades drove in and actually took **Battery Powell** — the high-water mark of the whole Confederate effort, a captured Union fort and its guns in Confederate hands. It did not last. A Union counterattack, Davies (North) and Hamilton (North) striking together, drove the Confederates back out and recaptured the cannon. The deepest Confederate penetration of the inner line was retaken almost as fast as it was made.

The climax was a few hundred yards south, at **Battery Robinett**. Robinett was a *redan* — an arrowhead-shaped earthwork, its point aimed out at the enemy — sitting on the northwest face of the defenses, exactly astride the Chewalla Road the Confederates were coming down. It mounted three 20-pounder Parrott rifles (heavy field cannon), and a five-foot ditch ran across its front like a dry moat. Maury's (South) division was sent straight at it. At the head of the vanguard, on Maury's order to lead the charge into the very front of the redan, rode **Col. William P. Rogers (South)**, commanding the 2nd Texas Infantry — a 42-year-old Texas lawyer and political man, by all accounts brave to the edge of recklessness.

What happened at Robinett is the battle in miniature, and it is terrible. The attackers ran into "a terrific musket and artillery fire" [Source narration — paraphrase the encyclopedia, don't quote it as a voice] from the works, crossed the killing ground, dropped into the five-foot ditch, and clawed up the parapet, where the fight became hand-to-hand on top of the earthwork itself. Rogers reached the crest. By the account preserved at the Texas State Historical Association, "he had just climbed to the top of the parapet and planted the colors, when strong Federal forces were seen on the right, and then a volley of fire brought him down and nearly all the men with him." [Source narration — TSHA — safe to paraphrase] He fell there, on the parapet. (Just *how* he was killed is genuinely disputed in the sources — a volley of rifle fire, a charge of grapeshot, canister — and the popular tale of a specific number of bullets is not something the records support, so we won't pretend to it.) Col. John Daly (South) of the 18th Arkansas was killed in the same assault. Maury pulled back what was left of his division, the fact pack's sources call it "arguably the hottest action of the two-day battle," and the ditch in front of Robinett was left a killing ground. [VERIFY: LOC death-photo of the Confederate dead in the Robinett ditch — exists, confirm caption/date before any citation]

Brig. Gen. Charles Phifer's (South) brigade actually broke clean into the streets of Corinth itself — for a moment the Confederates were *in the town* — before Union reserves under Sullivan (North) and the crossfire of the batteries drove them back out and, in the words of the account, went about "utterly routing them." [Source narration — paraphrase] By about one in the afternoon it was effectively over. Around four o'clock, Brig. Gen. James McPherson's (North) reinforcement column arrived from Jackson, too late to fight but in time to make Van Dorn's position hopeless. Van Dorn (South) ordered a general retreat.

He had lost roughly twice what he'd inflicted. The Union loss came to about 2,500 men, killed, wounded, and missing. The Confederate loss is genuinely contested in the records — somewhere in the range of 4,200 to 4,800 — but every version of it tells the same story: Van Dorn lost about twice as many men as Rosecrans, the signature price of throwing infantry against fortified earthworks. [VERIFY: Union total 2,400–2,520 range; Confederate range 4,233–4,838 — do not print false precision]

There is a coda at Robinett worth keeping. When the fighting was done, the victorious Rosecrans (North) ordered Rogers — an enemy colonel — buried where he fell, with full military honors, an honor usually reserved for generals. An obelisk stands by Battery Robinett over his grave today.

> "He was one of the bravest men that ever led a charge. Bury him with military honors and mark his grave so that his friends may claim him." — attributed to Rosecrans (North) [VERIFY verbatim/attribution]

*If the quote cannot be confirmed as Rosecrans's own words, render indirectly: Rosecrans called him one of the bravest men ever to lead a charge and ordered him buried with honor and his grave marked so his friends could claim him.*

> **MEANWHILE — the retreat to the Hatchie.** Van Dorn (South) pulled west, making for the crossings of the Hatchie River. The Union didn't let him go cleanly. On October 5, a force under Maj. Gen. Edward Ord (North) blocked the Confederates at Davis Bridge over the Hatchie, the Battle of Hatchie's Bridge, and Van Dorn had to scramble for another ford to escape south to Holly Springs, Mississippi. The bid to retake the crossroads was finished. A court of inquiry later cleared Van Dorn of charges of drunkenness and negligence, but he was superseded all the same — command in Mississippi passed to Lt. Gen. John C. Pemberton (South). Rosecrans, by contrast, became a Northern hero and was kicked upstairs to command the army soon renamed the Army of the Cumberland. (Grant grumbled that Rosecrans should have chased Van Dorn harder on the day of the battle — "Two or three hours of pursuit on the day of the battle ... would have been worth more than any pursuit commenced the next day," he wrote in his memoirs.) [VERIFY: Grant memoir quote, exact ellipsis]

## what-it-protected · The meaning · What the victory protected
### The proof of the cause, on the edge of town

**OUTCOME: Union victory.** The Federals held the crossroads of the Confederacy. Rosecrans's (North) garrison repulsed Van Dorn's (South) assault at roughly two-to-one cost to the attackers, the junction stayed in Union hands for the rest of the war, and the last serious Confederate bid to retake the western rail line collapsed.

The strategic payoff was large and quiet. Holding Corinth secured West Tennessee, denied the Confederacy the one rail crossing that knit the western theater together, and wrecked Van Dorn's army as an offensive force. Iuka and Corinth together turned out to be the last real Confederate offensives along the upper Mississippi in this theater. With his rear secure, Grant (North) was free to turn south toward the campaign that would matter most — Vicksburg, the fortress city whose fall the next summer would split the Confederacy in two. From the Confederate retreat out of Corinth to the surrender of Vicksburg on July 4, 1863, the initiative on the Mississippi belonged to Grant and the Union. The little town with the railroad crossing was a hinge, and it turned the right way.

But the deepest meaning of Corinth was not on the railroad. It was on the edge of town, and it is the answer to the question this story opened with — what were these two armies really fighting over.

While the soldiers fought over the depot, thousands of enslaved people were freeing themselves by walking into Union-held Corinth. They came to be called "contrabands," a clumsy wartime term for enslaved people who crossed into Union lines and would not be sent back. And the Union, instead of turning them away, built them a town. The **Corinth Contraband Camp**, established in 1862 under Brig. Gen. Grenville Dodge (North), was not a holding pen — it was a working community, with "numerous homes, a church, school and hospital." Its residents ran cooperative farms, raised and sold cotton and vegetables, and made the camp pay: by May 1863 it was turning a clear profit of four to five thousand dollars from its own enterprises. [VERIFY: the wider 300 acres cotton / 100 acres vegetables / ~$5,000-a-month figures — pack flags these as broader interpretive material, not the single NPS page] By that August, over a thousand Black children and adults at the camp had learned to read, taught through the work of benevolent organizations. [VERIFY: AMA-by-name — NPS says "benevolent organizations," do not assert the American Missionary Association unless confirmed] Across its life, from 1862 until it was relocated to Memphis in December 1863, roughly 6,000 formerly enslaved people passed through it.

And then the camp did the thing that closes the circle. Nearly 2,000 of the men there enlisted, and from them was raised the 1st Alabama Infantry Regiment of African Descent — later re-designated the **55th United States Colored Troops**. People who had been somebody's legal property a year earlier put on Union blue and took up arms, in sight of the battlefield where Van Dorn's army had just tried, and failed, to win the war for the other side. That is the whole argument of the war standing in one place: the railroad was the object, slavery was the reason, and the proof of the reason was a camp full of free people on the town's edge, sending soldiers of their own back into the fight.

The site is preserved today as part of Shiloh National Military Park — a walkway lined with six groupings of life-size bronze statues of the camp's residents, the freedom-seekers who turned an occupied railroad town into one of the war's first proofs of what victory was for.

> **MEANWHILE — what the railroad really carried.** It is tempting to file Corinth as a battle "about a railroad," and the strategists on both sides genuinely fought over the rails. But it is worth sitting with what the rails were *for*. The Mobile & Ohio and the Memphis & Charleston carried the men and the matériel of a republic founded to keep four million people in bondage. When Van Dorn (South) tried to retake the junction, he was trying to restore that supply line to its purpose. When Rosecrans (North) held it, he was helping to break the thing the supply line served. And on the edge of the town they were fighting over, the people that whole machinery existed to keep enslaved were quietly, decisively, walking out of it — and then walking back in uniform. The contraband camp is not a footnote to the battle of Corinth. It is the point of it.
