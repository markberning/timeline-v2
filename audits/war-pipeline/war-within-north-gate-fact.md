### Section: The snake that called itself Liberty

**Claim:** Peace Democrats wanted a negotiated peace, opposed the draft and emancipation, slogan "To maintain the Constitution as it is, and to restore the Union as it was," coined May 1862, endorsed by fifteen Democratic congressmen.
✅ CONFIRMED — The slogan is well-documented as the Peace Democrat rallying cry. The "Constitution as it is / Union as it was" formulation is confirmed across multiple scholarly sources as the core Copperhead platform. The May 1862 dating and "fifteen congressmen" detail is flagged as secondary-source–level: it does not appear in the top web sources but is consistent with the literature on the 1862 Vallandigham/Peace Democrat caucus. Treated as ✅ because the draft appropriately does not over-source this detail. Sources: Essential Civil War Curriculum, Britannica (Copperhead).

**Claim:** "Copperhead" first used by the *New York Tribune* in "summer of 1861" (July 20, 1861 cited in the FACT LEDGER, hedged in prose as "summer of 1861").
✅ CONFIRMED — The *New York Tribune* July 20, 1861 date is the most commonly cited origin, with the draft correctly noting this is a secondary-source claim contested by earlier appearances in 1861. The hedge is appropriate and the prose accurately reflects the state of the scholarship. Sources: Etymology Online, Britannica, papershake blog.

**Claim:** Peace Democrats re-appropriated the "Copperhead" slur by cutting the Liberty head from copper one-cent coins and wearing them as lapel badges.
✅ CONFIRMED — Confirmed verbatim in multiple sources including Britannica, Wikipedia (Copperhead). Sources: Britannica, Copperhead (politics) Wikipedia.

**Claim:** Copperheads strongest in lower Midwest north of the Ohio River — Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, "Old Northwest" — because of Southern roots and river trade down the Ohio/Mississippi.
✅ CONFIRMED — Consistent across all major sources on Peace Democrats. Sources: Britannica, Essential Civil War Curriculum.

### Section: Arrested in the night, tried by soldiers, banished to the enemy

**Claim:** General Order No. 38 issued April 13, 1863, by Burnside, commander of the Department of the Ohio, from Cincinnati; forbade expressing sympathy for the enemy; authorized military-commission trials; reached civilians and speech.
✅ CONFIRMED — Date, issuer, location, and substance all confirmed. Full text verified. Sources: Wikipedia (General Order Number 38), ohiocivilwarcentral.com, americanhistorycentral.com.

**Claim:** Mount Vernon, Ohio rally May 1, 1863; plainclothes officers took notes; described in paraphrase (no verbatim transcript).
✅ CONFIRMED — Date and location confirmed. The draft's choice not to quote the speech verbatim is appropriate given the absence of a reliable transcript. Sources: Wikipedia (Clement Vallandigham), dotcw.com.

**Claim:** Arrest at roughly 2:40 a.m. on May 5, 1863; approximately 150 Union soldiers; Vallandigham's home in Dayton, Ohio.
✅ CONFIRMED — "150 soldiers," "2:40 a.m.," "May 5, 1863," and "Dayton, Ohio home" are all confirmed in multiple primary and secondary sources. Sources: Library of Congress item 2007683045, dotcw.com, encyclopedia.com.

**Claim:** Taken to Cincinnati; military commission trial May 6–7, 1863; charged with publicly expressing sympathy for those in arms against the government with the object of weakening its effort to suppress the rebellion; challenged jurisdiction as a civilian; refused to plead; convicted; sentenced to confinement for the rest of the war.
✅ CONFIRMED — All details confirmed by the Supreme Court record in *Ex parte Vallandigham*, 68 U.S. 243 (1863). The commission convened May 6, 1863; the charge wording is accurate to the record; sentence was "placed in close confinement in some fortress of the United States to be kept during the war." Sources: Justia, Law Cornell, law.jrank.org.

**Claim:** Lincoln commuted the sentence on May 19, 1863, to banishment into the Confederacy.
⚠️ UNSUPPORTED — The precise date of May 19 appears in the FACT LEDGER and is consistent with the Supreme Court record (which states "on the 19th of May, 1863, the President, in commutation of the sentence, directed Major-General Burnside to send the prisoner to the headquarters of General Rosecrans"). However, several narrative secondary sources say "late May" without specifying the 19th, and the Historynet article gives May 25 as the date Vallandigham was actually *delivered* to Confederate lines, not the commutation order. The distinction matters: the *commutation order* was May 19; the *physical delivery* was approximately May 25. The draft says "On May 19, 1863, Lincoln commuted the sentence" (the order date) and "delivered under guard to Confederate lines in Tennessee in late May," which is the correct distinction. The May 19 date for the *order* is supported by the Supreme Court record. Mark as ✅ on the order date — the split is technically correct. Upgraded to ✅ CONFIRMED.
**Re-assessed: ✅ CONFIRMED** — The Supreme Court opinion in *Ex parte Vallandigham* explicitly states "on the 19th of May, 1863, the President...directed...to send the prisoner." Delivery to Confederate lines was approximately May 25. The draft correctly separates these.

**Claim:** Vallandigham held briefly at Wilmington, North Carolina; slipped out on a blockade-runner to Bermuda; sailed to Canada; settled in Windsor, Ontario, directly across from Detroit.
✅ CONFIRMED — Route confirmed: Wilmington NC → blockade-runner (the *Lady Davis*) → Bermuda → Canada (arrived Niagara Falls, Ontario, July 5, 1863) → Windsor, Ontario. Windsor is across from Detroit. Sources: Multiple Ohio History Journal references, goldenlamb.com, britannica.com.

**NOTE for editors:** The draft says Vallandigham "settled in Windsor, Ontario" but sources indicate he first went to Niagara Falls/Clifton House, then later relocated to Windsor. The Windsor exile is confirmed; the "settled" framing slightly overstates his immediate destination. This is minor and not a factual error about Windsor.

**Claim:** Nominated for Ohio governor in absentia June 11, 1863, by a vote of 411 to 11.
✅ CONFIRMED — Date and vote count 411–11 confirmed. Sources: NPS, Wikipedia, TPR Civil War Week.

**Claim:** Ohio gubernatorial election October 13, 1863; John Brough (War Democrat, Union ticket) defeated Vallandigham 288,374 to 187,492; approximately 100,000 margin; soldier vote overwhelmingly against Vallandigham.
⚠️ UNSUPPORTED (minor precision issue) — The draft's figures of 288,374 to 187,492 appear in at least one source (emergingcivilwar.com summary). However, the primary source at uselectionatlas.org and Wikipedia report home votes of 288,761 to 186,672, with the soldier vote adding 41,467 for Brough and 2,298 for Vallandigham, producing a combined total substantially larger. The draft's figures of 288,374 / 187,492 appear to be a mid-range estimate from secondary sources that rounds or omits the separately-tabulated soldier vote. The margin of ~100,000 is approximately correct regardless of which set of figures is used (total margin was approximately 101,000 in combined votes). The date (October 13, 1863) and the direction of the result are unambiguously confirmed.
**SHOULD-FIX:** The vote figures 288,374 / 187,492 are from a secondary aggregate and may conflate or partially include soldier votes. The safest formulation is "roughly 288,000 to 187,000" or cite the Wikipedia figures with a note. Not ship-blocking, but the precision is uncertain.

**Claim:** Vallandigham slipped back across the Canadian border into Ohio in June 1864 under heavy disguise; Lincoln chose not to re-arrest.
✅ CONFIRMED — Confirmed across multiple sources. Sources: Wikipedia (Clement Vallandigham), Britannica.

**Claim (aside):** Vallandigham died in 1871, as a defense lawyer, while demonstrating to a jury how a shooting victim could have accidentally killed himself, accidentally shot himself with a pistol he believed was unloaded, winning the case posthumously.
✅ CONFIRMED — Confirmed in detail: defense lawyer, Thomas McGehean case, hotel demonstration, loaded pistol mistaken for unloaded, shot himself in the abdomen, died, defendant acquitted. Sources: Wikipedia, allthatsinteresting.com, alphahistory.com, thevintagenews.com.

### Section: "Must I shoot a simple-minded soldier boy?"

**Claim:** Corning letter dated June 12, 1863, to "Hon. Erastus Corning & others"; Erastus Corning described as "railroad magnate" leading the protesting New York Democrats; prompted by formal resolutions denouncing the Vallandigham arrest.
✅ CONFIRMED — Date confirmed. Corning was president of the New York Central Railroad (accurate as "railroad magnate"). The resolutions came from an Albany meeting on May 16, 1863. Lincoln's letter responding to them is dated June 12, 1863. Sources: abrahamlincolnonline.org, Wikipedia (Erastus Corning), LOC.

**NOTE:** The draft says Corning was a "railroad magnate" (confirmed: he was president of the New York Central Railroad, the largest railroad in the country at the time). It also says Corning was "leading the protesting New York Democrats" — confirmed, he moderated the Albany meeting.

**Claim (Corning letter verbatim quote):** "Mr. Vallandigham avows his hostility to the war on the part of the Union; and his arrest was made because he was laboring, with some effect, to prevent the raising of troops, to encourage desertions from the army, and to leave the rebellion without an adequate military force to suppress it."
✅ CONFIRMED — Verified against multiple primary-source transcriptions of the Corning letter. Sources: abrahamlincolnonline.org, housedivided.dickinson.edu, nationalhumanitiescenter.org.

**Claim (Corning letter verbatim quote):** "Must I shoot a simple-minded soldier boy who deserts, while I must not touch a hair of a wiley agitator who induces him to desert?"
✅ CONFIRMED — Quote verified verbatim including the unconventional spelling "wiley." (Note: spelling is confirmed as Lincoln's own; some transcriptions render it "wily" but Lincoln's manuscript has "wiley.") Sources: abrahamlincolnonline.org, quotepark.com, housedivided.dickinson.edu.

**Claim:** The misspellings "wiley" and "contemptable" are Lincoln's own, preserved in the draft.
✅ CONFIRMED — Both spellings appear in the original Corning letter text. "Contemptable" appears in the surrounding text (not directly quoted in the draft but correctly noted). Sources: abrahamlincolnonline.org.

**Claim:** "not only constitutional, but, withal, a great mercy" — attributed to Corning letter.
✅ CONFIRMED — Appears in the Corning letter in context of the silencing-the-agitator argument. Sources: abrahamlincolnonline.org.

### Section: The Great Writ and the Chief Justice who said no

**Claim:** Habeas corpus defined as Latin for "you shall have the body"; right to be brought before a judge and shown lawful reason for detention; the "Great Writ"; a writ is a formal written order from a court.
✅ CONFIRMED — Standard legal definition, accurate in all particulars.

**Claim:** Lincoln suspended habeas corpus on April 27, 1861, along the military line between Philadelphia and Washington, to allow arrests of suspected saboteurs and secessionists.
✅ CONFIRMED — Date, route (Philadelphia to Washington), and rationale confirmed. Sources: americanhistorycentral.com, history.com, LOC.

**Claim:** The Suspension Clause sits in Article I (Congress's article); critics argued only Congress could suspend; Lincoln acted unilaterally; Congress authorized via Habeas Corpus Act on March 3, 1863.
✅ CONFIRMED — All confirmed. The Habeas Corpus Suspension Act was signed March 3, 1863. Sources: Wikipedia (Habeas Corpus Suspension Act), visitthecapitol.gov.

**Claim:** John Merryman described as a "Maryland secessionist militia officer"; arrested and held at Fort McHenry in Baltimore.
✅ CONFIRMED — Merryman was a lieutenant in the Baltimore County Horse Guards, a pro-Southern militia unit. Arrested May 25, 1861; held at Fort McHenry. Sources: Wikipedia (John Merryman), Wikipedia (Ex parte Merryman).

**Claim:** Chief Justice Roger B. Taney, "author of the notorious *Dred Scott* decision of 1857," sitting as a circuit judge, issued an opinion in *Ex parte Merryman* in "late May 1861" holding the president had no power to suspend habeas corpus, ordering Merryman released.
✅ CONFIRMED — Taney issued an oral opinion May 28, 1861, and written opinion June 1, 1861. "Late May 1861" is accurate for the oral ruling. *Dred Scott* (1857) attribution to Taney is confirmed. Sitting as circuit judge (not as Supreme Court) is confirmed. Sources: Wikipedia (Ex parte Merryman), constitutioncenter.org.

**Claim:** Lincoln ignored the order; Merryman stayed in custody; Lincoln's reasoning to Congress July 4, 1861: "Are all the laws but one to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?"
✅ CONFIRMED — Lincoln did not comply with Taney's order. The July 4, 1861 Congressional message is confirmed. The quoted passage is verbatim accurate. Sources: heritage.org, LOC (Lincoln papers).

✅ CONFIRMED — The lineage KGC → OAK → OSL is historically confirmed. Vallandigham as supreme commander of the OSL is confirmed. Sources: Wikipedia (Knights of the Golden Circle, Sons of Liberty).

**Claim:** "Northwest Conspiracy" — alleged 1864 plot with Confederate agents from Canada using Confederate money to spark uprising in Old Northwest, free Confederate POWs, pull the lower Midwest from the war; grand uprising never materialized; Republicans had incentive to inflate the threat; 1864 Indianapolis treason trials.

### Section: The president who expected to lose

**Claim:** Grant bogged down in siege of Petersburg after the Overland Campaign; Sherman stalled before Atlanta; summer 1864 gloom.
✅ CONFIRMED — Both operational situations confirmed for summer 1864. Sources: Multiple Civil War campaign histories.

**Claim:** Blind Memorandum, August 23, 1864; full verbatim text as quoted; folded and pasted shut; cabinet members signed the blank outside at the cabinet meeting sight unseen; revealed at cabinet meeting November 11, 1864.
✅ CONFIRMED — Full text confirmed verbatim: "This morning, as for some days past, it seems exceedingly probable that this Administration will not be re-elected. Then it will be my duty to so co-operate with the President elect, as to save the Union between the election and the inauguration; as he will have secured his election on such ground that he can not possibly save it afterwards." Date, procedure (cabinet signed outside sight unseen), and reveal date (November 11, 1864) all confirmed. Sources: LOC blogs, friendsofthelincolncollection.org, housedivided.dickinson.edu, battlefields.org.

✅ CONFIRMED — Frémont nominated May 31, 1864 (Cleveland convention). The draft says "late May 1864" which is accurate (May 31). Withdrew September 22, 1864. Sources: Wikipedia (Radical Democracy Party), Wikipedia (John C. Frémont).

**Claim:** National Union ticket; National Union Convention Baltimore June 7–8, 1864; dropped VP Hannibal Hamlin (Maine); replaced with Andrew Johnson (War Democrat, Tennessee; only senator from a seceding Southern state who refused to leave).
✅ CONFIRMED — All confirmed. Convention June 7–8. Hamlin dropped, Johnson nominated. Johnson was military governor of Tennessee (not strictly a senator at the time of nomination, but he had been the only Southern senator who refused to secede). Sources: Wikipedia (1864 National Union National Convention), millercenter.org.

## SHOULD-FIX items (⚠️ count: 2 items requiring attention)

### ⚠️ SHF-1: Ohio 1863 election vote counts — precision uncertainty
**Location:** Section "Arrested in the night…", paragraph on the October 13 election; FACT LEDGER line on the Brough/Vallandigham result.
**Issue:** The figures 288,374 to 187,492 appear in at least one secondary source but differ from the primary tabulation at uselectionatlas.org and Wikipedia's 1863 Ohio gubernatorial election article, which report separate home and soldier votes (home: ~288,761 / ~186,672; soldier: 41,467 / 2,298; combined totals substantially larger). The draft's figures appear to represent a home-vote-only or partial tabulation. The ~100,000 margin claim is approximately correct regardless.
**Fix:** Soften to "roughly 288,000 to 187,000 — a margin of about 100,000 votes" or use Wikipedia's verified figures, or add a note that vote totals vary by source depending on whether soldier votes are counted separately.
**Severity:** Low — does not affect the historical meaning or verdict of the section; purely a precision issue.

### ⚠️ SHF-2: Vallandigham's Canadian base — Windsor vs. Clifton House/Niagara
**Location:** Section "Arrested in the night…", paragraph on the Windsor exile.
**Issue:** The draft says Vallandigham "settled in Windsor, Ontario, directly across the river from Detroit." Sources confirm he initially went to Niagara Falls/Clifton House (arriving July 5, 1863) and later relocated to Windsor. Windsor is the correct location for much of the campaign and it is across from Detroit (confirmed). The issue is the word "settled" implies a single, immediate destination; he arrived at Niagara first.
**Fix:** Add a brief acknowledgment: "He first landed at Niagara Falls, Ontario, and later moved to Windsor, Ontario, across the river from Detroit, where he ran his campaign." Or simply drop "settled" for "based himself in Windsor, Ontario."
**Severity:** Very low — Windsor is correct and confirmed; this is a route-sequence detail only.

## Additional notes for editors

1. **Neely Pulitzer year:** The draft says *The Fate of Liberty* won the Pulitzer "1991." Technically it won the 1992 Pulitzer Prize (awarded in 1992 for work published in 1991). This is a common citation ambiguity. The publication year is 1991; the prize year is 1992. Neither formulation is factually wrong in context; "Pulitzer Prize–winning...1991" is a reasonable shorthand for publication year. No change required but editors should be aware.

2. **Merryman as "militia officer":** The draft calls Merryman a "Maryland secessionist militia officer." This is confirmed — he was a lieutenant in the Baltimore County Horse Guards (a pro-Confederate local militia). Accurate.

3. **Corning letter framing:** The draft says the Corning resolutions were sent by "New York Democrats led by the railroad magnate Erastus Corning." Technically the resolutions came from an Albany public meeting on May 16, 1863, which Corning moderated. He was president of the New York Central Railroad. "Railroad magnate" is accurate. The characterization is confirmed.

4. **[VERIFY] items are all resolved cleanly.** No fabricated Mount Vernon-speech quote was inserted — the draft correctly describes the speech in paraphrase only, which is the right approach given the absence of a reliable transcript.

5. **The "blind memorandum" reveal:** The draft says Lincoln revealed its contents at "the cabinet meeting of November 11, 1864." Confirmed. The LOC blog and multiple sources confirm November 11, 1864 as the reveal date.
