# Champion Hill — NEWCOMER/CLARITY Critic Gate (war pipeline step 4)

**Read cold as a sharp 15-year-old with ZERO Civil War knowledge.** Flagging undefined
terms/jargon, unexplained leaps, named actors lacking side-tag/scene-set, overwhelming
passages, and any `(see Vicksburg)` pointer that leaves a first-timer lost.

Verdict scale: **CLEAR** (a newcomer gets it) / **NEEDS-GLOSS** (term/leap needs an
inline definition or scene-set) / **LOST** (a first-timer is genuinely stranded).

Overall: the draft is unusually newcomer-friendly for a battle piece. The road/crossroads/
creek geography is built up beautifully and the "whoever holds the hill holds the road
home" logic is spelled out cleanly. The real clarity debt is **military unit vocabulary**
(corps / division / brigade / battery) and **one or two `see Vicksburg` pointers that
assume knowledge a first-timer doesn't have**. None of it is fatal; most are one-clause
glosses.

---

## A. MILITARY UNIT VOCABULARY — the biggest cluster (mostly MUST-FIX)

The draft leans hard on a hierarchy of unit words and **never defines a single one.** A
newcomer cannot tell whether a "corps" is bigger or smaller than a "division," or what a
"brigade" is. This matters because the whole tactical argument — "Grant won with half the
army," "a whole corps standing idle," "a third of Pemberton's army stood idle," "an entire
division lost" — depends on the reader feeling the *relative size* of these units. Without
a sense of scale, the climax (losing one "division") lands as an abstraction.

1. **"corps"** — §1 ("Sherman's corps," "McClernand's corps," "McPherson's corps"); §2
   ("a whole corps standing idle"). First appears line 74. Never defined.
   **NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX.** A first-timer has no idea this is the largest chunk of an
   army (tens of thousands). One inline gloss on first use ("a corps — the biggest building
   block of the army, several divisions strong") fixes every later use.

2. **"division"** — §1 (Hovey's division line 78), §2 (Logan's), §3 (Bowen's "finest
   division," Crocker's "fresh division"), §4 (Loring's "division," "an entire division
   lost"). Never defined. **NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX.** This is the single most load-bearing
   unit word in the piece — the climax is literally "the division lost forever." The reader
   must feel that a division is a large body of men (a chunk of a corps; thousands). Gloss
   on first use (line 78), ideally tied to the corps gloss so the nesting is clear:
   corps > division > brigade.

3. **"brigade"** — §2 ("a brigade of Alabamians," line 99); §4 ("Tilghman's brigade," line
   210). Never defined. **NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX.** Less critical than corps/division
   because the reader can infer "a body of troops," but if corps and division get glossed,
   brigade should get a half-clause too ("a brigade — a few regiments, smaller than a
   division") so the size ladder is complete. Note Stephen D. Lee's brigade being the unit
   that first *sees* the threat is a nice human-scale anchor — worth glossing here.

4. **"battery"** — NOT actually present in the draft prose. The task brief lists it as a
   term to watch for; it appears nowhere. Artillery shows up only as "a Union artillery
   shell" (line 212), which is self-explanatory. **CLEAR / N/A** — no action.

5. **"rearguard"** — §4 (line 209, "The job of holding the door open fell to the
   rearguard"). **CLEAR.** The draft self-glosses it immediately — "posted at the creek to
   buy time with their bodies while the rest of the army filed across." A newcomer gets it
   from context. No fix.

6. **"garrison"** (verb, line 167 "could not garrison" / noun line 244 "the city and its
   garrison"). **NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX.** Used two ways. Line 167's verb ("hold the
   ground with troops") is inferable from context. Line 244's noun ("the city and its
   garrison" = the troops defending it) is murkier for a first-timer. A one-word gloss
   ("its garrison — the troops defending it") on line 244 would tidy it.

7. **"works" / "safe works"** — §1 (line 45, "out of Vicksburg's safe works"); §4 (line
   243, "shut up inside Vicksburg's works"). Never defined. **NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX.** A
   newcomer won't know "works" = fortifications/defensive earthworks. Context ("safe")
   hints at protection but the word itself is jargon. One gloss on first use (line 45,
   "Vicksburg's safe works — its ring of fortifications") covers both.

8. **"siege"** — §4 (lines 244, 261; "the siege that followed — forty-seven days").
   **CLEAR (borderline).** The "forty-seven days, and finally the surrender of the city"
   surrounding text effectively glosses it. A first-timer gets "army surrounds city until
   it gives up." No fix required, but if a one-clause gloss is cheap, take it.

## B. FLANK / TACTICAL TERMS (mixed)

9. **"flank"** — §2 ("the very flank of the army," line 102; "the flank Grant meant to
   break," line 110; "the flank that held the road home," line 136); §3 ("the army's flank
   caved in," line 177). Never explicitly defined. **NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX.** "Flank" is
   used as the hinge concept of the entire battle ("the right blow in the right place... the
   part aimed at the flank"). A newcomer reads it as a vague body-part metaphor. It needs
   one inline gloss on first use (line 102): "flank — the exposed end of a battle line,
   where an army is easiest to get around and behind." The draft already gives the *why*
   ("if it got past, it would cut Pemberton off from Vicksburg") — it just never names what
   a flank physically *is*. The gloss makes "broke his flank" mean something concrete.

10. **"left" / "Confederate left" / "Stevenson's left"** — §2 (lines 110, 113,
    "Stevenson's division holding the Confederate left," "Stevenson's left came apart").
    **NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX.** A first-timer doesn't automatically know that an army's
    "left" and "right" are named from the army's own point of view, or that "the
    Confederate left" = "the northern end." The draft *does* couple it usefully — "the
    northern, exposed end of the Confederate line" (line 107) sits right before
    "Stevenson's division holding the Confederate left" — so a careful reader can map
    left=north. SHOULD-FIX: make the equivalence explicit once ("the Confederate left — the
    northern end of their line") so "left," "northern end," and "flank" all snap together.

11. **"crossroads" / "crossroads astride the road"** — §1 (lines 61–69). **CLEAR.** Fully
    built up: "Where the Jackson Road and the Middle Road came together... there was a
    crossroads," plus the chain-logic sentence ("Lose the hill, lose the crossroads...").
    Exemplary newcomer scaffolding. No fix.

12. **"ford"** — task brief lists it; **NOT in the draft.** The crossing is always "the
    bridge on the Raymond Road" / "the Baker's Creek bridge," which is self-explanatory.
    **CLEAR / N/A.**

13. **"in detail" (defeat in detail)** — task brief lists it; the *phrase* "in detail" does
    NOT appear. The concept (keeping the two Confederate armies apart so they can't combine)
    is present and **fully spelled out in plain language** in the §1 Meanwhile and §4 close
    ("Grant had wedged himself squarely between them," "the two Confederate armies could
    never join"). **CLEAR** — the draft renders the concept without the jargon, which is the
    right call. No action.

14. **"line" / "main line" / "battle line"** — used throughout (lines 102, 108, 165, etc.).
    **CLEAR (borderline).** A newcomer can infer "the row of troops facing the enemy" from
    context. If the §B-9 flank gloss is added, it can naturally introduce "line" at the same
    time. SHOULD-FIX only if convenient.

15. **"skirmishing"** — §2 (line 96, "Skirmishing opened at dawn"). **NEEDS-GLOSS →
    SHOULD-FIX.** First-timer won't know this is light, probing, advance-guard firing rather
    than the main fight. The follow-on ("Grant's three columns felt their way west") softens
    it but doesn't define it. A two-word gloss ("skirmishing — scattered, probing fire as
    the lead troops feel out the enemy") would help.

## C. NAMED ACTORS — side-tags and scene-setting (mostly CLEAR, a few gaps)

The draft is disciplined about side-tags `(North)`/`(South)` on first mention per section,
and re-tags across sections. Checked each:

16. **Grant, Pemberton** — §1. Both tagged AND scene-set (Grant's whole campaign; Pemberton
    "commander of the Confederate army charged with holding Vicksburg" + the contradictory-
    orders setup). **CLEAR.** Excellent — Pemberton's impossible-orders backstory is exactly
    the kind of scene-set a newcomer needs.

17. **Sherman, McClernand, McPherson** — §1 (lines 74–78). All `(North)` tagged. **CLEAR
    (borderline).** They're introduced functionally ("Sherman's corps was still back at
    Jackson," "McClernand... would sit nearly idle," "McPherson's corps and Hovey's
    division did the fighting"). A newcomer gets *what each contributed* even without a bio.
    No fix needed — but this is where the unmissable **corps** gloss (§A-1) must land, since
    three corps commanders arrive in one breath.

18. **Hovey** — §1 (line 78) tagged `(North)`. **CLEAR.** Introduced as commanding the
    division that "did the fighting" alongside McPherson; the human payoff ("hill of death,"
    "the cost fell hardest on Hovey's division") lands well.

19. **Joseph E. Johnston** — §1 Meanwhile (line 85) tagged `(South)`. **CLEAR.** Scene-set
    as "gathering a relief force near Jackson" — a newcomer understands he's a *second*
    Confederate force Grant is keeping apart from Pemberton. Good.

20. **Stephen D. Lee** — §2 (line 98) tagged `(South)` AND glossed "no relation to Robert E.
    Lee, despite the name." **CLEAR — and a model fix.** BUT note: this gloss assumes the
    reader knows who **Robert E. Lee** is. A true zero-knowledge 15-year-old may not. This
    is borderline. **SHOULD-FIX:** either trust that Robert E. Lee is famous enough to be
    common cultural knowledge (defensible), or add three words ("the South's most famous
    general"). Leaning SHOULD-FIX, low priority — Robert E. Lee is plausibly inside even a
    novice's general awareness.

21. **Logan** — §2 (line 108) tagged `(North)`. **CLEAR.** "the division of Major General
    John A. Logan" — functional intro, fine.

22. **Carter L. Stevenson** — §2 (line 110) tagged `(South)`, introduced as commanding "the
    Confederate left" Grant broke. **CLEAR.**

23. **Bowen** — §3 (line 154) tagged `(South)` AND scene-set richly ("commanded the finest
    division... hard-fighting Missourians and Arkansans"). **CLEAR.** Strong character intro;
    the §4 Meanwhile payoff (carries surrender flag, dies of dysentery) is well earned.

24. **Crocker** — §3 (line 170) tagged `(North)`, introduced as Grant's "fresh division."
    **CLEAR.**

25. **Loring** — §3 Meanwhile (line 194) tagged `(South)`, scene-set as the commander who
    "would not move." **CLEAR.** The villain-of-the-piece framing is set up well and pays off
    in §4.

26. **Tilghman** — §4 (line 210) tagged `(South)`, scene-set as the rearguard commander.
    **CLEAR.** His death is concrete and moving.

**Side-tag mechanics — one newcomer question:** the very first uses of `(North)` and
`(South)` (line 17, Grant `(North)`) are never explained. A first-timer meets "Grant
(North)" with no key telling them North = the United States/Union and South = the
Confederacy/rebellion. The §1 prose *does* establish "the Confederacy had broken away from
the United States" (line 31), which implicitly maps South=Confederacy — but the
North/South=tag convention itself is never stated. **SHOULD-FIX:** a single orienting
clause near the first tag, e.g. "the North (the United States, fighting to preserve the
Union)" vs "the South (the Confederacy)." This is probably handled app-wide by the war
section's framing, but flagging it because read in true isolation the parenthetical tags
are unexplained notation. (Per `feedback_war_person_side_tags`, North=Union/South=CSA is the
locked convention — worth one inline gloss on debut for a cold reader.)

## D. `(see Vicksburg)` POINTERS — does a first-timer who hasn't read Vicksburg get stranded?

This is the explicit ask. Eight pointers. Checked each for whether the *surrounding
sentence stands on its own* for someone who has NOT read the Vicksburg page.

27. **Line 21–22, "Port Gibson, Raymond... Jackson... (see Vicksburg for the full
    campaign)."** **CLEAR.** The sentence summarizes the campaign-so-far itself ("won a
    string of fights... wrecked and left smoking"); the pointer is genuinely just "more
    detail there." A newcomer is not lost.

28. **Line 38, "walking off the plantations toward the Union columns... (see Vicksburg)."**
    **CLEAR.** Self-contained — the self-emancipation point is fully made in the sentence;
    the pointer is optional depth. Good.

29. **Line 90, §1 Meanwhile, "the gap between them ran right through the crossroads...
    (see Vicksburg)."** **CLEAR.** The keep-the-two-armies-apart logic is fully explained in
    the same paragraph. Pointer is depth-only.

30. **Line 242, "ran Pemberton's rearguard off the field at Big Black River Bridge (see
    Vicksburg)."** **NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX.** "Big Black River Bridge" is named as a
    next-day event with zero scene-set — a new place and fight dropped in one clause. A
    first-timer reads it as "some other battle happened, go read elsewhere." It's not
    *lost* (the sentence's point — the beaten army got chased into Vicksburg — survives) but
    the bare named battle invites a reader to feel they're missing something. A three-word
    gloss ("at Big Black River Bridge, the last river crossing before the city") would let
    it land as a beat rather than a dangling reference.

31. **Lines 244–245, "the siege that followed — forty-seven days, and finally the surrender
    of the city and its garrison on the Fourth of July — ran straight out of this hill (see
    Vicksburg)."** **CLEAR.** The siege/surrender is fully described in-sentence (duration,
    outcome, date). Pointer is depth-only. This is the right way to do it.

32. **Line 248, "within days the river was open from Cairo to the Gulf and the Confederacy
    was split in two (see Vicksburg)."** **NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX.** "from Cairo to the
    Gulf" assumes the reader knows Cairo (Illinois — the northern end of the controlled
    river) and that "the Gulf" = Gulf of Mexico. A zero-knowledge reader may not place
    "Cairo" at all (and may read it as Cairo, Egypt!). The "split in two" payoff is clear,
    but the geographic bookends are unglossed. **SHOULD-FIX:** "open along its whole length,
    from the North down to the Gulf of Mexico" or gloss Cairo as "(Illinois)". The
    split-the-Confederacy stakes themselves are well established in §1, so the reader isn't
    *lost* on meaning — just on the place names.

33. **Line 269, §4 Meanwhile, "it was Bowen who helped carry the surrender flag out of
    Vicksburg, and days after that he was dead of dysentery (see Vicksburg)."** **CLEAR.**
    Self-contained; the pointer is depth. "dysentery" is plain enough. Good.

34. **§4 closing (line 247), "When Vicksburg fell, slavery fell with it in the city..."**
    **CLEAR.** No pointer issue; flagging only that "slavery fell with it in the city" is a
    touch compressed — a newcomer gets the gist (the city's enslaved people were freed when
    the Union took it), which is the intent. No fix.

**`see Vicksburg` summary:** the pointers are well-disciplined — 6 of 8 are depth-only and
the surrounding sentence fully stands alone (CLEAR). The two SHOULD-FIX cases (**Big Black
River Bridge**, line 242; **Cairo-to-the-Gulf**, line 248) are not stranding the reader on
the *argument*, only dropping an unglossed proper noun. Both are cheap one-clause fixes.

## E. UNEXPLAINED LEAPS / OVERWHELMING PASSAGES

35. **§1, the three-roads paragraph (lines 48–53).** Three road names (Jackson, Middle,
    Raymond), "north to south," plus "the Jackson and Edwards area." **CLEAR — borderline
    dense but well-built.** A lot of geography arrives at once, but the draft orders it
    cleanly (ridge → three parallel roads → names north-to-south → the key hill at the
    north end). A newcomer can follow it. "the Jackson and Edwards area" (line 49) names
    Edwards before §4 establishes it as "the little station" — minor, **SHOULD-FIX** only if
    convenient (one word: "Edwards station").

36. **§1, "thirty-two thousand... twenty-two or twenty-three thousand... half the army"
    (lines 72–81).** **CLEAR.** This is exactly where the corps/division glosses (§A) pay
    off — the numbers argument ("forget the picture of a larger army steamrolling a smaller
    one") is the clearest in the piece *once the reader has unit-scale*. Without the unit
    glosses, "McPherson's corps and Hovey's division — a force just about equal to
    Pemberton's" is the one genuinely confusing sentence for a novice (why does one corps +
    one division ≈ a whole 22,000-man army?). **This is the strongest argument for MUST-FIX
    on the corps/division glosses.**

37. **§4, the Loring-cutoff sequence (lines 221–229).** **CLEAR.** The "couldn't cross the
    creek, so he marched all the way around and joined the other army instead, and was gone"
    logic is spelled out step by step. The emotional payoff ("a loss with no body count to
    put on a casualty return") lands. Good.

38. **§4, casualty paragraph (lines 234–239).** **CLEAR.** "the casualty shape is the
    story" plus the plain-language unpacking ("the Confederate total is blown out by roughly
    2,441 men missing or captured") is genuinely newcomer-friendly statistics writing. The
    word "casualty" itself is borderline-jargon (a newcomer might think it means only deaths)
    — the draft's "killed and wounded... missing or captured" breakdown effectively glosses
    it. SHOULD-FIX at most.

## SUMMARY OF FIXES

### MUST-FIX (a newcomer is genuinely impeded — mostly the unit-scale ladder)
- **#1 "corps"** — gloss on first use (line 74). Load-bearing for the whole numbers argument.
- **#2 "division"** — gloss on first use (line 78). The climax word ("the division lost").
- **#9 "flank"** — gloss on first use (line 102). The hinge concept of the battle.

(These three are the spine: corps > division, and flank. Glossing them resolves the one
genuinely confusing sentence — line 78–79's "one corps + one division ≈ the whole
Confederate army" — and makes the §1 numbers argument and the §4 climax fully legible.)

### SHOULD-FIX (improves a cold read; not stranding)
- **#3 "brigade"** — half-clause gloss to complete the size ladder (line 99).
- **#6 "garrison"** (noun, line 244) — three-word gloss.
- **#7 "works"/"safe works"** — gloss on first use (line 45).
- **#10 "Confederate left" = northern end** — make the equivalence explicit once (line 110).
- **#15 "skirmishing"** — two-word gloss (line 96).
- **North/South tag convention** (§C closing) — one orienting clause at first tag (line 17).
- **#20 Robert E. Lee** — optional "the South's most famous general" gloss (line 98).
- **#30 "Big Black River Bridge"** (line 242) — three-word scene-set.
- **#32 "Cairo to the Gulf"** (line 248) — gloss Cairo / "the Gulf of Mexico."
- **#35 "Edwards"** (line 49) — one word ("Edwards station") to pre-place it.

### CLEAR / NO ACTION
crossroads (#11), defeat-in-detail-rendered-in-plain-language (#13), ford & battery (not
present, #4/#12), rearguard (#5), siege (#8), all side-tagged actors (#16–26), 6 of 8
`see Vicksburg` pointers (#27–29, 31, 33, 34), the three-roads build (#35), the Loring-
cutoff sequence (#37), the casualty paragraph (#38).

**Bottom line:** the prose, geography, stakes, and `see Vicksburg` discipline are
newcomer-strong. The only true blocker is unglossed **unit vocabulary** (corps/division/
flank) — three inline glosses fix the one sentence that genuinely confuses a novice and
unlock the numbers argument and the climax. Everything else is polish.
