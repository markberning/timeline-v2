# Jonesborough — NEWCOMER / CLARITY critic gate

> War pipeline, step 4 (clarity critic). Cold read as a sharp 15-year-old with
> ZERO prior Civil War knowledge. Flags undefined terms, unexplained leaps, named
> actors without side-tag or scene-set, and overwhelming passages.
> CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST · MUST-FIX vs SHOULD-FIX.

**VERDICT: PASS (with 2 MUST-FIX, several SHOULD-FIX).** The draft is unusually
disciplined about inline-defining its terms — *corps*, *entrenchment*, *salient*,
*picket line*, *Spencer repeating rifles*, *pontoon bridge* are all glossed on first
use, in plain language, exactly where a newcomer hits them. The disambiguation of
Jefferson C. Davis (Union) from Jefferson Davis (Confederate president) is handled
correctly and twice. The two MUST-FIX items are a geography assumption and one
genuinely overwhelming sentence; the SHOULD-FIX items are mid-tier units/places a
cold reader can't place.

---

## Term & gloss audit

### CLEAR — defined well on first use
- **corps** — §1¶5: "the largest building block of a Civil War army, several divisions
  and tens of thousands of men under one general." Perfect, and placed exactly where
  the first corps appears.
- **entrenchment** — §2¶1: "throwing up dirt into a defensive line, a rampart with a
  trench behind it, so that the men inside fight from cover while anyone who comes at
  them has to cross open ground." Concrete, visual, complete.
- **picket line** — §2¶2: "the thin screen of skirmishers thrown out in front of the
  main works." Glossed in the same breath it's used. Good. *(Minor: "skirmishers" is
  itself slightly inside-baseball — see SHOULD-FIX.)*
- **Spencer repeating rifles** — §2¶4: "lever-action weapons that fired several rounds
  before reloading where an ordinary rifle-musket fired one." Exactly the right amount —
  the reader understands *why* a thin line throws outsized fire.
- **salient** — §3¶1: "an angle that juts toward the enemy like the prow of a ship… the
  weak point of any defensive line, because the men holding its apex can be fired on
  from two directions at once." Best gloss in the piece — defines the term AND why it
  matters.
- **pontoon bridge** — §5¶4: "a temporary floating bridge of boats." Clean.
- **March to the Sea** — §5¶2: introduced with "the long destructive sweep across
  Georgia to Savannah." A cold reader gets enough. CLEAR.
- **Jefferson C. Davis disambiguation** — §3¶2 ("the *Union* general of that unfortunate
  name; he is no relation whatever to Jefferson Davis the Confederate president") and
  the §5 callback. This is the single most important clarity job in the piece and it's
  done right, twice. CLEAR.
- **S.D. Lee disambiguation** — §1¶5: "no relation to Robert E. Lee in Virginia." Good —
  though see SHOULD-FIX, because a zero-knowledge reader hasn't met Robert E. Lee yet.

### NEEDS-GLOSS
- **"cutting the supply line" / "cut the rail lines"** — the *concept* is actually
  explained beautifully in §1 ("it eats what the trains bring"), so the mechanism is
  CLEAR. But the phrase "cut it" / "severed" / "the last line was already severed"
  recurs and a cold reader may briefly picture literal cutting rather than "captured the
  track and tore it up so trains can't run." One clause at the first physical cut (§2¶6)
  — *tore up the track* — would nail it. NEEDS-GLOSS (light).
- **"flanking" / "wheeling march" / "swung… in a wide wheeling march"** — §1¶3 describes
  the maneuver vividly ("a great curling sweep around the city's flank") but never quite
  says what *flank* means (the side/end of an army's line, as opposed to its front). A
  sharp 15-year-old can infer it from "around… the flank," but "flank" is doing load-
  bearing work in §3 too (Lowrey "swinging wide," the salient hit "from the north…from
  the west"). One five-word gloss of *flank* at §1¶3 covers the whole piece. NEEDS-GLOSS.
- **"in echelon" / "rolling forward"** — the pack mentions "infantry in echelon"; the
  draft wisely says "the infantry rolling forward" instead, which is CLEAR. No action
  needed — noting it as a good avoidance.
- **"rifle-musket"** — §2¶4 uses it inside the Spencer gloss without defining it. Context
  carries it (the "ordinary" weapon that fires one round), so it's borderline. NEEDS-
  GLOSS (very light) or leave.

### LOST
- None fully lost. The two MUST-FIX items below are the closest to losing the reader.

## Actor / side-tag / scene-set audit

- **Side-tags are applied consistently** — every named person carries (North)/(South)
  on first mention per section, and the tags genuinely aid comprehension here given the
  two-Davises and two-Lees problem. CLEAR.
- **Scene-set is mostly good.** Sherman, Hood, Hardee, Howard, S.D. Lee, Govan,
  Cleburne are all given a role/army when introduced.
- **SHOULD-FIX — Robert E. Lee referenced before the reader has met him.** §1¶5 says
  S.D. Lee is "no relation to Robert E. Lee in Virginia." For a zero-knowledge reader,
  Robert E. Lee is a name with no attached meaning yet — so disambiguating *against* him
  doesn't help; it introduces a second unknown. Either add three words ("Robert E. Lee,
  the South's most famous general, in Virginia") or simply say "no relation to the famous
  Lee in Virginia." Small but real.
- **SHOULD-FIX — Grant arrives under-scene-set.** §4¶5: "Lt. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant's
  (North) Overland Campaign in Virginia had ground to a bloody stalemate in the trenches
  around Petersburg." A cold reader doesn't know Grant is the Union's overall top general
  (the reason his stalemate mattered to national morale). One clause — "the Union's
  overall commander" — makes the morale logic land. NEEDS-GLOSS / SHOULD-FIX.
- **CLEAR — McClellan** is scene-set adequately ("the army's own former commander").
- **CLEAR — Thomas, Schofield, Stanley, Slocum, Carlin, Morgan, Estey, Kilpatrick,
  Lowrey, Vaughan** are all given a unit/role on appearance.

## Unexplained-leap / overwhelming-passage audit

- **MUST-FIX — the geography is assumed, not established, and the reader can't picture
  the battle.** Atlanta is "above"/north; Jonesborough is "south"; the Flint River is
  "just west"; the Union approaches "from the west"; the salient is hit "from the north"
  and "from the west." All of this is in the prose, but it's scattered and a newcomer
  never gets the one orienting sentence that locks the map: *Atlanta to the north, the
  town and its railroad running north–south, the Flint River just west, the Union coming
  from the west.* The pack literally provides this (§4 "Map orientation summary"). Right
  now a cold reader cannot answer "which direction is the enemy" at any given moment.
  Add one short orienting sentence early in §2 (where Howard digs in near the Flint).
  This is the draft's biggest clarity gap. *(If the shipped section carries a battle
  map, this is softened — but the prose should still stand alone.)*

- **MUST-FIX — §3¶4 is an overwhelming sentence.** "Estey's regiments overran the
  Confederates holding the point — the 10th Kentucky and 74th Indiana rolling over the
  consolidated 6th and 7th Arkansas, the 14th and 38th Ohio punching clean through — and
  with Morgan's men closing from the other face, the salient was suddenly not a defensive
  angle but a trap." Six regiment numbers, two states, "consolidated," two converging
  directions, all in one sentence. A zero-knowledge reader cannot hold this — the unit
  numbers are noise they can't index, and they bury the one thing that matters (the apex
  got surrounded). Cut the regiment roll to at most one named unit and let the *trap* be
  the clear image. (The storytelling gate flags this same sentence for the same reason.)

- **SHOULD-FIX — "XIV Corps," "XVII Corps," "XX Corps," "IV Corps," "XXIII"** appear as
  roman-numeral corps designations throughout. The draft glosses *corps* (good), but the
  numbered designations still read as opaque codes to a newcomer. They're mostly fine as
  texture, but in §2 ("a Union division of the XVII Corps") and §3 ("The XIV Corps") the
  number is doing identification work the reader can't use. Where the corps matters
  (Davis's XIV Corps as the battle-winner), tie it to its commander/army once and let the
  roman numeral ride along; where it doesn't, the number is harmless texture.

- **SHOULD-FIX — Lovejoy's Station, McDonough Road** (§4¶1, ¶3) are place names dropped
  without orientation. Both are fine as "somewhere south / somewhere north" texture, and
  the prose does say Hardee slipped "south… to Lovejoy's Station," so the direction is
  there. No real loss — flag only because a map-less reader files them as unknown dots.
  Lowest priority.

- **SHOULD-FIX — "skirmishers"** (inside the picket-line gloss, §2¶2) is itself an
  undefined term used to define another term. Minor circularity. Swap to plain language
  ("the thin screen of soldiers thrown out ahead of the main line") and drop the jargon.

- **CLEAR — the election machinery (§4¶5–7)**, despite being dense (see storytelling
  gate), is *comprehensible* to a cold reader — every term (platform, peace plank,
  electoral votes, the Thirteenth Amendment) is either glossed or self-evident in
  context. It's a momentum problem, not a clarity problem.

## Punch list

**MUST-FIX**
1. Add ONE orienting geography sentence early in §2 (Atlanta north, town + railroad
   running north–south, Flint River just west, Union attacking from the west) so the
   reader can place every later "from the north/west" move. Pack §4 supplies it.
2. Break up §3¶4's six-regiment sentence — keep at most one named unit, make the
   *surrounded / trap* the clear image.

**SHOULD-FIX**
- §1¶5: don't disambiguate S.D. Lee against a Robert E. Lee the reader hasn't met —
  add three words identifying him, or say "the famous Lee in Virginia."
- §4¶5: scene-set Grant as "the Union's overall commander" so the Petersburg-stalemate
  morale logic lands.
- §1¶3: gloss *flank* in five words (the side/end of an army's line) — it's load-bearing
  in §1 and §3.
- §2¶6 (first physical cut): one clause clarifying "cut the line" = tore up the track so
  trains can't run.
- §2¶2: replace "skirmishers" inside the picket-line gloss with plain language (don't
  define one jargon term with another).
- Corps roman numerals: where a corps matters, tie it to its commander once; elsewhere
  fine as texture.
- Lovejoy's Station / McDonough Road / rifle-musket — lowest-priority; map-dependent.
