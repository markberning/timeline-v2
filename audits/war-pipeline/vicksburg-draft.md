## the-key · Vicksburg · The fortress on the bluffs · "The Key to the War"

In the spring of 1863 the Mississippi River was the spine of a continent, and the Confederacy was sitting on the one vertebra that mattered. Trace the river south from where it leaves Union hands and you eventually run up under a bluff town in western Mississippi where the channel doubles back on itself in a tight hairpin bend. The town sat on high ground on the east bank, and its guns looked straight down on the water below. Any boat coming downriver had to pass directly under those guns, slowly, in plain sight. That town was Vicksburg, and together with Port Hudson, Louisiana, downstream, it held the last Confederate-controlled stretch of the Mississippi. As long as Vicksburg stood, the river was closed.

That mattered for reasons both blunt and strategic. The Mississippi was how the farms of the Northwest — Ohio, Illinois, Iowa — got their grain and pork to market, and the Confederacy had corked the bottle. It was also the seam of the rebellion itself. East of the river lay the heart of the Confederacy; west of it lay Texas, Arkansas, and most of Louisiana, the so-called Trans-Mississippi, which funneled cattle, men, and supplies eastward across the water. Take Vicksburg and you opened the river to Union traffic and sawed the Confederacy clean in two. Abraham Lincoln understood this exactly, and is supposed to have told his cabinet that *"Vicksburg is the key. The war can never be brought to a close until that key is in our pocket."* `[VERIFY exact wording — the substance is standard; attribute as "Lincoln is supposed to have said" or cut the quote and keep the idea.]`

The man sent to turn the key was Major General Ulysses S. Grant (North), commanding the Army of the Tennessee. Grant was the Union's hardest-driving western general, the victor of Forts Henry and Donelson the year before, and by the end of 1862 he had a problem that no amount of hard driving had yet solved. Vicksburg could not simply be marched up to and attacked. North of the city lay a maze of swamp, bayou, and flooded bottomland; the bluffs themselves were a natural fortress; and the river batteries made a direct naval passage suicidal. Every obvious approach was blocked by water, mud, or guns.

The country he was trying to break in half was a country that existed for one reason. The Confederate states had seceded barely two years earlier to protect and extend the enslavement of four million Black people, on whose forced labor the cotton economy of the lower Mississippi Valley — Vicksburg's own valley — was built. The river plantations Grant's army would soon march past were worked by enslaved people, and the war to open the river was, underneath everything, a war against the slaveholding republic those plantations sustained. That was not incidental to the Vicksburg campaign. As we'll see, it was the campaign's deepest current: every mile Grant advanced down this valley, the institution the South had seceded to protect began dissolving behind him.

> **OUTCOME — Vicksburg was the prize the whole war in the West was organized around.** Whoever held the bluff town held the Mississippi, and whoever held the Mississippi could split the Confederacy and starve its western half. Lincoln called it the key. The problem facing Grant at the start of 1863 was that the key sat behind a wall of swamp, bluff, and heavy guns that no straightforward attack could get through.

**Meanwhile — the river out East.** Far to the northeast, the war in Virginia had stalled into a grinding deadlock around the Confederate capital at Richmond, and the headlines that winter were eastern headlines. But the men who thought longest about how to actually win the war kept coming back to the river. Whatever happened in Virginia, a Confederacy cut off from Texas beef and split down its own midline could not last. Vicksburg was the quiet hinge on which the whole map turned, even while the country's attention pointed somewhere else.

## the-failed-approaches · Vicksburg · The winter of mud · Canals, Bayous, and a Drowning Army

For four months over the winter of 1862 and 1863, Grant (North) tried to get at Vicksburg through the water, and for four months the water beat him. It made for a miserable, mud-caked, disease-ridden season that nearly cost him his command. Each scheme had the same logic: if the army couldn't pass the batteries on the river, maybe it could go around them through the tangle of swamps and side-channels that laced the bottomland north and west of the city.

The first attempt was a literal ditch. Across **De Soto Point** — the low, swampy peninsula on the Louisiana side, inside the hairpin bend opposite the city — Grant's men dug a canal, hoping to cut a new channel that would let the boats skip the bend and the guns entirely. The river refused to cooperate; the cut wouldn't take the current, and **Grant's Canal** was abandoned around March 1863. Upstream they tried **Lake Providence**, a scheme to open a long looping bayou route through Louisiana to rejoin the river far below Vicksburg — a project so unpromising that the campaign's own historians later nicknamed it the "Lake Providence Boondoggle." It too was given up in late March.

The army went deeper into the swamp after that. The **Yazoo Pass Expedition** blew a hole in a levee near Moon Lake and sent boats winding down a chain of smaller rivers — the Coldwater into the Tallahatchie into the Yazoo — to try to land troops on dry, high ground above the city. They were stopped cold by a fort the Confederates threw up at Greenwood out of cotton bales, **Fort Pemberton**, which sat squarely in the only navigable channel. Then came the strangest of all: the **Steele's Bayou Expedition**, in which Rear Admiral David Dixon Porter (North), commanding the river fleet of gunboats and ironclads, pushed his warships up a twisting bayou through the Yazoo delta to get around Fort Pemberton from behind. The Confederates simply felled trees into the narrow water ahead of the boats and behind them, boxing the fleet in. Porter's gunboats got stuck in a flooded forest and came within a hair of being captured, until Major General William T. Sherman (North) marched infantry through the swamp to pry them loose.

Every one of these had now failed. From a distance — and the Northern press was watching from a distance — it looked like Grant was finished: bogged in the mud, his army sick and dying of camp fever in the flooded lowlands, the man himself drinking. *(The whiskey stories were rumor and political knife-work, the kind of charge that trailed Grant his whole career; treat them as a press attack of the moment, not fact.)* What the press could not see was that the failures were teaching Grant the one thing the swamp had to teach: there was no way through the water from the north. If he wanted Vicksburg, he would have to do something far more dangerous.

> **OUTCOME — the winter approaches all failed, and the failure was the point.** Four separate schemes to bypass Vicksburg's guns through canal and bayou came to nothing but mud, disease, and humiliation in the press. But by spring they had closed off every easy option and forced Grant toward the bold, base-cutting gamble that actually worked. Sometimes the value of a dead end is that it proves the road is dead.

**Meanwhile — inside the fortress, confidence.** For Lieutenant General John C. Pemberton (South), the Confederate commander charged with holding Vicksburg, the winter looked like one repulse after another handed to him for free. Every Union scheme had broken against the swamps and his guns without his army having to fight much for it. The river still belonged to the Confederacy. The bluffs were as strong as ever. It was easy, that winter, for the defenders to believe that the geography itself was on their side — that Vicksburg could not be taken, only failed against. The spring would test that belief to destruction.

## the-gamble · Vicksburg · The spring run · Past the Guns and Loose in Mississippi

What Grant (North) decided to do in April 1863 was the kind of plan that gets a general either remembered or relieved. If he couldn't get above or around Vicksburg, he would get *below* it — march his army down the Louisiana side of the river, past the city out of range of its guns, then cross the Mississippi back to the eastern bank somewhere south of the fortress and come at it overland from the rear, the one direction the swamps didn't protect. The trouble was the fleet. The army could march down the west bank, but it needed Porter's (North) gunboats and transports on the *same* side of the river to ferry it across — and the only way to get them downstream was to run them straight past the Vicksburg batteries.

On the night of April 16, 1863, Porter did exactly that. He took a fleet of gunboats and transports downriver in the dark, hugging the far bank, and for two and a half hours the Vicksburg guns lit up the night and hammered them as they passed. Most of the fleet got through; only a single transport was lost. A second run followed on April 22. It was a stunning thing to watch and a stunning thing to pull off, and it put Porter's boats below the city, exactly where Grant needed them.

To keep Pemberton's (South) eyes anywhere but the crossing point, Grant ran two deceptions. He sent Sherman (North) to feint an attack up at Haynes' Bluff, north of the city, where the Confederates still expected the blow to fall. And he turned loose a cavalry raid under Colonel Benjamin Grierson that slashed roughly 600 miles straight down through the length of Mississippi, tearing up railroads and drawing Confederate forces in a frantic chase across the state. While Pemberton looked north and east, Grant moved south. On April 30, Porter's fleet ferried the army across the river to **Bruinsburg**, on the Mississippi shore well below Vicksburg — at the time the largest amphibious landing in American history, a record it held until the D-Day invasion of Normandy in 1944. The army stepped onto dry Mississippi soil with the fortress behind it and the interior of the state open ahead.

Then Grant did the thing that made the whole campaign legend: he cut himself loose. Rather than tie his army to a vulnerable supply line stretching back to the river, he largely abandoned his base and lived off the country, the army carrying what it could and taking the rest from the Mississippi countryside as it marched. Freed from the supply tether, he drove inland fast, and over the next eighteen days he fought five battles. He landed and won at **Port Gibson** on May 1, securing the beachhead. He beat a Confederate brigade at **Raymond** on May 12. Then, instead of turning straight for Vicksburg, he wheeled northeast and on May 14 took **Jackson**, the Mississippi state capital, scattering a small relief force gathering there under General Joseph E. Johnston (South) and wrecking the city's railroads and war industry so it could not be used against him. Only then did he turn west toward Vicksburg itself, deliberately keeping his own army planted between Johnston's relief force to the east and Pemberton's garrison to the west so the two could never combine.

The decisive collision came on May 16 at **Champion Hill**, about 20 miles east of Vicksburg, the hardest-fought battle of the campaign. Grant's army broke Pemberton's in a brutal day on the ridges, and the result sealed Vicksburg's fate: Pemberton was thrown back toward the city, and an entire Confederate division under Major General William W. Loring (South) was cut off in the rout and never made it back inside the works at all — it wandered off to join Johnston instead. Grant reportedly said the day's result *"assured of our position between Johnston and Pemberton, without a possibility of a junction of their forces."* `[VERIFY exact wording — attribute loosely or paraphrase; the gist that Champion Hill kept the two armies split is well attested.]` The next day, May 17, Pemberton's rearguard tried to hold the crossing of the **Big Black River** just east of the city and was routed, the survivors streaming back over the bridges and into the Vicksburg fortifications. The gamble had worked. In eighteen days Grant had crossed a major river, cut his own supply line, won five battles, taken a state capital, and driven the enemy's whole field army inside its walls.

> **OUTCOME — the spring gamble was one of the most daring campaigns in American military history, and it won the war's western half.** By running the batteries, crossing at Bruinsburg, and cutting loose from his base, Grant got behind Vicksburg's defenses entirely. Five battles in eighteen days — capped by Champion Hill — drove Pemberton's army inside the city and kept it permanently separated from Johnston's relief force. Vicksburg could now be besieged instead of stormed at the front door.

**Meanwhile — Johnston outside, helpless.** While Grant tightened his grip, Johnston (South) was supposed to be assembling the army that would rescue Vicksburg. He never could. He started with only a few thousand men, was scattered out of Jackson before he could concentrate, and spent the rest of the campaign gathering a relief force that was always too small and always too late. From the eastern edge of the action he could see the trap closing on Pemberton and could do almost nothing about it. The relief that the defenders inside the city kept waiting for, all through what came next, was a relief that was never going to break through.

## the-siege · Vicksburg · Forty-seven days · The Caves, the Trenches, and the Mule Meat

Grant (North) did not want a siege. He wanted to take the city by storm and be done with it, and on May 19 and again on May 22 he tried. The first assault, on May 19, threw his men at the **Stockade Redan**, one of the strongpoints in the roughly nine-mile arc of earthworks — redans and redoubts and lunettes, the angled forts and bastions of the era — that ringed Vicksburg's landward side. It was thrown back with about a thousand Union casualties against perhaps seventy Confederate. The second, on May 22, was bigger and worse: a broad assault across a three-mile front at ten in the morning that failed bloodily, costing roughly 3,200 Union men against fewer than five hundred Confederate. Part of that slaughter came when Major General John A. McClernand (North), one of Grant's corps commanders, overstated a breakthrough on his front and drew reinforcements into a position that wasn't there — a piece of self-promotion that fed more men into the killing ground and helped get McClernand relieved of his command weeks later.

Two failed assaults in three days settled it. Grant would not bleed his army against those works again. *"I now determined upon a regular siege — to 'out-camp the enemy,' as it were, and to incur no more losses,"* he wrote. `[VERIFIED — Grant, Personal Memoirs, per WP-Siege.]` So the army dug. Union engineers ran some fifteen miles of approach trenches zigzagging toward the Confederate works, brought up around 220 guns, and settled in to strangle the city while Porter's (North) fleet shelled it from the river. For forty-seven days, from May 18 to July 4, the lines tightened and the bombardment never really stopped.

Inside the perimeter were not only Pemberton's (South) soldiers but more than three thousand civilians, trapped in their own town under a continuous rain of shells. They went underground. Residents dug something like five hundred caves — "bombproofs," they called them — into the soft yellow loess clay of the hillsides, some single rooms, some big enough to hold several families, and moved their lives into them. They learned the rhythm of the shelling and timed their movements above ground to the gaps between bombardments. It is one of the campaign's stranger statistics that despite roughly 22,000 shells fired into the town over the siege, fewer than a dozen civilians were killed. One of them was Mary Webster Loughborough, twenty-six years old, trapped in the city while visiting and writing it all down in a diary that became the book *My Cave Life in Vicksburg* in 1864 — one of the few civilian eyewitness accounts of what the siege felt like from inside. *"I shall never forget my extreme fear during the night, and my utter hopelessness of ever seeing the morning light,"* she wrote of one bombardment. `[VERIFY exact wording — Loughborough is a real, quotable cave diarist, but verify this line against the memoir or paraphrase.]`

What finally broke Vicksburg was not the shelling but hunger. The food ran out. The beef went first, then the garrison and the townspeople began eating their mules and horses; dogs went into the pot; rats appeared for sale in the public meat market, reportedly around $3.50 apiece, roughly two weeks' pay for a private. People baked bread out of dried peas and cornmeal, and one account records that "shoe leather became a last resort of sustenance for many adults." Disease did the rest. By late June, half the garrison was sick or in the hospital — scurvy from the ruined diet, malaria and dysentery and chronic diarrhea from the swamp and the filth. Grant's army, fed and resupplied by the open river behind it, grew larger through the siege, swelling toward 70,000 men as reinforcements came in to guard against Johnston. Pemberton's army did the opposite: it shrank, starved, and sickened, with no relief in sight. That widening gap — one army growing while the other wasted away — was the whole arithmetic of the siege. The Union engineers even tunneled a mine under the **Third Louisiana Redan** and blew it up on June 25, opening a crater the storming party couldn't hold, with a second mine fired July 1. But by then the explosions were almost beside the point. The city was being starved to death.

> **OUTCOME — the 47-day siege ground Vicksburg down without another grand assault.** After two costly failed storms on May 19 and 22, Grant settled into a siege that strangled the city: 15 miles of trenches, 220 guns, constant bombardment, and an open river feeding his army while the garrison and 3,000 civilians starved on mule, horse, and rat. One army swelled toward 70,000; the other wasted to half-sick. The result was no longer in doubt — only the date.

**Meanwhile — the country waits on the river.** Beyond the lines, the whole war seemed to pause over Vicksburg. Lincoln had called it the key, and for forty-seven days the North watched to see whether Grant could finally turn it. The siege coincided with the Confederacy's great gamble in the East, General Robert E. Lee's invasion of Pennsylvania, and as June turned to July the two crises came to a head together — one army dug into the bluffs over the Mississippi, another marching north toward a Pennsylvania town called Gettysburg. The country did not yet know that both would break the same week.

## emancipation-made-real · Vicksburg · Freedom along the river · The Cause Made Flesh

Here is the part of the Vicksburg story that the maps and the casualty tables miss, and it is the heart of it. The war was fought over slavery — the Confederacy had seceded to protect it — and on this campaign, more than almost anywhere else, the abstraction turned into something you could watch happen on the ground. As the Army of the Tennessee pushed through the Mississippi Valley, slavery dissolved in front of it. The institution the South went to war to preserve came apart, plantation by plantation, in the army's path.

It did not come apart because Grant (North) freed people, exactly. It came apart because enslaved people freed themselves. The National Park Service, which keeps the history of this ground, stresses that the enslaved men and women of the valley "more often already had taken steps to secure their own emancipation" — young men slipping away in small groups to Union lines, families walking off the plantations toward the soldiers — rather than waiting to be liberated. Thousands self-emancipated as the army advanced, until the surrender itself ended slavery in the city: *"July 4, 1863: Slavery Ends in Vicksburg"* is the actual title the Park Service gives the day. The campaign that split the Confederacy also split open slavery along the river, and the people doing the splitting were, in large part, the enslaved themselves.

The army had to figure out what to do with the tide of people coming to it, and the answer was both a lifeline and a hard place. Grant established refugee camps — "contraband" camps, in the awkward legal language of the time, the word the Union used for enslaved people who reached its lines — across the Mississippi Valley, sheltering thousands of freedpeople. Conditions in them were often brutal: disease ran through the crowded camps, prejudice and violence followed the refugees, and many were put to wage labor on leased plantations. But the camps were also where free Black life along the river began to organize itself. And they were a recruiting ground. The Union's adjutant general, Lorenzo Thomas, came down the valley to enlist freedmen into the army, and from the Mississippi Valley alone something like 70,000 formerly enslaved men joined the United States forces. The men the South had owned were now being handed rifles to fight the South.

Whether those men would fight was a question a lot of people, North and South, claimed to know the answer to, and they were about to be proven wrong. On June 7, 1863, while the siege ground on, a Confederate force struck a Union supply depot on the Louisiana bank at **Milliken's Bend**, about fifteen miles above Vicksburg. The depot was held largely by the **African Brigade** — newly recruited, formerly enslaved men in the 9th, 11th, and 13th Louisiana Infantry (African Descent) and the 1st Mississippi Infantry (African Descent), days into uniform, barely trained and poorly armed, stiffened by the white 23rd Iowa. At dawn a Texas brigade of roughly 1,500 under General Henry McCulloch (South) came at them. What followed was some of the most savage hand-to-hand fighting of the entire war: bayonets and clubbed muskets across the levee and the cotton-bale barricades, men killing each other at arm's length. Union gunboats on the river, the *Choctaw* and the *Lexington*, finally broke the attack with their heavy guns.

The cost was terrible, and the meaning was enormous. Union casualties at Milliken's Bend ran to roughly 500 to 650 — the figure is genuinely disputed across sources, with the Park Service citing around 652 and others closer to 492 — against perhaps 185 Confederate. `[contested — give the range, do not assert a single number.]` The 9th Louisiana, the formerly enslaved regiment at the center of it, lost something on the order of 68 percent of its strength, the greatest proportional loss of any Black regiment in the war; its dead were the most any single Union regiment lost on any one day of the whole Vicksburg campaign. They had stood, and fought hand-to-hand, and held until the gunboats came. Milliken's Bend, together with the fights at Port Hudson and Fort Wagner that summer, shattered the claim that Black men would not or could not fight. The Secretary of War, Edwin Stanton, said the formerly enslaved soldier had *"proved his manhood"* in these battles. `[VERIFY exact phrasing — Stanton's words via NPS; verify or paraphrase the gist.]`

And it was Lincoln who tied it all together — the river and the men who freed themselves to open it. In a public letter dated August 26, 1863, to James C. Conkling, written just after the victory, Lincoln bound the unblocked Mississippi directly to the Black soldiers who had helped win it. On the river, he wrote that *"The Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea."* `[VERIFIED exact — Conkling letter, Aug 26, 1863.]` On the soldiers, in the same letter, he was just as plain. They had to be fighting for freedom, he reasoned, because that was the only motive strong enough — *"If they stake their lives for us, they must be prompted by the strongest motive — even the promise of freedom. And the promise being made, must be kept."* And he looked ahead to how they would be remembered: *"there will be some black men who can remember that, with silent tongue, and clenched teeth, and steady eye, and well-poised bayonet, they have helped mankind on to this great consummation."* `[VERIFIED exact — Conkling letter.]` The man in the White House had drawn the line himself, from the opened river straight back to the cause: slavery, and the people ending it.

> **OUTCOME — Vicksburg is where emancipation stopped being a proclamation and became a fact on the ground.** Slavery dissolved along the army's path as thousands of enslaved people freed themselves; Grant's contraband camps and Lorenzo Thomas's recruiting put some 70,000 freedmen into Union uniform; and at Milliken's Bend on June 7 formerly enslaved soldiers fought hand-to-hand and held. Lincoln's Conkling letter bound the open river to those Black soldiers explicitly. The campaign that split the Confederacy also broke the institution the Confederacy existed to defend.

**Meanwhile — the argument that wouldn't die quietly.** For two years, North and South had argued bitterly over whether to make Black men soldiers, and a loud chorus had insisted they would break and run at the first fire. Milliken's Bend, fought by men who had been enslaved weeks earlier, was an answer written in blood on a Louisiana levee, and it carried far beyond the riverbank. Recruiters used it, the War Department cited it, and Lincoln folded it into his case to a skeptical Northern public. The fighting on the Mississippi that summer didn't just open a river. It helped settle, for good, the question of who got to be a soldier in the war to end slavery.

## the-surrender · Vicksburg · The Fourth of July · The Father of Waters Goes Unvexed

By the start of July 1863, Pemberton (South) was out of road. His garrison was starving, half of it sick, the relief from Johnston (South) was not coming and he knew it, and the Union lines were practically on top of his works. On July 3 he sent out a flag of truce — carried by Major General John S. Bowen (South), his best division commander, the man who had fought the rearguard actions of the campaign — and opened negotiations with Grant (North). Grant, true to the nickname he had earned at Fort Donelson, first demanded unconditional surrender. Then he reconsidered the arithmetic. Shipping nearly thirty thousand prisoners north up the river would tie up his boats and his men for weeks. So he offered terms instead: parole. The garrison would lay down its arms and sign an oath not to fight again until formally exchanged, and then go home.

On July 4, 1863 — Independence Day — Vicksburg surrendered. About 29,495 Confederate soldiers marched out, stacked their arms, and signed their paroles, many of them gaunt from weeks of mule meat and pea bread. Grant's army took the city, the garrison, around 172 cannon, and tens of thousands of small arms. There is a long-told story that Pemberton, who was Northern-born — a Pennsylvanian who had cast his lot with the South — deliberately chose the Fourth of July because he believed Union pride in the date would win his men better terms; the exact words attributed to him are not reliable, but the gist that he banked on the date is reasonably well attested. `[VERIFY Pemberton's July-4 reasoning and any quoted wording — the gist is attested via Grant's Memoirs; do not quote him verbatim without confirmation.]` Whatever his calculation, the surrender on that particular date carved itself into local memory: by reputation, Vicksburg did not officially celebrate the Fourth of July again for something like 81 years, into the 1940s. `[VERIFY the "81 years" span — commonly cited but figures vary; soften to "for generations" if unverified.]` The human toll outlasted the surrender too. Bowen, who had carried the flag of truce, was already dying of dysentery and was dead within days, on July 13 — one more casualty of the disease that had hollowed out the garrison.

The numbers around the surrender tell the story of how the siege ended without another grand battle. The siege itself cost the Union roughly 4,800 men killed, wounded, and captured; the full campaign back to the spring landings cost something over 10,000 on each side in killed and wounded. `[campaign casualty totals vary by source and by whether the inland battles are folded in — use the fact-pack ranges, not single hard figures.]` But the figure that mattered was the surrender: nearly 30,000 men taken out of the war at a stroke, an entire field army gone.

What it changed was the map of the war. Five days after Vicksburg fell, Port Hudson, Louisiana, the last Confederate post downstream, gave up too — there was no longer any point in holding it — and with that the Union controlled the entire Mississippi River, end to end. Texas, Arkansas, and most of Louisiana, the whole Trans-Mississippi, were now cut off from the rest of the Confederacy, severed from the beef and men they had been sending east. The river that had been closed was open, and Northwestern grain could float to the sea again. This was the moment Lincoln captured in the Conkling letter with the line that has outlived almost everything else about the campaign: *"The Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea."* `[VERIFIED exact — Conkling letter, Aug 26, 1863.]`

And it did not happen alone. Vicksburg surrendered on July 4, the day after the Confederate army was thrown back for the last time at Gettysburg, in Pennsylvania, hundreds of miles away. The two together — the great eastern invasion turned back, the great western fortress fallen, in the same 48 hours — are conventionally counted as the military turning point of the entire Civil War, the week the Confederacy lost the initiative for good and never got it back. For Grant, the campaign made his name; the boldness of it, the river crossing, the army cut loose and living off the land, the wedge driven between two enemy armies, put him on the road to general-in-chief of all the Union armies within the year. The key was in the pocket at last.

> **OUTCOME — the July 4 surrender split the Confederacy and opened the Mississippi for the rest of the war.** Nearly 30,000 men surrendered and were paroled; Port Hudson fell five days later; the Union held the whole river end to end, cutting off the Trans-Mississippi. Coming the day after Gettysburg, Vicksburg is half of the war's twin turning point — the week the Confederacy lost the initiative permanently. It made Grant, opened the river, and, in Vicksburg itself, ended slavery on the Fourth of July.

**Meanwhile — the day after, at Gettysburg.** Seven hundred miles to the northeast, the other half of the turning point had just finished. On July 3, Lee's last great assault was shattered on a Pennsylvania ridge, and on July 4 his beaten army began the long retreat back toward Virginia — the same day Pemberton's men were stacking their arms on the bluffs over the Mississippi. The North got both pieces of news almost together, and the meaning was unmistakable: the Confederacy had reached for victory in the East and gambled on survival in the West, and in a single week it had lost at both. After that July, the war's outcome was no longer really in doubt. Only its length was.
