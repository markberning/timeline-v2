## MUST-ADD 1 — Northern (not just British) economic complicity
The shipped section frames the cotton economy's world entanglement as Britain-centric. The
North was also financially anchored to cotton — this matters for an honest *road-to-war* frame
(it's why Northern antislavery politics was slow and contested even in the North).
- New England mills consumed roughly **two-thirds (~67%) of domestically-used U.S. cotton in
  1860**. `[VERIFY]`
- **Massachusetts alone ran ~30% of the nation's cotton spindles.** `[VERIFY]`
- Northern banks financed plantations and **accepted enslaved people as loan collateral**; a
  predecessor of a major modern bank (J.P. Morgan Chase lineage) accepted ~13,000 enslaved
  people as collateral. `[VERIFY — keep general if the precise institution can't be pinned]`
- Takeaway sentence the section needs: the North was not a horrified bystander — its mills and
  banks ran on the same cotton, which is part of why ending slavery was so politically hard
  even in the "free" states.
- Sources: PBS "Many Rivers to Cross" (Why Was Cotton King?); *The American Yawp* ch.11;
  TIME "How Wall Street Funded Slavery" (2024); Sharon Ann Murphy, *Banking on Slavery* (2023).

## MUST-ADD 2 — The Slave Power / Three-Fifths political bridge
The section explains the *economic* certainty of the planter class but not the *political*
machinery that backed it — so the handoff to "Road to War" reads as an assertion. Add 2–3
sentences bridging cotton wealth → federal political power.
- **Three-Fifths Compromise (1787):** enslaved people counted as three-fifths of a person for
  House apportionment, handing slave states **roughly 14 extra House seats in the first
  Congress** (and proportional electoral-college weight). `[VERIFY seat figure]`
- This structural overrepresentation persisted for decades: enslavers dominated the
  presidency (**11 of the first 15 presidents were enslavers**), the Supreme Court, and key
  committees. `[VERIFY count]`
- Northerners came to call this bloc the **"Slave Power"** by the 1840s–50s — slavery as a
  *nationally protected* interest, not a local Southern practice. This is why Lincoln's 1860
  election felt to the South like losing a system it had always controlled.
- Sources: Wikipedia "Three-fifths Compromise"; American Battlefield Trust "Slavery and the Constitution".

## SHOULD-CONSIDER (non-blocking)
- One clause of resistance: enslaved people were not only acted upon — work slowdowns, flight,
  and the feared specter of revolt (Nat Turner, 1831) shaped the system's daily brutality.
- The planter class was tiny: by 1860 only ~1.4% of white Southerners owned 20+ enslaved
  people (the "planter" threshold) yet controlled state governments and the regional narrative. `[VERIFY %]`
