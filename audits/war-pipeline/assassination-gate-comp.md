### THIN — present but underdeveloped

**T1. The Surrattsville tavern stop (checklist item 32)**
The draft says Booth and Herold "retrieved supplies" — the phrase buries what is actually a critical evidentiary detail: Mary Surratt rode to the tavern that afternoon, told tenant John Lloyd to have the "shooting irons" ready for men coming that night, and delivered field glasses. That errand is the main piece of physical evidence used to argue she knowingly participated in the assassination plot (vs. merely owning the boardinghouse where the plot was hatched). The draft's Gallows section debates her guilt, but never tells the reader what the actual evidence was — the "shooting irons" errand is the weight-bearing beam of the prosecution's case against her, and it is entirely absent from the manhunt narrative where it belongs. Readers are asked to evaluate a contested verdict without the fact that makes the contested verdict feel real.

**T2. Easter Sunday / Good Friday martyrdom mechanics (checklist item 45)**
The draft notes Lincoln died on Good Friday and was transformed into a martyr/secular saint/Father Abraham, but it says nothing about *how* that transformation happened so fast. The Easter Sunday sermons are the mechanism: ministers across the country scrapped their Resurrection messages and instead preached Lincoln-as-Christ, reaching congregations before newspapers could. The timing (Good Friday death, Holy Saturday death, Easter Sunday news arriving from the pulpit) is what made the religious framing not just an analogy but the literal first framing the country received. The draft's "Father Abraham" section asserts the transformation without explaining the engine — "the timing did half the work" is too vague; the Easter sermon phenomenon is the work.

**T3. Black Americans' particular grief (checklist item 46)**
The draft says mourning was mass and national. It does not single out Black Americans' grief as distinctive or note that it carried a specific fear-for-the-future dimension. Douglass's "unspeakable calamity" line or its equivalent, or the observation that African Americans felt Lincoln's death with a particular urgency (the Great Emancipator struck down, uncertainty about their future), is missing. This is a short addition, not a whole section — but its absence is a real thinning of the aftermath's human stakes.

**T4. Henry Rathbone's long aftermath (SHOULD-CONSIDER)**
The draft correctly covers Rathbone's stab wound the night of the assassination and moves on. The post-traumatic arc (Rathbone blamed himself for the rest of his life; he and Clara Harris married 1867; he eventually shot and stabbed his wife on Christmas Eve 1883 in Germany and lived out his days in an asylum) is a striking example of assassination's long shadow. This is SHOULD-CONSIDER rather than MUST-ADD — the section is not primarily about Rathbone — but a single sentence noting his destroyed life would earn its place.

## FACT-PACK BLIND SPOTS

Items the draft does not cover that also have no fact-pack entry:

**FP-1. The Surrattsville "shooting irons" errand.**
The fact-pack mentions the tavern as part of the escape route ("They retrieved supplies at Surrattsville") in Beat 7, but does not surface the Mary Surratt errand of that afternoon — the field glasses delivery and the "have the shooting irons ready" instruction to Lloyd — as a distinct entry in Beat 8 (the Surratt guilt section). This is the load-bearing prosecution evidence. Both draft and fact pack treat the tavern purely as a logistics stop rather than as the evidentiary crux of the Surratt case.

**FP-2. Easter Sunday sermons as martyrdom mechanism.**
Beat 9 says Lincoln died on Good Friday and was transformed into a martyr. Neither the draft nor the fact pack records the Easter Sunday sermon phenomenon as the specific cultural engine of that transformation — an event now well-documented in scholarship (the Emory University digital archive of 1865 Easter sermons; Martha Hodes, "Mourning Lincoln"; Friends of the Lincoln Collection). This is an omission shared by draft and fact pack.

**FP-3. Black Americans' distinctive grief.**
Beat 9 covers mass mourning. Neither draft nor fact pack specifically identifies Black Americans' reaction or fear for the future as a distinct element of the mourning story, despite its historical salience (Douglass, the Christian Recorder, the Hodes and Hudson scholarship on the subject).

**FP-4. Rathbone's long aftermath.**
Not in either document. SHOULD-CONSIDER tier — see T4 above.

**FP-5. The broader Confederate-government conspiracy question.**
The fact-pack does not flag the question of Confederate high-command complicity (the Montreal meeting, the cipher evidence presented at trial, Jefferson Davis's ambiguous comments). The draft handles this correctly by not asserting it, but a reader or author might want to know: historians treat Confederate government direct involvement as unproven but not dismissed. This is an omission only at the fact-pack level — the draft's silence on it is correct given the contested state of the evidence — but the fact-pack should flag it as context the author consciously chose not to assert.

## MUST-ADD (blocking — revise before ship)

**MA-1. The Surrattsville "shooting irons" errand.**
In the manhunt section, before or alongside the Mudd/Zekiah Swamp account, name the Surrattsville tavern stop and tell the reader what happened: Booth and Herold stopped at the Surratt House tavern around midnight and collected the carbines and field glasses John Lloyd had been told to have ready. Then — critically — in the Gallows section where Mary Surratt's guilt is debated, the reader must know *what the actual evidence was*: that afternoon, before the murder, she rode to the tavern and told Lloyd to have the "shooting irons" ready for men coming that night. That errand is the prosecution's main physical evidence. Right now the draft asks the reader to assess a contested verdict without the most important fact that makes the verdict contested. One or two sentences in the manhunt section; three or four in the Gallows section, integrated into the existing Surratt guilt paragraph.

**MA-2. Easter Sunday sermons as the mechanism of Lincoln's instant martyrdom.**
The Father Abraham section asserts the Good Friday timing "did half the work" but does not say *how*. Easter Sunday morning, April 16 — one day after Lincoln died — ministers across the North scrapped their Resurrection sermons and preached Lincoln as a Christ figure: killed on Good Friday to save his people, dying so the Union could live. This is not a metaphor applied later by historians; it is what happened in real time, and it is why the transformation was instantaneous rather than gradual. Three or four sentences here would give the draft's strongest thematic section (Father Abraham) the causal spine it currently lacks.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER (not blocking)

**SC-1. Black Americans' particular grief.**
A sentence or two noting that African Americans — freedmen especially — experienced Lincoln's death with a specific, pointed fear: the Great Emancipator had been struck down, and the future under his successor was suddenly unknown. Douglass's "unspeakable calamity" line is quotable, born-verifiable, and adds a human dimension the current mourning narrative elides.

**SC-2. Henry Rathbone's destroyed life.**
One sentence in or after the Ford's Theatre section noting that Rathbone never recovered psychologically from that night — blamed himself for the rest of his life, eventually killed his wife in Germany in 1883, and died in an asylum. A striking illustration of the assassination's long shadow.

**SC-3. Confederate-government complicity question (fact-pack note only).**
Add a flag to the fact pack (Beat 2 or Beat 8) noting that Confederate high-command involvement was alleged at the tribunal (the Montreal meeting, cipher evidence, Davis testimony context) but was never proved and Davis was never tried for it. The draft's silence on this is correct — but the fact pack should document it as a conscious omission, not a blind spot.
