# the-valley | The Valley Campaign

In the spring of 1862, the Confederacy was in trouble. A huge United States army under Maj. Gen. George B. McClellan (North) had landed on the Virginia coast and was grinding its way up the peninsula toward Richmond, the Confederate capital, and there did not seem to be enough Southern soldiers to stop it. So the Confederates went looking for a way to make the North flinch — and they found it about a hundred miles to the west, in a long green valley that pointed, like a loaded rifle, straight at Washington, D.C.

The man they handed that rifle to was Maj. Gen. Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson (South), a secretive, deeply religious former college professor who had earned his nickname the previous summer by holding his brigade (a brigade being a block of several regiments, themselves blocks of several hundred men each, so a few thousand soldiers in all) steady "like a stone wall" while the line around him broke. Over the next six weeks Jackson would run one of the most studied campaigns in military history through that valley — and it is worth being clear, before any of the marching and the glory, about what the campaign was actually in service of. The army Jackson commanded fought for a country, the Confederacy, that had been created for one overriding purpose: to keep millions of human beings enslaved. Every brilliant march in this story was a march in defense of slavery. That is the war underneath the war.

## Jackson's campaign | The granary aimed at Washington

The ground Jackson was defending is called the Shenandoah Valley, a corridor of farmland roughly 150 miles (240 km) long running southwest to northeast through western Virginia, walled in by mountains on both sides. Two things made it priceless. First, it was a granary: its farms produced something like a fifth of Virginia's entire wheat crop, plus cattle and other food the Confederate armies and the city of Richmond needed to keep eating. Lose the Valley and you starve the war.

Second, and more dangerous, was its geography. Because the Valley ran southwest to northeast, "down" the Valley (toward its lower, northern end) meant moving toward the Potomac River — and just beyond the Potomac sat Washington itself. An army marching north through the Shenandoah was an army walking toward the back door of the Union capital. That fact terrified Washington, and Jackson's whole job was to make it terrifying. Running up the middle of the Valley was the Valley Turnpike (a turnpike being a maintained toll road, in this case the region's main paved-stone highway), and the Turnpike ran straight through the town of Winchester, the strategic hinge at the Valley's northern end.

Jackson's orders came down from Gen. Joseph E. Johnston (South), who commanded the main Confederate army standing between McClellan and Richmond. The instruction was not to win some grand victory. It was simpler and sneakier: tie down the Union forces in the Valley, keep them busy, and above all keep them from being sent east to reinforce McClellan's drive on Richmond. In particular, Jackson was to keep one Union army, under Maj. Gen. Nathaniel P. Banks (North), pinned in the Valley — and to keep a second, larger Union corps under Maj. Gen. Irvin McDowell (North), waiting near Fredericksburg, too nervous to march south. If Jackson could keep those tens of thousands of men looking over their shoulders at him, they could not be looking at Richmond.

## Jackson's campaign | The foot cavalry

To do that with a small army, Jackson did the one thing nobody expected: he made his infantry move like horsemen. His soldiers earned the nickname "foot cavalry" — foot soldiers who covered ground the way mounted cavalry was supposed to. The numbers behind the nickname are genuinely hard to believe. Across the full campaign, Jackson's men marched 646 miles in 48 days, an average of better than 13 miles a day, day after day, for a month and a half. In the week leading up to Winchester they covered about 140 miles in seven days, roughly 20 miles a day. Later in the campaign the famous Stonewall Brigade would march 42 miles in a single day, through rain and mud, with nothing to eat.

Jackson got this out of men by sheer iron discipline. He drove hard starts, allowed few breaks, and had no patience at all for stragglers. He also kept his plans a secret from nearly everyone, including his own generals — they often had no idea where they were going until they got there, which was maddening for them and baffling for the enemy. Nobody could counter a move they couldn't see coming.

Through April and May, Jackson used that speed to wrong-foot the Union forces one after another. At the Battle of McDowell (May 8) he beat back a Union force in the western mountains, keeping two Union armies from joining up against him. Then, on May 23, he played his sharpest card yet at the small town of Front Royal. Jackson and Maj. Gen. Richard S. Ewell (South) fell on the little Union garrison there and shattered it, capturing most of its men and inflicting around 773 casualties while losing only about 36 of their own. Front Royal mattered far beyond its size: it sat on the flank of Banks's army and on the rail line feeding it, which meant Jackson was now suddenly beside and behind Banks, threatening to cut off his line of retreat.

Banks got the message that his Front Royal garrison was gone and did the only sensible thing: on May 24 he ordered his army to fall back north up the Valley Turnpike from Strasburg, racing for the safety of Winchester before Jackson could get across his path. It was not a clean retreat. Confederate cavalry and infantry harried his columns all day at Middletown and at Newtown (Stephens City), and in the running fight Jackson's men overran piles of abandoned Union supplies — the first of the hauls that would soon make Banks a Confederate punchline. By the evening of May 24, Banks had gotten what was left of his army into Winchester. Jackson was right behind him.

### Meanwhile in Washington | The capital starts to sweat

Before a shot was fired at Winchester, the alarm had already begun ringing in Washington. The news from Front Royal — a Union garrison wiped out, Jackson loose and driving north toward the Potomac — landed on President Abraham Lincoln (North) and his Secretary of War as a genuine fright. On May 24, the day after Front Royal, Lincoln made a fateful decision: he telegraphed McClellan that "in consequence of General Banks's critical position I have been compelled to suspend General McDowell's movements to you." That was exactly what Johnston had wanted. McDowell's corps, the reinforcement McClellan was counting on for the final push to Richmond, was being frozen in place — and Jackson hadn't even reached Winchester yet.

# the-rout | The Rout of Banks

By the gray, foggy morning of May 25, 1862, Nathaniel Banks (North) had perhaps 5,000 to 6,500 exhausted soldiers strung out in a thin line on the hills just south and southwest of Winchester. (His command on paper was far larger — figures as high as 22,500 get thrown around — but that was the entire sprawling department he was responsible for, not the worn-down force actually present for this fight. The men who would do the bleeding numbered only a few thousand.) Banks reportedly understood that holding the town was hopeless. He was not fighting to win. He was fighting to buy time for his 550 supply wagons to escape north toward the Potomac before Jackson's army arrived to take them.

Facing him, Stonewall Jackson (South) had something like 16,000 men coming up out of the fog.

## May 25 | Dawn on Bowers Hill

Jackson attacked at first light, his men moving forward through a dense morning fog that hid them until they were close. He split the assault. Richard Ewell's (South) division came in on the Union left, east of town, pushing up the Front Royal Pike toward the Union brigade dug in near Camp Hill on the southeast edge of Winchester. Jackson himself drove up the center and right along the Valley Turnpike, toward the key piece of ground: Bowers Hill, a rise southwest of town where Col. George Gordon's (North) brigade anchored the Union right flank (the flank being the exposed end of a battle line, the place every general most fears having turned). Whoever held Bowers Hill held Banks's whole position.

For a while it was a slugging match. Ewell's push on the Union left made only limited headway. On Bowers Hill, Confederate cannon and Union cannon hammered each other at close range along the Turnpike, an artillery duel with neither side budging. Banks's tired men, for all their exhaustion, were holding.

## May 25 | "I will send you up Taylor"

So Jackson reached for the move that would end the battle. He turned to Brig. Gen. Charles Winder (South) and, when Winder suggested hitting the Union right flank up on Bowers Hill, Jackson answered simply: "I will send you up Taylor."

Taylor was Brig. Gen. Richard Taylor (South) — son of former U.S. President Zachary Taylor, brother-in-law of Confederate President Jefferson Davis, and commander of a roughly 2,000-man brigade of Louisiana troops. Under cover of the same morning fog, around 7:00 to 7:30 a.m., Taylor swung his Louisianans wide to the west, out around the end of the Union line, until they were positioned to come up the slope of Bowers Hill and roll up the Union right from the side. As they moved into place, Federal artillery found them and opened fire; some of the men flinched and ducked for cover.

Then Taylor did something almost theatrical. Rather than rush, he rode out in front of his whole brigade and led them up the hill, in his words, "in a steady walk" — no running, no premature firing, just a disciplined line climbing into the guns. A Confederate staff officer named Henry Kyd Douglas, who watched it happen and later wrote the memoir *I Rode with Stonewall*, called it:

> "a more beautiful charge… a line of glistening bayonets bright in that morning sun, its formation straight and compact."

The Louisianans walked through Federal musket fire and canister (canister being an artillery round that turns a cannon into a giant shotgun, spraying iron balls into massed men), closed the holes blown in their line, and crashed into the Union right. The 27th Indiana and 29th Pennsylvania regiments broke. The 2nd Massachusetts had to fall back. Gordon's brigade came apart on Bowers Hill — and once the right flank went, the whole Union line went with it. Within minutes Banks's entire position disintegrated. One Confederate sergeant remembered Taylor's charge as "the grandest I saw during the war… every man was in his proper place."

## May 25 | The streets of Winchester

What was left of Banks's army poured back north through the streets of Winchester in disorder. And Winchester was the worst possible town to retreat through, because Winchester was fiercely pro-Confederate. As the Union soldiers streamed past, the town's residents lined the streets in jubilant excitement, ringing church bells and cheering Jackson's men into the town they had just been liberated from. (By some accounts, civilians also fired on the retreating Union soldiers from windows and doorways; this is reported rather than firmly established, and the better-documented scene is the cheering crowd, not the shooting.)

The retreat was a shambles. When Banks tried to rally his men, one Federal soldier is supposed to have called back that he was "trying to get to it as fast as I can" — trying, that is, to reach the road north and keep running. The numbers tell the rest: of Banks's roughly 2,019 casualties for the day, 1,714 — about 85 percent — were captured or missing. An army that loses that many men as prisoners has not retreated in good order. It has broken. Against that, Jackson's whole army lost only about 400 men, killed and wounded. The fight was lopsided to the point of embarrassment, around five Union losses for every Confederate one.

Jackson ordered a hard pursuit, hoping to turn the rout into the destruction of Banks's army. But here his luck and his "foot cavalry" finally ran out. His infantry was exhausted from the dawn fight, and he had too little cavalry to keep the pressure on. North of town the pursuit lost its coordination and finally sputtered out around Stephenson's Depot, a few miles up the Turnpike. Banks's beaten army, harassed but not finished, streamed away north toward Martinsburg and the Potomac beyond.

### Meanwhile in the Valley | The cost of one morning

The two days of Front Royal and Winchester together had cost Banks something on the order of 3,000 men against roughly 400 Confederate losses across both fights. In a war that would be remembered for its slaughter, Jackson had just wrecked a Union army at almost no cost to his own — and he had done it not by overwhelming numbers but by marching faster, hiding his plans, and hitting the one spot, the flank on Bowers Hill, where a single brigade could unravel a whole line.

# commissary-banks | Commissary Banks

The chase north did not stop at the edge of Winchester. Nathaniel Banks (North), his army shattered, kept his men moving up the Valley Turnpike all day on May 25, and the distances are their own kind of testimony. His lead elements reached Martinsburg, about 22 miles north, by early afternoon; the rest came straggling in by around 5:00 p.m. And he did not stop even there. That same evening, still May 25, Banks pushed the remnants of his command across the Potomac River at Williamsport, Maryland, leaving Virginia behind entirely. From Winchester to the far bank in Maryland was something like 35 miles, covered in a single brutal day. He had lost nearly a third of his force — but the army, as an organized body, had survived the run, helped along by the fact that Jackson's tired pursuit never closed the trap.

## The aftermath | The general who fed the rebels

What Banks left strewn behind him on the roads of the Shenandoah is the reason his name became a joke in the Confederate ranks. In the retreat from Strasburg through Winchester, his army abandoned a genuine mountain of supplies — and Jackson's perpetually under-equipped soldiers fell on it like Christmas morning. The captured haul, tallied across the retreat, ran to something like half a million rounds of ammunition, more than 9,300 small arms (muskets and pistols), tens of thousands of pounds of food including some 15,000 pounds of bacon, over a hundred head of cattle, and wagonloads of clothing and blankets.

To half-starved, half-shod Confederate soldiers, this was almost too good to be true: a Union general who kept showing up to hand them everything they needed. So they gave him a nickname that stuck to him for the rest of the war and beyond — "Commissary Banks," the commissary being the officer in an army whose job is to supply the troops with food and provisions. Banks, in their telling, was the best commissary the Confederate army had. It was a cruel joke, and Banks never lived it down.

## The aftermath | The alarm that saved Richmond

The deeper damage was not measured in bacon. It was measured in the panic Jackson set off in Washington. Lincoln (North) and his Secretary of War had spent the past days watching Jackson tear north toward the Potomac, and their worry was no longer just about Banks — it was that Jackson might be the leading edge of a Confederate thrust at Washington itself.

That alarm had already begun before Winchester. Recall that on May 24, in response to the disaster at Front Royal, Lincoln had telegraphed McClellan that he was "compelled to suspend General McDowell's movements to you" — freezing the corps reinforcement McClellan needed for Richmond. Winchester did not start that decision; it deepened it and seemed to confirm it. With Banks now routed and across the Potomac, Lincoln went further, scrambling to trap Jackson in the Valley. He redirected some 20,000 of McDowell's troops away from the march on Richmond and toward the Shenandoah, set Maj. Gen. John C. Frémont's (North) army marching to converge from the west, and tried to spring a three-army pincer to catch Jackson before he could escape south.

The pincer failed — Jackson, faster than all of them, slipped out of the trap and went on to win again at Cross Keys (June 8) and Port Republic (June 9) before vanishing east. But the failure almost didn't matter, because the strategic damage to the Union was already done. With roughly 17,000 men, Jackson had tied down elements of three separate Union armies totaling more than 50,000 — by some counts over 60,000 — soldiers, all of them now committed to the Valley and the defense of Washington instead of to the one place it counted: the gates of Richmond.

That was the whole point. McDowell's corps, the reinforcement Lincoln had promised McClellan for the final push, never came south. And a month later, when Gen. Robert E. Lee (South) struck McClellan outside Richmond in the Seven Days Battles and drove the Union army back from the capital, he was fighting an enemy that Jackson, a hundred miles away in his valley, had quietly helped to weaken. The small campaign in the Shenandoah had reached all the way to the front gate of the Confederate capital and helped hold it.

For Jackson, Winchester was the high-water mark — the campaign's signature victory, Taylor's picture-perfect charge up Bowers Hill and Banks's army in full flight across the Potomac. The Valley Campaign made Stonewall Jackson the most celebrated soldier in the Confederacy and a name spoken with dread in the North, and it became a textbook study, taught for generations, in how a small, fast army can run rings around larger ones. The legend was set. So, beneath it, was the cause it served.

### Meanwhile in Richmond | The diversion pays off

While Jackson was wrecking Banks in the Valley, the thing he had been sent to prevent was quietly not happening east of him. McDowell's corps stayed put, then turned the wrong way, pulled toward the Shenandoah by the alarm Jackson had created. McClellan, already cautious, now stood before Richmond without the reinforcement he had counted on. Johnston's orders to Jackson had been to keep those men away from Richmond, and Jackson had done exactly that — not by fighting near the capital at all, but by being so loud and so fast a hundred miles away that Washington could not look anywhere else. When Lee saved Richmond a few weeks later, part of the work had already been done in the Valley.
