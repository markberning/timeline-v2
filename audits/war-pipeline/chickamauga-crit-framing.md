## SLAVERY-NAMED CHECK (run FIRST — ship-blocking)

**RESULT: YES — PASS.** The section names slavery as the cause in its OWN prose,
explicitly and early, exactly where the brief's hook wants it (the Chattanooga
campaign driving at the Deep South's slave economy / gateway).

`the-maneuver` opening (lines 15):
- "the Confederacy was, at its root, a country built to keep four million Black
  people enslaved."
- "The Southern states had broken away from the United States to protect and extend
  slavery, the institution their whole economy and social order rested on."
- Georgia as "plantation country, and its plantations ran on enslaved labor; cotton
  had made slaveholding explode after the cotton gin, and the South was fighting to
  keep that system alive."
- "By 1863, after the Emancipation Proclamation, the North's war aim had become the
  destruction of slavery itself. Cracking open Chattanooga meant cracking open the
  gate to the slaveholding interior. That is what the campaign was for, and it is what
  the killing two weeks later was ultimately about."

This is not a token mention — it is a sustained, causal, unambiguous statement of
slavery as the war's cause, tied directly to the battle's objective. It is then
reinforced in the closing `Meanwhile · the road to Atlanta` (line 105): "the rail
gateway to the Deep South, the heart of the slave economy the war was being fought
over … the campaign that would, the following year, march through Georgia and help
break the back of the slaveholding South." The cause is named at both ends of the
section and threaded through the gateway motif. Fully satisfies the NAME-THE-CAUSE
rule.

Web-confirmed: Chattanooga was the "Gateway to the Lower South," the rail junction
to Atlanta and the Deep South interior — the gateway framing is historically sound,
not invented to carry the slavery point.

## FRAMING FINDINGS

### 1. Thomas the "Rock" — romanticization watch (the brief's flagged risk) — FAIR
The brief specifically warns the gallant-Thomas / "Rock of Chickamauga" tone must not
tip into romanticizing the FIGHT itself. The draft threads this well:
- Thomas is admired for SAVING AN ARMY (turning a rout into an organized retreat),
  not for valor-as-spectacle. The praise is operational ("bought the time," "let the
  broken army escape … as an army, not a mob"), not glory-worship.
- Critically, the draft front-loads the irony that disarms any Lost-Cause reading of
  Thomas: he was "a Virginian and a former slaveholder — born into the world the
  Confederacy was fighting to preserve — and he had chosen the Union over his native
  South" (line 67). This is the OPPOSITE of romanticizing the Confederate cause; it
  uses the most celebrated Southern-born figure on the field to underline that the
  Union side was the side fighting against slavery. Web-confirmed and even
  understated: Thomas's family disowned him, and he became a civil-rights defender in
  Reconstruction — the draft's framing is accurate and if anything generous to
  nuance. (Author already flagged exact-framing for the fact-checker; framing-wise it
  is sound.)
- The nickname itself is handled with explicit skepticism (line 71–73): the draft
  calls the clean coinage story "too tidy to fully trust" and refuses to assert
  Garfield coined it on the spot. That is anti-legend hygiene, the opposite of myth-
  making.
**Verdict: FAIR.** Thomas is lionized for preventing a slaughter, the celebration is
attached to the man's loyalty-against-his-class, and the myth is openly flagged as
myth. No romanticization of the Confederate fight.

### 2. "The bloodiest Confederate victory" framing — FAIR, not glorifying
The draft repeatedly calls Chickamauga the Confederacy's biggest/only major Western
victory. Lost-Cause risk: dwelling on a rare Confederate win can read as a triumph
ballad. The draft neutralizes this structurally:
- The win is consistently framed as PYRRHIC and SQUANDERED: "the side that won the
  field lost more men doing it" (line 85, 101, 103); "A victory thrown away" (the
  section title); Bragg "threw the victory away" (line 95); "tactical triumph quietly
  turned into a strategic catastrophe" (line 99).
- The closing inverts it entirely: the win became "the doorway through which Grant
  arrived … and threw the gate to the Deep South wide open" (line 101). The
  Confederate victory is the setup for the Confederacy's undoing.
**Verdict: FAIR.** The win is reported, never celebrated; the frame is "they won the
field and lost the war's logic," which is accurate, not Lost-Cause.

### 3. "Élan of the Southern soldier" quote (D. H. Hill) — TILTED (caution, not fail)
The block quote "The élan of the Southern soldier was never seen after Chickamauga"
(line 97) is a romantic-register line from a Confederate general about Confederate
fighting spirit. In isolation it carries faint Lost-Cause "gallant South" coloring
("élan"). It is NOT a distortion because the draft deploys it to make a COST point
(the win hollowed out the victors), and it sits inside a paragraph hammering that
Bragg squandered the win. The surrounding frame defuses it. Still: it is the one spot
where Confederate-valor diction is voiced approvingly without an immediate counter-
clause in the same beat.
**Verdict: TILTED — SHOULD-FIX (optional).** Keep the quote (it's making a cost
argument, not a glory argument), but consider one half-clause anchoring that the
"élan" was spent killing for the slaveholders' cause, OR ensure the fact-checker's
exact-phrasing verification (already flagged in the ledger as VERIFY #5) doesn't let
it stand as an unframed romantic flourish. Low severity.

### 4. Ben Hardin Helm / Lincoln's grief — FAIR
The Confederate general married into Lincoln's family, killed at Chickamauga, with
Lincoln "reportedly mourn[ing] the young man personally" (line 93). This is a human-
reality beat that could risk unfair sympathy for a Confederate. The draft handles it
straight: it's noted as the war reaching "into the First Family on the LOSING side of
this Confederate win" — sympathy is Lincoln's private grief, not the narrator
ennobling the Confederate cause. No glorification of Helm's side; the pathos is
about the war's reach, not Confederate nobility.
**Verdict: FAIR.**

### 5. Human reality — neither sanitized nor gratuitous — FAIR
`the-reckoning` (line 87) handles the carnage honestly: ~34,000 casualties, men
firing at muzzle flashes, "the wounded lay where they fell, some of them in burning
underbrush," Hood's leg amputated on the field. This is unflinching but not lurid —
it serves the point that the blind woods made the battle uniquely cruel. Casualties
are given for BOTH sides without false balance (the draft notes the winners bled
harder — an accurate, non-both-sides observation). No sanitizing euphemism for the
killing; no gratuitous gore.
**Verdict: FAIR.**

### 6. No false both-sides balance, no euphemism, no presentism — FAIR
- No "war between brothers / states' rights / heritage" framing anywhere.
- Secession is stated as "to protect and extend slavery," not euphemized as
  "Southern independence" or "way of life."
- "Enslaved labor" / "enslaved people" used — not the softer "slaves" exclusively
  (line 15 uses "enslaved labor" and "four million Black people enslaved").
- No presentist editorializing; the moral frame is carried by period-accurate causal
  statement, not anachronistic lecturing.
**Verdict: FAIR.**

## DISPOSITION

- **MUST-FIX: 0.** The ship-blocking slavery-named requirement is met emphatically;
  no distortion, no Lost-Cause framing, no false balance, no soft-pedaling.
- **SHOULD-FIX: 1 (Finding #3).** The D. H. Hill "élan" quote is the lone spot with
  unframed Confederate-valor diction; optional half-clause or tighter framing
  recommended. Already independently flagged in the fact ledger for exact-phrasing
  verification (#5), so the fact-checker pass will touch it regardless.

**Overall framing verdict: FAIR.** This is a model section for the cause-first
directive — slavery named in the section's own prose at both ends, the gateway motif
historically grounded, the most romanticizable figure (Thomas) used to UNDERCUT Lost
Cause rather than feed it, and the rare Confederate victory framed as pyrrhic and
self-defeating.
