# Cold Harbor — CRAFT PANEL, Gate: NEWCOMER / CLARITY

> War content pipeline, step 4 (critic gate, parallel). Cold read as a zero-knowledge,
> sharp 15-year-old who has never taken a Civil War class. Flags: undefined terms,
> unexplained leaps, named actors with no side-tag or scene-set, passages that
> overwhelm. Verdict scale: CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST per item; action scale: MUST-FIX
> (ship-blocking) vs SHOULD-FIX (polish). Does NOT add or invent facts — where a gloss
> needs a fact the draft lacks, it's flagged for the fact pack.

**Overall gate result: PASS (no MUST-FIX; several SHOULD-FIX).**
This draft is unusually disciplined about defining-on-first-use. The terms the brief
specifically worried about — entrenchments, frontal assault, the Chickahominy,
salient, "no harbor" — are all either inline-defined or handled. ("Salient" never
appears, so there's nothing to gloss.) A zero-knowledge reader can follow this start to
finish. The flags below are local roughnesses and a few named actors who arrive a touch
faster than the reader can place them, none of them ship-blocking.

---

## Terms the brief asked me to watch

- **"no harbor" / the name** — **CLEAR.** This is the *opening move* and it's defined
  immediately and memorably: no harbor, no water, period-English "cold harbour" = an
  inn that gives shelter but no hot meal. A newcomer is never confused; they're
  delighted. Best-glossed item in the piece.
- **entrenchments / works / fieldworks** — **CLEAR.** §2 ¶2 defines it explicitly:
  "dug-in defensive lines: a trench scraped into the dirt with the spoil thrown up in
  front as a rampart." "Works" and "fieldworks" are both named as synonyms in the same
  breath. A 15-year-old gets it on first read.
- **frontal assault** — **CLEAR.** Defined in the same paragraph: "charging straight at
  the works across open ground." Good.
- **enfilade** — **CLEAR** (and this is hard to gloss). "That sideways, raking fire down
  the flank of a charging column is called enfilade... you can't see where it's coming
  from and you can't shoot back at it." Defined *and* given its emotional weight before
  it's used to explain Smith's ravine in §3. Model gloss.
- **the Chickahominy** — **CLEAR.** First appearance (§1 MEANWHILE) defines it: "a
  sluggish, swampy stream," names why it matters twice (the river Grant must cross; the
  1862 river), and re-uses it consistently. Good.
- **salient** — **N/A.** The word does not appear in the draft. Nothing to fix.
- **rifle (vs smoothbore)** — **CLEAR.** §2 defines it inline: "a musket with a grooved
  barrel that spun the bullet, accurate and lethal at hundreds of yards, far past the
  old smoothbore." A newcomer learns why 1864 was deadlier than they'd assume.

## CLEAR (a newcomer follows with no help)

- The **why-a-crossroads-matters** logic ("a road junction in 1864 was what a highway
  interchange is now — control it and you control how fast an army can move"). CLEAR.
- **Grant's role** is scene-set carefully: "not just an army commander but
  general-in-chief of all the United States armies, riding with the Army of the Potomac
  and making its decisions." This pre-empts the reader's natural confusion about why one
  general "makes the decisions" for an army someone else commands. CLEAR.
- **Continuous contact** is explained by contrast (old generals retreated; Grant slid
  around and came back) rather than as jargon. CLEAR.
- **truce** is glossed inline in §4: "a temporary ceasefire to let stretcher parties go
  out and bring them in." CLEAR.
- **USCT** is spelled out and defined on first use: "United States Colored Troops —
  Black soldiers in Union uniform." CLEAR.
- **enfilade re-used in §3** ("swept from the sides by exactly the enfilade musket and
  artillery fire described earlier") explicitly back-references its own definition — a
  newcomer who half-remembered the term gets a free reminder. CLEAR, and good practice.

## NEEDS-GLOSS (a newcomer stumbles; fix is small)

1. **"VI Corps," "XVIII Corps," "II Corps," "V Corps," "IX Corps" — roman-numeral corps
   designations are never explained as a unit-size concept.** — **SHOULD-FIX.** A
   sharp 15-year-old can infer "a corps is a big chunk of army" from context, and the
   draft does consistently pair each with its commander, so no one is *lost*. But the
   reader is never told a corps is roughly tens of thousands of men, nor that roman
   numerals are just unit ID numbers (XVIII = "the 18th"). One short parenthetical at
   first use ("VI Corps — a corps was a major formation, on the order of [N] men") would
   convert inference into knowledge. *(Fact pack: the corps-size figure is not in the
   pack — flag for the fact-checker if a number is to be printed; otherwise gloss
   qualitatively as "a major formation of many thousands.")*

2. **"Army of the James" vs "Army of the Potomac"** — **SHOULD-FIX.** §2 says Smith's
   XVIII Corps was "on loan from the separate Army of the James" and §1/§2 reference the
   Army of the Potomac, but a newcomer is never told there were *multiple Union armies*
   with different names operating in Virginia, or what distinguishes them. The word
   "separate" hints at it but doesn't resolve it — a reader may wonder "wait, how many
   Union armies are there?" One half-clause ("the Army of the James, a second Union army
   operating nearer Richmond") would settle it. Not lost, just briefly puzzled.

3. **The §3 ¶5 number block** — **SHOULD-FIX (overwhelm, not term-confusion).** This is
   flagged by the storytelling gate too, but it's also a *clarity* problem: a
   zero-knowledge reader hits roughly six different casualty figures (3,500–4,000 /
   ~6,000 / 1,000–1,500 / 3,500–7,000 / "several times the rate") in one paragraph and
   cannot hold them apart — which is the *morning*, which is the *whole day*, which is
   *Confederate*. The takeaway the reader is supposed to keep ("the famous 7,000-in-
   20-minutes is a myth; the killing was real and lopsided") survives, but it's doing so
   *despite* the number density, not because of it. Compressing to the two contrast
   figures (the legend's 7,000 vs. the morning's ~3,500–4,000) plus "lopsided" would
   make it land cleanly. Overwhelm flag, not a LOST.

4. **"lodgment"** (§3: "the only real lodgment of the day") — **SHOULD-FIX.** The draft
   actually self-glosses it well right beside it ("Union soldiers physically inside the
   rebel trench"), so a careful reader is fine, but the bare word "lodgment" then recurs
   ("the only real lodgment of the day," "the one doorway slammed shut" for the same
   idea). The gloss is present but trails the word by a clause; tightening so the plain
   meaning comes first ("Hancock's men actually broke through and got physically inside
   the rebel trench — the only real lodgment of the day") removes the half-second of
   "what's a lodgment?"

5. **Marye's Heights / Fredericksburg** (§5: "the most one-sided beating... since
   Marye's Heights at Fredericksburg a year and a half before") — **SHOULD-FIX.** This
   is a *comparison to another battle a newcomer hasn't read about*, used to convey
   scale, but it assumes the reader already knows Fredericksburg was a famous lopsided
   slaughter — which is the whole point of the comparison. A four-word gloss ("at
   Fredericksburg — another doomed uphill charge into a stone wall — a year and a half
   before") would make the comparison actually *land* for someone meeting both battles
   for the first time. As written, the reference is inert for the target reader. *(Fact
   pack: the "stone wall / sunken road" detail of Fredericksburg is not in this pack;
   keep any gloss generic — "another notorious one-sided repulse" — unless the
   fact-checker supplies the detail.)*

## Named actors — scene-set / side-tag check

- **Side-tags are consistently applied** on first per-section mention: Grant, Lee,
  Sheridan, Wright, Smith, Hancock, Warren, Burnside, Breckinridge, Porter, Hinks all
  carry (North)/(South). A newcomer always knows which side a name is on. CLEAR.
- **Breckinridge (South)** (§3) appears only as "Maj. Gen. John C. Breckinridge's
  (South) front." Side-tagged, fine — a newcomer doesn't need more than "the Confederate
  commander Hancock briefly broke through." **CLEAR**, no fix.
- **Sheridan, Wright, Smith, Hancock** in §2 ¶3–4 arrive in quick succession, each with
  rank + corps + side-tag, which is a lot of new names in two paragraphs — but each is
  given a *clear job* ("seized the junction," "arrived in the morning," "threw
  themselves at the line"), so the reader tracks them by *action* rather than having to
  memorize names. This is the right technique for a dense roster. **CLEAR.**
- **Warren (V) and Burnside (IX)** (§3) are the one spot where two new names arrive
  *after* the main action and do little — a newcomer may not retain them, but they also
  don't need to (the draft tells them the attacks "gained nothing"). Not a clarity
  failure; they're disposable by design. **CLEAR.**
- **Edward Hinks (North)** (§5) is introduced cleanly with rank, side, and job
  (commanding the USCT division). CLEAR.
- **Gordon Rhea** (§3) is introduced as "the historian Gordon Rhea" — exactly the gloss
  a newcomer needs to know why this name suddenly outranks "a century" of belief.
  CLEAR.

## Unexplained leaps

- **None that lose the reader.** The campaign chronology (Rapidan → Wilderness →
  Spotsylvania → North Anna → Cold Harbor) is given as a sequence with a unifying idea,
  so a newcomer reads it as "a month of nonstop fighting moving toward Richmond," which
  is exactly the right takeaway — they don't need the prior battles explained, and the
  draft correctly doesn't try.
- **The June 15 jump to Petersburg** (§5) could read as abrupt — the story leaves Cold
  Harbor and lands at a different battle — but the draft bridges it explicitly ("Take
  Petersburg's railroads and you strangle Richmond"), so the *why* of the jump is clear.
  CLEAR.

## Action items

### MUST-FIX (ship-blocking for the clarity gate)
- **None.** No term leaves a zero-knowledge reader genuinely lost.

### SHOULD-FIX (polish)
1. Gloss **"corps"** once on first use (qualitatively if no figure is verified) so the
   roman-numeral corps designations read as units, not codes.
2. One half-clause distinguishing **the Army of the James from the Army of the
   Potomac** at first mention of "the separate Army of the James."
3. Compress the **§3 ¶5 casualty-number block** so a newcomer can hold the morning vs.
   whole-day vs. Confederate figures apart (also flagged by storytelling gate).
4. Put the plain meaning of **"lodgment"** ahead of the word, not after.
5. Add a four-word gloss to the **Fredericksburg / Marye's Heights** comparison so it
   lands for a reader meeting that battle for the first time.

---

## Summary
This is a clarity-strong draft. Every term the brief flagged is defined on first use,
side-tags are complete and consistent, and the dense command roster is made trackable
by giving each general a concrete action instead of asking the reader to memorize
names. There is no MUST-FIX — nothing leaves the target reader lost. The five
SHOULD-FIX items are small: two missing unit-concept glosses (corps; the two Union
armies), one overwhelm spot (the casualty-number stack, shared with the storytelling
gate), and two reference-glosses (lodgment, Fredericksburg) where the meaning trails
the word or assumes prior knowledge. Clean each up and a zero-knowledge 15-year-old
reads the whole thing without a stumble. **PASS.**
