# Shiloh — NEWCOMER / CLARITY Gate

Reader persona: sharp 15-year-old, ZERO Civil War knowledge.
Scope: undefined jargon, missing side-tags on first mention, unexplained leaps.

## Section-by-section findings

### Section 1 — The Surprise at Pittsburg Landing (intro paragraphs)

**[C-01] "Maj. Gen." — MUST-FIX**
Passage: _"the man making it work was Maj. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant (North)"_
"Maj. Gen." appears cold — the full "Major General" is never spelled out. A newcomer doesn't know this is a military rank, let alone where it sits. First use = define it briefly.
Suggested gloss: "Major General" on first appearance, or "Grant, one of the top Union commanders, Major General Ulysses S. Grant (North)"

**[C-02] "Gunboat" — partially handled, SHOULD-FIX in intro**
Passage: _"A Union army that controlled them could float supplies, men, and gunboats deep into the Confederate heartland"_
"Gunboat" appears here without definition. It is defined later (Section 3, Webster's line section) but the reader hits it cold in paragraph 1. Move the gloss forward or add a brief inline definition here.
Suggested gloss: "…and gunboats (river steamers armed with heavy cannon) deep into…"

**[C-03] "Confederate heartland" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"float … deep into the Confederate heartland"_
A newcomer doesn't know the geography. "Heartland" is abstract — which direction? What states? A two-word fix would help: "…deep into the Confederate interior — the lower South."

**[C-04] "The Confederacy he was trying to split apart was a new country…" — no side-tag on Beauregard, first-mention**
Beauregard appears in the same section (paragraph 6) with zero introduction: _"Beauregard, Johnston's second-in-command, was sure the delay had cost them all surprise"_. He gets no first-name, no rank, no side-tag on his very first mention (the side tag arrives only much later, in Section 3). — MUST-FIX (see C-12 below for the full call-out; listed here for section context)

**[C-05] "Corinth, Mississippi, a railroad town where the Memphis and Charleston line crossed the Mobile and Ohio" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"Take Corinth and you cut the South's main east-west rail artery."_
The rail lines are named but a newcomer has no mental map. The logic is explained ("main east-west rail artery"), which is good — but "Memphis and Charleston" and "Mobile and Ohio" are two proper nouns that do no work for a newcomer. Consider cutting the line names and keeping only the strategic explanation, or a one-line geography note.

**[C-06] "Army of the Tennessee" / "Army of the Ohio" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passages: _"his Army of the Tennessee"_ and _"Maj. Gen. Don Carlos Buell's (North) Army of the Ohio"_
These names appear without explanation that armies in the Civil War were often named after rivers. A newcomer will puzzle over why there's an "Army of the Tennessee" and an "Army of the Ohio" — are these from those states? Named for the rivers? One sentence of context on the naming convention would help, or drop the formal army names in favor of "Grant's army" / "Buell's army."

**[C-07] "Jump-off positions" — MUST-FIX**
Passage: _"they did not reach their jump-off positions until the night of April 5"_
"Jump-off positions" is military jargon for the start line before an attack. A newcomer will not know this.
Suggested gloss: "…their jump-off positions (the start lines from which they would attack)…"

### Section 1 — "The camps overrun" subsection

**[C-11] "Skirmishers" — MUST-FIX**
Passage: _"they ran straight into Confederate skirmishers, and the first shots cracked out"_
"Skirmishers" is military jargon — lightly armed soldiers sent ahead to probe for the enemy. A newcomer will not know what distinguishes a "skirmisher" from any other soldier, or why this triggers the battle.
Suggested gloss: "…Confederate skirmishers (advance scouts probing ahead of the main army)…"

**[C-12] "Beauregard" — missing first name, rank, and side-tag on first mention — MUST-FIX**
Passage: _"Beauregard, Johnston's second-in-command, was sure the delay had cost them all surprise"_
This is the first time Beauregard appears in the entire draft. He gets no first name, no rank, and no side-tag. Johnston was introduced with "(South)" earlier in paragraph 2, but Beauregard gets nothing. A newcomer must guess who he is.
Suggested fix: _"Gen. P.G.T. Beauregard (South), Johnston's second-in-command, was sure…"_

**[C-13] "The Federals" — MUST-FIX**
Passage: _"that the noise and the days of marching must have alerted the Federals"_
"Federals" = the Union army. This term appears without definition. A newcomer who has just learned "Union = North" will not know "Federals" is a synonym.
Suggested gloss: first use → "the Federals (the Union army)" or replace with "the Union troops."

**[C-14] "Rout" — SHOULD-FIX (implicit only; see "run" / "broken regiments")**
The word "rout" does not appear explicitly, but its concept — the army nearly breaking and fleeing — is described without that label. Not a hard flag, but when Section 2 refers to the stand holding the army together, the newcomer might not grasp the severity without the word being named and defined once.

### Section 2 — The Hornet's Nest

**[C-15] "Corps" — MUST-FIX**
Passage: _"Maj. Gen. Braxton Bragg (South), commanding one of the Confederate corps, fed brigades into the meat grinder"_
"Corps" is a military unit above a division (two or more divisions, 20,000–30,000 men) but below an army. The draft defined division/brigade/regiment in Section 1, but never defined corps, which appears here for the first time.
Suggested gloss: "…commanding one of the Confederate corps (a grouping of divisions, the layer above division)…"

**[C-16] "Brig. Gen." — SHOULD-FIX (consistent with C-01)**
Passage: _"Brig. Gen. Benjamin Prentiss (North)"_ and repeated for Wallace, Hurlbut, Ruggles.
"Brig. Gen." = Brigadier General, one rank below Major General. Since "Maj. Gen." was never spelled out either (C-01), a newcomer is accumulating unexplained military abbreviations. Either spell them all out once or add a note in the first occurrence: "Brigadier General (one rank below Major General)."

**[C-17] "Hub to hub" — MUST-FIX**
Passage: _"he gathered roughly 62 cannons hub to hub into one enormous battery"_
"Hub to hub" means wheel hub touching wheel hub — cannons lined up with no space between them. A newcomer will not know what "hub" refers to in this context, or what "battery" means in military terms (a grouped emplacement of artillery).
Suggested gloss: _"62 cannons wheel to wheel — no gaps between them — into one enormous battery (a massed artillery position)"_

**[C-18] "The pocket sealed around him" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"with the pocket sealed around him, held on until about 5:30"_
"Pocket" is military slang for an encirclement — troops surrounded on all sides with no escape route. A newcomer won't parse this without the definition.
Suggested gloss: _"with Confederate troops now closing in on all sides around him (the 'pocket' sealed)…"_ or simply _"completely surrounded…"_

**[C-19] "Gunboat" — definition lands too late — MUST-FIX (promote gloss)**
Passage: _"And they ran into the navy. Anchored in the Tennessee River off the landing were two Union gunboats … A gunboat is exactly what it sounds like: a river steamer armored and armed with heavy naval cannon…"_
The gunboat definition appears here — but "gunboat" was used undefined in paragraph 1 of the whole draft (C-02). The gloss is excellent; it just needs to move to first use. Move it or add a brief inline gloss at first use in the intro paragraph.

**[C-20] "Lobbing big naval shells" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"lobbing big naval shells into the Confederate infantry massing for the final push"_
"Lobbing" implies a high-arc trajectory, which is accurate for naval guns of this period but might confuse a newcomer who pictures guns firing flat. Not critical, but "firing heavy shells" is cleaner.

### Section 3 — Buell Arrives

**[C-21] "Steamboats ferried Buell's divisions — Nelson's, Crittenden's, McCook's" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"steamboats ferried Buell's divisions — Nelson's, Crittenden's, McCook's, roughly 18,000 fresh men"_
Nelson, Crittenden, and McCook appear without ranks or first names on first mention (no side-tags needed since they're clearly Buell's = Union, but ranks are missing). Not a hard comprehension-breaker, but adding "General" before each would signal these are commanders, not unit names.

**[C-22] "Confederate cavalryman Brig. Gen. Nathan Bedford Forrest (South)" — WELL HANDLED**
Side-tag present, title present, role present. Clean.

**[C-23] "The chain of command" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"He carried the warning up the chain of command"_
"Chain of command" is an idiom most 15-year-olds will know from movies, but in context "reported to his superiors" is plainer and avoids any ambiguity.

**[C-24] "Confederate left" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"Nelson's three brigades striking first against the Confederate left"_
"Left" in a battle line depends on which direction you're facing — a newcomer has no mental map of the battlefield orientation. This is the only occurrence of directional battle-line language without a brief spatial anchor. Suggested addition: _"the Confederate left (the southern end of their line)"_ or a brief orientation note.

**[C-25] "Rear guard" — MUST-FIX**
Passage: _"Maj. Gen. John Breckinridge's (South) corps formed a rear guard near Shiloh Church around 2:00 p.m. to cover the retreat"_
"Rear guard" = a force that stays behind to slow enemy pursuit while the main army retreats. A newcomer may understand "rear" directionally but not what "forming a rear guard" means as a military action.
Suggested gloss: _"formed a rear guard (a force left behind to slow any Union pursuit) near Shiloh Church"_

**[C-26] "Fallen Timbers" ambush — scene-setting gap — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"a Union force probing south near a place called Fallen Timbers was ambushed by Forrest's cavalry — about 350 horsemen who charged into the pursuing infantry"_
"Cavalry" has not been defined. A newcomer knows horses are involved from "horsemen," but "cavalry" as a military term (mounted soldiers, their tactical role) is not explained.
Suggested gloss: _"Forrest's cavalry (mounted soldiers on horseback)"_ — or since "horsemen" is used in the same sentence, it's partially handled; a minor fix.

### Section 4 — The Reckoning

**[C-27] "Casualties — killed, wounded, and missing" — WELL HANDLED**
The draft defines it inline: _"around 23,700 casualties — killed, wounded, and missing — combined."_
Clean. No flag.

**[C-28] "First Bull Run, Wilson's Creek, Fort Donelson, Pea Ridge" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"The biggest battles of the war up to that point — First Bull Run, Wilson's Creek, Fort Donelson, Pea Ridge — had together produced something like 12,000 casualties."_
Fort Donelson was introduced in Section 1 so a reader who started there has context. But First Bull Run, Wilson's Creek, and Pea Ridge appear with zero introduction — a newcomer will have no frame for what they were. One clause would help: _"…battles like First Bull Run (the first big fight of the war, mid-1861), Wilson's Creek, Fort Donelson, and Pea Ridge…"_ The core point (they all combined to ~12,000) lands fine without full explanations, so this is SHOULD-FIX, not MUST-FIX.

**[C-29] "Vicksburg" — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"The road from Shiloh ran south down the rivers, through Corinth and on to Vicksburg, where Grant would split the Confederacy in two for good."_
Vicksburg appears for the first time here without location or context. A newcomer won't know it's a city on the Mississippi River. One-word gloss: _"Vicksburg (a fortress city on the Mississippi River)"_.

## Unexplained leaps

**[L-01] Why Buell's army was marching to join Grant — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"That second army was Maj. Gen. Don Carlos Buell's (North) Army of the Ohio, marching overland to link up."_
The narrative states Halleck ordered Grant to wait for Buell before advancing, but never explains WHY two armies needed to combine — i.e., that Grant alone wasn't strong enough to take Corinth and hold it. The strategy is implied but the newcomer has to infer it.

**[L-02] What "the Western Theater" means — SHOULD-FIX**
The "Meanwhile in the Western Theater" sidebar label uses a term never defined for a newcomer. The Civil War was fought in two main regions — the Eastern Theater (Virginia, mainly) and the Western Theater (the Mississippi/Tennessee river valleys). A newcomer reading "Western Theater" for the first time will wonder if that means Texas or California. One sentence in the sidebar or a gloss at first use would close it.

**[L-03] Johnston's decision to attack rather than defend — WELL HANDLED**
The "Corinth" sidebar explains the strategic logic clearly. No flag.

**[L-04] Why Grant was at Savannah (9 miles away) rather than at the camp — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"Grant himself was nine miles (14 km) downriver at his headquarters in Savannah, Tennessee"_
A newcomer will ask: why wasn't the general with his army? The draft doesn't explain. Grant routinely operated from a separate headquarters to manage logistics and communications — a brief clause would resolve the confusion: _"…at his separate field headquarters in Savannah — command generals often kept a distance to manage the larger campaign…"_

**[L-05] How Beauregard got a false report about Buell — SHOULD-FIX**
Passage: _"fed a false report that Buell's fresh army was nowhere near"_
The draft doesn't say where the false report came from. A newcomer wonders: who reported this? Was it a scout? A spy? A mistake? One clause: _"…fed a false report — from a mistaken Confederate scout — that Buell's fresh army was nowhere near"_ would close the gap, or cut "false report" and say simply "mistaken that Buell's army was still far away."
