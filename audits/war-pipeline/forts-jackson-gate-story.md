## Overall

A solid, trustworthy first draft. The framing is clear, the stakes are well-set, and the fact discipline is excellent — the fact ledger reflects serious restraint, and the draft never overreaches. But the section that should be the set-piece of the whole piece — the night run, the thing the entire setup is building toward — is the weakest passage in the draft. It gives us a list of ship actions where it should give us the chaos, the smell, the feel of fighting blind on a burning river. The other two sections are doing their jobs well; the center section is doing half of one.

---

## Section 1: the-great-port | GOOD

### What works

**Hook and stakes.** "The biggest city in the Confederacy sat almost undefended at the bottom of a river" is a strong opener — concrete, slightly absurd, immediately legible. The Anaconda Plan framing ("the snake that kills by squeezing") lands with a good metaphor-to-mission payoff: the plan has a name, and the name earns its keep.

The Confederate misreading ("the threat was from upstream, from the north") is the sharpest structuring move in the section. It answers "why was the biggest city left open?" in one sentence, and it makes the Confederacy look tragic rather than incompetent — they were wrong about the direction of the threat, not asleep. That is the better story.

The "Meanwhile" on Farragut is economical and does exactly what the Antietam "Meanwhile" blocks do — gives the reader the human behind the event, holds one sentence on what's coming. "The South thought the river couldn't be forced from below. Farragut had come to prove it could." That's clean.

**Human dimension.** Farragut's biographical parenthetical ("first went aboard a warship at age nine, raised by the naval officer whose first name he later took") is the right kind of detail — one quick window into a person, not a résumé. It earns the character, doesn't explain him.

### What to fix

**The ending italic is passive.** The _ITALIC_ ("The South was about to lose the city it could least afford to lose, in defense of the thing it had gone to war to keep") is the section's closing thought, and it's a good thought — but it lands between the intro paragraphs, not at the end where it would punch. The section ends on Farragut arriving to prove he could force the river, which is actually a stronger close. The italic reads like a thesis statement dropped early when it should either be cut or moved to close.

**"About 75 miles below" needs an anchor.** The rule (miles first, anchor abstract numbers) applies here: 75 miles between the Gulf and New Orleans is a real distance and affects what the reader pictures about the strategic layout, but it floats without a handle. One word — "a roughly 75-mile run upriver from the Gulf" or "75 miles, about the drive from Baltimore to Philadelphia" — makes it physical.

**The armament inventory in "The gauntlet" is detailed but flat.** The list of CSS Louisiana's guns ("2+3+4+7=16") surfaces in the fact ledger but wisely doesn't surface in the prose — but the prose still moves through ironclad → CSS Mississippi → CSS Manassas in a paragraph that reads like a ledger rather than an argument. The point the passage is building to — "the Confederacy had bet its river on iron ships, and the iron ships weren't ready" — is an excellent kicker. It's just separated from the reader by too much inventory. Consider consolidating: one sentence on the two monster ironclads that couldn't move, one on the *Manassas* that could but was undersized, then the kicker.

## Section 2: running-the-forts | NEEDS WORK

This is the section that the entire draft is building toward. The night of April 24 is where the storytelling gold is. It is not yet vivid enough to be that set-piece.

### The bombardment passage (April 18–24)

**What works.** The mortar flotilla intro ("branches to hide them against the trees of the riverbank") is good visual texture — it's the kind of concrete detail that earns the reader's trust. Duncan's quote lands naturally and the framing ("what followed was a bombardment on a scale that is hard to picture") acknowledges the problem of scale without hiding behind it.

**What doesn't work.** The "careful way was failing" paragraph tells instead of shows. "Day after day of the heaviest bombardment the war had yet seen, and the forts could still rake any ship that tried to pass" is a conclusion, not a scene. What did five days of that feel like for the men inside Fort Jackson — crawling out each morning to check how much of the wall was left, sleeping in flooded lower floors, waking to the next morning's barrage? The Duncan quote is there, but one quote is one moment. The section earns the bombardment's duration (five or six days is a lot of shells) but not the grinding logic of why Farragut finally had to make his decision.

The pivot sentence — "Farragut had seen enough" — is doing load-bearing narrative work and is not strong enough for the job. That decision moment is the hinge of the whole piece. It needs a beat.

### The night run — the core problem

The night-run passage ("The night they ran the gauntlet") is the set-piece. It should feel like the cornfield passage in Antietam — chaotic, close, visceral, human. Right now it is a well-researched summary. That is not the same thing.

**Specific diagnosis:**

1. **The opening is good but doesn't commit.** "The river caught fire" is a strong close to the first sentence. The problem is the very next sentence abandons the fire: "It was a battle fought almost blind, by muzzle flash and burning rafts." That's a fine summary sentence, but as the second sentence of the set-piece it tells us what we're about to see rather than showing it. The cornfield equivalent at Antietam goes straight into the corn: "The corn was tall and ripe, taller than a man, and it hid soldiers until they were close enough to kill each other in the face." That's the move — into the scene, not above it.

2. **The Hartford sequence is the best passage in the section.** "Farragut's own flagship, the USS Hartford, swerved to dodge one and ran aground; a small Confederate steamer shoved a burning raft against her hull, and the flagship caught fire — for a few minutes the admiral's own ship was in flames — before her crew beat the fire out and worked her free." This is doing it right: a sequence of specific actions, tight verbs, a human-scale crisis. The rest of the passage should move at this pace, not slower.

3. **The ship-by-ship accounting is flat where it should be vivid.** "The cottonclad ram *Governor Moore* drove again and again into the USS Varuna" — "drove again and again" is the phrase that should produce a sensation, but it doesn't yet have the weight to carry it. What does it look like to have a ram strike you again and again on a dark river? What does "in the shallows" mean for the men aboard? The *Manassas* sequence suffers from the same problem: "her blows glanced off without crippling either; she was driven ashore, set afire, drifted off burning, and sank" is five beats in one clause, all at the same pace, none of them landing separately. The *Manassas* catching fire and drifting off burning is the kind of image that should stop a reader. It doesn't.

4. **There is no character aboard the fleet.** Antietam has Hooker shot through the foot, Hood's men pulled off their half-cooked breakfast and thrown into the corn, Mansfield dying, the chaplain giving absolution before the Irish Brigade went forward. This passage has ships and flags and Confederate naval commanders being "mortally wounded" (unnamed, passive). Farragut himself is present only as the possessor of a flagship. Where is he during the run? What was the experience of being the admiral on that deck, watching his own ship catch fire? The pack has Farragut — use him.

5. **The Winters quote is misplaced.** The passage ends the night-run with an academic historian's judgment that the Confederates "made a sorry showing." That is a fine fact-check observation. It is a terrible place to end the dramatic climax of the section. The reader should end the night run with a feeling, not a scholar's verdict. The quote can move to a follow-on beat on Confederate disorganization, or simply be cut from this position.

**Beat fix (night run):** The passage needs a ground-level perspective for at least a portion of the run. Pick one ship — the Hartford is already in the draft — and stay with it from the moment the guns open up to the moment the fire is out and the crew works her free. That single continuous thread running through the broader chaos is what makes the Antietam cornfield passage work: it has a shape (drive, counterattack, grind) and you feel it building. The night run as written has events but not an arc. Give it one.

### The "Meanwhile in the forts" coda

This is the best-written passage in the section. "A fort is only as good as the thing it blocks. Farragut had stopped letting them block anything." That is close to perfect. The observation is unexpected, the logic is tight, and the phrasing is clean. This is the voice the night-run should be reaching for.

## Section 3: the-fall-of-new-orleans | GOOD

### What works

**Opening.** "Found a great city with nothing to defend it" is strong. "What greeted Farragut was less a surrender than a tantrum" is better — exactly the right voice register, it tells you what's coming, and it's plainly true. That sentence is doing the work that "The man who could organize an army better than anyone could never quite bring himself to spend it" does in Antietam: a clean, memorable encapsulation.

**Mumford sequence.** The flag-tear story is told efficiently: who, what, where, and then the bill that came due later. The handling of the execution ("from a flagstaff jutting out of the very Mint where he had pulled the flag down") is good spatial memory — it earns the grotesque deliberateness of it.

**Strategic summary.** "Step back from the flags and the executions and look at what the Union had actually won" is the right move at the right moment. The turn from the personal-drama of Butler to the strategic-significance paragraph is clean, and the ending — the Anaconda/throat metaphor closed back from the opening — provides a real bookend. The reader who started with "the snake that kills by squeezing" gets it paid off here.

### What to fix

**The Chesnut attribution is conspicuously hedged.** "The Confederate diarist Mary Chesnut, taking the news in her journal, captured the despair in a single line that has echoed ever since — that with New Orleans gone, the Confederacy itself felt gone." The fact ledger rightly flags provenance concerns and instructs paraphrase. But the reader is being told that a "single line has echoed ever since" and then not given the line. That is a tease that underdelivers. Either strengthen the attribution enough to quote (if the pack permits), cut the "echoed ever since" setup so the paraphrase doesn't follow a promise of a direct quote, or rephrase: "Mary Chesnut, the Confederate diarist whose wartime journal has become the era's most-read first-hand account, wrote in despair that with New Orleans gone, the Confederacy itself felt gone." That earns the paraphrase without overpromising.

**"One Union official"** is weak for the Fox quote given the setup it carries ("the great event of the war second only to the final surrender of Lee"). If the fact pack flags the attribution, use "a senior Navy official" or just cut the attribution and let the claim stand on its own — "It was, by any measure, the great event of the war second only to Lee's final surrender" needs no external sourcer.

**The Nevins passage (Europe coda)** works but is slightly over-quoted. The six-item Nevins list ("poverty, disorganization, lack of skilled engineers and craftsmen, friction between State authorities and Richmond, and want of foresight") is accurate and sourced, but it reads like a bullet list inside a quote rather than prose. Consider paraphrasing four of the six and keeping only the sharpest two in the quotation marks — or cutting the attribution frame and working the ideas into the prose directly. The "Meanwhile in Europe" coda is doing good structural work (it mirrors the Anaconda open and the European-recognition thread from "the-great-port's" Farragut framing), but the Nevins quote stops the momentum.

**The ending italic.** "The real blow had already landed" is fine but is positioned at the end of the Butler/Woman Order section, mid-section, not at the close. It functions as a scene-transition thought more than a kicker. The actual closing paragraph of the section — about the Anaconda tightening, the South's slave economy, the cost of the night — is the real ending of the piece, and it's good. It doesn't need that italic ahead of it to set it up.

## Flat Passages with Concrete Beat Fixes

| Location | Flat passage | Beat fix |
|---|---|---|
| running-the-forts, night run | "Both forts opened up the moment the ships came into range, hammering them from either bank as they crowded through the narrow break in the chain." | Anchor on one experience: the Hartford's deck — the shock of the first gun, smoke so thick officers can't see the bank, only muzzle flashes marking where the fort is. |
| running-the-forts, night run | "*Governor Moore* drove again and again into the *Varuna*, ramming her until she sank in the shallows" | Give the ramming a physical scale — what does it sound like/feel like aboard a rammed wooden ship? What does "sank in the shallows" mean for the men? Did they survive? |
| running-the-forts, night run | "*Manassas*... driven ashore, set afire, drifted off burning, and sank" | This is five beats in one clause. Slow down on "drifted off burning" — that image is the payoff, let it land. One sentence: she caught fire, broke free, and drifted downriver like a torch in the dark. |
| running-the-forts, night run | Winters quote ending the passage | Move the quote out of the night-run's final position. The night run's last beat should be the fleet emerging above the forts at sunrise — the first light showing a battered, improbable fleet past the guns that were supposed to stop it. |
| the-fall, Chesnut | "captured the despair in a single line that has echoed ever since — that with New Orleans gone..." | Either deliver the direct quote or remove the "single line that has echoed ever since" setup; the paraphrase after that framing is a let-down. |

## Priority Fix

**The night run.** It is the reason this section exists and the thing readers will remember or forget. At minimum, thread a continuous Hartford perspective through the chaos — stay on Farragut's deck from the moment the guns open to the moment the fire is out — and cut or relocate the Winters quote to let the scene end on the fleet clearing the forts at dawn. That one restructure, even without adding new facts, moves this section from NEEDS WORK to GOOD.
