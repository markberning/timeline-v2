# Vicksburg Campaign + Siege — COMPREHENSIVENESS critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

Independent, web-built must-cover checklist for the Vicksburg campaign+siege, scored
against the draft (`vicksburg-draft.md`). Verdict per item: COVERED / THIN / MISSING.
Selective coverage is fine — only GENUINELY ESSENTIAL gaps are flagged MUST-ADD.

**Bottom line:** The draft is strong and covers nearly every essential turning point
well. There is **one essential gap** (the campaign's actual *opening* — the failed
1862 overland approach, Holly Springs + Chickasaw Bayou) and **one essential-adjacent
gap** (Grand Gulf, the failed crossing that *produced* Bruinsburg). Everything the
brief named as a required beat — running the batteries, Bruinsburg + cutting loose,
the five inland battles, the failed assaults → siege, the emancipation dimension, the
July 4 surrender + strategic meaning — is COVERED, several of them excellently.

## MUST-ADD (genuinely essential — these block)

1. **The 1862 first attempt (Holly Springs + Chickasaw Bayou).** [checklist B1–B3]
   This is the only true essential omission. The draft's whole argument — that the
   winter water-schemes "closed off every easy option" and *forced* the cut-loose
   gamble — silently assumes the reader knows the overland road and the northern
   bluffs were already tried and had already failed. They were, in Dec 1862, and they
   are the campaign's opening act. Two specific load-bearing losses:
   - **Holly Springs** is the origin of the cut-loose doctrine. Grant lost his army's
     supply base to one cavalry raid in Dec 1862 and had to retreat; six months later
     he deliberately threw the tether away at Bruinsburg *because* of that lesson. The
     draft calls the cut-loose move "the thing that made the whole campaign legend"
     but never shows where Grant learned it. A two-sentence callback closes the loop.
   - **Chickasaw Bayou** is the first proof the bluffs can't be carried by direct
     assault from the north — the premise the entire "winter of mud" section rests on.
   The fact-pack already carries Chickasaw Bayou (§5.1) and Holly Springs context is
   implicit; the author simply dropped them. Recommend a short paragraph at the top of
   §the-failed-approaches (or a tail to §the-key) establishing the failed 1862
   overland/bluff approach before the canal schemes. Need not be long — this is a
   compression fix, not a new section.

2. **Grand Gulf as the failed crossing that produced Bruinsburg.** [checklist D3]
   Borderline-essential. As written, Bruinsburg reads as Grant's plan from the start;
   in fact Porter's ironclads tried and failed to beat down the Grand Gulf bluff
   batteries on Apr 29, so Grant marched the army further south and crossed at
   *undefended* Bruinsburg (the Confederates expected Rodney). This is *why* the
   largest amphibious landing before D-Day went across "without opposition" — a fact
   the draft states but doesn't explain. One sentence ("Porter's ironclads couldn't
   silence the bluff guns at Grand Gulf on April 29, so Grant pushed further
   downstream and crossed where no one was waiting, at Bruinsburg") fixes it and makes
   the unopposed landing make sense. Promote to MUST-ADD if cheap; otherwise the
   strongest SHOULD-CONSIDER.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER (enriching, not blocking)

3. **Jefferson Davis's "nailhead" framing.** Davis called Vicksburg "the nailhead
   that holds the South's two halves together" — the Confederate mirror of Lincoln's
   "key," and a clean way to show *both* sides understood the stakes identically. A
   one-line add to §the-key. (Not in fact-pack — would need a verify pass; ABT carries
   it.)

4. **Grant's pre-existing reputation cost.** The draft notes the press thought Grant
   "finished" but doesn't connect it to the real political jeopardy (Lincoln under
   pressure to relieve him). Minor; the current "nearly cost him his command" line is
   adequate.

5. **Number marched / "200 miles in 17–18 days."** Draft says "eighteen days, five
   battles"; ABT's signature framing is "5 battles, 200 miles, 17 days." The mileage
   makes the cut-loose drive more visceral. Optional one-clause add.

## FACT-PACK BLIND SPOTS (flag for the pack, not the draft)

- **Chickasaw Bayou is in the fact-pack (§5.1) but treated as scene-setting only**, and
  the pack's §12 explicitly downgrades it to "secondary… include only as much as the
  arc needs." That guidance is what let the author drop it — but combined with **Holly
  Springs (which the pack never gives its own line)**, the result is that the
  campaign's entire opening act has no dedicated pack entry. Recommend the pack
  elevate "the failed 1862 overland approach (Holly Springs raid + Chickasaw Bayou)"
  to a named sequence beat so it isn't optional, since the cut-loose doctrine depends
  on it.
- **Grand Gulf** is in the pack's map labels (§10) and the geography is implied, but
  there is **no §5 sequence entry for the Apr 29 Grand Gulf bombardment failure** —
  so the pack presents Bruinsburg as the plan, exactly as the draft does. Add a
  one-line §5 beat: "Apr 29 — Porter's ironclads fail to silence Grand Gulf; Grant
  crosses further south at undefended Bruinsburg instead."
- **Davis "nailhead" quote** absent from the pack's quote list (§7) despite being a
  standard, well-sourced counterpart to Lincoln's "key." Worth pulling so the author
  can use the both-sides framing without a memory-assert.
- Minor: the pack's §5.9 Pemberton-quote sourcing ("I know my people… I shall
  surrender on the Fourth of July") is shakier than the draft's careful hedge already
  reflects — no change needed in the draft, which correctly refuses to quote him.

## Compression check (per the brief)
The campaign is heavily compressed — appropriately. The five inland battles are
condensed to roughly a sentence each, which is correct: Champion Hill, the one
genuine turning point among them, is given its due as "the decisive battle" with the
Loring detail and the wedge logic intact, so no turning point is lost *inside* the
inland march. The siege, the assaults, the emancipation throughline, and the surrender
are all full-weight. **The only place compression loses an essential turning point is
at the front end: the 1862 overland failure (Holly Springs/Chickasaw Bayou) is
compressed to zero**, which removes the origin of the cut-loose gamble and the premise
of the winter-schemes section. That is the one place the compression goes too far.
