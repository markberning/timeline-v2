**Inside 1862:**
- July 13, 1862 — Lincoln privately reads a draft to Seward and Navy Secretary Gideon Welles.
- July 22, 1862 — Lincoln presents the draft to the full cabinet; Stanton backs immediate release; Seward advises delay until after a Union victory.
- August 19, 1862 — Horace Greeley publishes "The Prayer of Twenty Millions" in the NY Tribune.
- August 22, 1862 — Lincoln's public letter to Greeley.
- September 17, 1862 — Antietam (Lee stopped, withdraws from Maryland; ~23,000 combined casualties). Lincoln had privately resolved to issue the proclamation after a Union victory.
- September 22, 1862 — Preliminary proclamation: Confederate states have until January 1, 1863, to return or have their enslaved declared free.
- January 1, 1863 — Final proclamation signed in the afternoon, after the New Year's reception.

## 2. What It Actually Said / Did
- **Operative phrase (preliminary):** enslaved in rebelling areas "shall be then, **thenceforward, and forever free**." Final clause: "are, and henceforward shall be free."
- **Legal basis:** Lincoln's authority as "Commander-in-Chief," framing it "a fit and necessary war measure for suppressing said rebellion." NOT a general power to abolish — the military-necessity framing is both justification and limit.
- **States in rebellion named (10):** Arkansas, Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Florida, Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina, Virginia.
- **Exempted (loyal border states):** Missouri, Kentucky, Maryland, Delaware. Also all of **Tennessee** (under Union military govt), 13 Union-occupied **Louisiana** parishes (incl. Orleans/New Orleans), and the **Virginia** counties forming West Virginia + Norfolk/Portsmouth area.
- **Numbers:** covered >3.5 million of ~4 million enslaved; ~300,000 exempted. Immediately freed (zones already under Union control): est. **20,000–75,000** [ESTIMATE]; the rest needed Union armies to reach them.

## 3. What It Did NOT Do
- Did not free the border-state enslaved (slavery legal there until the 13th Amendment).
- Did not immediately free Confederate-held enslaved — liberation tracked the army's advance.
- Was not a constitutional abolition — a war measure, reversible post-war (hence the 13th Amendment).
- No compensation to enslavers; no enforcement mechanism in Confederate territory.

## 4. The Lead-Up / Pressure
- **Confiscation Acts:** First (Aug 6, 1861) — freed enslaved used against the U.S. Second (July 17, 1862) — slaves of rebels "shall be forever free," in occupied Confederate territory, and authorized employing African Americans against the rebellion. (March 13, 1862: army barred from returning fugitives.)
- **Greeley letter (Aug 22, 1862), key passage:** "My paramount object in this struggle **is** to save the Union, and is **not** either to save or to destroy slavery. If I could save the Union without freeing **any** slave I would do it, and if I could save it by freeing **all** the slaves I would do it; and if I could save it by freeing some and leaving others alone I would also do that." Closes: "I intend no modification of my oft-expressed **personal** wish that all men every where could be free." **CONTEXT:** Lincoln had ALREADY privately decided on the proclamation — historians read the letter as strategic public framing (and border-state management), not indifference.
- **Seward's advice:** wait for a Union victory, or emancipation after defeats would read as "its last shriek of retreat."
- **Antietam as trigger:** Welles recorded Lincoln had "made a covenant with God" — if the army drove the enemy from Maryland, he'd issue it. Preliminary proclamation came five days later.
- **Cabinet:** Stanton (War, North) backed immediate release; Chase (Treasury, North) supportive; Seward (State, North) urged delay; Welles (Navy, North) — diary is a key source.

## 5. Exact Quotes — Verified
- **"thenceforward, and forever free"** — Preliminary Proclamation, Sep 22, 1862 (Lincoln's text).
- **Greeley letter** — see §4 (verified vs. AbrahamLincolnOnline / House Divided).
- **Signing day (Jan 1, 1863)** — all from witness memoirs (Carpenter, *Six Months in the White House*, 1866; Frederick Seward, *Reminiscences*, 1891) — RELIABLE BUT NOT primary documents in Lincoln's hand; attribute as "Lincoln reportedly said":
  - "I never in my life felt more certain that I was doing right, than I do in signing this paper."
  - "I have been receiving calls and shaking hands since nine o'clock this morning, till my arm is stiff and numb." (the New Year's-handshaking stiff hand)
  - "Now this signature is one that will be closely examined, and if they find my hand trembled they will say 'he had some compunctions.'"
  - "If my name ever goes into history, it will be for this act, and my whole soul is in it." [Carpenter 1866 — "reportedly"]
- **Frederick Douglass**, watching for news at Tremont Temple, Boston, Jan 1, 1863 (*My Life and Times*, 1892): "We were waiting and listening as for a bolt from the sky, which should rend the fetters of four millions of slaves... we were longing for the answer to the agonizing prayers of centuries." The celebration "did not break up till near the dawn of day." (NPS "We shout for joy that we live to record this righteous decree" — widely cited but pin attribution lightly: "Douglass wrote.")

## 6. Consequences
- **Recast war aims:** "restore the Union" → "restore the Union AND end slavery in the rebel states."
- **Black military service (USCT):** proclamation authorized Black enlistment; General Order No. 143 (May 22, 1863) created the Bureau of U.S. Colored Troops. ~**178,000–180,000** Black soldiers (≈one-tenth of Union manpower); ~185k–186k total USCT records incl. white officers; ~20,000 more in the Navy. [ESTIMATE — use "roughly 180,000."]
- **Foreign policy:** reframed the war as anti-slavery → made British/French recognition of the Confederacy politically untenable despite the cotton famine. Palmerston dropped fall-1862 mediation talk after Antietam + the proclamation; Britain never recognized the Confederacy. Manchester cotton workers backed the Union despite hardship; Lincoln praised their "sublime Christian heroism."
- **Path to permanent abolition:** war measure, not a constitutional change → 13th Amendment (Senate Apr 1864, House Jan 31 1865, ratified Dec 6, 1865).

- **USCT total = RANGE** (~178k–180k soldiers; up to ~186k with officers).
