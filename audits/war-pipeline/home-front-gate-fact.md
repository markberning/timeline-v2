### BEAT 1: The Confederate Draft — First Conscription in American History

**Confederate Conscription Act signed April 16, 1862**
✅ CONFIRMED. Multiple independent sources (Wikipedia "Confederate Conscription Acts 1862–1864," Library of Congress "Civil War Conscription Laws," Fold3, Ancestry) confirm April 16, 1862. First national draft in American history, predating the Union by nearly a year.
Source: Wikipedia "Confederate Conscription Acts 1862–1864"; blogs.loc.gov/law/2012/11/civil-war-conscription-laws/

**First Act liability: white men aged 18–35, three years' service**
✅ CONFIRMED. Consistent across all sources.

**Second Act raised upper age to 45, September 1862**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia confirms "September 27, 1862, the Second Conscription Act extended the age limit to 45 years."
Note: Draft says "a second act that September" — the exact date is September 27, not just "September." The vague form used in the draft ("that September") is acceptable for prose; exact date is September 27, 1862.

**Third Act (February 1864) extended range to 17–50**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia: "approved February 17, 1864, changed this to 17 to 50 years old, for service of an unlimited period." Draft says "February 1864" — correct in substance.

**Substitution abolished December 28, 1863**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia and multiple sources: "abolished on December 28, 1863."

**Twenty-Negro Law passed October 11, 1862**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia "Twenty Negro Law" confirms October 11, 1862, as part of the Second Conscription Act. One white man exempt per 20 enslaved people owned; two or more plantations within five miles counting together if collectively 20+ enslaved. All confirmed.

**Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation September 22, 1862**
✅ CONFIRMED. Standard historical record.

**Insurrection-control rationale; timing 19 days after Preliminary Proclamation**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia "Twenty Negro Law": "The law was a reaction to United States President Abraham Lincoln's preliminary Emancipation Proclamation, which was issued just 19 days earlier."

**Exemption numbers: ~200 VA, 120 NC, 201 GA, 300 SC vs ~38,000 overseers**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia "Twenty Negro Law" cites these exact figures from Confederate exemption records and the ~38,000 overseers figure.

**1863 amendment: pre-April-16-1862 overseer requirement, no post-Oct-11-1862 subdivision, $500 fee**
⚠️ SOFT DISCREPANCY ON DATE OF AMENDMENT. Draft says "an 1863 amendment." Fact pack says "May 1863." Wikipedia "Twenty Negro Law" confirms this was a May 1863 amendment. The draft says only "1863 amendment" without specifying May — that is accurate but imprecise. No factual error; no correction needed but worth specifying "May 1863" if precision is desired.
Sam Watkins quote on the $500 requirement: the $500 fee is confirmed as a requirement to "process the application." ✅

**February 1864 revision: threshold drops from 20 to 15; 100 pounds of meat per slave at fixed prices**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia "Twenty Negro Law" confirms the threshold dropped to 15 enslaved people, and required selling "one hundred pounds of bacon and/or beef for each slave" to the Confederacy at government-set prices. ✅

**Sam Watkins, *Co. Aytch*, 1st Tennessee, verbatim quote**
✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM. Full-text archive (gutenberg.org pg13202) and search results return the exact quote:
> "A law was made by the Confederate States Congress about this time allowing every person who owned twenty negroes to go home. It gave us the blues; we wanted twenty negroes. Negro property suddenly became very valuable, and there was raised the howl of 'rich man's war, poor man's fight.'"
This is verbatim-confirmed. ✅

### BEAT 2: The Union Draft — The Enrollment Act

**Enrollment Act signed March 3, 1863**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia, American History Central, and multiple sources confirm "signed into law by President Abraham Lincoln on March 3, 1863."

**Liability: male citizens + immigrants who filed intent to naturalize, ages 20–45**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia Enrollment Act: "every male citizen and those immigrants who had filed for citizenship, between 20 and 45 years of age, unless exempted." ✅

**$300 commutation fee**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia, History.com, and multiple sources confirm the $300 fee. The "year's wages for an unskilled laborer" framing is supported (Wikipedia: "roughly the equivalent of a full year's wages for an unskilled laborer").

**Commutation progressively restricted; 1864 amendment limited it to single call; July 1864 Lincoln bill effectively ended commutation except for conscientious objectors**
✅ CONFIRMED IN SUBSTANCE. Wikipedia Enrollment Act and fact pack confirm 1864 amendments limited then ended commutation. The Wikipedia Enrollment Act article does not detail the exact July 1864 date for the bill eliminating commutation, but the fact pack cites this and it aligns with standard conscription history. Framing is accurate.

**First genuine federal draft in U.S. history (first "real" federal conscription)**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia and all sources confirm this — distinguished from the 1862 militia call-up.

### BEAT 8: The Northern War Economy

**Homestead Act signed May 20, 1862; 160 acres; small filing fee; five years' continuous residence/improvement**
✅ CONFIRMED. National Archives, Britannica, History.com all confirm May 20, 1862; 160 acres; small fee; five years' residence and improvement. ✅

**Pacific Railway Act signed July 1, 1862; authorized transcontinental railroad; Union Pacific and Central Pacific; federal land grants and bonds**
✅ CONFIRMED. National Archives, U.S. Senate, Wikipedia all confirm July 1, 1862 signing; Union Pacific and Central Pacific; land grants and 30-year government bonds. ✅

**Revenue Act of 1861, August 5: first federal income tax; flat 3% on incomes over $800**
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia "Revenue Act of 1861" confirms August 5, 1861; flat 3% on incomes above $800. The draft correctly attributes it as the first federal income tax. ✅
Note: The Revenue Act of 1861 was never actually enforced/collected — the income tax provision was repealed by the Revenue Act of 1862 before any revenue was collected. The draft does not assert it was collected or enforced, only that it "created" the first federal income tax — that framing is correct as a legal/legislative first, though the historical nuance (never collected) is worth knowing. Not a factual error in the draft as written.

**Legal Tender Act February 25, 1862; greenbacks; paper not gold/silver-backed but declared legal tender; first $150M; up to $450M total; first U.S. currency issued as legal tender**
✅ CONFIRMED. U.S. Capitol Visitor Center, Wikipedia "United States Note," History.com all confirm: February 25, 1862; $150M initial authorization; subsequent acts raised total to $450M; declared legal tender. ✅

**War bonds: Salmon P. Chase launched mass bond drive; Jay Cooke sold to ordinary citizens**
✅ CONFIRMED. Historic Geneva and standard financial history confirm Chase and Cooke's roles. ✅

**National Banking Acts of 1863 and 1864**
✅ CONFIRMED. Federal Reserve History "National Banking Acts of 1863 and 1864" confirms both acts, the requirement to buy government bonds, and the issuance of uniform national banknotes. ✅

**"Shoddy" / "shoddy aristocracy" — reprocessed wool uniforms / war-profiteering etymology**
⚠️ VERIFY FLAG CARRIED THROUGH APPROPRIATELY — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED. Web research confirms: (1) "shoddy" cloth is real — reprocessed/ground wool rags, pre-dated the Civil War (origin ~1813 in Yorkshire, England); (2) shoddy cloth was indeed sold to the Union Army as inferior uniform material; (3) the contractors who profited were called "the shoddy aristocracy." However, the draft's claim that "shoddy" the word for cheap goods derives from/was popularized by the Civil War-era shoddy cloth is complicated: the word "shoddy" predates the Civil War and originally described the specific textile product. The Civil War gave it broader currency as a general adjective meaning cheap/inferior. The "shoddy aristocracy" nickname for profiteers IS confirmed. The draft's phrasing — that shoddy cloth "is sometimes said to have given the era its term for cheap goods" — correctly hedges with "sometimes said" and notes the [VERIFY] flag. The claim as written is acceptable; it should not assert the etymology as certain. ✅ (Verify flag correctly maintained.)
