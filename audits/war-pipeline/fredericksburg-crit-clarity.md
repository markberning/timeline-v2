# Fredericksburg — NEWCOMER/CLARITY Critic Gate (War Pipeline Step 4)

> Read COLD as a sharp 15-year-old with ZERO Civil War knowledge. Every reader-facing
> surface evaluated: prose, OUTCOME blocks, MEANWHILE sidebars, figure captions.
> Verdict per item: CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST. MUST-FIX blocks the gate; SHOULD-FIX
> is a polish recommendation. The Fact Ledger (lines 117+) is internal and not graded.

**Overall verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS.** The narrative arc is genuinely clear and gripping —
a newcomer follows the pontoon-delay → fortified-heights → futile-charges → frozen-field
spine without effort. But the draft leans on a cluster of **military-unit and rank terms
it never defines** ("corps," "division," "brigade," "grand division" via "wing," "battery"
implied), and several **named actors appear with no side-tag and no scene-set**, which is
exactly the newcomer-killer the gate exists to catch. Most fixes are one-clause inserts.

---

## MUST-FIX (gate-blocking)

### M1 — "division" used heavily, never defined · NEEDS-GLOSS
The whole turning point of Section 3 (Meade's breakthrough) turns on the reader feeling
how *small* one division is against "Lee's whole army." But "division" is never sized or
explained. First use is line 55 ("the single division of Maj. Gen. George G. Meade...
about 4,500 men"). The "~4,500 men" parenthetical is good and does most of the work —
but it arrives mid-sentence and the reader still doesn't know a division is a *subunit*,
not a synonym for "force." The Meade quote ("did they think my division could whip Lee's
whole army?") only lands if the reader already grasps one division ≪ one army.
**Fix:** on first use, gloss division as a major chunk of an army (a few thousand men;
several make up a wing/corps). A newcomer needs the *relative scale*, not just the count.

### M2 — "brigade" used as the unit of the slaughter, never defined · NEEDS-GLOSS
Section 3 is built on "one brigade at a time," "fourteen separate charges," "Kimball's
brigade," "the Irish Brigade," "two brigades to either side." Brigade is the load-bearing
unit of the entire Marye's Heights massacre (line 67) and never sized. A newcomer can't
tell whether a brigade is 50 men or 5,000, so "fourteen brigade-by-brigade charges"
(line 71) has no scale. The Irish Brigade's "545 of about 1,200" gives an implicit size
but only after the term has been used several times.
**Fix:** gloss brigade on first use (line 67 region) as a unit of a few regiments,
roughly a couple thousand men — smaller than a division. Pairs naturally with M1.

### M3 — "wing" / Franklin's "huge wing" / "grand division" · NEEDS-GLOSS → LOST risk
Line 55: "Franklin... commanded a huge wing of the army — and committed only a sliver of
it." Line 61: "Franklin, sitting on the rest of his enormous wing." The drama of the
breakthrough's failure is *entirely* "Franklin had a giant force and used almost none of
it." But "wing" is never sized or related to division/corps, so the reader can't feel how
criminal the underuse was. The factpack/ledger references "grand division" (Burnside's
real org structure) but the prose never uses or explains it — a newcomer has no mental
model of how the army was chunked (army → grand division/wing → corps → division →
brigade). **This is the single biggest comprehension gap in Section 3.**
**Fix:** when Franklin's wing is introduced, give it a rough size or fraction ("tens of
thousands," "about a third of the whole army") so "committed only Meade's ~4,500" reads as
the scandal it was. One clause.

### M4 — "corps" undefined · NEEDS-GLOSS
Line 89/93: Burnside vows to "personally lead his old corps" / "his old IX Corps." "Corps"
and "IX Corps" appear with zero gloss. A newcomer doesn't know a corps is a large standing
formation (bigger than a division), nor what "IX" means, nor why it's "his old" one.
**Fix:** one-clause gloss ("the IX Corps, the large formation he'd led before taking
command of the whole army"). The "his old" emotional beat needs the reader to know he used
to command just that piece.

### M5 — "battery" / "horse-artillery" / "guns" · NEEDS-GLOSS
Line 57: Pelham is "a young horse-artillery officer" who "ran a pair of guns out onto the
flank." "Guns" = cannon is inferable from context (and "two guns" is clear enough), so
"battery" itself is never used in prose (good). But "horse-artillery" is undefined jargon
— a newcomer won't know it means fast-moving cannon that keep pace with cavalry, which is
the whole reason Pelham could dart out, fire, and reposition (line 57: "shifting position
when the Union artillery tried to silence him"). The mobility is the point of the story.
**Fix:** gloss "horse-artillery" in a clause (fast, mobile cannon meant to move quickly),
or drop the term and just say "a young artillery officer" + lean on the mobility in the
action. Low effort.

### M6 — "enfilade" · CLEAR (already glossed) — keep, do not strip
Line 57 defines it inline: "an enfilade, the most lethal angle there is, where a single
shot can travel the whole line of a formation and hit man after man." Exemplary. Flagged
here only because the gate brief named it: **this one is CLEAR and is the model the other
terms (division/brigade/corps) should follow.** No change needed.

### M7 — "pontoon bridges" · CLEAR (already glossed) — keep
Line 17 defines it fully: "floating bridges built by anchoring a line of flat-bottomed
boats across the river and laying a plank roadway over them." Exemplary, and load-bearing
for the whole Section 1 logic. No change. (Same model as enfilade.)

### M8 — Named actors with NO side-tag on first mention · NEEDS-GLOSS
The war-pipeline side-tag rule (tag every named person on a side, first mention per
section) is a *clarity* requirement: a newcomer can't follow a battle if they can't tell
who's shooting at whom. The author's own ledger (lines 188–193) already flags these as
untagged — confirming the gap. Missing tags found in prose:
- **John Gibbon** (line 55) — North, no tag. He's "supported by John Gibbon" with no side.
- **Maj. Gen. John F. Reynolds** (line 61) — North, no tag. Appears only in the Meade
  quote ("My God, General Reynolds..."); newcomer has no idea which side Reynolds is on or
  why Meade is yelling at him (Reynolds was Meade's wing/corps superior — also unexplained).
- **Kimball** (line 67) — North, appears only as "Kimball's brigade," no tag, no rank,
  never introduced as a person.
- **John Pelham** (line 57) — tagged "(South)" — CLEAR.
**Fix:** tag Gibbon (North) and Reynolds (North) on first mention; either tag Kimball
(North) or recast as "a Union brigade under Kimball." MUST-FIX because untagged actors in
a two-army battle is the core newcomer failure mode the gate guards.

### M9 — "McClellan" appears with no side-tag and thin scene-set · NEEDS-GLOSS
Line 15: "inherited the Army of the Potomac from George B. McClellan, relieved in early
November for letting Lee's army escape after Antietam." McClellan gets no (North) tag (the
author's ledger line 189 explicitly flags this as undecided). A newcomer meeting McClellan
for the first time can infer he's Union from "Army of the Potomac," but it's not explicit,
and "relieved" (= fired) is mild jargon. **Fix:** tag McClellan (North); consider "relieved
(fired)" or "removed from command" on first use. The Antietam cross-ref ("Chapter on
Antietam") is acceptable as a terse back-reference per house style.

### M10 — "Army of the Potomac" vs "Army of Northern Virginia": which is which · NEEDS-GLOSS
Both army names appear early (lines 15) and a newcomer must hold "Potomac = North/Union,
Northern Virginia = South/Confederate" — counterintuitive because "Northern Virginia"
*sounds* Northern but is the Southern/Confederate army, and "Potomac" sounds neutral. The
draft never says plainly "the Army of the Potomac (the Union/Northern army)" / "the Army of
Northern Virginia (Lee's Confederate army)." This is a genuine trap for a zero-knowledge
reader. **Fix:** on first use of each army name, append the side in a clause. This is the
highest-value single fix in Section 1.

## SHOULD-FIX (polish; not gate-blocking)

### S1 — "salient" — NOT present · CLEAR
The gate brief named "salient" to check; it does **not** appear in the draft. Nothing to fix.

### S2 — "sunken road" / "Telegraph Road" / "millrace" / "canal ditch" — CLEAR
Lines 65–67 define all of these inline well: "a sunken farm lane, Telegraph Road,"
"a canal ditch — a millrace running north to south... crossed only by a few narrow
bridges." A newcomer follows the funnel geography cleanly. No change. Strong work.

### S3 — "Stafford Heights" / "Marye's Heights" / "Prospect Hill" / "Lee's Hill" — mostly CLEAR
"Stafford Heights" is introduced as "the high east bank known as Stafford Heights" (line
37) — CLEAR. "Marye's Heights" is well set ("a ridge about six hundred yards west," line
65) — CLEAR. "Prospect Hill and Hamilton's Crossing" (line 55) arrive as bare place-names
with no orientation ("Down past the town, near...") — a newcomer can't picture where; this
is minor since the action is clear, so SHOULD-FIX. "Lee's Hill / Telegraph Hill" (line 73
sidebar, region label line 73 "Telegraph Hill") — slight naming inconsistency (the section
calls it "Lee's Hill," the region tag says "Telegraph Hill") that could confuse; pick one
or note they're the same knoll.

### S4 — "horse-artillery officer" rank/role density · SHOULD-FIX
See M5. If M5 is fixed, this resolves.

### S5 — Rank abbreviations ("Maj. Gen.," "Brig. Gen.," "Lt. Gen.," "Col.," "Lt. Col.") · SHOULD-FIX
The draft uses abbreviated ranks throughout (Maj. Gen., Brig. Gen., Lt. Gen., Col., Lt.
Col.) with no gloss of the pecking order. A sharp 15-year-old will guess these are ranks,
but won't know Lt. Gen. > Maj. Gen. > Brig. Gen., which subtly matters (Longstreet and
Jackson are Lt. Gens. — Lee's two top subordinates; the brigadiers are lower). Not
gate-blocking because seniority is rarely load-bearing for the *story*, but a one-time
gloss or spelling out "Lieutenant General" on first use would help. Low priority.

### S6 — "the wing of the army assigned to the southern front" (line 39) · SHOULD-FIX
This phrasing forward-references the two-front structure before the reader knows there are
two fronts (the two-front split is only explained at the top of Section 3, line 53). Mild
"unexplained leap." Resolves naturally once M3 (wing sizing) is fixed and could be smoothed
with "the part of the army that would attack downstream, south of the town."

### S7 — "the Irish Brigade... many of them Irish immigrants fighting under green flags" · CLEAR
Line 67 — nicely self-glossing and humanizing. No change. (Strong newcomer touch.)

### S8 — Emancipation Proclamation · CLEAR
Lines 27, 109, 111 explain it plainly each time ("all persons held as slaves in the
rebelling states would be... free"; "the war's stated purpose openly became the destruction
of slavery"). A newcomer with zero knowledge gets the full stakes. No change. Satisfies the
"name slavery as the cause in the section's own prose" requirement at a clarity level too.

### S9 — "Vandals" (line 43) · SHOULD-FIX (light)
"Lee... likened them to Vandals." A sharp 15-year-old may or may not catch the historical
allusion (the Vandals sacked Rome). Context ("looted and wrecked it") carries the meaning
well enough that this is borderline, but a 3-word gloss ("Vandals — the ancient sackers of
Rome") would secure it. Optional.

### S10 — "aurora borealis — the northern lights, rare that far south" · CLEAR
Line 89 glosses it inline. No change.

### S11 — "Chickamauga" (line 85) · SHOULD-FIX (light)
Kirkland "had been killed at Chickamauga in 1863" — a bare battle-name forward-reference a
newcomer won't recognize. Non-load-bearing (it just dates his death), so light. Could add
"(a later battle in 1863)" for zero-knowledge clarity. Optional.

### S12 — "Chancellorsville" and "Gettysburg" (line 113 sidebar) · CLEAR enough
Line 113 sets both up in context ("another lopsided win at Chancellorsville the following
May," "a small Pennsylvania town called Gettysburg"). Gettysburg is nicely scene-set; the
forward-pointing arc is a strength, not a leak. No change.

### S13 — Overwhelming-passage check · MOSTLY CLEAR
Section 3 ("the-gap-and-the-wall") is the densest — two simultaneous battles, ~12 named
people, two geographies — and is the one place a newcomer could drown. The author's
"two completely different battles fought at the same time" framing (line 53) is a strong
guardrail that mostly prevents overload. The single longest at-risk paragraph is line 67
(Marye's Heights: Telegraph Road + stone wall + canal ditch + millrace + seven divisions +
fourteen charges + Kimball + Irish Brigade + Humphreys + Cobb + Kershaw, all in one block).
It's followable but heavy. **SHOULD-FIX:** consider a paragraph break inside line 67
(split the geography setup from the roll-call of failed charges) to give the reader air.
Not gate-blocking — the prose is clear, just dense.

## What's working (so the revise pass doesn't break it)
- "pontoon bridges" (M7) and "enfilade" (M6) are model inline glosses — replicate that
  exact technique for division/brigade/corps/wing.
- Geography (sunken road, millrace, the funnel) is taught cleanly as it's used.
- Emancipation stakes are fully accessible to a zero-knowledge reader on every mention.
- The Kirkland "contested legend" framing (line 85) is crystal clear about what's known vs
  believed — a newcomer is never misled. No clarity issue there (accuracy is the
  fact-checker's lane, not this gate's).

## Gate disposition
**NEEDS-GLOSS** — not LOST. The story is followable; the blockers are a tight, fixable set
of undefined unit/rank terms (M1 division, M2 brigade, M3 wing/grand-division, M4 corps,
M5 horse-artillery) and missing side-tags/scene-sets (M8 Gibbon/Reynolds/Kimball, M9
McClellan, M10 which-army-is-which). Every MUST-FIX is a one-clause insert; none requires
restructuring. Clear the 10 MUST-FIX items and this passes.
