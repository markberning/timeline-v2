## 0. SLAVERY-NAMED CHECK (ship-blocking, run first) — PASS

The section names slavery as the cause **in its own prose, in every one of the four
sections**, not as a bolted-on disclaimer. It is woven into the tactical stakes:

- **§1 the-crossroads-hill** — "the rebellion was, at bottom, a slaveholders' republic:
  the Confederacy had broken away from the United States to protect the enslavement of
  four million Black people." Names the Champion plantation as worked by enslaved people;
  names self-emancipation along the march ("thousands of enslaved people had been freeing
  themselves along his line of march").
- **§2 the-jackson-road** — "They were climbing a slaveholding plantation… The army
  Hovey's men were breaking on that crest existed to defend that arrangement… that was not
  incidental to the war; it was the war."
- **§3 the-hill-changes-hands** — "the campaign… to cut the slaveholders' republic in half
  at the river" (carried via the OUTCOME + the established §1 frame).
- **§4 the-lost-division** — "a slaveholders' plantation… When Vicksburg fell, slavery
  fell with it in the city… a slave city freed."

All three of the user's required hooks are present and load-bearing: (1) battle fought on
plantation land, (2) self-emancipation along Grant's march, (3) the split-the-Confederacy
campaign built on slavery. "Slavery / enslaved / slaveholders" appears in all four
sections. **No automatic MUST-FIX triggered.** This is the strongest part of the draft —
the cause isn't gestured at, it's made the spine of the tactical argument.

**Web spot-check of the framing hooks:** the plantation-ownership hook is solid — the
ground was owned by Sid and Matilda Champion and worked as a plantation (NPS / American
Battlefield Trust; Sid Champion left to serve in the 28th Mississippi Cavalry, Matilda ran
the plantation). The county-worked-by-enslaved-people and valley-wide self-emancipation
framing is the documented general pattern of Grant's 1863 march and is correctly hedged in
the draft (no Champion-family headcount asserted; pointer to "see Vicksburg"). The
*direction* of the self-emancipation framing — enslaved people freeing **themselves**,
walking off plantations toward the columns, not passively "being freed" — is exactly the
anti-Lost-Cause framing the gate wants. FAIR.

## FINDINGS

### F1 — Loring's idle division / "the lost division" pathos — FAIR (watched, clears)
The brief flagged this as the highest pathos risk ("watch the Loring's-lost-division
pathos"). The draft handles it correctly and does NOT romanticize:
- Loring's refusal to march is presented as **dereliction with consequences**, not noble
  tragedy: "Loring would not move… moved far too late to make any difference," "a third of
  Pemberton's army stood idle on the wrong end of the line while the other end was
  overrun."
- The §4 framing — "the division lost forever," "doom of the city" — is about the
  **operational cost to the Confederate war effort**, told from the Union-victory frame
  (this loss helped doom Vicksburg). It is not framed as a Lost-Cause grievance ("if only
  Loring had marched, the South might have won"). The blame is left genuinely two-sided
  ("the blame ran both ways and never stopped running") without tipping into sympathy.
- No gallant-Confederate halo. FAIR.

### F2 — Tilghman's death at the rearguard — TILTED (SHOULD-FIX, mild)
"Tilghman bought it and paid for it… He died holding the way open for the men streaming
past him." This is the one spot that leans toward heroizing a Confederate officer. The
factual content (killed by a Union artillery shell holding the rearguard crossing) is fine
and the death is concrete and earned, but the cadence ("bought it and paid for it,"
"holding the way open") gives a slaveholding-army's rearguard commander a faint
noble-sacrifice glow. It's mild — one sentence, not a pattern — and it's offset by the
section's strong slaveholders'-republic frame. **SHOULD-FIX:** trim one beat of the
valor cadence (e.g. drop "and paid for it" OR "He died holding the way open for the men
streaming past him" — keep one, not both) so the rearguard death is reported, not
eulogized. Not ship-blocking.

### F3 — Bowen "the man who almost saved the hill" / dies of dysentery thread — FAIR
§4's closing Meanwhile follows Bowen to the surrender and his death. This reads as a
narrative-thread device (Champion Hill → July 4 surrender), not Confederate
romanticization. "The man who almost saved the hill lived just long enough to give the
city away" is wry house-voice, and it ends on the anti-Lost-Cause note — "the Confederacy
cut in two and a slave city freed." The dual-POV treatment of Bowen's skill ("the finest
division in the Confederate force") is military assessment, not glorification. FAIR.

### F4 — Both-sides balance — FAIR, no false equivalence
Dual POV is present (Lee's warning, Bowen's charge, Pemberton's contradictory orders,
Loring's feud) but it never drifts into "two equally legitimate causes." The Confederacy
is consistently named as the slaveholders' army defending slavery; the Union campaign is
named as the campaign to split that republic. The tactical respect paid to Bowen/Lee is
competence-level, never cause-level. No "war of Northern aggression," no "states' rights,"
no sectional-misunderstanding framing. FAIR.

### F5 — Euphemism / sanitizing — FAIR
No euphemism for slavery ("peculiar institution" / "servants" absent; "enslaved people,"
"human property," "four million Black people" used plainly). Human cost is neither
sanitized nor gratuitous: "the hill of death," "Hovey's soldiers bore the brunt of the
day's killing," the casualty shape (2,441 missing/captured) is given meaning ("the price
of a broken army funneling back across a single creek") rather than splatter. FAIR.

### F6 — Presentism — FAIR
The slavery framing is grounded in the secession-era record (the Confederacy's own stated
purpose), not anachronistic moralizing. No presentist verdicts imposed on individuals
beyond what the period record supports. FAIR.
