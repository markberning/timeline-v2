### "The Great Writ and the Chief Justice who said no"

**NEEDS-GLOSS** — this is the most legally concentrated section. It does better than most legal writing but has two MUST-FIX blockers.

- **"Habeas corpus"** — defined carefully and clearly. CLEAR.
- **"writ"** — defined ("a formal written order from a court"). CLEAR.
- **"Article I of the Constitution"** — mentioned but a newcomer doesn't know what Article I is or why its location matters. MUST-FIX: add a brief gloss ("Article I lays out the powers of Congress — not the president — which is why critics argued only Congress could suspend the writ, not Lincoln acting alone"). The sentence that follows makes the argument but doesn't tell the reader why Article I specifically is the crux.
- **"Ex parte Merryman"** — the Latin label is explained ("the matter was brought on one party's behalf") which is OK, but "Ex parte" is not quite right as "one party's behalf" — it means the case was brought BY one party without the other (i.e., no adversary present, an emergency petition). This is a minor accuracy issue but could confuse a sharp reader. SHOULD-FIX: "a case brought by one side alone, without the other party present."
- **"Dred Scott decision of 1857"** — mentioned ("denied Black Americans citizenship") without explaining who Dred Scott was or why this matters for Taney's credibility. For a newcomer, this is a name-drop that carries weight but no explanation. SHOULD-FIX: one sentence — "Taney had authored the *Dred Scott* ruling eight years earlier, the Supreme Court's most infamous pre-war decision, which had declared Black people were not citizens and had no rights the government was bound to respect; it had made him a reviled figure in the North."
- **"circuit judge"** — Taney is Chief Justice but acts as circuit judge; a newcomer won't know what a circuit court is vs. the Supreme Court. SHOULD-FIX: "Taney wore two hats — as Chief Justice he led the Supreme Court, but like all justices in that era he also rode circuit, presiding over lower federal courts in his region; it was in that lower-court role that he heard Merryman's case."
- **"Suspension Clause"** — not named in the text; it's referred to as "the clause permitting suspension." Fine for clarity. CLEAR.
- **"Habeas Corpus Act of March 3, 1863"** — mentioned in passing, fine. CLEAR.

## MUST-FIX (Comprehension Breakers)

1. **"lame-duck congressman"** — used as section header and opening sentence of the Vallandigham section without any definition. A first-timer will not know what "lame-duck" means. Add gloss: "(still in office but already voted out of his seat, so politically powerless)."

2. **"commuted the sentence"** — "Lincoln commuted the sentence: instead of prison..." The colon tells you the outcome but not what commutation is. Add: "Lincoln reduced (commuted) the sentence."

3. **"border states"** — used in the National Union ticket paragraph as if the reader knows the term. They don't. Essential to understanding why Andrew Johnson was chosen. Add: "(the slave states that stayed in the Union — Kentucky, Maryland, Missouri, Delaware — whose loyalties Lincoln couldn't afford to lose)."

4. **"Article I of the Constitution"** — the habeas argument hinges on Article I being Congress's article, but this is asserted without explanation. A zero-knowledge reader will not know what Article I is. Add gloss inline.

5. **"electoral votes"** — the election result table uses "Electoral votes" as a column header and the text mentions "212 electoral votes," but the concept is never explained. A zero-knowledge reader will not know how electoral votes differ from popular votes or why 212 vs. 21 is a landslide while the popular vote was 55–45. Add a one-sentence explainer before or with the table.

6. **"POW camps"** — "Confederate prisoner-of-war camps" should be spelled out on first use ("prisoner-of-war camps (POW camps)").
