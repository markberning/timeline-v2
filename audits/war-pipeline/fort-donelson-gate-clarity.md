## MUST-FIX (real comprehension breakers — 4 items)

1. **"the Union right was rolled up"** (section the-breakout, "The road thrown open")
   — Quoted phrase: *"by early afternoon the Confederates had shoved the Union right back roughly 1 to 2 miles… The Union right was rolled up, Wynn's Ferry Road lay open"*
   — Why it breaks: "rolled up" is pure military argot. A newcomer reads it as something like "they ran away." The TACTICAL meaning (the whole flank folded inward and collapsed) is load-bearing for understanding WHY the escape road was suddenly open. Getting this wrong means the reader doesn't understand the Confederate success.
   — Suggested fix: *"The Union right was rolled up — the entire flank shoved sideways and backward, the defensive line collapsing — Wynn's Ferry Road lay open"*

2. **"asked for an armistice"** (section unconditional-surrender, "An old friend asks for terms")
   — Quoted phrase: *"He sent a note across to Grant asking for an armistice and a meeting to discuss surrender terms."*
   — Why it breaks: The entire drama of the scene is that Buckner is NOT yet surrendering — he wants a temporary stop-the-guns so they can negotiate, and Grant's answer refuses exactly that. If a reader doesn't know what an armistice is, Grant's reply reads like a non sequitur instead of a brutal shutdown.
   — Suggested fix: *"asking for an armistice (a temporary cease-fire while they negotiated terms)"*

3. **Winfield Scott / Mexican War** (section unconditional-surrender, "An old friend asks for terms")
   — Quoted phrase: *"They'd been at West Point together in the 1840s and served side by side under Winfield Scott in the Mexican War, including a climb up the volcano Popocatépetl in 1847."*
   — Why it breaks: Scott and the Mexican War are dropped with zero context. "Winfield Scott" is a name that means nothing to a 2026 teenager who hasn't studied 19th-century history. The Mexican War (1846–48) is frequently unknown or confused with the Spanish-American War. The Popocatépetl detail is vivid and earns its place — but only if the reader knows what these two were doing together.
   — Suggested fix: *"served side by side under Gen. Winfield Scott in the Mexican War (the 1846–48 U.S. war that took Texas and California from Mexico)"*

4. **"Flag Officer"** (intro)
   — Quoted phrase: *"Flag Officer Andrew H. Foote (North), the naval commander running a brand-new fleet of river gunboats"*
   — Why it breaks: Foote is one of the two protagonists of the whole section. The title "Flag Officer" is never explained, and the phrase after it ("the naval commander") glosses his role but not his rank. Unlike "Brig. Gen." which is at least recognizable as a general rank, "Flag Officer" sounds like a job description (the guy who waves flags). The reader finishes the whole draft not knowing whether Foote is equivalent to a general or a lieutenant.
   — Suggested fix: *"Flag Officer Andrew H. Foote (North), the naval equivalent of a general and commander of the Union's new river gunboat fleet"*

## Overwhelm / pacing flags

- **The Pillow controversy paragraph** (section the-breakout, "The order that gave it back") is the longest unbroken prose block in the draft. It handles the controversy responsibly, but a newcomer may lose the thread on the third or fourth "whether it was…" clause. Not a must-fix, but consider one sentence of hard bottom-line before the equivocation: "Most historians call it one of the war's great command failures."
- **The opening paragraph of "The Rivers into the South"** covers geography, strategy, slavery, and two major characters in one breath. Strong writing, but a first-time reader may need the geography to land before the strategy argument. Not a blocker — flagged for awareness.
