# FACT PACK — Siege of Charleston, March 29 – May 12, 1780

*American Revolution (the southern campaign). ONLY source for the battle page. The catastrophe battle: Clinton sails south with the biggest combined operation of the war, locks an American army inside a peninsula city it was politically forbidden to leave, and takes the whole thing — the worst American defeat of the entire war, and the fuse of the southern civil war that follows. Confidence tags: HIGH / MED / DISPUTED; HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags inline.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **Siege of Charleston, 1780** — Charleston (then "Charles Town"), South Carolina; the city sits on a peninsula between the Ashley River (west) and Cooper River (east), opening onto a barred harbor guarded by Fort Moultrie on Sullivan's Island. British land approach Feb 11 – Mar 29; formal siege Apr 1 – May 12; **surrender May 12, 1780**. [HIGH]
- **Result: British victory — the worst American defeat of the Revolutionary War.** Carl Borick (the standard modern study, *A Gallant Defense*, 2003) calls it exactly that; an entire American army, the whole Continental establishment in the South, surrendered at once. [HIGH; "worst defeat of the war" is the standard historians' judgment — safe to state]
- **The shape of the story [HIGH]:** (1) the **southern strategy** — after stalemate in the North, Britain bets the war on the South and its supposed loyalist masses; (2) the **political trap** — Benjamin Lincoln knows the army can still get out, and the civil leaders of South Carolina (Lt. Gov. Christopher Gadsden and his council) refuse to let it, one of them threatening to open the gates to the British if the Continentals try; (3) the **methodical European siege** — Clinton does almost nothing rash: parallels, batteries, the escape route across the Cooper snuffed out by Tarleton at Monck's Corner and Lenud's Ferry, Fort Moultrie taken without a shot; (4) the **surrender with the honors of war refused** — colors cased, forbidden to play a British march — the precise humiliation Washington returns to Cornwallis at Yorktown eighteen months later; (5) the **aftermath that ignites the partisan war** — prison hulks for the Continentals, and Clinton's June 3 proclamation tearing up the militia paroles, forcing every man in South Carolina to choose a side.
- **Signature numbers [HIGH at range level]:** more than 5,000 men captured (Clinton's return: 5,466 — see DISPUTED), including roughly 2,500–2,800 Continentals; seven generals; more than 300 guns; three Continental Navy frigates. Largest American surrender of the war.
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — superlative scope:** "Worst American defeat of the war" — yes. "Largest surrender of US troops until [Bataan / the Civil War]" — DO NOT USE; Harpers Ferry 1862 (~12,400) was larger, and the Bataan comparison is a magazine cliché with a counting problem. Stay inside the war: "the largest surrender of American troops in the entire war."
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — "Tarleton's quarter":** the massacre cry belongs to the **Waxhaws (May 29, 1780)** — the NEXT battle, not this one. Tarleton appears here twice (Monck's Corner, Lenud's Ferry) as a fast, ruthless cavalry officer; do NOT import the Waxhaws atrocity or the "Bloody Ban" nickname into the siege page beyond, at most, a closing handoff. The nickname postdates Waxhaws.

## 2. BACKGROUND
- **The southern strategy [HIGH]:** with France in the war (1778) and the North a stalemate after Monmouth, Britain pivoted south on a theory: the southern colonies were rich (rice, indigo — the export wealth financing the rebellion), strategically near the Caribbean theater, and supposedly full of loyalists who would rise and hold reconquered ground with minimal British garrisons. The proof-of-concept came at **Savannah: taken December 29, 1778**, then held in October 1779 against a Franco-American siege (a bloody allied failure — Pulaski killed; Lincoln commanded the American part and retreated to Charleston). Savannah's successful defense convinced Clinton the moment had come for the real prize. [HIGH]
- **Why Charleston [HIGH]:** the largest and richest city in the South and the fourth-largest in America (~12,000 people, roughly half of them enslaved); the South's principal port; the seat of rebel government in South Carolina. Take it and the South's door is open.
- **Clinton sails — the brutal winter passage [HIGH]:** Clinton left New York **December 26, 1779** with ~8,500 troops and ~5,000 sailors aboard ~90 transports escorted by 14 warships under Vice Adm. **Marriot Arbuthnot**, Cornwallis second in command. The winter of 1779–80 was one of the worst of the century (the same "Hard Winter" that froze Washington's army at Morristown). Storms scattered and battered the fleet for five weeks (a ~10-day run): an ordnance ship foundered; one transport with ~200 Hessians aboard was blown clean across the Atlantic and fetched up in **Cornwall, England**; and **nearly all the cavalry and artillery horses died or were thrown overboard** — Tarleton's dragoons arrived horseless and remounted on seized Carolina horses. Jäger captain Johann Hinrichs counted 15 of his 36 days at sea as outright storm. [HIGH on all; the Cornwall transport and horse losses are standard in the accounts]
- **The cautious approach [HIGH]:** the fleet regrouped off Savannah (Feb 1), and Clinton — who had been repulsed at Charleston once before, off Sullivan's Island in **1776** — took no chances. He landed 30 miles south of the city (Simmons Island, now Seabrook Island, **Feb 11–12**), then spent six weeks working methodically up the sea islands: across the Stono, James Island by early March, the Wappoo Cut, then up the west bank of the Ashley. On **March 29** the army crossed the Ashley River unopposed near Drayton Hall and stood on Charleston Neck — the peninsula's only land door. [HIGH]
- **Lincoln's position [HIGH]:** Maj. Gen. **Benjamin Lincoln**, commanding the Southern Department since September 1778, held the city with roughly 2,500–2,800 Continentals (Carolinians and Georgians, plus Woodford's ~750 Virginians who arrived April 7 after a forced march of hundreds of miles from Morristown — arriving just in time to be captured) and 2,000–3,000 militia and armed citizens: ~5,500–6,000 in all, behind a fortified line across the Neck fronted by a wet ditch/canal, with a tabby strongpoint (the **Hornwork**, at today's Marion Square) at the gate. [HIGH at range level; see DISPUTED on counts]
- **The political trap — documented [HIGH on the dynamics; MED on exact dates/words]:** Lincoln's professional options were to fight the siege or save the army by evacuating across the Cooper River while the route was open. The civil government made the second politically impossible. Gov. **John Rutledge** left the city around **April 13** at Lincoln's urging (with several councilmen, to keep a government alive in the state) — but Lt. Gov. **Christopher Gadsden** stayed, and when Lincoln's council of war (~April 19–20, after the second parallel closed in) discussed evacuation, Gadsden — admitted to the council with members of the Privy Council (Thomas Ferguson, Benjamin Cattell, Richard Hutson, David Ramsay) — opposed abandoning the city, and **Ferguson declared that if the Continentals tried to withdraw, the citizens would open the gates to the British and help attack the retreating army in its boats**. Generals McIntosh and Moultrie favored getting the army out; Lincoln, pressured from both sides, stayed. The threat scene comes through Lachlan McIntosh's journal and is standard in Borick. [HIGH that the threat was made and recorded; frame the words as reported speech, not verbatim quotation]
- **Lincoln also asked for Black soldiers [MED-HIGH]:** Lincoln pleaded with the South Carolina authorities to arm enslaved men for the defense (echoing John Laurens's 1779 proposal); the state refused outright. Use carefully and briefly; one clause, sourced to the standard accounts.
- **Fort Moultrie, legend vs. reality [HIGH]:** on **June 28, 1776**, the palmetto-log fort on Sullivan's Island (then Fort Sullivan, ~400 men under William Moultrie) had repulsed Sir Peter Parker's nine warships in a day-long battle — the spongy palmetto logs swallowed shot, the fort's flag (Moultrie's own blue-and-crescent design) became the icon of southern resistance, and the victory kept the British out of the South for three years. The 1780 siege is the deliberate, methodical undoing of that legend (see §4). [HIGH; Sgt. Jasper's flag rescue is attested 1776 lore — if used, flag as "the story told ever after"]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
### Strengths (dossier stats)
- **British: ~10,000 rising to ~12,800–14,000 troops** (initial ~8,500; Brig. Gen. Paterson's ~1,500 marched up from Savannah in April; Lord Rawdon arrived **April 18** with ~2,500 from New York), plus ~4,500–5,000 sailors and a fleet (14 warships at the bar; 8 warships ran the harbor). [HIGH at range level — Wikipedia/Clodfelter: 12,847 + 4,500 sailors]
- **American: ~5,500–6,600** (Continental regulars ~2,500–2,800 + militia + armed citizens + Continental sailors; Clinton's tally of the garrison ran to 6,577). [HIGH at range level; see DISPUTED]
- **Duration:** ~6 weeks of formal siege (ground broken Apr 1–2; surrender May 12); ~3 months from first landing. [HIGH]

### American commanders (bio material, verified)
- **Benjamin Lincoln (1733–1810) [HIGH]:** Hingham, Massachusetts farmer and militia officer, the sixth child of a prosperous colonel; major general by 1777, wounded in the ankle in the Saratoga campaign, then sent south in September 1778 as commander of the Southern Department. Solid, honest, liked, and unlucky: the failed Franco-American assault on Savannah (October 1779) was his, and at Charleston he was caught between a professional's duty to save the army and a city government that threatened to turn on him if he tried. He surrendered the army on May 12, was paroled, **exchanged in November 1780 for Maj. Gen. William Phillips**, and at **Yorktown, as Washington's second in command, received the surrendering British army — the man who gave up Charleston took the sword at Yorktown** (see §6). A court of inquiry was contemplated; no charges were ever brought. Later first U.S. Secretary at War; led the militia that put down Shays's Rebellion. [HIGH throughout]
- **William Moultrie (1730–1805) [HIGH]:** South Carolina planter-soldier, the hero of June 28, 1776 — the palmetto fort that repulsed Parker's fleet was renamed for him. In 1780 a brigadier (soon major general) and Lincoln's senior subordinate inside the lines; favored getting the army out; his 1802 *Memoirs* are the indispensable American eyewitness source for the siege (the May 9–10 bombardment passage, §7). Spent his captivity refusing British blandishments; later twice governor of South Carolina.
- **Christopher Gadsden (1724–1805) [HIGH]:** Charleston merchant-radical, designer of the "DON'T TREAD ON ME" rattlesnake flag, lieutenant governor in 1780 — and the loudest civilian voice against evacuating the city. He helped force Lincoln to stay; then, on May 11, led the citizens' petition begging Lincoln to surrender. Paroled as a civil officer, he was seized by the British in August 1780 with other leading men and shipped to St. Augustine, where he refused a second parole and spent ~10 months in a dungeon of the Castillo de San Marcos. [HIGH; his arc is the political trap personified]
- **Brig. Gen. Isaac Huger (1743–1797) [HIGH]:** South Carolinian commanding the cavalry force posted OUTSIDE the city at Monck's Corner to keep the Cooper River door open; surprised at 3 a.m. on April 14 by Tarleton and Ferguson; escaped on foot into the swamps. (Pronounced "you-JEE" — include pronunciation if the page names him aloud.)
- **Brig. Gen. William Woodford (1734–1780) [HIGH]:** led ~750 Virginia Continentals into Charleston on April 7 after a ~500-mile, 28-day march from Morristown — straight into the trap. Captured May 12; **died November 1780 aboard a British prison ship in New York harbor.** [HIGH; the bitter arc is documented]
- **Commodore Abraham Whipple (1733–1819) [HIGH]:** Continental Navy commander of the little squadron in the harbor (frigates *Providence*, *Boston*, *Ranger*, *Queen of France*, plus smaller craft). Judged the harbor bar indefensible, fell back, and finally scuttled ships (the *Queen of France* among them) in a boom across the Cooper's mouth, sending guns and sailors to the land batteries. The surrender cost the Continental Navy three frigates captured — a naval disaster nested inside the military one. [HIGH]
- Also present/captured: Brig. Gen. **Lachlan McIntosh** (favored evacuation); Brig. Gen. **James Hogun** of North Carolina (**died a prisoner at Haddrell's Point, January 1781**); the French engineer **Louis Duportail**, Washington's chief engineer, who slipped INTO the city about April 25, pronounced the works untenable, urged evacuation, was refused, and was captured with everyone else. **Seven Continental generals** went into captivity. [HIGH on the roster; Duportail arrival date MED]
- Cavalry outside: Lt. Col. **William Washington** and Col. **Anthony Walton White** (both escaped Lenud's Ferry by swimming the Santee). [HIGH]

### British commanders
- **Lt. Gen. Sir Henry Clinton (1730–1795) [HIGH]:** Commander-in-chief in North America since 1778; planned and led the expedition in person. Charleston was the masterpiece of his careful, methodical style — and the greatest British victory of the war. He had been humiliated off Charleston in 1776; in 1780 he refused every shortcut. He quarreled with Arbuthnot (joint-command friction ran the whole siege) and with Cornwallis (their feud poisoned the rest of the war), issued the June proclamations, and sailed back to New York in early June believing the South was won. **In-repo portrait: rev-longisland-clinton.jpg / rev-monmouth-clinton.jpg / rev-stony-point-clinton.jpg (REUSE).**
- **Lt. Gen. Charles, Earl Cornwallis (1738–1805) [HIGH]:** second in command; once the Cooper River side opened, he commanded the force east of the river (~2,300+) that sealed the landward escape. Left behind on Clinton's departure with ~8,300 men to hold and extend the conquest — Camden, the backcountry war, and the road to Yorktown all start here. **In-repo portrait: rev-longisland-cornwallis.jpg (REUSE; also rev-monmouth-cornwallis.jpg, rev-princeton-cornwallis.jpg).**
- **Vice Adm. Marriot Arbuthnot (c. 1711–1794) [HIGH]:** naval commander; got the heavy ships over the bar (March 20) and ran them past Fort Moultrie on **April 8** in under two hours with trifling loss — the moment the 1776 legend died as a strategic fact. Cooperated badly with Clinton throughout; the captured Fort Moultrie was renamed "Fort Arbuthnot" for him.
- **Lt. Col. Banastre Tarleton (1754–1833) [HIGH]:** 25-year-old commander of the British Legion (loyalist cavalry + light infantry), Liverpool merchant's son, a cornet who had captured Charles Lee in 1776. His horses died on the winter passage; he remounted on seized country horses and then, in two night-and-dawn strokes — **Monck's Corner (April 14)** and **Lenud's Ferry (May 6)** — destroyed the American cavalry outside the city and slammed the Cooper River door. The Waxhaws and the "Bloody Ban" legend are two weeks PAST the end of this page (see §6 handoff). The Reynolds portrait is the image (see §8).
- Also: Maj. **Patrick Ferguson** (rode with Tarleton at Monck's Corner; later the loyalist commander killed at King's Mountain — a forward-reference candidate); Brig. Gen. **James Paterson** (Savannah column); **Lord Rawdon** (April reinforcement); Capt. **Charles Hudson, RN** (took Fort Moultrie's surrender May 7); Capt. **Johann Ewald** and Capt. **Johann Hinrichs** (Hessian jäger officers whose diaries are prime eyewitness sources). [HIGH]

## 4. THE SIEGE
### The ring closes (Feb 11 – Apr 1) [HIGH]
- Landing Feb 11–12 at Simmons Island, 30 miles south; six weeks of careful island-hopping (Stono ferry, James Island, Wappoo Cut, the Ashley's west bank — Hinrichs: "What a land to wage war in!", §7); March 20 Arbuthnot's heavy ships crossed the Charleston bar unopposed (Whipple had already conceded it); **March 29** the army crossed the Ashley at Drayton Hall and walked down the Neck. On the night of **April 1–2** the British broke ground ~800 yards from the American lines: the **first parallel**, the textbook opening move of a European siege (a trench dug parallel to the enemy works so the diggers never face the guns broadside; then saps zigzag forward and a closer parallel repeats the process). Clinton, repulsed here in 1776, was taking the city by arithmetic. [HIGH]

### April 8 — the fleet runs past Fort Moultrie [HIGH]
- On April 8 Arbuthnot ran eight warships past Fort Moultrie into the harbor on a flood tide, losing only a handful of men and one supply vessel — the passage took well under two hours. The fort that had wrecked a British fleet in 1776 was simply *sailed past* in 1780; Moultrie's guns barked at ships that did not stop to fight them. The harbor was now British water; the city could no longer be supplied or evacuated by sea. The same day Woodford's 750 Virginians marched in — the last reinforcement to make it. [HIGH]
- **April 10:** Clinton and Arbuthnot jointly summoned Lincoln to surrender; refused ("duty and inclination" pointed to defense "to the last extremity" — Lincoln's reply, standard in the accounts; MED on exact wording, frame as paraphrase). **April 13:** the British batteries opened; the bombardment of the city began. Gov. Rutledge left the same day. [HIGH on dates]

### Monck's Corner, April 14 — the back door kicked in [HIGH]
- The one escape route left ran north out of the city, across the Cooper River and up the roads east of it — held open by Huger's ~500 cavalry and militia at Monck's Corner / Biggin Bridge, ~30 miles up the Cooper. Tarleton (with Ferguson's corps, ~1,400 men) marched at night, scooped up a captured letter that betrayed the American dispositions en route, and hit the camp at **3 a.m. on April 14** without firing a warning shot. The surprise was total: 14–20 Americans killed, ~60–70 captured, the rest (Huger and William Washington included) escaping on foot into the swamps — and the British took the thing they needed most, **the horses** (their own had gone overboard in the winter storms; now the Legion was superbly mounted). Biggin Bridge and the Cooper's east bank were British. [HIGH; casualty figures vary slightly by source — keep approximate. A documented postscript: Legion dragoons assaulted local women at a nearby plantation after the fight, and British officers (Ferguson reportedly furious, demanding punishment) had the men flogged rather than executed — MED, in Stedman and the standard accounts; use only if the page wants the early dark-streak beat, and keep it at one careful sentence.]
- **April 18–23:** Cornwallis crossed to the east of the Cooper with 2,300+ men; the American posts at Lempriere's Point/Hobcaw folded back. The investment was complete on every side. [HIGH]

### The trap debated, the trap refused (Apr 19–26) [HIGH on dynamics]
- With the second parallel done (~April 17–19, ~250–450 yards out) and provisions counting down, Lincoln's councils of war took up evacuation. This is where the city fathers closed the door (§2): Gadsden scolded, Ferguson threatened the boats, the militia could not be compelled, and Duportail's professional verdict (the works could not hold; the army should go) changed no votes that mattered. On **April 21** Lincoln offered Clinton a capitulation: the garrison to march out with the honors of war and go free. Clinton refused flatly — he held every card. A spirited American sortie before dawn on **April 24** (Virginians and South Carolinians against the approaches) drew blood but changed nothing; it was the garrison's only offensive act of the siege. [HIGH; sortie casualty figures vary — keep to "drew blood"]

### May 6–8 — the week everything fell [HIGH]
- **May 6, Lenud's Ferry:** the regrouped American cavalry under White and William Washington, caught resting on the Santee River bank ~40 miles north, was destroyed by Tarleton a second time — ~30–40 killed/wounded, dozens captured, the commanders swimming the river to escape. Organized American force outside Charleston ceased to exist. [HIGH; exact figures vary — range it]
- **May 7, Fort Moultrie:** a Royal Navy landing force (~500 sailors and marines under Capt. Charles Hudson of HMS *Richmond*) summoned the fort; its ~200-man garrison (Lt. Col. William Scott — roughly 117 Continentals and 100 militia) was cut off, outnumbered, and beyond rescue, and **surrendered without firing a shot**. The 1776 shrine fell to a summons. The British renamed it Fort Arbuthnot. [HIGH]
- **May 8:** Clinton summoned Lincoln to unconditional surrender. Negotiations over May 8–9 broke on two points: the militia's status and the honors of war. Lincoln still asked for the full honors; Clinton refused them again. [HIGH]

### The night of May 9–10 — the great bombardment [HIGH, with Moultrie's own words]
- When the truce lapsed (about 9 p.m. on May 9), both sides opened with everything — by Moultrie's count the heaviest cannonade of the siege, ~180–200 pieces firing through the night, shells crossing overhead, magazines and ammunition chests exploding along the lines. Moultrie: "it was a glorious sight, to see them like meteors crossing each other, and bursting in the air; it appeared as if the stars were tumbling down," and in the same passage, "it was a dreadful night!" (full quote §7). [HIGH — Moultrie's *Memoirs*]
- **May 11 — the burning houses and the petition:** British guns firing **hot shot** (cannonballs heated red in furnaces to set wooden buildings alight) started fires inside the town; with houses burning, the third parallel at the canal's lip, and the wet ditch drained, **Gadsden and the leading citizens — the same men who had refused to let the army leave — petitioned Lincoln to surrender on the best terms he could get.** The militia in garrison sent word the British terms were acceptable to them. Lincoln reopened negotiations that evening. [HIGH on the sequence; exact day of the worst fires (May 10 vs 11) varies by account — "in the siege's final days" is safe]

### May 12 — the surrender [HIGH]
- At about 11 a.m. the Continental troops marched out of the Hornwork and laid down their arms between the lines. **The honors of war were refused**: colors cased (furled and sheathed), and the drummers forbidden to beat a British march — the formal, deliberate humiliation of an enemy denied the courtesies of a respected foe. By Moultrie's account the drums beat **a Turkish march** — the permitted nothing-tune. British and Hessian grenadiers lined the works; the British flag went up over the Hornwork. The militia turned in arms in the following days (May 13–14) and were paroled to their homes; the Continentals went into captivity. Lincoln, by the standard account, was allowed the empty courtesy of riding out; his army was gone. [HIGH on ceremony mechanics — colors cased + no-British-march are in the capitulation articles; the Turkish-march detail is Moultrie's memoir, MED-HIGH, attribute it]
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — "Long live Congress":** one circulating account has paroled men shouting "Long live Congress!" as arms were grounded, with officers' swords confiscated afterward in retaliation. The capitulation did let officers keep their swords, and swords WERE later taken at Haddrell's Point after disturbances — but the cause-and-effect anecdote is loosely sourced. [MED — omit or hedge hard]

## 5. CASUALTIES & THE SCALE (dossier ranges)
- **Siege casualties were light; the catastrophe was the capture.** American: ~89 killed, ~138 wounded. British: ~76–99 killed, ~120–220 wounded (land + naval returns vary). Fewer than 300 dead on both sides combined for a six-week siege — then everything else. [HIGH at range level]
- **Captured — the DISPUTED number, handle as follows:** Clinton's official return counted **5,466 prisoners** (some tallies 5,500–5,700) — but it swept in every armed male: Continentals, militia, armed citizens, sailors. The British return of "3,371 Continentals" similarly over-counts (it includes invalids and non-effectives); Borick puts genuinely effective Continentals near **2,650**, and the usual modern statement is **~2,500–2,800 Continental regulars** plus militia/citizens/sailors. SAFE PAGE FRAMING: "more than five thousand men — the entire Continental Army in the South, about half of them regulars." NEVER print 5,466 as a clean count of soldiers. [HIGH on the framing; the precision is the dispute]
- **Also taken [HIGH at range level]:** seven Continental generals; **more than 300 guns** (sources run 300–400); ~5,000–6,000 muskets; large powder stores; 15 stands of colors; the Continental frigates *Providence*, *Boston*, and *Ranger* (the *Queen of France* already scuttled).
- **The prisoners' fate [HIGH]:** the paroled **militia went home** (until June 3 — §6). The **Continental privates went first to the barracks/Haddrell's Point and then, from later that summer, into prison hulks in the harbor** (six ships in the standard account); over the following year **roughly 800 of ~2,500 Continental prisoners died** of disease, heat, and starvation in the harbor hulks — about one in three. Hundreds more escaped the hulks only by enlisting in British regiments bound for the West Indies. Officers sat in crowded "parole" at Haddrell's Point, where Brig. Gen. Hogun died (Jan 1781); Woodford died on a New York prison ship (Nov 1780). The general exchange that freed the survivors did not come until mid-1781. [HIGH on shape and ~800 figure (standard); MED on the six ship names — *Esk, Fidelity, King George, Success-Increase, Concord, Two Sisters* per the secondary lists — verify before printing names]

### The May 15 magazine explosion [HIGH — documented in multiple eyewitness diaries]
- **Three days after the surrender (May 15, 1780)**, British work parties were stockpiling thousands of captured muskets in a brick magazine/arsenal building near Magazine Street, close to the main powder store (the neighborhood held some 10,000 lb of powder). American officers had warned that many of the surrendered muskets were still loaded; the warnings were ignored. One musket went off in handling, the loose powder caught, and the magazine blew up — touching off stored cartridges and thousands of muskets, throwing burning debris across the neighborhood, and burning the nearby barracks, jail, and house of correction. Bodies and parts were thrown a quarter-mile; one corpse was reportedly flung against the steeple of the Independent Church; loaded muskets kept cooking off in the wreckage, hitting rescuers. Capt. Johann Ewald, a hundred yards away, recorded "charred arms and legs scattered in every direction… a deplorable sight"; Capt. John Peebles of the 42nd called it "a melancholy accident" born of "Very Strange Management, to Store up loaded Arms in a Magazine, of Powder." [HIGH — Ewald, Hinrichs, Peebles, Moultrie all record it]
- **Death toll — DISPUTED, give the range:** contemporary estimates ran from "near a hundred" (Maj. William Croghan) to rumors "upwards of 300"; the commonly printed figure is **about 200 killed** — British and Hessian soldiers (including Capt. Collins RA and Lt. Gordon of the 42nd, ~17 British artillerymen), American prisoners, civilians, and inmates of the burned jail. SAFE: "perhaps two hundred people — more than had died on both sides in the whole siege." That comparison (explosion dead ≈ 2× siege combat dead) is in the standard accounts and is the right closing note for it. [HIGH on the comparison; the count itself is a range]

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **The Continental position in the South ceased to exist [HIGH]:** no army, no cavalry, no navy, no southern department — until Congress sent Gates (smashed at Camden, August 16) and then Greene. South Carolina's government was a governor-in-exile (Rutledge) with emergency powers and no state.
- **Clinton's proclamations — the fuse [HIGH]:** Clinton initially offered easy terms: paroles, protection, neutrality for the quiet. Then, on **June 3, 1780**, days before sailing, he issued the proclamation that undid it all: **existing militia paroles were declared void as of June 20; every man was required to take an oath of allegiance and actively support the Crown — or be treated as a rebel.** Neutrality was abolished at a stroke. Thousands of paroled men who had been content to sit out the war concluded that if they had to fight for somebody, it would not be the king; the proclamation is standardly credited with helping ignite the partisan war (Sumter, Marion, Pickens) that consumed the British conquest from the inside. Clinton, with one piece of paper, re-armed the rebellion he had just disarmed. [HIGH — the proclamation text is on the American Battlefield Trust's primary-source page; the "guaranteed the continuance of the Revolution in the South" judgment is the standard one]
- **Clinton sails north [HIGH]:** in early June (June 5 in most accounts; June 8 in some) Clinton sailed for New York with about a third of the force, fearing a French fleet on the northern coast, **leaving Cornwallis ~8,300 men and the South** — and a poisonous, escalating feud between the two men that runs all the way to Yorktown.
- **Two weeks to the Waxhaws — the handoff, set up but NOT told [HIGH]:** one Continental force had missed the trap: Col. Abraham Buford's ~350–400 Virginians, marching to reinforce Charleston, turned back on news of the surrender. Cornwallis sent Tarleton after them; on **May 29, 1780**, at the **Waxhaws** near the North Carolina line, Tarleton's Legion ran them down — and what happened when the Virginians tried to surrender became the atrocity story ("Tarleton's quarter") that named the southern war's rage. THAT is the next battle's story; this page ends with the column riding north. [HIGH on date/setup; all massacre content belongs to the Waxhaws page]
- **The fairness read on Lincoln [HIGH — adopt this frame]:** Lincoln's defeat was as much political as military. He could have saved the army in early April and was forbidden to by the government he was defending; the men who threatened to open the gates if he left were the men who petitioned him to surrender five weeks later. Congress and Washington treated him accordingly: exchanged within six months, never charged, restored to Washington's side — and at **Yorktown (October 19, 1781)** Washington, refusing Cornwallis's deputy O'Hara, directed the British surrender to **Lincoln**, and granted the garrison only what Lincoln's had received: "The same Honors will be granted to the Surrendering Army as were granted to the Garrison of Charles Town" (Washington to Cornwallis, Oct 18, 1781). Colors cased, the march denied. The war's most precise piece of bookkeeping. [HIGH; the quote chain is solid — LOC/Founders]
- **British high-water mark [HIGH]:** Charleston + Camden made summer 1780 the nadir of the American cause in the South — and the conquest unraveled within a year (King's Mountain Oct 1780, Cowpens Jan 1781, Greene's campaign). Frame Charleston as the victory that won Britain a city and lost it a countryside. (Keep this judgment at one sentence; the southern-turn chapter owns the full arc.)

## 10. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)
- **Date:** March 29 – May 12, 1780 (formal siege from April 1; surrender May 12) · **Where:** Charleston, South Carolina
- **Forces:** British ~12,000–14,000 troops (after reinforcement) + ~5,000 sailors and a fleet, under Clinton, Cornwallis, and Arbuthnot · American ~5,500–6,000 (about 2,500–2,800 Continentals + militia, citizens, and sailors) under Benjamin Lincoln
- **Duration:** six weeks of formal siege; three months from the first landing
- **Casualties:** American ~89 killed, ~138 wounded — and **more than 5,000 captured, the entire southern army**; British ~76–99 killed, ~120–220 wounded. Plus ~200 dead in the May 15 magazine explosion, more than the siege killed on both sides together.
- **Winner line material:** British victory — the worst American defeat of the war. Clinton took the South's greatest city, an entire army, seven generals, three hundred guns, and a navy squadron, at the cost of fewer than a hundred dead — then signed a proclamation that abolished neutrality in South Carolina and handed the rebellion back its soldiers. The army that marched out with cased colors got the gesture returned at Yorktown, to the man: Benjamin Lincoln took the British surrender there, on Charleston's terms.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. **Captured total**: 5,466 (Clinton's return) vs 5,500–5,700 (other tallies) vs ~2,650 effective Continentals (Borick) — print "more than 5,000," gloss the counting.
2. **British strength**: 10,000 → ~12,800–14,000 with reinforcements + ~4,500–5,000 sailors — give the range and the reinforcement arc, never one number.
3. **British casualties**: returns vary (76 k/89 w land + 23 k/28 w sea, vs 99 k/217 w in one tally) — "around a hundred dead, a few hundred wounded."
4. **Guns captured**: 300 / 311 / 400 across sources — "more than 300."
5. **Explosion toll**: ~100 (Croghan) to 300 (rumor); "perhaps two hundred" is the safe printed figure, with the bigger-than-the-siege comparison.

7. **The fires**: hot-shot house fires are firmly attested in the last days (May 10–11); exact night varies by account — "in the siege's final days."
8. **The Turkish march**: Moultrie's memoir detail, widely repeated — attribute to Moultrie ("by Moultrie's account…").
9. **Prison-hulk figures**: ~800 dead of ~2,500 Continentals over the following year is the standard figure [HIGH as standard], but the six ship names are from secondary lists [MED] — drop the names unless re-verified.
