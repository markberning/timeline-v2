## FILE 1 — guerrilla-war-final.md

### Confederate Congress Retaliatory Act, May 1, 1863
**Claim:** Captured Black Union soldiers to be re-enslaved / turned over to state authorities; white officers liable to death for "inciting servile insurrection."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** House Divided / Dickinson College primary-text transcript; EJI calendar May 1, 1863; Civil War Emancipation blog. The precise framing matches: Black soldiers treated as fugitive/recaptured slaves under state law; white officers subject to death "at the discretion of the court" for inciting servile insurrection. The prose uses "could be put to death" which correctly reflects the discretionary language.

### Edmund Kirby Smith "no quarter" instruction, June 13, 1863
**Claim:** Trans-Mississippi commander wrote to a subordinate approving "the propriety of giving no quarter to armed negroes and their officers."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED (with minor date nuance)  
**Notes:** Two related letters are documented — one dated June 3, 1863 and one dated June 13, 1863, both from Kirby Smith's headquarters to Richard Taylor. Both contain essentially the same "no quarter" language. The June 13 date used in the file is documented in multiple secondary sources and appears in War of the Rebellion OR records. The core claim — the command-level "no quarter" authorization — is confirmed regardless of which letter is cited. No error.  
**Sources:** cwmemory.com; Encyclopedia of Arkansas; multiple secondary-source aggregations of the OR records.

### Lincoln's Order of Retaliation, July 30, 1863 (General Orders No. 252)
**Claim:** One Confederate prisoner executed for every Union soldier murdered; one at hard labor for every one sold into slavery.  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Teaching American History primary-text transcript; American History Central; Lincoln Studies blog; Ford's Theatre NPS social post. The order number (No. 252), date (July 30, 1863), and provisions (execution-for-execution, hard labor for enslavement) all confirmed.

### Achilles Clark letter — full passage including "Gen. Forrest ordered them shot down like dogs"
**Claim:** Confederate Sgt., 20th TN Cavalry, wrote to his sisters two days after Fort Pillow; the quoted passage including the "Gen. Forrest ordered them shot down like dogs" sentence is the full letter.  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Wikipedia "Battle of Fort Pillow"; Tennessee State Parks; History.com. The full quotation as given in the file — including the previously omitted final sentence attributing the order to Forrest — matches the documented text of the Clark letter across multiple independent sources.

### Order No. 11 — ~25,000 displaced
**Claim:** "roughly 25,000 people (estimates run from about 20,000 up to 25,000)" displaced.  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Wikipedia "General Order No. 11 (1863)"; Civil War on the Western Border encyclopedia entry. Sources confirm "approximately 25,000 civilians" displaced; the range framing (20,000–25,000) is well within documented estimates.

### Lawrence raiders ~400–450
**Claim:** "roughly 400 to 450 raiders."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Wikipedia "Lawrence Massacre"; Civil War on the Western Border; BlackPast. Multiple sources cite "around 450 guerrillas" with the range 400–450 used by scholarly sources; the figure is confirmed.

### Lawrence damage ~$2M
**Claim:** "something on the order of $2 million in damage in 1863 money."  
**Result:** ⚠️ NEEDS-SOFTENING  
**Notes:** Sources vary. Multiple sources (Wikipedia, Civil War on the Western Border) give the damage as "$1 million to $1.5 million." The $2 million figure appears in some sources (BlackPast cites it as a high estimate). The file already uses "something on the order of $2 million," but the more authoritative consensus is $1–1.5 million with some sources reaching $2 million. The current phrasing is at the top of the attested range. Softening to "somewhere between $1.5 and $2 million" or "as much as $2 million" would be more accurate. This is a hedged claim, not an error, but the conservative scholarly consensus sits lower.  
**Sources:** Wikipedia "Lawrence Massacre"; Civil War on the Western Border; BlackPast.org.

### Centralia — 23 on train / 22 killed / Goodman spared
**Claim:** "23 unarmed Union soldiers pulled off; 22 killed; one spared — Sgt. Thomas Goodman, kept as a hostage."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Wikipedia "Centralia Massacre (Missouri)"; Civil War on the Western Border. Wikipedia states "23 are Union soldiers" on the train; "the guerrillas shot the line of 22 unarmed Union soldiers"; Goodman spared as a hostage and later escaped. The 23/22/Goodman framing is confirmed.

## FILE 2 — war-within-north-final.md

### Ex parte Vallandigham (February 1864) — SCOTUS unanimously declined jurisdiction over military commissions
**Claim:** "In February 1864 the Court answered — and dodged. In a unanimous ruling (*Ex parte Vallandigham*), the justices held that they had no jurisdiction to review the proceedings of a military commission at all."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** LII / Cornell Law (68 U.S. 243); Justia; Constituting America. The Court issued a unanimous ruling declining jurisdiction over military commission proceedings; it did not address the constitutional merits. The February 1864 date is confirmed. "Unanimous" is confirmed.

### Vallandigham exile route — Niagara / Clifton House then Windsor
**Claim:** "first came ashore near Niagara Falls, taking rooms at the Clifton House on the Canadian side. Only later did he relocate to Windsor, Ontario, directly across the river from Detroit."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Ohio History Journal (Vol. 74, pp. 151–168). Vallandigham arrived at Niagara / Clifton House first (documented as July 1863); relocated to Windsor by January 1864. The sequence (Clifton House → Windsor) and the geographic descriptions are confirmed. Note: the Bermuda stop is also confirmed in the route; the file's sequence (Bermuda → Canada → Clifton House → Windsor) is accurate.

### Vallandigham voted against repealing Ohio's "Black Laws"
**Claim:** "Vallandigham himself … had voted against repealing Ohio's 'Black Laws,' the statutes that stripped Black Ohioans of basic civil rights."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Wikipedia "Clement Vallandigham"; The Ohio Project; American History Central. Multiple sources confirm Vallandigham voted against repeal of Ohio's Black Laws during his time in the Ohio state legislature (mid-1840s), though he framed his opposition as preferring a voter referendum. The core fact — voted against repeal — is confirmed.

## FILE 3 — assassination-final.md

### Mary Surratt's "shooting irons" errand — afternoon of April 14, told John Lloyd to have them ready
**Claim:** "she herself had ridden out to the tavern that very afternoon and told Lloyd to have the 'shooting irons' ready, because men would be calling for them that night."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Famous-trials.com Mary Surratt page; LincolnConspirators.com; Wikipedia "John M. Lloyd"; Spartacus-Educational. Lloyd's trial testimony confirmed: Mary Surratt appeared at the tavern on the afternoon of April 14, told him to have the "shooting irons" ready that night, and gave him a field glass. The afternoon timing and the specific phrase "shooting irons" are confirmed.

### Easter Sunday April 16, 1865 — Northern ministers preached Lincoln-as-Christ sermons
**Claim:** "The news reached most of the country on Easter Sunday morning, April 16 — and it reached them in church, from the pulpit, before the newspapers could shape it. Ministers across the North threw out the Resurrection sermons they had prepared and preached Lincoln instead."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Notes:** The date (Easter Sunday, April 16, 1865) is confirmed. The phenomenon of ministers scrapping Easter sermons to preach on Lincoln is extensively documented. The Cephas Crane / Hartford quote in the file ("Jesus Christ died for the world; Abraham Lincoln died for his country") is attributed to a "Hartford preacher" — the Lehigh Library sermon archive and Friends of the Lincoln Collection document this phenomenon. The specific preacher attribution to "Cephas Crane" in the fact ledger is a name that appears in the record; the prose only says "one Hartford preacher," which is appropriately hedged.  
**Sources:** Friends of the Lincoln Collection "We Mourn Our Fallen Father"; Lehigh Library "Sermons After the Assassination"; Internet Archive sermon texts.

### Boston Corbett's shot — "back of the neck / cervical spine"
**Claim:** "Boston Corbett fired through a gap in the wall and hit Booth in the back of the neck — the bullet entered the cervical spine, a couple of inches above the collarbone, severing his spinal cord and leaving him paralyzed."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** New England Historical Society (Boston Corbett); LincolnConspirators.com (John Wilkes Booth's vertebrae); Wikipedia "Boston Corbett." The autopsy and medical records confirm: ball entered "just behind the sterno-cleido muscle — 2½ inches above the clavicle — passing through the bony bridge of the fourth and fifth cervical vertebrae — severing the spinal cord." "Back of the neck, a couple of inches above the collarbone" is accurate. The parallel-to-Lincoln wound is appropriately caveated as "doesn't really hold up" in the prose.

### Funeral route — "some 180 cities"
**Claim:** "winding back through some 180 cities across seven states."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** History.com "Abraham Lincoln's funeral train leaves D.C."; Illinois History & Lincoln Collections; HistoryNet. Multiple sources confirm "180 cities and seven states" as the standard description of the route. (Some sources say 444 communities passed through vs. 13 stops vs. 180 cities depending on what is counted; the "180 cities" figure is the standard cited formulation and is not inflated.)

### Ex parte Milligan (1866) and John Surratt's 1867 civilian acquittal
**Claim:** "*Ex parte Milligan* (1866) ruled civilians can't be tried by military courts where civil courts are open; John Surratt tried by a civilian jury [in 1867], jury deadlocked, walked free."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Wikipedia "Ex parte Milligan"; Wikipedia "John Surratt"; Oyez; Civil War on the Western Border. Milligan decided April 3, 1866; Surratt arrested November 1866, tried in civilian court 1867, jury deadlocked, never retried, walked free. Both facts confirmed.

## FILE 4 — reckoning-final.md

### Convict leasing — "slavery by another name," ~200,000 people, into 1930s–40s, via 13th Amendment punishment clause
**Claim:** "As many as 200,000 Black Americans were funneled into it, and it ran in some states well into the *1930s and '40s*."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED (with minor precision note)  
**Notes:** EJI and PBS/Blackmon confirm convict leasing ran "until the 1930s" broadly; Alabama was the last state to formally abolish it (1928); county-level practices in some states persisted into the 1940s. The "1930s–40s" formulation is supportable and is the framing in Douglas Blackmon's *Slavery by Another Name*. The ~200,000 figure is cited by multiple sources including EJI (though exact totals are contested — some sources say "tens of thousands"). The text uses "as many as 200,000," which is an upper estimate; this is appropriately hedged and consistent with EJI and PBS sources.  
**Sources:** EJI "Convict Leasing"; Wikipedia "Convict leasing"; Encyclopedia of Alabama; Alabama News Center.

### Lynching — ~4,000 (EJI, 1877–1950) and 200+ anti-lynching bills killed in Congress
**Claim:** "The Equal Justice Initiative has documented roughly 4,000 racial-terror lynchings of Black Americans across the South between 1877 and 1950 … More than 200 anti-lynching bills were introduced in Congress over the following decades; Southern senators filibustered every one to death."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Notes:** EJI's core figure is 4,084 racial terror lynchings in 12 Southern states, 1877–1950 (with a broader figure of 4,440+ including non-Southern states). "Roughly 4,000" is accurate for the core Southern dataset. The 200+ anti-lynching bills figure is confirmed; Congress.gov and NAACP records document well over 200 (the House Judiciary Committee report cites 240+) bills introduced, all failing before 2022.  
**Sources:** EJI "Lynching in America" report; EJI "Reconstruction in America"; Time magazine EJI coverage; Congress.gov H.Rept. 116-267.

### KKK founded "late 1865 (Dec 24 1865, Pulaski TN)"
**Claim:** The guerrilla file says "founded by six Confederate veterans, Pulaski, Tennessee, 1866 — some accounts date the very first meeting to December 24, 1865, but 1866 is the year the thing took shape." The reckoning file has it corrected to "founded in Pulaski, Tennessee, in late 1865."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Notes:** December 24, 1865 is the dominant documented founding date (Zinn Education Project; Wikipedia; ICT News; multiple other sources confirm this date). The reckoning file's corrected "late 1865" is accurate. The guerrilla file's dual-framing (Dec 24, 1865 first meeting / 1866 the year it took shape) is also defensible as the organization took its definitive form in 1866–1867. No error in either file.  
**Sources:** Zinn Education Project "Dec. 24, 1865: Ku Klux Klan Founded"; Wikipedia "Ku Klux Klan"; ICT News.

### Revels represented Mississippi but did NOT literally fill Jefferson Davis's seat (Davis's seat → Adelbert Ames)
**Claim:** "He represented Mississippi, the very state whose secession had been led by Jefferson Davis … (The story that Revels literally inherited Davis's old seat is a myth — that seat went to a white Union general; Revels filled a different Mississippi vacancy — but the deeper irony … was entirely real)"  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Notes:** NPS, U.S. Senate historical records, Wikipedia, and Smithsonian Magazine all confirm: Revels was elected to fill the seat vacated by Albert G. Brown (not Jefferson Davis). Davis's former Senate seat went to Adelbert Ames. The file correctly identifies this as a myth AND correctly names the corrected facts (Davis's seat → Adelbert Ames). Confirmed accurate.  
**Sources:** NPS "Hiram Rhodes Revels"; Wikipedia "Hiram R. Revels"; U.S. Senate "Hiram Revels: First African American Senator"; Smithsonian Magazine.

### Hacker death-toll ~750k as a range
**Claim:** "roughly 750,000, with a plausible range running from 650,000 all the way to 850,000."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Notes:** Hacker's December 2011 paper established 750,000 as the central figure with the 650,000–850,000 range. The file appropriately frames this as an estimate of a range. Note: a 2024 study revised the figure to approximately 698,000 — lower than Hacker's 750,000. The file does not claim to represent current consensus, only Hacker's 2011 finding, and frames it correctly.  
**Sources:** Binghamton University Research News; HNN "J. David Hacker Rethinks Civil War Death Toll"; PubMed 2024 follow-up study.

### "Revolution through Korea" combined-deaths formulation
**Claim:** "more American soldiers died in the Civil War than in every other American war from the Revolution through the Korean War combined."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** American Battlefield Trust; NPS. This is the standard well-documented ABT/NPS formulation, confirmed accurate as stated.

## FILE 5 — prisons-final.md

### Lieutenant = 6 privates (Dix–Hill)
**Claim:** "a lieutenant 6" privates in the Dix–Hill equivalency scale.  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Wikisource "Dix-Hill Cartel" primary text; Wikipedia "Dix–Hill Cartel"; 47th Pennsylvania Volunteers site. The primary text states: "A lieutenant or a master in the Navy … could be … exchanged for … six privates or common seamen." Confirmed at 6 privates per lieutenant.

### Andersonville peak ~33,000 (~August 9, 1864)
**Claim:** "At its peak, around August 9, 1864, it held roughly 33,000 prisoners — the most precise count put it at 33,006."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Wikipedia "Andersonville Prison"; Andersonville Timeline 1864 (HubPages); multiple secondary sources. The precise figure of 33,006 on August 9, 1864 is confirmed. (One search result cited August 12 as "almost 33,000" — the August 9 date with 33,006 is the more precise documented figure per the prison's own records.)

### Sultana ~1,200
**Claim:** "killing something like 1,200 men (the modern accounting puts it near 1,164; older books say 1,700 to 1,800, but recent scholarship has revised it sharply downward)."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Wikipedia "Sultana (steamboat)"; Salecker, *Destruction of the Steamboat Sultana* (2022); Sultana Association (1,169). The ~1,164 modern figure is confirmed by recent scholarship; the "older books say 1,700–1,800" acknowledgment is accurate. The "~1,200" rounding in the prose is consistent with the evidence (Hoffman's 1865 report gave 1,238; the Sultana Association gives 1,169). The framing is honest and accurate.

### Union camp average ~12%
**Claim:** "Union camps, which sat near 12 percent."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** Standard Civil War prison scholarship. This is the accepted figure for Union camp mortality, confirmed accurate and distinguishable from the Confederate camp aggregate (~14–15%). The correction from the original "6–8%" is correct.

### Atwater court-martial + Johnson pardon
**Claim:** Atwater court-martialed for larceny (refused to surrender his own death list), served ~2 months, pardoned by President Andrew Johnson (with Clara Barton lobbying) in late/December 1865.  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** NPS Dorence Atwater page; Clara Barton Museum; New England Historical Society; Wikipedia "Dorence Atwater." All sources confirm: court-martialed for refusing to return the list, sentenced, served a short period, pardoned by Johnson in December 1865 after Barton lobbied. Confirmed.

### Davis/Lee in the original Wirz indictment, then struck
**Claim:** "The original conspiracy charge named Jefferson Davis and Robert E. Lee themselves, along with Winder and others, as Wirz's co-conspirators — and then the War Department struck Lee's name before the final indictment and never brought Davis to trial for it at all."  
**Result:** ✅ CONFIRMED  
**Sources:** UMKC Famous Trials summary (law2.umkc.edu); John Reeves, *The Lost Indictment of Robert E. Lee*. Both Lee's name appearing in the original conspiracy charge and the War Department striking it before the final indictment are documented. Davis was named but never tried. Confirmed.

## Summary of Findings

**CLEAN.** All specific new-material claims across all five files are confirmed or fall within the documented range of evidence. No ❌ WRONG findings.

**Two ⚠️ NEEDS-SOFTENING items:**

1. **Lawrence damage "$2 million" (guerrilla-war-final.md):** The figure is at the top of the attested range. The conservative scholarly consensus is $1–1.5 million, with some sources extending to $2 million. The file's current phrasing ("something on the order of $2 million") is at the high end. Consider softening to "somewhere between $1.5 and $2 million" or "as much as $2 million." This is not a factual error — the figure appears in attested sources — but it is not the consensus midpoint.

2. **EJI lynching figure — the file says "roughly 4,000" while EJI's most recent figure for the South is 4,084, with a broader national figure of 4,440+.** "Roughly 4,000" is accurate for the core dataset (12 Southern states, 1877–1950) and is how EJI's original report was widely covered. No change required; flagged for awareness that EJI has revised upward since their 2015 report.

**One clarifying note (not an error):** The Kirby Smith "no quarter" instruction is listed in the file as "June 13, 1863." Two related documents exist — one dated June 3 and one dated June 13 — both from Kirby Smith's command to Richard Taylor, both containing substantially the same language. The June 13 date is attested in multiple secondary sources and appears in OR records. No correction needed.
