# Two Governments — Clarity Audit (Newcomer / Zero-Knowledge Read)

Critic persona: a sharp 15-year-old who has NEVER studied the Civil War, opened a
U.S. history textbook, or looked up any of these names. Reading cold. Question: can
they follow the argument and the story?

Rating scale per item:
- **CLEAR** — no gloss needed; the draft already explains it
- **NEEDS-GLOSS** — newcomer can guess or muddle through but will mis-understand something
- **LOST** — newcomer has no foothold; comprehension breaks here

Severity:
- **MUST-FIX** — comprehension-breaker; blocks understanding the argument
- **SHOULD-FIX** — friction that adds confusion or loses the reader's trust

---

## SECTION: Opening (paras 1–3)

---

### 1. "secession" (para 3, first use)
**NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX**

> "This chapter picks up the morning after: what those seven states actually *built*
> once they had walked out. Because secession is just leaving."

The draft treats "secession is just leaving" as the definition — smart, but it
arrives *inside* a sentence that already assumes you know the word. A zero-knowledge
reader who hits "the secession winter" in para 3 (before the gloss) sees the word
defined in passing only after context has already used it twice. More critically, the
word appears earlier in the same paragraph — "seven Deep South states already gone"
is a reference to the sibling chapter the reader may NOT have read. If this is a
standalone entry, those seven departures have no explanation at all.

**Concrete fix:** In the opening sentence of para 3, add a single parenthetical:
"secession (the legal term these states used for formally leaving the United States)"
before the breezy "is just leaving" gloss. Or open the sentence with "Walking out —
the act they called secession — is just leaving."

---

### 2. "the sibling chapter (the election of 1860 and the secession winter)" (para 3)
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

The draft assumes the reader has read a preceding chapter. A standalone zero-knowledge
reader has no idea what the "secession winter" was, which seven states went, or why.
The parenthetical names an event ("secession winter") that is itself undefined.

**Concrete fix:** Either (a) briefly gloss "secession winter" as "the months between
Lincoln's election in November 1860 and his inauguration in March 1861, when Southern
states began voting to leave," or (b) make the seven states' departure self-contained
in one short sentence before calling it a sibling-chapter handoff.

---

### 3. "states' rights" (para 3's implicit setup, fully used in "In Their Own Words")
**CLEAR at first use — flagged for downstream**

The draft handles this well when it arrives; no fix needed here.

## SECTION: The Government in Montgomery

---

### 4. "provisional constitution" / "provisional president" / "provisional Congress"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX**

> "they had adopted a provisional constitution — a temporary founding charter, meant
> to hold the new nation together until a permanent one could be written."

"Provisional constitution" IS glossed here — good. But "provisional president" (para
after) and "provisional Congress" (appears in FACT LEDGER but bleeds into the reading
experience) are never glossed. The draft says Davis was chosen as "provisional
president" right after explaining what provisional meant, so carry-over should work —
*except* the explanation of "provisional constitution" is in a subordinate clause and
a reader who skimmed will miss it. More importantly, the word "provisional" never
appears in the reader-facing prose again, so a re-read won't rescue them.

**Concrete fix:** After "provisional president," add "(temporary, until elections
could be properly organized)" — five words that nail it for skimmers.

### 7. "Secretary of War under President Franklin Pierce from 1853 to 1857. He had *run the United States Army*."
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

A zero-knowledge reader may not know what "Secretary of War" is — it sounds
important, but the word "Secretary" in American government is a cabinet minister
(not a secretary who takes notes), and that distinction is non-obvious to a 15-year-old.
The draft immediately says "He had *run the United States Army*" which rescues it —
barely. But "ran" the Army for a politician is different from actually commanding
it militarily, and the draft doesn't clarify that this is a civilian role.

**Concrete fix:** Replace "Secretary of War" with "Secretary of War — the civilian
cabinet minister who oversees the army — under President Franklin Pierce."

### 9. The Davis/Lincoln contrast paragraph
**CLEAR overall**

### 10. "Alexander H. Stephens (Confederate) of Georgia took his oath as the new nation's vice president. Stephens was a frail, sickly, sharp-tongued lawyer who had actually argued *against* secession right up until his state left, then gone with it."
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

The draft says Stephens argued against secession "then gone with it" — a zero-knowledge
reader doesn't know *why* he'd go with Georgia if he disagreed. The draft relies on
the reader inferring that state loyalty could override personal conviction, a concept
the draft explains beautifully later for Lee — but not here, and at first mention it
reads as simply inconsistent behavior with no engine.

**Concrete fix:** One clause: "...then gone with it when his state voted to leave —
putting loyalty to Georgia above his own judgment." This primes the reader for the
Lee payoff later.

---

### 11. Davis inaugural address: "the right solemnly proclaimed at the birth of the States," he said, recognized "in the people the power to resume the authority delegated."
**LOST → MUST-FIX**

> He grounded the new nation's existence not in slavery but in the Declaration of
> Independence, in the language of self-government and the consent of the governed —
> "the right solemnly proclaimed at the birth of the States," he said, recognized
> "in the people the power to resume the authority delegated."

This primary-source fragment is left to do interpretive work it cannot do for a
zero-knowledge reader. "The power to resume the authority delegated" is 19th-century
legalese. The draft does NOT follow it with a plain-language gloss — the next sentence
immediately moves to Davis bristling at "revolution." A zero-knowledge reader is lost:
what authority? Delegated to whom? Resumed from whom?

**Concrete fix:** After the quote, add one sentence in plain language: "In plain terms:
he was arguing that the Southern states had originally *lent* their sovereignty to the
federal government, and now they were simply taking it back." Then proceed to the
"revolution" reframing.

---

### 12. "it is by abuse of language that their act has been denominated a revolution"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

Another archaic fragment left without a plain-language follow-up. The draft surrounds
it with the gloss ("This was no rebellion, in his telling. It was a lawful resumption
of self-rule.") — but the sentence before the quote ("He bristled at the word
*revolution*") sets up the quote, and the gloss appears *after*. For a zero-knowledge
reader the sequence works only if they re-read. The word "denominated" (meaning "called"
or "labeled") is opaque.

**Concrete fix:** A quick inline bridge: "...which he rejected: 'it is by abuse of
language' — he meant *wrong* language — 'that their act has been denominated
[called] a revolution.'"  Or simply rephrase the surrounding prose to gloss it
before the quote, not after.

**CLEAR**

Good inline definition. No fix needed.

### 16. Mississippi quote: "by an imperious law of nature, none but the black race can bear exposure to the tropical sun"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

The draft lets this line stand without a gloss. The argument Mississippi is making —
that enslaved people were biologically suited for field labor in hot climates — is
racist pseudoscience that a zero-knowledge reader may not immediately parse as a
*justification for slavery* rather than a random climatological claim. The surrounding
prose says it's a "defense of slavery grounded in economics and a frank claim of racial
fitness," which is the gloss — but it comes *before* the quote, not after, so the
reader lands on the quote cold.

**Concrete fix:** After the quote, add: "In other words: they were arguing that Black
people were naturally meant for the work, which is why it should continue."

---

### 17. "The choice, Mississippi declared, was stark: 'There was no choice left us but submission to the mandates of abolition, or a dissolution of the Union.'"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

"The mandates of abolition" — a zero-knowledge reader may not know what "abolition"
means (the movement to end slavery), and they've just read a long block of quotes.
The draft uses the word "abolitionist" later (in the Lincoln section, with a gloss)
but here it appears without one.

**Concrete fix:** The simplest fix is to do the abolitionist gloss here at first use:
"the mandates of abolition (the movement demanding an end to slavery)" — so it's
already defined when Lincoln's section uses it again.

---

### 18. Confederate Constitution comparison — "three-fifths of all slaves" / "three-fifths of all other persons"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX**

> "Where the U.S. Constitution counted three-fifths of 'all other persons' for
> representation, the Confederate version counts 'three-fifths of all slaves.'"

The draft never explains what the three-fifths clause *means*: why would you count
three-fifths of anyone? What is "representation"? A zero-knowledge reader has no idea
this was a formula for how many seats in Congress each state got, based partly on
population — including enslaved people counted as less than a full person. Without
that, the comparison between "other persons" and "slaves" is just a nomenclature
difference, not the moral bombshell the draft intends.

**Concrete fix:** One sentence before the quote: "The U.S. Constitution had a formula
that gave Southern states extra seats in Congress by counting each enslaved person as
three-fifths of a person for population purposes — without, of course, letting them
vote." Then the quote lands with force.

---

### 19. "Article I forbade the Confederate Congress from ever passing any 'law denying or impairing the right of property in negro slaves.' Read that again: the new nation's founding charter made it constitutionally *impossible* to weaken slavery"
**CLEAR**

The "Read that again" + plain-language restatement works perfectly. No fix needed.

---

### 20. "settling the very question that had torn the 1850s apart"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

> "it declared that in any future Confederate territory, 'the institution of negro
> slavery, as it now exists in the Confederate States, shall be recognized and protected.'
> The great fight of the previous decade had been whether slavery could spread into
> new western lands."

A zero-knowledge reader has no idea what "the question that had torn the 1850s apart"
refers to. The draft *does* immediately gloss it in the next sentence ("The great fight
of the previous decade had been whether slavery could spread into new western lands"),
so the fix is already there — but the sequencing means the reader sees the reference
before the explanation. Works on a re-read; first-pass readers lose it for a beat.

**Concrete fix:** Simply reverse the order: explain the 1850s fight *before* the quote,
then show how the Confederate clause settled it. Minor reordering, not rewriting.

## SECTION: "We Are Not Enemies, but Friends"

---

### 21. "the four wavering slave states of the upper South — Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, Arkansas — were watching every syllable to decide whether to follow"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

This is the first mention that these four states haven't yet left. A zero-knowledge
reader who hasn't read the sibling chapter may wonder: why would Virginia etc. want to
leave at all? The draft hasn't connected Virginia's hesitation to the slavery
question. It implies it, but doesn't say it plainly. The full explanation comes much
later in "Choose" — but the reader meets these four states two sections early, without
knowing why they matter.

**Concrete fix:** One clause on first mention: "...the four wavering slave states of
the upper South — Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, Arkansas — were watching every
word, weighing whether to follow the Deep South out of the Union." That "follow the
Deep South" makes the stakes clear without a long detour.

### 22. "an abolitionist (someone demanding the immediate destruction of slavery)"
**CLEAR**

The parenthetical gloss is exactly right. (See also note in item 17 — this gloss
arrives *after* "abolition" is used unglossed in the Mississippi section, so it should
be moved earlier.)

### 23. Lincoln quote: "The power confided to me will be used to hold, occupy, and possess the property and places belonging to the Government."
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

The draft DOES gloss this: "There were federal forts, arsenals, and customs houses
scattered across the seceded states, and the Confederacy regarded them as foreign
installations on sovereign soil." But this gloss comes *before* the quote, not
immediately after. By the time the reader hits the quote, they've moved on from the
gloss. The quote itself — "property and places belonging to the Government" — is
indirect language, and a zero-knowledge reader may not immediately connect it to Fort
Sumter.

**Concrete fix:** After the quote, add: "(Translation: he would not give up the
federal forts inside Confederate territory — including one in Charleston harbor called
Fort Sumter.)" This makes the connection explicit and sets up the next section.

**Concrete fix:** Expand to: "his incoming Secretary of State — the most powerful
cabinet minister, in charge of foreign affairs — William Seward." The word "incoming"
already signals pre-confirmation status, so just add the role description.

### 28. "General P.G.T. Beauregard (Confederate)... Pierre Gustave Toutant Beauregard, a Louisiana Creole and West Point graduate"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

"Louisiana Creole" is likely opaque to a 15-year-old outside Louisiana. "Creole"
has several meanings (French-speaking mixed-heritage community in Louisiana is the
intended one), and the draft never explains why the writer mentions it.

**Concrete fix:** Either drop "Louisiana Creole" (it's color, not load-bearing) or
add: "a Louisiana Creole — from the French-speaking community of old Louisiana
families — and a West Point graduate." Otherwise cut it; it distracts from the
teacher-student irony, which is the point.

### 29. "before the war he had been superintendent of West Point, and one of his old instructors there had been Major Robert Anderson. The teacher was now penned inside the fort; the student was now arranging the guns to blow it apart."
**CLEAR**

Beautifully explained. The irony lands cleanly. No fix needed.

### 30. "a flaming mortar round"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

A 15-year-old may not know what a mortar is. It's different from a cannon — fires at
a high arc, lands nearly vertically, useful for lobbing shells over walls.

**Concrete fix:** "the first Confederate shell — a mortar round (a shell fired in a
high arc to drop down over walls)" — one parenthetical. Or just "artillery shell,"
which loses precision but removes the confusion.

### 31. "a hundred-gun salute to the United States flag"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

"Gun salute" is a military tradition a 15-year-old may not know: you fire a set number
of blanks as a formal ceremony of honor. Without that, the reader may wonder why they
were firing more guns in a fort that just surrendered.

**Concrete fix:** "Anderson asked permission to fire a hundred-gun salute — a
ceremonial volley of blank cannon shots, a formal military honor — before he
hauled the flag down."

## SECTION: Choose

---

### 32. "75,000 state militia volunteers to put down what he called the rebellion — ninety-day men, raised from the loyal states"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX**

Two things collapse here for a zero-knowledge reader:

(a) "State militia" — a 15-year-old probably doesn't know that in 1861 there was no
large standing national army; each state maintained its own militia, and the federal
government had to *ask* states to send men. This is the reason Lincoln's call went to
the states at all, and it's the reason the upper-South states felt personally targeted.

(b) "Ninety-day men" — this is jargon meaning volunteers enlisted for a 90-day term,
which implies everyone expected the war to be over quickly. That expectation is the
dramatic irony the draft is building toward, but it's dropped in a dash without
explanation.

**Concrete fix:** "Lincoln issued a proclamation calling for 75,000 state militia
volunteers — soldiers drawn from each state's own citizen-soldiers, since the United
States had no large standing army of its own — to put down what he called the
rebellion. He asked for ninety-day commitments, assuming the crisis would be settled
in three months."

following prose ("He would in two days resign his Union commission and take command
of Virginia's forces"), or use a different convention: "(U.S. Army, soon Confederate)."

### 41. The habeas corpus passage
**LOST → MUST-FIX**

> "he suspended the writ of *habeas corpus* along the military corridor between
> Washington and Philadelphia."
>
> "*Habeas corpus* — Latin for 'you shall have the body' — is the ancient legal
> right that protects you from being jailed without cause..."

The draft *does* define it — and the definition is good. But the sequence is
backwards for a newcomer: Lincoln does something first, then we learn what it is.
A zero-knowledge reader reads "suspended the writ of habeas corpus" with zero
foothold — it could be a war tactic, a tax policy, or a magic spell. The word "writ"
is itself unexplained (a formal legal document/order). The Latin "you shall have the
body" in the definition is vivid but abstract.

The deeper problem: the draft explains what habeas corpus *is* but not immediately
what *suspending* it means in practice. It gets there ("hold them without trial,
without charge, indefinitely") but only after a full dense paragraph of legal context.

**Concrete fix:** Flip the order. Before the action, give a one-sentence version:
"Lincoln reached for a drastic legal weapon: he suspended *habeas corpus* — the
centuries-old rule that the government cannot lock someone up without taking them
to court and proving why — along the military corridor between Washington and
Philadelphia." Then the definition paragraph can follow and expand, and the reader
has a practical foothold from the start.

### 42. "Chief Justice Roger Taney — the same justice who had written the notorious *Dred Scott* decision declaring that Black Americans could never be citizens"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

"Notorious" signals the reader they should already know this is bad, but a
zero-knowledge reader doesn't know the *Dred Scott* decision at all. The gloss
("declaring that Black Americans could never be citizens") is correct and helpful.
But a reader who wants to understand WHY Taney is the one ruling here gets nothing —
he's the Chief Justice (highest judge in the country), which the draft does state.

Actually, the draft handles this adequately given space constraints. The only issue
is that "citizens" undersells the decision's scope (it also said Congress could never
ban slavery anywhere). But for this section's purpose — establishing who Taney is —
it's sufficient.

**Verdict: CLEAR enough. Minor improvement possible but not a MUST-FIX.**

### 43. "ruled in *Ex parte Merryman*"
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

Latin prefix is opaque.

**Concrete fix:** Drop the Latin case name entirely and just say: "Chief Justice
Roger Taney ruled in an emergency court decision that Lincoln's suspension was
unconstitutional." The case name can appear in a footnote or source note if needed;
it doesn't do work in the reading experience.

### 46. "a Union officer named Brigadier General Nathaniel Lyon, commanding regiments largely made up of pro-Union German immigrants, surrounded and captured a state militia encampment outside the city called Camp Jackson, where the militia had been arming itself with cannon covertly seized from a federal arsenal"
**LOST → MUST-FIX**

This sentence is 63 words of compacted information with three overlapping actors
(Lyon, German regiments, state militia), two locations (St. Louis, Camp Jackson), a
historical backstory (how the militia got cannon from a federal arsenal), and a
detonator (the street shooting) — all in one compound sentence followed immediately
by its consequences. A zero-knowledge reader cannot track who is doing what to whom.

Additionally: "Brigadier General" is a rank the draft hasn't explained (it's
one tier above colonel), and "regiment" hasn't been defined (a military unit of
several hundred men). Neither is load-bearing, but the sentence's density swamps
the reader before they can worry about individual terms.

**Concrete fix:** Break into three sentences:
"On May 10, 1861, a Union commander named Nathaniel Lyon cornered a pro-Confederate
state militia force at their camp outside St. Louis. The militia had quietly armed
itself with cannon taken from a federal weapons depot — and Lyon had come to take
them back. As his men marched their prisoners through the city streets, Southern
sympathizers in the crowd attacked; after someone in the mob fired on the soldiers,
Lyon's troops fired back, killing roughly twenty-eight civilians."

**NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX (see item 32 above)**

The draft assumes the reader understands why *asking states for militia* felt like a
declaration of war against the South. The mechanism (states send their own men to
march against other states) is gestured at but not made explicit. Covered above.

### Leap E: Why Davis "took the bait" at Sumter — what he stood to lose by waiting
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

> "Davis took the bait, or rather, walked into the trap with his eyes open... On
> April 9, Davis and his cabinet decided to strike before the food arrived — to take
> the fort by force rather than let it be resupplied."

The draft explains Lincoln's clever moral positioning, but not why Davis chose to
strike *anyway* knowing the trap. A zero-knowledge reader is left wondering: if Davis
knew this was a trap, why not just... let the food in? The draft implies but doesn't
state: Davis couldn't let a Union fort in the Confederacy's most symbolic harbor be
resupplied indefinitely — it would mean accepting permanent federal presence in
Confederate territory.

**Concrete fix:** One sentence after "decided to strike": "Letting the fort be
resupplied meant accepting a permanent Union military post in the Confederacy's most
famous harbor — a symbol Davis could not afford." Then proceed to the bombardment.

### Leap F: Why Anderson moved from Fort Moultrie to Fort Sumter
**NEEDS-GLOSS → SHOULD-FIX**

> "he had moved there himself, on his own initiative, back on December 26, 1860,
> slipping his small command across the harbor by night from the older, more exposed
> Fort Moultrie to the far more defensible Sumter."

"More exposed" and "far more defensible" are gestured at but not explained. A
zero-knowledge reader doesn't know the geography. Why was Moultrie more exposed?
(It had land approaches; Sumter, on an island, could only be attacked by sea or
shelled from shore batteries.) The strategic logic matters because it's why Anderson
can hold out for 34 hours against 3,000 shells.

**Concrete fix:** "Fort Moultrie sat on the shore and could be stormed from land;
Fort Sumter, on its man-made island, could only be reached by water — far harder
to take." One sentence.

## WORST MUST-FIX

**M5 — the habeas corpus passage.**

Lincoln suspends habeas corpus in the middle of a dense Maryland section already
loaded with riot deaths, bridge-burning, and a mayor, and the draft explains what
habeas corpus IS *after* Lincoln has already used it. "Writ" is unexplained. The
Latin definition ("you shall have the body") is vivid but abstract. A zero-knowledge
reader has no practical foothold — they don't know if Lincoln just cancelled a tax,
closed a court, or deployed secret police — until four sentences later. Given that
this is the MOST CONTESTED decision in the section (Lincoln ignoring the Chief Justice
to hold Maryland) and the one the draft needs the reader to feel the moral weight of,
arriving at it confused is the single most damaging clarity failure in the piece.

**Count: 6 MUST-FIX comprehension-breakers.**
