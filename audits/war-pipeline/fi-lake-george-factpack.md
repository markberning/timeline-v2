# FACT PACK — Battle of Lake George (September 8, 1755)

*French and Indian War. The ONLY permitted source. Stakes = imperial (Britain vs France) + Native (Haudenosaunee kinship, land, trade). NO slavery angle. Use ranges for casualties.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- Date: September 8, 1755. Location: south end of Lake George, Province of New York (near present-day Lake George village). Coords ~43.42,-73.71. State = New York.
- Campaign: the British 1755 Crown Point expedition (one prong of that year's offensive), aimed at the French Fort St. Frédéric on Lake Champlain.
- Result: British/provincial victory — a rare bright spot in a bad year (Braddock's defeat on the Monongahela was the year's disaster); Britain "needed a military hero."

## 2. BACKGROUND
- British plan: take French Fort St. Frédéric at Crown Point on Lake Champlain.
- William Johnson led the expedition: Irish-born (c.1715, Co. Meath), New York's agent to the Iroquois, NOT a regular army officer (held a major-general's commission despite little military experience); adopted as an honorary Mohawk sachem (Warraghiyagey, "A Man who undertakes great Things").
- Force = provincial militia (Mass, Conn, NY, NH, RI) + Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee allies (via Johnson's close ties). Built a road from the Hudson (Fort Lyman) north to the lake; Johnson renamed the lake "Lake George" for George II.
- French commander Baron Jean-Armand Dieskau marched south to pre-empt the British, aiming first at the supply post (Fort Lyman/later Fort Edward), then Johnson's main camp.

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
- British/provincial/Haudenosaunee: Maj. Gen. William Johnson (overall cmdr, provincial/agent, not a regular); Maj. Gen. Phineas Lyman (Connecticut, 2IC — took effective command after Johnson was wounded); Col. Ephraim Williams (Massachusetts, led the Bloody Morning Scout, KILLED; by bequest founded what became Williams College; age ~40); Hendrick Theyanoguin ("King Hendrick," eminent elderly Mohawk sachem, led the Mohawk, KILLED). Junior figures later famous: John Stark, Israel Putnam, Philip Schuyler. Strength engaged: ~1,500 colonial militia + ~200-300 Mohawk/Iroquois allies; 3 cannon; no regulars. (The larger expedition/encampment is sometimes cited ~3,000 provincials + ~300 Mohawk — distinguish engaged vs expedition.)
- French: Maj. Gen. Baron Jean-Armand Dieskau (cmdr; WOUNDED and CAPTURED); Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre (led the Canadian/Native contingent, KILLED in the morning, dismaying the French-allied Natives); Chevalier de Montreuil (took command after Dieskau's capture). Dieskau's detachment that fought: ~220 French regular grenadiers (Régiments de la Reine and Languedoc) + ~600 Canadian militia + ~600-700 Native allies (Caughnawaga/Kahnawake Mohawk, Abenaki, Nipissing); ~1,500 total (his total Canadian army was ~4,000).

## 4. THE THREE PHASES
(a) The "Bloody Morning Scout" (~8-9am): Johnson detached ~1,000 provincials under Ephraim Williams + ~200-250 Mohawk under Hendrick south toward Fort Lyman. Dieskau, warned by a deserter, set an ambush in a ravine ~3 mi south (grenadiers blocking the road, Canadians/Natives on the flanks). BOTH Williams and Hendrick killed (Williams shot after climbing a rock to rally his men; Hendrick's horse shot from under him, then he was bayoneted and scalped). Survivors made a fighting retreat (~3-4 mi) under Whiting/Pomeroy; Saint-Pierre killed in the pursuit. Hendrick had opposed splitting the force (the famous "sticks" remark — see Quotes/Uncertainties).
(b) The main battle at Johnson's fortified camp (midday-afternoon): provincials dug in behind a barricade of wagons, overturned boats, and felled trees, with cannon. Critical Native dynamic: the French-allied Caughnawaga (Kahnawake) Mohawk refused to assault the entrenchment because it was defended by their own Mohawk kinsmen; the Abenaki wouldn't advance without them; the Canadians wouldn't advance without the Natives. Dieskau formed his French grenadiers in column (six abreast) to shame the others into attacking. British artillery fired grapeshot, cutting "lanes, streets and alleys" through the French. Johnson wounded early (hip/thigh, ball remained for life), retired; Lyman took effective command. Dieskau severely wounded (legs, then shot through the groin while propped against a tree); the assault collapsed; ~20 severely wounded French abandoned incl. Dieskau, who was CAPTURED (Lt. Daniel Claus guarded him from Johnson's Native allies).
(c) The "Bloody Pond" (late afternoon, ~2 mi south): reinforcements from Fort Lyman/Edward (Capt. Nathaniel Folsom's ~80 NH provincials + ~40 NY under Capt. McGinnis) struck the retreating French baggage/rearguard (~300 Canadians and Natives resting near a pool), firing from cover and driving them off at nightfall. Capt. McGinnis mortally wounded; Folsom lost ~6. French dead reportedly thrown into the pool, which ran red — still called "Bloody Pond." (Those killed here largely Canadian colonials and Native allies, not French regulars.)

## 5. CASUALTIES & OUTCOME (ranges — heavily contested)
- British/provincial (incl. Mohawk): ~330 total killed/wounded/missing is a common composite (component splits vary; heaviest losses in the morning ambush). 
- French (incl. Canadians and Natives): ~228-339 total, possibly higher (Native losses under-reported); the Régiment de la Reine and Languedoc/grenadiers took disproportionately heavy losses in the camp assault.
- Dieskau captured (wounded). Strategic stop-short: the British did NOT push on to Crown Point; instead Johnson built Fort William Henry at the south end of Lake George and the Hudson post (Fort Lyman) was completed and renamed Fort Edward. Tactically real, strategically limited. Historian Julian Gwyn: "Never was such an insignificant encounter so generously rewarded."

## 6. CONSEQUENCES / RECOGNITION
- William Johnson made a baronet and voted £5,000 by Parliament (1755).
- Credit controversy (per popular/secondary sources): New Englanders held the credit belonged to Phineas Lyman, who directed the actual camp defense after Johnson was wounded early, yet Lyman got little recognition while Johnson got the title and money. Present as a contemporary New England grievance.
- Fort William Henry (built here) becomes the site of the 1757 French siege/surrender — a separate, future battle; do not conflate.
- Mohawk alliance: central to victory but the Mohawk paid heavily (Hendrick + others killed in the ambush); most Iroquois on both sides withdrew after the morning rather than kill kin — a lasting strain on Haudenosaunee participation.

## 7. NATIVE PERSPECTIVE / STAKES
- Centrality of the Mohawk under Hendrick (aged, eminent sachem who trusted Johnson and led the morning column to his death).
- Haudenosaunee on BOTH sides — kin fighting kin: Johnson's Mohawk faced the French-allied Caughnawaga (Kahnawake) Mohawk (a Mohawk community relocated to the St. Lawrence). A tragedy of relatives potentially killing relatives. The kinship bond materially shaped the battle: Johnson's Mohawk reportedly sent an embassy to the French-allied Iroquois (Sept 4) and used a signal of three gunshots to declare peaceful intent; on Sept 8 the Caughnawaga refused to storm a camp full of kin, stalling Dieskau's assault. Most allied Iroquois on both sides withdrew after the morning. (Sept 4 embassy / three-gunshots rest on one source — corroborate.)
- The land: the contest was over the Lake Champlain-Lake George-Hudson corridor (the invasion highway between New France and the British colonies) and over empire and the fur/Indian trade.

## 8. VERIFIED QUOTES
- Hendrick, opposing splitting the force: "If they are to be killed, they are too many; if they are to fight, they are too few." (Wording varies across retellings; the authoritative Dictionary of Canadian Biography does NOT carry it — treat as well-attested tradition, not documentary; attribute carefully.) Stick-breaking demonstration is traditional/anecdotal.
- Hendrick at the Albany Congress (1754, authentic, useful as character color, NOT a battle quote): "Look at the French, they are men; they are fortifying every where; but we are ashamed to say it; you are like women, bare and open, without any fortifications." Also: "The Governor of Virginia and the Governor of Canada are both quarrelling about lands which belong to Us."
- "I smell Indians" (Hendrick, on the morning march) — [UNVERIFIED/anecdotal].
- Julian Gwyn (modern historian): "Never was such an insignificant encounter so generously rewarded."
- No reliable first-person quote from Dieskau or Johnson surfaced — do not invent.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. Casualty counts heavily contested (both sides ~220-340+; Native losses under-reported) — use ranges. 2. Force sizes (engaged ~1,500+200-300 vs whole expedition ~3,000+300; Dieskau's detachment ~1,500). 3. Hendrick's "too few/too many" quote — wording varies, DCB doesn't carry it; well-known tradition not documentary. 4. Hendrick's age/birth year (c.1680-1692; indisputably old). 5. Bloody Pond details traditional/local. 6. Lyman credit controversy framed by secondary sources. 7. "Three gunshots" peace signal / Sept 4 embassy rest on one source — corroborate.
