# Shiloh — Storytelling Critic Gate

**Date:** 2026-05-30
**Draft:** `audits/war-pipeline/shiloh-draft.md`
**Voice bar:** `ANTIETAM_NARR` in `section-narrative.tsx` + `WRITING-RULES.md`
**Gate:** STORYTELLING ONLY — no edits, no commits.

---

## Overall Grade: GOOD

Strong bones throughout. The voice is locked to the house standard, the side-tagging is clean, the italic asides are earned, and the section structure follows the Antietam bar. Two sections hit STRONG; one hits NEEDS WORK; none requires a full REWRITE. The ceiling-lifter fix is concentrated in one place: **the-second-day** is the weakest section by a wide margin, and fixing it alone raises the overall from GOOD toward STRONG.

## Section Grades

### 1. the-surprise | The Surprise at Pittsburg Landing

**Grade: STRONG**

**Hook & stakes:** The opening paragraph earns its length. Naming slavery in the first paragraph as the thing Grant's river campaign is strangling is exactly the Antietam pattern, and "a campaign to strangle a slaveholding republic" is one of the draft's best lines. The "He was about to be very nearly proven wrong" italic under the Grant quote is textbook Antietam-voice — a held breath before the fall.

**Human dimension:** Sherman's day is the section's emotional center and it delivers. The three horses shot out, the prior reputation collapse, the precise double wound — these are earned details, not inventory. The Fraley Field patrol as the tripwire is specific and has a named soldier at its center (Peabody), which grounds the abstraction.

**Play-by-play tedium:** None that damages the section. The army-size parenthetical ("an army being the largest field force, here roughly 45,000 men organized into six divisions, each division ten to twelve thousand men…") in the "Grant's orders were to wait" paragraph is the only stop-dead-in-the-road passage. It is necessary context for a zero-knowledge reader but as written it stacks four definitional layers in one sentence and stalls the forward momentum right before the camps-overrun sequence.

**Fix (no invented facts):** Break the parenthetical into two moves: give the top-line size in the main sentence, then put the org-chart breakdown in its own quick italic aside after the "40,000 men" figure is established — matching the pattern used for "corps" in Antietam's Cornfield section (`a corps being the army's biggest building block, on the order of ten to fifteen thousand men, itself made of smaller divisions…`). The inline parenthetical in the current draft stops the reader for ~70 words at the exact moment tension should be building toward "Grant made the decision that would nearly destroy him."

**Voice:** Clean. "He simply did not expect to be attacked here" — good Antietam-flat declarative. The Johnston-overruling-Beauregard sequence has the right level of drama without overselling.

**Pacing:** The three subsection breaks (Dawn / Breakfast Interrupted / Sherman's stand) are well-timed. The "Meanwhile in Corinth" card is the weakest card of the four — it explains the stakes of a battle already under way in language slightly too abstract ("a fight over a railroad junction the soldiers dying in the cornfields would never see"). It works but is the section's flattest moment.

**Ending:** "He survived, in part, because the president stood by him" — wait, that's section 4. The section ends on "He was wrong on every count, and the proof was already crossing the river behind Grant." That line is clean and exact and does its job. Strong close.

### 2. the-hornets-nest | The Hornet's Nest

**Grade: STRONG**

**Hook & stakes:** "a section of the line firmed up in the center along an old farm lane" — quiet, specific, good setup. The stakes are established correctly: everything else is falling back; this patch alone is holding. The reader understands what "holding the center" means before the fight begins.

**Human dimension:** Johnston's death is the emotional peak of the entire draft and this section handles it at exactly the right pitch. The 1837 dueling wound leaving the area numb, the physician sent away to treat Union prisoners, the boot quietly filling with blood — three details that cost nothing in wordcount and pay off everything. "His boot quietly filled with blood" is the best sentence in the draft. The W.H.L. / Lew Wallace disambiguation ("no relation, despite the matching surname") is correct Antietam technique — this is exactly how the Irish Brigade's absolution works in the Bloody Lane section: one specific human detail anchors an otherwise-abstract tactical position.

**Play-by-play tedium:** The "eight to fourteen assaults" framing ("estimates of how many separate assaults they made range from eight to fourteen, and the true number can't be pinned down") is accurate hedging but sits right next to the myth-busting italic on the Sunken Road, and those two corrective blocks back-to-back briefly feel like a lecture. Neither block is wrong or unnecessary; the pacing dip is real but recoverable.

**Fix:** Put the Sunken Road myth paragraph *before* the assault-count paragraph, not after it. Opening with "a note on the lore" then going straight to the action-count reestablishes forward motion faster. Current order: count → lore. Better order: lore → count. No facts change.

**Voice:** The Ruggles battery line ("the largest concentration of field artillery yet assembled on the continent") is one of those Antietam "jaw-drop" moments done right — number, context, consequence in one sentence.

**Pacing:** The Hornet's Nest falls across three beats (stand / Johnston dies / Ruggles breaks it), and they have good rhythm. The Prentiss-vs-Wallace historiography italic at the end is a nice honest note and doesn't overstay.

**Ending:** "the sun was getting low" — correct understated close. The reader feels the clock ticking on the Confederate offensive. The "Meanwhile in Richmond" card is the best Meanwhile in the draft: the telegram aging badly in one sentence is sharp.

### 3. the-second-day | Buell Arrives

**Grade: NEEDS WORK**

This is the weakest section. It is not bad writing — it is structurally sound, the facts are clear, and the Forrest shield-escape is a great specific moment. The problem is that it reads like a summary of the second day rather than a story told from inside it. Compared to how the Antietam sections handle McClellan's missed chance (the Porter corps passage) or Hood's counterattack, this section stays at a higher altitude for too long and never lands a human-scale moment that the reader will remember.

**Hook & stakes:** The opening ("Beauregard halted his army at dusk on April 6 believing the hardest part was done") is competent but not arresting. It is doing scene-work (reversing the previous section's "He was wrong") and it works mechanically, but it doesn't hook. The Antietam bar for an opening is a sentence that implies a story, not a summary. Compare: "The day did not happen all at once. Antietam was really three battles fought one after another." That's a reframe that makes the reader curious. The Shiloh second-day opener gives the reader a fact.

**Fix for the hook:** The most powerful material in this section is the overnight counterreversal — Buell's boats crossing through a thunderstorm, Forrest scouting the river and being ignored, the Confederate army waking up outnumbered and not knowing it. Lead with that scene rather than Beauregard's belief. Something like: "While Beauregard's army slept among the Union camps they'd just captured, the river behind Grant was filling with boats." That drops the reader into action and makes Beauregard's false confidence the ironic reveal, not the setup.

**Human dimension:** There is no sustained human-scale moment in this section at the level of Johnston's boot or Sherman's three horses. The Lew Wallace detour ("He went on to write Ben-Hur") is a fun aside but it's trivia, not character. Forrest's shield-escape is the section's only real human flash, and it is compressed into two sentences at the close of the retreat passage. It deserves a beat of its own — a brief H2 sub-section — rather than being tucked inside the pursuit paragraph.

**Fix (Forrest):** Promote Forrest's Fallen Timbers ambush to its own H2 beat with an eyebrow ("April 8 / The cavalry bites back" or similar). This requires no invented facts — just isolating what's already in the draft into its own breathing room. The moment is too good to treat as a footnote to the pursuit-fizzled passage.

**Play-by-play tedium:** The counterattack subsection ("At first light on April 7, Grant did exactly what Beauregard never imagined…") runs through the retaking of lost ground in a way that risks becoming exactly the kind of play-by-play list the pipeline guards against. "By around noon, Buell's men had retaken the Hornet's Nest area. On the western side of the field, the advance of Sherman and Maj. Gen. John McClernand (North) ground slowly forward against a stubborn Confederate division near Shiloh Church." These two sentences are a scorecard, not a story. The Antietam analog — when the battle slides south from Cornfield to Bloody Lane — is handled by giving the reader a specific human character (the Irish Brigade, Father Corby's absolution) rather than a position recap.

**Fix:** Replace the position-recap sentences with one specific scene from the day-2 fighting — not an invented fact, but a choice about which documented detail to expand. The Buell-leads / Nelson-first attack has good material in the fact pack (Nelson's three brigades striking first against the Confederate left). Give Nelson's advance one concrete sentence — what did the Confederate left look like when fresh Union troops hit it? That grounds the "exchange ran in reverse" metaphor in something physical.

**Voice:** Solid where it exists. "The exchange from yesterday simply ran in reverse" is a strong line — almost the whole section's thesis in seven words.

**Pacing:** The section's three subsections (overnight / counterattack / retreat) feel slightly unequal. The "Reinforcements in the night" beat is the richest; the counterattack feels rushed; the retreat is adequate. The Forrest cameo at the end picks it back up, but it arrives late.

**Ending:** "the Union let the broken Confederate army limp back to Corinth largely unmolested" — accurate close but slightly flat. The "Meanwhile in the Western Theater" card ("Shiloh swung shut a door the Confederacy could not reopen") has better energy than the main section's final sentence.

### 4. the-reckoning | The Bloodiest Day Yet

**Grade: GOOD (approaching STRONG)**

**Hook & stakes:** "Start with the number, because the number is the whole story" — exact same structural move as Antietam's "Start with the cost, because the cost is the story." It is deliberate mirroring and it works. The reader who has just come from Antietam hears the echo and understands they are in the same kind of reckoning section. If this is a concern (two sections with identical openers across two battles), the fix is a single-word shift; as written it is probably fine given these are different battles.

**Human dimension:** The Samuel Todd detail (Mary Todd's brother killed at Shiloh) is pitch-perfect — it makes the casualty number land in a specific house. The "Lincoln line is almost certainly too good to be true" italic is excellent truth-telling and the best use of the italic-aside format in the section. The hedging on the McClure 1892 sourcing is exactly the right level of granularity.

**Play-by-play tedium:** None — this section is explicitly reckoning, not action, so the form is different. The movement from casualties → Brady analog → Grant nearly relieved → Grant's memoirs quote → the short-war illusion dying is clean and logical.

**Voice:** "The man who had to answer for the casualties was Grant" — strong declarative transition. The Grant Memoirs quote ("I gave up all idea of saving the Union except by complete conquest") is the draft's second-best line, and the payoff sentence — "That sentence is the meaning of Shiloh" — earns its weight.

**Pacing:** One pacing note: the "What Shiloh Meant" subsection is doing heavy lifting for both the battle's meaning AND the short-war illusion dying, and the final paragraph tries to close both at once. "The road from Shiloh ran south down the rivers, through Corinth and on to Vicksburg, where Grant would split the Confederacy in two for good" is good macro framing, but it arrives in the same paragraph as the short-war thesis, slightly diluting both. These two beats — the end of the short-war illusion and the forward-looking road to Vicksburg — could each be one clean sentence rather than woven together. No new facts needed; just sequencing.

**Ending:** "Shiloh was where the country found out it was there" — the best closing sentence in the draft. The "Meanwhile in the North" card is the weakest of the four — it largely repeats the section's thesis without adding a new angle. The three other "Meanwhile" cards each have a distinct hook (the Richmond telegram aging badly; the Corinth door swinging shut; the Grant vote). The North card is the only one that restates rather than extends.

**Fix for the Meanwhile:** The most underused angle in the draft is the home-front grief — the casualty lists running in the newspapers. This card is the right home for a specific Northern reaction (a mayor's response, a newspaper's specific editorial line, or the Todd family as a thread running from the section body into the Meanwhile card). No new facts are needed — the Samuel Todd detail already exists in the section body and could anchor the card directly.

## Priority Fix List (in order of impact)

1. **the-second-day hook** — reframe the opening around the overnight river scene, not Beauregard's belief. Single highest-impact fix in the draft.
2. **the-second-day Forrest** — promote Fallen Timbers ambush to its own H2 beat. The section's only real human-scale action moment is buried.
3. **the-second-day play-by-play** — replace the day-2 position-recap sentences with one concrete scene from the Nelson / Confederate left engagement.
4. **the-surprise org-chart parenthetical** — break the four-layer army-size definition out of the main sentence and deliver it as its own italic aside, matching the Antietam Cornfield pattern.
5. **the-hornets-nest lore/count order** — swap the Sunken Road myth italic before the assault-count sentence to reestablish forward motion.
6. **the-reckoning Meanwhile North** — anchor the card to the Samuel Todd thread or a specific newspaper reaction rather than restating the section's thesis.

## No-facts-changed note

Every fix above is structural (sequencing, paragraph-break placement, beat isolation, opening reframe). None requires inventing a fact, introducing a new source, or contradicting the fact ledger. The draft's factual handling is strong and does not require critic intervention here.
