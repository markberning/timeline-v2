## §1 — "Two fronts, one plan": the maturing Union strategy

**The strategic frame the author must establish first.** By 1863 the Union had stopped fighting
the war as a collection of separate campaigns and started fighting it as one design with two
fronts. The shorthand:
- **Hold and bleed Lee in the East; win the war in the West by taking the Mississippi.**

**Roots — the Anaconda Plan (1861).** General-in-Chief **Winfield Scott (North)** proposed in
spring 1861 a strategy with two prongs: **(1)** a naval **blockade** of every Confederate
Atlantic and Gulf port, and **(2)** a strong land-and-naval thrust **down the Mississippi River
to cut the Confederacy in two** (Scott's plan called for ~80,000 men using the river as a
highway). The press mocked it as the "Anaconda" — slow strangulation rather than a quick knockout
— but its logic became Union grand strategy. [WP-Anaconda] [ECWC-Strategy]
- **The economic core:** isolate the Confederacy diplomatically AND economically — choke its
  cotton/tobacco exports and its arms/food imports. Strangle, don't just smash. [WP-Anaconda]

**Why the Mississippi was the prize (state it plainly).** The river was the spine of the western
Confederacy. Open it and you **split the rebellion in two** — Texas, Arkansas, and most of
Louisiana (the "Trans-Mississippi," the source of cattle, food, and men) get cut off from the
rest. By autumn 1862 the Confederacy held only one ~150-mile stretch of the river: between
**Vicksburg, Mississippi (north)** and **Port Hudson, Louisiana (south)**. Take both, and the
river runs Union from source to the Gulf. [from `port-hudson-factpack`; WP-Anaconda]

**The two-front division of labor in 1863.** The East was where Lee's Army of Northern Virginia
(South) had to be *contained* — a war of attrition against the Confederacy's best army and its
political shield over Richmond. The West was where the war could be *won* — accessible river
geography, a string of strategically decisive targets, and aggressive Union generals (Grant). The
modern historiography validates this: historian **Steven Woodworth** argues that "events in this
western theater decided the result of the war" — accessible geography, valuable targets, and
aggressive generals combined to make it the decisive theater. [Woodworth-DH] *(Attribute as
historians' interpretation, not a participant claim.)*

**The commanders-in-chief — set up the contrast (carry through to §6).**
- **Abraham Lincoln (North)** moved toward a *coherent national strategy*: simultaneous advances
  on multiple fronts, aimed at **destroying Confederate armies and resources**, not just taking
  ground. He spent 1862–63 hunting for a general who would actually fight (see §7). [ECWC-Strategy]
- **Jefferson Davis (South)** planned an **"offensive-defensive"** strategy — defend the whole
  Confederacy, then use interior lines to concentrate against invading columns. But he ran the
  war through a **rigid departmental command system**: department commanders had wide autonomy
  and Davis usually refused to order interdepartmental cooperation, which **defeated the very
  concentration his doctrine required.** The South tried to defend everywhere (a "cordon
  defense") and ended up strong nowhere. [ECW-Davis] [ECWC-Strategy]

## §2 — "Winning the wrong way": Chancellorsville and the cost of victory in the East

**Strategic role:** the East in 1863 is where Lee wins battles and still loses ground he can't
afford. Chancellorsville is the case study in a victory that *cost more than it bought*. Give the
strategic meaning; LINK DOWN for the fighting.

**The battle in one line (then link down).** **April 30 – May 6, 1863**, in the tangled Wilderness
of Spotsylvania County, Virginia. **Gen. Robert E. Lee (South)**, outnumbered roughly **2:1**
(~60,000 vs ~130,000), beat **Maj. Gen. Joseph Hooker's (North)** Army of the Potomac by dividing
his army *twice* — Lt. Gen. **Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson's (South)** ~28,000-man flank march
crushed the Union XI Corps. It is widely called **"Lee's perfect battle," his masterpiece.**
[from `chancellorsville-factpack`]

**THE STRATEGIC POINT — the cost of "winning."**
- **The casualties Lee couldn't replace.** In raw numbers the North lost more (~17,300 vs
  ~13,000), but the South lost a far larger **share of a much smaller army** — roughly **21–22% of
  Lee's force** vs ~13% of Hooker's. A victory the Confederacy could not afford to keep winning.
  [from `chancellorsville-factpack`]
- **Jackson is killed.** Shot the night of May 2 by his own men (the 18th North Carolina), left
  arm amputated, dead of pneumonia **May 10, 1863, age 39.** Lee's most aggressive corps commander
  — the engine of the flank attack — and **irreplaceable.** The Second Corps had to be reorganized
  before Gettysburg, and historians draw a straight line from Jackson's death to the command
  failures of that battle. This is the strategic toll: the South's manpower and *leadership*
  arithmetic was running the wrong way even in its triumphs. [from `chancellorsville-factpack`]
- **The springboard.** Chancellorsville's confidence is exactly what convinced Lee to **invade the
  North again** in June 1863 — the road to Gettysburg. So the East's "best" Confederate victory of
  1863 set up its worst defeat. [from `chancellorsville-factpack`; `gettysburg-factpack` §1]

**The framing for the chapter:** the East is a meat-grinder where even Confederate wins **drain
the side that can least afford it.** This is "hold and bleed Lee" working — not by Union victory,
but by attrition that the South's smaller population can't sustain. Hold the contrast for §4.

## §3 — "The river that split the rebellion": the Vicksburg Campaign (the true hinge)

**Strategic role: THIS IS THE CHAPTER'S CENTER OF GRAVITY.** Vicksburg is the war's true hinge.
Tell the *strategic* story of Grant's gamble and what its success meant; LINK DOWN for the
fighting.

**The strategic problem.** Vicksburg sat on **high bluffs on the east bank** of the Mississippi,
above a hairpin bend, its guns commanding the channel — the last great Confederate lock on the
river. Through the winter of 1862–63 **Maj. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant (North)** tried four water
"experiments" (canals, bayou expeditions) to get at it; **all failed.** The Northern press called
him stuck, drunk, finished. [from `vicksburg-factpack`]

**Grant's audacious gamble (the operational beats to carry — these ARE the strategy):**
1. **Running the batteries (night of April 16, 1863).** Rear Adm. **David Dixon Porter's (North)**
   fleet ran gunboats and transports *south* past the Vicksburg guns under a 2½-hour bombardment —
   putting the fleet *below* the city. [from `vicksburg-factpack`]
2. **Cutting loose from his supply line.** Grant marched the army down the Louisiana (west) bank,
   ferried it across at **Bruinsburg (April 30)** — the largest amphibious operation in U.S.
   history before D-Day — then **abandoned his base and lived off the country.** A daring move that
   defied conventional logistics. [from `vicksburg-factpack`]
3. **Five battles in 18 days, driving a wedge between two Confederate armies:** Port Gibson (May 1)
   → Raymond (May 12) → **took Jackson, the state capital (May 14)**, scattering **Gen. Joseph E.
   Johnston (South)** → wheeled west → **Champion Hill (May 16, the decisive battle)** → Big Black
   River (May 17). **Champion Hill sealed Vicksburg's fate** — it kept Grant *between* the two
   Confederate armies (Johnston outside, **Lt. Gen. John C. Pemberton (South)** inside) so they
   could never unite. Loring's whole division was cut off and never rejoined Pemberton.
   [from `vicksburg-factpack`; `champion-hill-factpack`]
4. **The 47-day siege (May 18 – July 4, 1863)** + **surrender JULY 4, 1863.** Pemberton surrendered
   the city and **~29,495 men** (paroled), plus ~172 cannon. Grant's army *grew* (resupplied by
   river) while Pemberton's *shrank* and starved (mule, horse, rat meat; ~500 civilian "bombproof"
   caves). [from `vicksburg-factpack`]

**WHAT IT MEANT (the strategic payoff — state it big):**
- **The Mississippi falls; the Confederacy is split in two.** With Vicksburg's fall — and **Port
  Hudson surrendering five days later, July 9, 1863** — the Union controlled the **entire river**,
  source to Gulf. Texas, Arkansas, most of Louisiana: cut off. [from `vicksburg-factpack`;
  `port-hudson-factpack`]
- **Lincoln's line (VERIFIED exact, Conkling letter, Aug 26, 1863):** *"The Father of Waters again
  goes unvexed to the sea."* [Conkling]
- **It made Grant** — and put him on the path to general-in-chief (§7). [from `vicksburg-factpack`]

## §4 — "Twin July turning point — but Vicksburg mattered more": pairing Gettysburg with the river

**Strategic role:** the chapter's signature argument. Gettysburg is the famous turn; pair it with
Vicksburg as the twin July hinge — then argue, on evidence, why **Vicksburg mattered MORE
strategically.** This is the "West is where it was won" thesis made explicit.

**Gettysburg in one line (then link down).** **July 1–3, 1863**, Adams County, Pennsylvania. **Gen.
Robert E. Lee (South)** vs **Maj. Gen. George G. Meade (North)**. Lee's second invasion of the
North turned back; the climactic failed assault — **"Pickett's Charge"** (a misnomer; it was
Pickett's, Pettigrew's, AND Trimble's men) — broke against Cemetery Ridge, the "high-water mark of
the Confederacy." Combined casualties **~46,000–51,000** (killed/wounded/captured-missing). **Lee
never invaded the North again** — that is the turning-point *mechanism*. [from `gettysburg-factpack`]

**THE TWIN-TURN FRAMING (the contiguity to exploit):**
- **The same week.** Vicksburg surrendered **July 4, 1863** — the day after the Confederate repulse
  at Gettysburg (and the day Lee's retreat began). The two are conventionally the **military
  turning point of the war**; the Confederacy never recovered the initiative. [from
  `vicksburg-factpack` / `gettysburg-factpack`]

**WHY VICKSBURG MATTERED MORE — the argument, with evidence (the chapter's thesis #1):**
1. **Gettysburg was a battle Lee *survived*; Vicksburg destroyed an army and a strategic line.**
   Lee's army was bloodied but escaped back to Virginia and fought two more years. At Vicksburg an
   entire Confederate field army (~29,495 men) was *captured*, and a permanent strategic asset —
   the Mississippi River and the Trans-Mississippi's supply — was lost *forever*. Gettysburg ended
   an *invasion*; Vicksburg ended a *theater*. [from both packs]
2. **Geography of the gain.** Gettysburg restored the status quo ante (Lee back in Virginia).
   Vicksburg **permanently split the Confederacy in two** and opened the river as a Union highway
   into the Deep South — a structural, irreversible change. [WP-Anaconda; from `vicksburg-factpack`]
3. **The historiography.** Modern scholarship (Woodworth and others) holds that the **West was the
   decisive theater** — accessible rivers, decisive targets, aggressive Union generals. The East
   got the headlines and the monuments; the West got the result. [Woodworth-DH]
4. **The forgotten companion that proves the point — the Tullahoma Campaign.** While Gettysburg and
   Vicksburg grabbed the headlines, **Maj. Gen. William Rosecrans (North)** ran one of the war's
   most brilliant maneuvers (**June 24 – July 3, 1863**), feinting **Gen. Braxton Bragg (South)**
   clean out of Middle Tennessee and toward Chattanooga **with almost no casualties.** It ended the
   same week as the two famous victories and was instantly overshadowed. Rosecrans's protest is the
   chapter's argument in miniature: *"I beg on behalf of this army that the War Department may not
   overlook so great an event because it is not written in letters of blood."* — Rosecrans (North),
   to Sec. of War Stanton. [WP-Tullahoma] [ECW-Tullahoma] *(The West kept winning the war while the
   East kept getting the attention.)*

## §5 — "Emancipation becomes a weapon": Black soldiers enter the fight

**Strategic role: THE CHAPTER'S THESIS #2.** In 1863 emancipation stopped being a proclamation
and became a *military fact*: Black men in the ranks, in combat. Take the **operational/manpower
angle** (the social/agency depth links down to the USCT and Freedom Struggle themes).

**The policy machinery (the strategic fact).**
- The **Emancipation Proclamation (effective Jan 1, 1863)** explicitly authorized enlisting freed
  people into Union service. [from `emancipation` theme; Conkling context]
- **General Order 143 (May 22, 1863)** created the **Bureau of Colored Troops** and formally
  established the **United States Colored Troops (USCT).** This is the institutional birth of Black
  combat units as Union policy. [NARA-GO143] [ABT-USCT]
- **The scale (a strategic manpower resource).** By war's end roughly **186,000 Black men served in
  the Union army** (~9% of all Union soldiers); Lincoln in his **December 1863** annual message to
  Congress put **"full 100,000"** former slaves already in U.S. service, "about one-half of which
  number actually bear arms." [ABT-USCT] [MrLincoln]

**Lincoln names it as a war WEAPON (verified quotes — the strategic argument in his own words).**
- *"I believe it is a resource which, if vigorously applied now, will soon close the contest. It
  works doubly, weakening the enemy and strengthening us."* — Lincoln (North), Aug 26, 1863, on
  Black soldiering. [Conkling] **[VERIFY exact wording against the full Conkling text — paraphrased
  in some sources; the gist "works doubly, weakening the enemy and strengthening us" is the line
  to print.]**
- From the same Conkling letter (VERIFIED exact, from `vicksburg-factpack` §7): *"...there will be
  some black men who can remember that, with silent tongue, and clenched teeth, and steady eye, and
  well-poised bayonet, they have helped mankind on to this great consummation."* And: *"If they
  stake their lives for us, they must be prompted by the strongest motive — even the promise of
  freedom. And the promise being made, must be kept."* [Conkling]

**THE COMBAT DEBUT — three fights in 1863 that proved Black men would and could fight (the
operational evidence):**

1. **Port Hudson — May 27, 1863 (the first major assault by Black troops in a set-piece battle).**
   The **1st and 3rd Louisiana Native Guard** (USCT, many formerly enslaved, raised in occupied New
   Orleans) charged the Confederate bluffs in the failed Union assault. **Capt. André Cailloux
   (North)** — among the *first African American officers* in any North American army — led his
   company forward, kept gesturing his men on with his sword after his left arm was shattered, and
   was killed by a shell. His body lay on the field ~47 days (the Native Guard were not permitted to
   recover their dead under the truce) until the surrender; his New Orleans funeral (July 29, 1863)
   drew thousands and made him a national martyr-hero and recruiting symbol. [from
   `port-hudson-factpack`]
2. **Milliken's Bend, Louisiana — June 7, 1863 (the hand-to-hand proof).** A Union supply depot on
   the west bank ~15 mi above Vicksburg, garrisoned largely by the **African Brigade** — newly
   recruited, poorly armed formerly enslaved men — held against a Confederate attack in some of the
   fiercest hand-to-hand fighting of the war. The **9th Louisiana (African Descent) lost ~68% of its
   strength** — the greatest percentage loss of any Black regiment in the war. [from
   `vicksburg-factpack` §6]
3. **Fort Wagner, South Carolina — July 18, 1863 (the famous charge).** The **54th Massachusetts
   Volunteer Infantry** — a celebrated Black regiment — led the doomed twilight assault on Battery
   Wagner outside Charleston. Its commander **Col. Robert Gould Shaw (North)** was killed on the
   parapet and buried by Confederates in a mass grave with his Black soldiers (intended as an
   insult; it became an honor). The 54th suffered the **heaviest regimental loss of the assault —
   ~281 of ~600 engaged** (54 killed/mortally wounded, 48 never accounted for; over 1,500 total
   Union casualties to ~222 Confederate). **Sgt. William Harvey Carney (North)** saved the regimental
   flag after the color-bearer fell, carried it through the battle though wounded, and **became the
   first African American awarded the Medal of Honor** (awarded May 23, 1900) for the action.
   Tactically a defeat; strategically, it convinced a doubting North that Black troops would fight.
   [NPS-Wagner] [WP-Wagner] [ABT-Wagner]

**THE STRATEGIC CONCLUSION (tie it off).** **Sec. of War Edwin Stanton (North)** said the formerly
enslaved man had *"proved his manhood"* in these fights. [from `vicksburg-factpack` §6 — **[VERIFY]
exact phrasing**]. The point for the chapter: emancipation didn't just free people — it **added a
strategic manpower resource the Confederacy by definition could not match**, and it turned every
mile of Union advance into a recruiting ground. Emancipation became a weapon of war in 1863.

## §6 — "The arithmetic of the war": the manpower crisis on both sides

**Strategic role:** manpower/supply/casualties as a *system*. 1863 is when both governments had to
*compel* men into the ranks — and the way each did it exposed the war's deepest fault line. This is
the operational angle; the *political* dissent links down to the War Within the North theme.

**THE UNION — the Enrollment Act and the New York City Draft Riots.**
- **The Enrollment Act (March 3, 1863)** — the first true *national* conscription law. It made every
  male citizen (and immigrants who had filed for citizenship) **aged 20–45** liable to a draft, with
  quotas by congressional district filled by conscription where volunteers fell short. [WP-Enroll]
- **The provisions that enraged the public: substitution and commutation.** A drafted man could
  escape service by **furnishing a substitute** OR **paying a $300 commutation fee** — roughly a
  year's wages for a laborer. Both were meant to soften the draft's blow on the propertied classes;
  both gave rise to the slogan **"a rich man's war and a poor man's fight."** (Congress repealed the
  commutation fee in July 1864.) [WP-Enroll]
- **The NYC Draft Riots (July 13–16, 1863).** Working-class (largely Irish immigrant) anger at the
  draft — and at being conscripted to fight, as many saw it, for the freedom of Black workers who
  competed for jobs — exploded into the worst urban riot in American history to that point. Mobs
  burned the **Colored Orphan Asylum** (more than 200 children inside; they escaped) and lynched
  Black New Yorkers. **The strategic point:** the rioting was put down only when **Union troops
  *returning from Gettysburg* were diverted into the city** — combat regiments pulled off the very
  week of the battle to police the home front. **Death toll: officially 119–120; estimates run far
  higher** (some as high as ~1,200, contested); at least ~2,000 injured. [WP-NYDraft] **[VERIFY /
  contested: the death toll — print the official "about 120" and note higher contested estimates;
  do NOT assert ~1,200 as fact.]**
  - *Manpower-system reading:* the draft that was supposed to *fill* the armies instead briefly
    *drained* them (troops to NYC), and exposed how shallow Northern enthusiasm for the war — and
    for emancipation — could run. (The political/Copperhead/election dimension is the **War Within
    the North** theme's; this chapter keeps the manpower-and-troops-diverted angle.)

**THE CONFEDERACY — conscription earlier, harsher, and self-undermining.**
- **The South conscripted FIRST.** The **First Conscription Act (April 16, 1862)** made white men
  **18–35** liable to three years' service — *a year before* the Union's draft. The Second Act (Sept
  27, 1862) raised the ceiling to **45**; the Third (Feb 17, 1864) widened it to **17–50** for
  *unlimited* service. The Confederacy was scraping its manpower barrel far earlier and far deeper
  than the Union — the arithmetic of a smaller white population fighting a larger one. [WP-CSConscript]
- **The "Twenty-Negro Law" (Oct 11, 1862)** exempted one white man (an overseer) on **any plantation
  with 20 or more enslaved people.** It was meant to keep slavery — and its control — running while
  the white men were away. Its effect was to make the war's class fault line explicit: it "raised the
  howl of *'rich man's war, poor man's fight'*" in the South, the same cry heard in the North, and
  fed Confederate desertion and internal dissent. (Substitution was also allowed, then abolished
  Dec 28, 1863.) [ABT-20Negro] [WP-CSConscript]
- **The strategic verdict.** Both sides resented the draft; but the Confederacy's was a *symptom of a
  losing arithmetic* — it had to conscript more, younger, older, and longer because it simply had
  fewer men, and it could not (until the war's very end) tap the four million enslaved people whose
  bondage was the war's purpose. Emancipation (§5) handed that resource to the *other* side. **The
  manpower system, read as a whole, was the war's deepest asymmetry — and 1863 is when it bit.**

## §7 — "Lincoln finds his general": Chickamauga, Chattanooga, and the rise of Grant

**Strategic role:** the year's last act and the gateway forward. The Confederacy's last big western
win (Chickamauga) is undone at Chattanooga; Grant rises to overall western command; the road to
making him general-in-chief is set; the door to the Deep South opens.

**The Confederacy's last great Western victory — Chickamauga (Sep 18–20, 1863).** After Rosecrans
(North) maneuvered Bragg (South) out of Chattanooga, Bragg — reinforced by **Lt. Gen. James
Longstreet's (South)** corps sent by rail from Lee's army — turned and won the **bloodiest battle in
the Western theater** along West Chickamauga Creek in Georgia. A fatal gap opened in the Union line;
much of the army was routed back into Chattanooga. Only **Maj. Gen. George H. Thomas's (North)** stand
— "the Rock of Chickamauga" — saved it from destruction. Bragg then **besieged** Chattanooga.
[from `chickamauga-factpack`]
- *Strategic reading:* this is the South's last big offensive success in the West — and it's
  immediately squandered (Bragg fails to follow up, his generals revolt), proving the point about
  Davis's broken command system (§1).

**Lincoln consolidates the western armies under one man (the pivotal command decision).** On
**October 16, 1863**, the War Department (General Orders No. 337) created the **Military Division of
the Mississippi** — combining the Departments of the Ohio, the Cumberland, and the Tennessee, i.e.
**all Union armies between the Mississippi River and the Appalachians** — and gave it to **Maj. Gen.
Ulysses S. Grant (North).** Grant promptly **relieved Rosecrans and put Thomas in command of the Army
of the Cumberland (Oct 18).** For the first time the western war had a single hand on it. [WP-MilDivMiss]
[from `missionary-ridge-factpack`]

**The Chattanooga breakout undoes Chickamauga (November 1863).** Grant opened the "Cracker Line"
to feed the starving garrison, then broke the siege:
- **Lookout Mountain (Nov 24, 1863)** — Hooker's (North) "Battle Above the Clouds," a small fight
  with a giant name, pries open the Confederate left. [from `lookout-mountain-factpack`]
- **Missionary Ridge (Nov 25, 1863)** — the Army of the Cumberland's men, *without orders*, charged
  straight up the ridge and shattered Bragg's army. The Confederates were routed off what should have
  been an impregnable position. [from `missionary-ridge-factpack`]
- *Result:* **Chattanooga — the gateway to the Deep South (and to Atlanta) — is firmly Union.** The
  Confederacy's last western win (Chickamauga) is erased inside two months. [from
  `missionary-ridge-factpack`]

**THE ROAD TO GENERAL-IN-CHIEF (the chapter's closing arc).**
- Lincoln had spent two years cycling through Eastern generals who wouldn't fight (McClellan,
  Burnside, Hooker). The line he'd used to defend Grant after Shiloh's bloodbath in 1862 — **"I can't
  spare this man; he fights"** — now looked like prophecy. [WP-Grant] **[VERIFY: this is the
  traditional, widely-attributed phrasing; print as "Lincoln is said to have…" unless a primary cite
  is confirmed.]**
- Vicksburg + Chattanooga made Grant the obvious choice. Congress revived the rank of **lieutenant
  general** (last held by Washington); **Lincoln signed Grant's commission March 10, 1864**, making
  him **general-in-chief of all U.S. armies** (Halleck stepped down to chief of staff March 12).
  [LOC-Comm] [WP-Grant]
- *The chapter ends here:* 1863 closes with the Union having found, at last, both a *strategy* (two
  fronts, win in the West) and the *general* to run all of it. The man who took the Mississippi is
  about to take command of the whole war. **The turning is complete.** *(This sets up Chapter 4.)*

## §8 — NAME THE CAUSE (ship-blocking; woven through, collected here)

> **STANDING SHIP-BLOCKING RULE (`feedback_name_the_cause`).** The chapter MUST, in its own prose,
> name **slavery** as the cause the war (and each side) was fought over — and 1863's distinctive
> point: **the enslaved-made-soldiers turned emancipation into a weapon.** Omission is the default
> Lost Cause failure and an automatic MUST-FIX at the framing gate. This is not a separate section
> to bolt on; it is the chapter's spine. Below is the material the author must state plainly.

**The cause, named (use the chapter's own voice):**
- The Confederacy existed to **preserve and extend slavery**; eleven states broke away rather than
  accept any limit on the enslavement of ~**four million people**, and built their armies, economy,
  and society on it. The strategic targets in this chapter — the Mississippi River, Vicksburg, the
  Trans-Mississippi supply line — fed a war effort **in defense of slavery.** [standing throughline;
  consistent with `corinth-final.md` and every gated war section]
- **1863's distinctive turn — emancipation as a weapon (the chapter's thesis #2).** The Emancipation
  Proclamation (Jan 1, 1863) and General Order 143 (May 22, 1863) turned the war's *purpose* into a
  *military instrument*: the people the South enslaved became soldiers fighting to destroy slavery.
  Lincoln said so himself — Black soldiering *"works doubly, weakening the enemy and strengthening
  us."* [Conkling] [NARA-GO143] The combat debuts at **Port Hudson, Milliken's Bend, and Fort
  Wagner** are the cause made flesh: men who had been legal property a year earlier charging the
  army that fought to keep them property. (§5.)
- **The cause as strategic asymmetry (ties §5 to §6).** The war's deepest military fact was demographic
  and moral at once: the Union could arm the enslaved; the Confederacy, by the very thing it fought
  for, could not. Emancipation handed the North a manpower resource the South's cause forbade it to
  touch. **Name it plainly: the thing the South seceded to protect became the engine of its defeat.**

**Placement guidance:** name the cause early (the §1 strategic frame is a natural home — "what these
armies were fighting over"), reassert it at §5 (the weapon), and close on it at §8/§4's "West won the
war" verdict. Do NOT let the warmth of Lee's generalship (§2) or the drama of the charges fill the
vacuum where the cause should be.
