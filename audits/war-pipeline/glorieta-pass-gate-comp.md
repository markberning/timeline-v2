## Battle of Glorieta Pass, March 26–28, 1862
**Gate:** COMP critic · **Draft reviewed:** `glorieta-pass-draft.md` · **Fact pack:** `glorieta-pass-factpack.md`
**Independent sources consulted:** NPS Pecos, NPS Shattered Dream (Teaching with Historic Places), American Battlefield Trust (battle facts + article), Wikipedia (Battle of Glorieta Pass; Manuel Antonio Chaves; New Mexico Campaign), TSHAonline Handbook, Legends of America, civilwarmonths.com, Warfare History Network

## Checklist: What a Strong Selective Telling Must Cover

Each item is rated: **COVERED** (well handled in the draft), **THIN** (present but underdeveloped), or **MISSING** (absent or so brief as to leave no impression).

---

### 1. The Confederate Pacific / Slavery-Extension Aim
**COVERED.**
The draft opens with the Pacific dream in paragraph 1 and names the slavery extension explicitly in paragraph 2 ("at bottom, a bid to push slavery across the continent"). The slavery framing is not buried or hedged. The gold/silver/ports motive is also clearly laid out. This is one of the draft's strongest passages.

---

### 2. Valverde and the Road to Santa Fe
**COVERED.**
Valverde (Feb. 21, 1862) is covered — Confederate field win, Canby refusing surrender, supplies not captured, the strategic irony. Albuquerque (March 2) and Santa Fe (March 13) fall noted, with the key point that Union supplies were evacuated both times. Fort Craig as the thorn-in-Sibley's-rear is noted. Casualty figures are correctly omitted (pack flagged them as disputed).

**One thin edge:** The draft says Santa Fe was taken on "March 13" but Wikipedia gives "March 10" for some sources (Santa Fe / the ABT article says "sent forces toward Santa Fe" with occupation variously dated March 10–13 depending on source). The fact pack gives March 13; this is defensible and consistent with the pack. Flag as MINOR, not a gap.

---

### 3. Pike's Peakers — The Forced March
**COVERED.**
Denver origin, mining-camp recruitment, the "Pike's Peakers" nickname, the 67-miles-in-24-hours forced march, arrival at Fort Union around March 10–11, and the total distance as a range (300–400 miles) are all present. Slough as overall commander and Chivington as advance are identified. The draft handles the contested total-distance figure correctly by using a range.

---

### 4. Glorieta Pass as the Gateway to Fort Union
**COVERED.**
The "gate" framing is explicit and repeated: "the one practical way for an army and its wagons to move along the Santa Fe Trail between Santa Fe and Fort Union." Fort Union as the true target — the food/ammunition/gear prize Sibley had to have — is clearly established. The "whoever held Fort Union held the territory" line is clean and accurate.

---

### 5. Apache Canyon, March 26
**COVERED.**
Chivington's ~400 picked men vs. Pyron's ~300–420; the canyon-wall flanking tactic; the mounted charge; Pyron driven back and flanked again; ~70–75 captured; truce; both sides pulling back east/west. Timing (~2:30 to ~4:00 p.m.) is accurate. This section is well-handled.

---

### 6. Pigeon's Ranch, March 28 — The Tactical Confederate Win
**COVERED.**
The mutual-advance collision around 11 a.m.; hours of hard fighting; 3–5 Confederate assaults; Confederate numerical edge; Confederate sharpshooters on the ridge pinning Union infantry; Shropshire killed; Raguet mortally wounded; Scurry flanking the Union right; Slough retreating to Kozlowski's Ranch. The Scurry quote is present and sourced. The tactical Confederate victory is stated plainly and not soft-pedaled.

**One thin edge:** Lt. Col. Samuel F. Tappan (Union second-in-command at Pigeon's Ranch) is not mentioned. Wikipedia and civilwarmonths.com give him credit for holding the Union left/center for ~30 minutes in the early fighting. A selective narrative does not require every officer, so this is SHOULD-CONSIDER, not MUST-ADD.

**One thin edge:** "Windmill Hill" (the Union artillery position early in the fight) and "Sharpshooters Ridge" (named in several sources) are not named in the draft. Named terrain features add spatial clarity and help the reader picture the action. SHOULD-CONSIDER.

---

### 7. The Supply-Train Raid — Over the Mesa
**COVERED.**
Chivington's ~450 men, Glorieta Mesa (~2,000 ft above the valley floor), the ~16-mile route, the descent in ~half an hour, the overwhelmed guard, the spiked cannon, the 17 paroled prisoners, the 60–80 wagons burned, the 500–600 horses and mules killed or driven off, the contents of the train (food/ammo/medicine/forage/clothing). The draft correctly hedges all contested figures. The structural "two halves of March 28" synthesis in "A victory you cannot eat" is the narrative's strongest beat.

---

### 8. Manuel Chaves (Spelled "Chaves" in Draft vs. "Chavez"/"Chaves" Across Sources)
**COVERED — with a spelling note.**
Chaves is introduced as "Lt. Col. Manuel Chaves (North), a Hispanic New Mexican frontiersman of the 2nd New Mexico Mounted Volunteers, chosen by Canby and Chivington precisely because he knew this terrain." His role as guide over the mesa is clear; "local knowledge essential" is stated in the fact ledger. The closing "Meanwhile" panel also names him. Coverage is solid.

**Spelling flag:** The draft uses "Chaves." Wikipedia's article on the man uses "Manuel Antonio Chaves." TSHAonline uses "Chaves." NPS Shattered Dream uses "Chavez" in some places and "Chaves" in others. The Wikipedia article on the person (the more authoritative source for his name) spells it **Chaves** — so the draft is correct. No change needed.

**One thing missing from the coverage of Chaves:** Wikipedia's Chaves article notes that official military records "barely mentioned Chaves" despite his pivotal role — a pointed irony about the erasure of Hispanic contributions. The draft's closing "Meanwhile" panel does credit him and the New Mexico volunteers, but the specific note that he was largely written out of the official record is a meaningful story beat that adds depth to the "recognition" theme. SHOULD-CONSIDER.

### 9. The Role of New Mexico Hispanic Volunteers
**COVERED — but thin on texture.**
The draft mentions Chaves is a "Hispanic New Mexican frontiersman" and the closing panel credits "New Mexico's frontiersmen" and specifically Chaves as providing the "knowledge of how to get over the mesa." The fact pack notes that "official military records barely mentioned Chaves" and that New Mexico Territorial Legislature later disputed Chivington's narrative.

**What is not in the draft:** The broader New Mexico volunteer presence — beyond Chaves personally — is not fleshed out. The fact pack notes Union forces included "New Mexico Volunteers" as a distinct belligerent; the NPS Shattered Dream source explicitly says "Colorado's Pike's Peakers provided the manpower; New Mexico's volunteers provided the geography." That framing appears in the draft's closing panel but does not extend to any named unit (the 2nd New Mexico Mounted Volunteers is named only in the fact ledger entry for Chaves, not in the prose body). The NPS source also notes that Hispanic New Mexico residents viewed Confederate forces as "thieves who would steal their livestock, food, and money" — a civilian perspective the draft does not include. For a selective narrative, these are texture gaps. SHOULD-CONSIDER for the broader volunteer presence; SHOULD-CONSIDER for civilian hostility framing.

### 10. The Starving Confederate Retreat Ending the Campaign
**COVERED.**
April 12 evacuation of Albuquerque; blocked by Canby; forced through mountains and the Jornada del Muerto (named and translated); civilians looted; ~2,500 marched up, ~1,500 returned to San Antonio; ~500 dead, ~500 captured; ~40% losses; Confederacy never again threatened the Southwest or Pacific. The retreat is handled with the right weight — it reads as a slow catastrophe, not an afterthought.

### 11. The Chivington → Sand Creek Forward Reference
**COVERED.**
The Sand Creek note is present, factual, and calibrated — "scored of people, most of them women and children," the "one of the worst perpetrators of anti-Native violence in the West" framing, the "both of those things are true, and the second does not erase the first" construction. It does not over-melodramatize and is appropriately brief. The fact pack's caution about the wide kill-count range ("70+ to over 150") is honored by using "scores" — correct choice.

### 12. Sibley's Absence from the Battlefield
**COVERED — correctly hedged.**
The draft says Sibley "had not even been present on the battlefield at Glorieta" without asserting intoxication, honoring the pack's flag. This is the right call.

### 13. The Chivington / Capt. Lewis Contested Credit Claim
**COVERED — correctly flagged.**
The draft notes the 1864 Rio Abajo Press claim, identifies it as unverified and politically motivated, and credits Chivington per mainstream sources. The fact pack's "UNVERIFIED" flag is honored.

## MUST-ADD Items (essential — the draft is incomplete without these)

**None that are fully missing.** The draft covers every item on the core checklist at least adequately. However, two items rated THIN rise to near-MUST-ADD level:

### MUST-ADD (borderline) — The "acoustic shadow" / Chivington-did-not-reinforce-Slough question
**THIN / borderline MISSING.**
The draft does not address why Chivington did not send word back to Slough during the mesa operation, or why Slough — fighting a hard defensive action on the trail — could not coordinate with his flanking force. Wikipedia raises the "acoustic shadow" as one explanation for why neither commander heard the other's battle. This is not a trivial gap: a reader naturally wonders why the two halves of the Union force operated in total ignorance of each other all day. The draft gestures at this ("both armies were walking toward the same... stretch... and neither quite knew the other was so close") before the battle starts, but never explains the operational disconnect on March 28 itself. Without this, the "two halves of the day" structure feels like a lucky accident rather than a product of terrain and communication failure.
**Recommendation:** One sentence on the communication breakdown — the terrain and distance meant neither column knew the other's situation until it was over — would close this gap. The acoustic shadow claim is contested and need not be asserted; the operational fact (no contact all day) is not.

### MUST-ADD (borderline) — The Scurry second quote
**THIN.**
The fact pack includes a second Scurry quote — "I do not know if I write intelligently. I have not slept for three nights." — sourced to his official after-action report (NPS Shattered Dream). The draft uses the supply-train quote but omits this one. The exhaustion quote is the most human line in the entire source record: a man writing a battle report while barely conscious, having just won a fight and lost everything. It is the kind of detail that makes the ITALIC kicker ("The Texans had won the field. They had lost everything they needed to keep it.") land harder. It is currently in the fact pack but not in the draft.
**Recommendation:** Insert the Scurry exhaustion quote alongside or just after the supply-loss quote in the Pigeon's Ranch section.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER Items (would strengthen the draft, not required)

1. **Lt. Col. Samuel F. Tappan** — Union second-in-command at Pigeon's Ranch; led the provisional battalion that held the line for the early phase of the March 28 fight. Multiple sources (Wikipedia, civilwarmonths.com) give him a named role. Adding his name gives the Union defense more texture beyond Slough.

2. **Named terrain features at Pigeon's Ranch** — "Windmill Hill" (Union artillery emplacement) and "Sharpshooters Ridge" (Confederate sharpshooter position that pinned Union infantry) are in multiple sources. Naming them makes the spatial picture of the March 28 fight more concrete for the reader.

3. **The near-erasure of Chaves in official records** — The Wikipedia Chaves article notes official military records "barely mentioned Chaves" despite his pivotal role. This irony about the treatment of Hispanic contributions to the Union cause is a meaningful beat that fits the narrative's existing framing of Chaves and the New Mexico volunteers.

4. **New Mexico civilian hostility to Confederate forces** — NPS Shattered Dream: Hispanic New Mexico residents viewed Confederate forces as "thieves who would steal their livestock, food, and money." This is a ground-level civilian dimension the draft does not touch. It also contextualizes why New Mexican volunteers fought, not just guided.

5. **The broader New Mexico volunteer presence** — The 2nd New Mexico Mounted Volunteers (and other NM units) are part of the Union order of battle, not just Chaves personally. The draft mentions "New Mexico's frontiersmen" but never names the unit or its broader participation. One sentence would give the NM volunteer presence its own identity.

6. **Fort Fillmore / early Confederate wins before Valverde** — TSHAonline notes Confederate forces took Fort Fillmore before Valverde. The draft starts the campaign with Valverde, which is the right selective choice; Fort Fillmore is not required. SHOULD-CONSIDER only if the "momentum of Confederate success" tone is wanted.

7. **Col. Tom Green** — Commander of the 5th Texas, present in the campaign; ABT article names him. Not required for a selective narrative but would give the Confederate side more depth.

## Fact-Pack Blind Spots (gaps in the pack itself, not just the draft)

These are elements raised by independent sources that are not in the fact pack and therefore were not available to the author:

1. **The "acoustic shadow" / communication failure on March 28** — Wikipedia raises this explicitly as a reason neither half of the Union force knew the other's situation. The fact pack does not mention it. Worth adding to the pack if the draft is revised to address the operational disconnect.

2. **New Mexico Territorial Legislature's post-battle credit dispute** — Wikipedia notes the NM legislature disputed Chivington's narrative and credited other officers (Lewis, Carey). The fact pack mentions the Rio Abajo Press claim but not the legislative dispute. This is a slightly stronger corroboration of the contested-credit issue.

3. **Sibley's description as "heavy-drinking" / unreliable** — ABT article describes Sibley this way; the fact pack flags intoxication at Glorieta as unverified (correctly) but does not discuss his general reputation. The author correctly avoided the Glorieta-specific claim; the broader reputation is a separate fact that could be used carefully.

4. **The California Column** — Wikipedia mentions that a Union column approaching from California (summer 1862) would have "seriously jeopardized Confederate control" even had Sibley succeeded. The fact pack does not mention the California Column, which is a useful counterfactual for the "what if Sibley had won" framing.

5. **Pigeon's Ranch three-room adobe building** — Legends of America notes the structure still stands and is about 20% of the extant battlefield (now part of Pecos NHP). This is color/provenance detail, not narrative-required.

6. **High altitude / cold / snow conditions** — NPS Pecos notes the battle was fought above 7,000 feet in cold, snowy, forested terrain. The fact pack does not include weather/elevation detail. This is a physical texture element that reinforces the harshness of the Pike's Peakers march and the battle setting.
