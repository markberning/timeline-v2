## Section 1: the-gibraltar — GOOD

### What works

The Anaconda framing is exactly right: it gives the reader a war-level stakes frame before
zooming to the river, so the ten-gun island feels load-bearing rather than obscure. The
"compass becomes a liar" line is the draft's best piece of geography writing — concrete,
surprising, and directly explains why the fortress is a fortress. Columbus's silent retreat
("A fortress you can simply walk around is no fortress") is delivered at the right pace:
establish, subvert, explain. The Pope impasse closing with the italicized kicker is solid
Antietam-voice mechanics.

### Problems

**Hook flatness.** The opener ("In the spring of 1862, the most important thing in the
American war was a river") is reasonable but not gripping. Compare the Antietam bar: "Lee
decided to make it worse." Island Number Ten has a stronger opening beat available in the
material: the Confederates named a place "the Gibraltar of the West" and then had to abandon
it without firing a shot — that's the real hook. The draft buries that irony inside the
second paragraph instead of opening with it.

BEAT FIX: Consider inverting the opening. Lead with Columbus's impossible-to-attack
reputation; pay it off in two sentences (Grant walked around it); then open out into the
Anaconda strategy as context for why the next cork matters. The reader's first question
becomes "okay, so what IS the next one?" — which the section then answers.

**Halleck entrance.** Halleck appears only as the man who "handed [Pope] a hard assignment"
and never again. He's a named dead-end. Per writing rules, a name-drop without payoff should
be cut or promoted. Cut "Gen. Henry Halleck (North), the western theater commander" to "his
commanders" — Halleck does nothing in this story.

**Meanwhile pacing.** The "Meanwhile in the Tennessee River" aside is accurate and useful as
a connective tissue beat, but the phrase "Grant (North), fresh off Forts Henry and Donelson"
is vague for a reader who just read that Grant took those forts earlier in the same section.
The callback reads like a reminder note to self, not a reader-facing line. Tighten: "Grant,
who had cracked open the river line in February, was now marching toward a church called
Shiloh..."

### Voice check

The Antietam voice runs on short declarative punches followed by slightly longer context
sentences, with occasional single-sentence italicized kickers as paragraph enders. The
gibraltar section mostly hits this but runs a little long in the "The trouble was that the
South knew the plan" paragraph — three compound sentences in a row. Break the rhythm once
with a standalone short sentence.

## Section 2: the-canal — STRONG

### What works

This is the best section. The structure is textbook: establish the problem (bombing doesn't
work, with vivid concrete casualty detail), introduce the engineer and the audacious idea,
build the canal in specific numbers and specific methods (hawsers, capstans, submerged
saws — the reader can picture this), land the catch (too shallow for gunboats), pivot
immediately to the solution (Walke volunteers). The Carondelet run is the best sustained
narrative beat in the draft: the ship-as-floating-fortress-of-improvisation paragraph is
patient and specific in exactly the right way, and the payoff — 47 shots, 2 hits, both in
the barge — delivers the nerve-to-victory ratio the story is built on.

The Pittsburg run is handled correctly: brief (it's the second run, not the first), with the
right future-payoff note (Farragut, Porter). The "Meanwhile in New Orleans" aside earns its
place because it doesn't just name-drop — it shows the lesson being learned.

### Problems

**One flat passage: the transition between bombing failure and Bissell's idea.** "Foote was
going to need another idea, and the army handed him one out of the swamp" does the work but
too flatly — it's the connector sentence a first draft leaves in. The shift from failure to
genius deserves a beat more friction. The bombing "dashed" everything; Foote had no answer;
and then an obscure colonel with 600 soldiers and a look at the flooded Missouri lowland
proposed the strangest plan of the campaign. The reader should feel the gap between the two
ideas — massive naval bombardment versus one engineer sawing trees underwater — before the
pivot.

BEAT FIX: Add one sentence of Foote's position after the bombing verdict: something like
the ironclad captains looking at the batteries and seeing a problem they can't solve from
a safe distance. Then the army's answer arrives not as "the army handed him one" but as a
surprise from an unexpected quarter (the engineering regiment, not the gunboats). The
transition is already close; it just needs one more sentence of texture before Bissell's
name arrives.

**One minor voice slip:** "almost ridiculous piece of engineering" hedges. The Antietam
voice commits — "ridiculous" alone is bolder and accurate to the facts (the Confederates
thought it would fail; it didn't). The "almost" is editorial softening.

## Section 3: the-trap — NEEDS WORK

### Problems

**Stakes collapse at the opening.** The section begins: "Everything now happened fast. Pope
had spent five weeks assembling his pieces... and on April 7 he snapped them all shut at
once." This is a summary of a payoff, not the payoff itself. The Antietam bar for a closing
section is "Start with the cost, because the cost is the story." Island Number Ten's cost IS
the stunning asymmetry — 5,000 men surrendered for the price of a few dozen casualties — and
that revelation should drive the section's energy, not arrive as an afterthought at the end
of "April 7–8."

The section as written front-loads logistics (which transports, which landing, the road
south to Tiptonville) and back-loads the payoff. This inverts the natural dramatic
structure. The reader should feel the snap of the trap closing, then be given the numbers
that make it almost unbelievable.

BEAT FIX: Open the-trap with the numbers, not the logistics. "It took five weeks to set
up and about eighteen hours to finish. When Island Number Ten surrendered — flag lowered to
the river flotilla, garrison stacked their arms near Tiptonville — roughly 5,000 Confederate
soldiers went into prisoner-of-war camps in exchange for fewer than three dozen Union dead.
This is what a well-sprung trap costs." Then pull back and show how the pieces came
together — the crossing, the road cut, the race. The reader already knows the logistics;
they need to feel the stakes of the result first.

**Mackall is a ghost.** The Confederate commander — the man who inherits a lost position,
watches the trap close around him, and surrenders 5,000 men — gets one flat sentence:
"grasped what had happened, the door was already closing." The story's human dimension is
entirely on the Union side (Pope, Walke, Bissell). Mackall doesn't need a full portrait, but
he needs one moment: the moment he understands the crossing has happened and there is no
move left. Even one sentence of his position — new to command, holding a garrison whose
only escape road is already cut — gives the surrender emotional weight.

BEAT FIX: After "grasped what had happened," give Mackall a line of situation before his
men start moving. Something like: he had been in command nine days; the road south was the
only way out; by the time he knew the Union infantry was moving to cut it, it was already
cut. Then the retreat toward Tiptonville, the gunboats closing off the river, the 2 a.m.
surrender in the rain. The trap's snap is currently told from outside. Put the reader inside
the closing jaw for one paragraph.

**The prisoner-count murk section is well-handled but structurally misplaced.** The
discussion of whether it was 7,000 or 5,000 prisoners is correct (fact-pack accurate,
properly attributed to Pope's probable inflation) but it arrives at exactly the moment the
drama should be peaking — the surrender itself — and deflates the emotional landing. The
prize-measured-in-miles-of-river ending is strong and should come right after the surrender.
The prisoner arithmetic should be a subordinate clause or a brief parenthetical, not a
multi-sentence digression mid-climax.

BEAT FIX: Compress the prisoner-count hedging to one sentence: "Pope claimed 7,000 in
the bag; the real figure was probably 4,500 to 5,000, his numbers almost certainly padded."
Then move straight to the gun count and the cost line ("fewer than three dozen Union dead").
Let the asymmetry land without the accounting detour.

**Foote/Pope/aftermath paragraph is competent but rushes.** Pope's subsequent disaster at
Second Bull Run and Foote's death are handled in four sentences that pack too much into too
little space. These are the draft's only sketched-out character fates, and they arrive after
a longer digression on prisoner counts. Sequencing: cut the prisoner-count digression down,
give the character fates the four sentences they have, then land the Anaconda-closing kicker.
That order works.

**Ending is the second-best line in the draft.** "The Anaconda had its first real coil
around the Mississippi, and it would not let go until the whole river belonged to the United
States and the Confederacy was cut clean in two." Strong — it closes the ring back to the
opener without re-explaining it. Keep it.

## Cross-Cutting Notes

### Human dimension (overall)
The draft's strongest human moment is the Carondelet run — Walke's improvised fortress,
Hoel at the pilot wheel, the orders to wreck the engines. That section has the right DNA
(specific names doing specific things under specific pressure). Every other human element is
thinner. Pope exists as a logistician. Bissell gets no face — he notices something nobody
else noticed, but the reader doesn't feel the moment of recognition. The Confederate side
(McCown abandoning New Madrid in the rain; Mackall inheriting a doomed position) is almost
entirely absent. The storytelling challenge named in the brief — "make a low-casualty
engineering/siege story gripping" — is solved for Walke; it still needs solving for Bissell
and Mackall.

### Play-by-play tedium
Not a significant problem in this draft. The canal-building paragraph is the most
technically dense but earns its detail because the method IS the story. The April 7 crossing
paragraph (transport names, Watson's Landing) risks being a list, but stays just on the right
side by framing the move as the payoff of the whole campaign. No sustained stretches of
calendar-flipping.

### Voice
Mostly at or close to the Antietam bar. The italicized kicker device is used correctly
(the end of the Gibraltar section; the failed-bombardment verdict). One voice slip flagged
above ("almost ridiculous"). The meanwhile asides are handled correctly — they connect, they
don't just name-drop, and they don't overstay. The Pope telegram and canal quote are both
deployed well (short, sourced, not over-introduced).

### Pacing
the-gibraltar and the-canal move at the right pace — the-gibraltar a little leisurely
at the opening, but that is a hook problem, not a pacing problem. the-trap is paced
backwards: slow at the front (logistics), fast at the back (payoff), when it should be
the reverse.

1. **the-trap: restructure to open with the payoff asymmetry, not the logistics.** (Stakes
   collapse; this is the highest-impact fix in the draft.)
2. **the-trap: give Mackall one moment of interiority when the trap closes.** (Human
   dimension; one paragraph.)
3. **the-trap: compress prisoner-count hedging to one sentence, move it before the cost
   line.** (Structural; five minutes of editing.)
4. **the-gibraltar: invert the opener to lead with Columbus's embarrassing abandonment.**
   (Hook; moderate impact.)
5. **the-gibraltar: cut Halleck as a dead-end name-drop.** (Quick cut.)
6. **the-canal: add one sentence of transition texture before Bissell arrives.** (Minor;
   doesn't change the grade.)
7. **the-canal: change "almost ridiculous" to "ridiculous."** (Voice micro-fix.)

Items 1–3 are required (the-trap grade is NEEDS WORK). Items 4–7 are improvements on an
already-passing draft.
