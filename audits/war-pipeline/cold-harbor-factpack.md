## 1. Identity / Date / Location / Theatre

- **Name:** **Battle of Cold Harbor.** The name is the gut of the section's first hook — there
  is **no harbor**. "Cold Harbor" was the name of a **rural crossroads**, after an old **tavern**:
  a "cold harbour" was period English for an inn that offered **shelter (a "harbor") but no hot
  meal**. There were two crossroads — **Old Cold Harbor** (about **two miles east of Gaines's
  Mill**) and **New Cold Harbor** (about a mile southeast). **[WIKI] [ABT]** *(The "Burnett
  family tavern" attribution to the name appears in [ABT]; [VERIFY] the family name before
  printing it, but the no-harbor / shelter-not-hot-meal explanation is well-attested.)*
- **Dates:** **May 31 – June 12, 1864** (the engagement spans ~12 days; the **climactic frontal
  assault is the dawn of June 3**). **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Location:** **Hanover County, Virginia**, near Mechanicsville, **about 10 miles northeast of
  Richmond.** Coordinates ~37°35′N 77°17′W. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Theatre:** **Eastern Theater.** The **bloody culmination of Grant's Overland Campaign**
  (May–June 1864) — the last great battle before the war settled into the Petersburg siege. **[WIKI]**
- **Result:** **Confederate victory.** The June 3 Union assault failed utterly to break Lee's
  line. **[WIKI] [ABT]**

### Why Cold Harbor mattered — the climax of the Overland Campaign (strategic spine)
- Cold Harbor was the **last stop in Grant's relentless month-long drive on Richmond**. Since
  crossing the Rapidan in early May, the Army of the Potomac had fought Lee at the **Wilderness
  (May 5–7), Spotsylvania (May 8–21), and the North Anna (May 23–26)** — a campaign of
  *continuous contact*, Grant sliding left around Lee's flank again and again rather than
  retreating after each bloody draw. **[WIKI] [ABT]** *(Those prior battles are prelude context;
  full facts are out of scope of this pack — reference lightly.)*
- The ground itself was old killing ground: **Cold Harbor sits beside the 1862 Gaines's Mill
  battlefield** (Lee's victory in the Seven Days). Both armies were fighting **on the doorstep
  of Richmond**, the Confederate capital. **[WIKI]**
- **What made it a slaughter:** by June 1864 the armies had learned that **entrenched troops with
  rifles could shred any frontal assault.** Lee's men dug **elaborate fieldworks** at Cold Harbor;
  Grant ordered a **frontal assault straight into them.** The battle is widely cited as a key
  **foreshadowing of World War I trench warfare** — fortified lines, the futility of the charge,
  the long stalemate that followed. **[ABT] [ECWC]**

## 3. Casualties (both sides; scholarly range — HANDLE THE LEGEND CAREFULLY)

> **The single most important discipline in this pack.** The popular line — *"7,000 Union dead
> and wounded in the first 20 minutes (or first hour) of June 3"* — is **inflated and contested.**
> Modern scholarship (Gordon Rhea) revises it **sharply downward.** The author must give the
> **range and the historiographic caveat**, and must NOT print the legend's number as fact.

**Whole battle (May 31 – June 12) — well-agreed:**
- **Union (North): ~12,738 total** (≈1,845 killed; ≈9,077 wounded; ≈1,816 captured/missing).
  Round to **~12,000–13,000** in prose. **[WIKI]**
  *(ABT lists a near-identical Union total ~12,737. The two agree; use ~12,000–13,000.)*
- **Confederate (South): ~4,500–5,300 total.** **[WIKI]** gives ~5,287 (≈788 killed; ≈3,376
  wounded; ≈1,123 captured/missing); **[ABT]** lists ~4,595. Use the **range ~4,500–5,300.**
  *([VERIFY] which total the fact-checker prefers; the disproportion — roughly 2-to-3× the
  Confederate loss — is the point, not the exact figure.)*

**The June 3 dawn assault specifically — DO NOT print the legend:**
- **The legend:** ~**7,000 Union casualties in the first 20–30 minutes** (some versions say "first
  10 minutes," "8 minutes," or "first hour"). Repeated by many popular writers and even some
  historians. **[ABT]** itself prints *"an estimated 7,000 men are killed or wounded within the
  first 30 minutes."* — **flag this as the contested popular figure, not settled fact.**
- **Modern scholarship (Rhea):** the grand assault **"belongs more to the dustbin of Civil War
  mythology than to real history"** — there was **no single massive line-wide onslaught**; the
  attack **sputtered for about an hour, not eight minutes**; some sectors saw appalling slaughter
  while along much of the line losses were light (many Confederates didn't realize an attack had
  even been made). **[RHEA]** Rhea's accounting:
  - **June 3 morning assault:** roughly **3,500–4,000 Union** killed/wounded/missing
    (he breaks it down as ~1,500 in Smith's XVIII Corps, up to ~2,500 in Hancock's II Corps,
    ~600 in Wright's VI Corps, plus ~1,200 in the northern sector). **[RHEA]**
  - **Entire day of June 3:** about **~6,000 Union** vs. **~1,000–1,500 Confederate.** **[RHEA]**
- **Author guidance:** write something like *"the June 3 assault cost roughly 3,500 to 7,000 Union
  casualties — for a century the figure was given as 7,000 in the first half-hour, but modern
  scholarship puts the morning assault nearer 3,500–4,000 and the whole day around 6,000"* — and
  name the disproportion (Confederate loss June 3 ≈ 1,000–1,500). **NEVER print "7,000 in 20
  minutes" as plain fact.** This is the WWI-foreshadowing image, but the legend has been corrected.

**The campaign-to-date number (the "butcher" backdrop):**
- **Overland Campaign cumulative Union casualties (Wilderness → Cold Harbor): ~50,000–52,000.**
  **[WIKI] [ABT]** (Wikipedia: "over 52,000"; ABT: "some 50,000 cumulative casualties.")
- **Confederate cumulative (same span): ~33,000.** **[WIKI]**
- This ~50,000 figure in a single month — culminating in the Cold Harbor repulse — is what earned
  Grant the **"Butcher" label in the Northern press.** **[ABT]** *(The disproportion in numbers
  was smaller in percentage terms for Lee, who could not replace his losses — author may note this
  as the strategic counter-argument: Grant could absorb the arithmetic; Lee could not.)*

## 4. Geography (for map accuracy)

Relative positions (**north = up**):

- **The crossroads** is the literal objective: **Old Cold Harbor** (the key junction Sheridan's
  cavalry seized May 31) and **New Cold Harbor** about a mile southeast. Several roads meet here;
  the road net is why both armies raced for it. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Gaines's Mill** lies **about two miles west** of Old Cold Harbor (the 1862 battlefield —
  useful as a labeled landmark, not the 1864 objective). **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Richmond** is **~10 miles southwest** (the strategic prize both armies orient on). **[WIKI]**
- **The Chickahominy River** runs **south/southeast of the battlefield**, between the lines and
  Richmond; it is the river Grant's army would have to cross — and the river the armies fought
  with their backs to in 1862. A map should show it as a **swampy line to the S/SE.** **[WIKI]**
  *(The Totopotomoy Creek lies to the north — the prior days' fighting drifted south from there to
  Cold Harbor; [VERIFY] before labeling Totopotomoy on a Cold Harbor map.)*
- **The entrenched lines (the heart of the map):** Lee's **Army of Northern Virginia dug an
  elaborate, roughly 7-mile-long line of fieldworks** facing the Union, running roughly
  **north–south**, anchored to take advantage of swamps and ravines. The Union line faced it a few
  hundred yards off. **[ABT] [ECWC]** *(The "7 miles" length is widely cited [ABT]; treat as
  approximate. The works were laid with overlapping fields of fire — the enfilade pockets that
  did the killing on June 3.)*
- **The June 3 assault axes:** the three assaulting corps were arrayed **north to south as Smith
  (XVIII) — Wright (VI) — Hancock (II)**, attacking **westward into the Confederate line.** Arrows
  point **west**, into the works. Smith's men funneled into a **ravine/pocket** raked by **enfilade
  (flanking) fire** — the deadliest sector. Hancock's (south end) achieved the only lodgment, into
  **Breckinridge's** front, before being thrown out. Warren (V) and Burnside (IX) were **farther
  north**, making separate, unsuccessful attacks. **[WIKI] [ECWC]**
- **Map orientation summary:** crossroads + entrenched lines center, lines running **N–S**;
  Confederate works on the **west**, Union assault arrows striking **west** into them (Smith N,
  Wright center, Hancock S; Warren/Burnside farther N). **Gaines's Mill** label to the **W**,
  **Richmond** off-map to the **SW (~10 mi)**, the **Chickahominy** as a swampy line to the **S/SE**
  (the river Grant later crosses toward the James). North = up.

## 5. Sequence & turning points

**Setup:** After the inconclusive bloodbaths of the Wilderness, Spotsylvania, and the North Anna,
Grant **(North)** slid southeast yet again, and both armies raced for the **Old Cold Harbor
crossroads** — the road junction that controlled the approach to Richmond. **[WIKI] [ABT]**

### May 31 – June 2 — the race and the first assaults
- **May 31, ~4 p.m. — Sheridan's cavalry (North) seizes the Old Cold Harbor crossroads** and holds
  it against Confederate pressure until infantry arrives. **[WIKI]**
- **June 1, ~9 a.m. — Wright's VI Corps (North) arrives**; **Smith's XVIII Corps (North) comes up
  in the afternoon.** **[WIKI]**
- **June 1, ~6:30 p.m. — Wright and Smith (North) assault** the forming Confederate line, making
  some gains but no breakthrough; the fighting is sharp. **[WIKI]**
- **June 2 — both sides entrench.** A planned Union attack is postponed (Hancock's II Corps is late
  arriving after a grueling night march). **Lee's men use the day to complete their elaborate
  fieldworks** — the delay that makes June 3 a slaughter. **[WIKI] [ECWC]** This is a quiet
  **turning point**: every hour of June 2 deepened the trench the Federals would charge into.

### June 3 — the dawn assault (the climax)
- **~4:30 a.m. — the grand assault opens.** Three corps step off in **thick ground fog** — **Smith
  (XVIII, north), Wright (VI, center), Hancock (II, south).** **[WIKI] [ECWC]**
- **Hancock's II Corps (North)** briefly **breaks into the Confederate line** (Breckinridge's
  (South) front) — the only real lodgment — but is **quickly thrown back with heavy loss.** **[ECWC]**
- **Smith's XVIII Corps (North)** marches into a **ravine/pocket** swept by **enfilade musket and
  artillery fire** and is **butchered** — the deadliest sector of the field. **[ECWC]**
- **Wright's VI Corps (North)** advances a short distance and **stops to dig in** rather than press
  into the fire. **[ECWC]**
- **Later in the morning — Warren (V) and Burnside (IX) (North)** make **disjointed attacks in the
  northern sector** and gain nothing. **[ECWC]**
- **The assault is broken in roughly an hour** (NOT the "eight minutes" of legend — see §3); by
  **~noon / 12:30 p.m. Grant calls it off**, judging it a failure. **[WIKI] [RHEA]** *(There is a
  widely repeated story that when ordered to renew the attack, the men simply lay still and refused
  to rise — a soldiers'-vote-with-their-feet image; [VERIFY] before printing as fact, it's
  often-told but sourcing varies.)*

### June 4 – June 12 — the trenches, the wounded, the disengagement
- The armies settle into **trench warfare** — lines a few hundred yards apart, constant
  sharpshooting, men unable to lift their heads by day. The **WWI-foreshadowing stalemate.** **[ABT] [ECWC]**
- **The wounded between the lines (see §6):** hundreds of Union wounded lie in the no-man's-land in
  front of the works. **Grant and Lee trade notes from June 5 to June 7** over a truce to recover
  them; the negotiation **stalls** (Grant reluctant to request a formal ceasefire that would read as
  conceding defeat). By the time a truce is arranged, **nearly all the wounded have died of
  exposure, thirst, and their wounds.** **[WIKI] [GRANT-MEM]**
- **June 12 — Grant disengages.** Under cover of night the Army of the Potomac slips out of its
  trenches to begin the move **southeast across the Chickahominy and toward the James River** (see
  §9). **[WIKI] [ABT]**

## 6. Human cost — named people / vivid documented detail

- **The coat-pinning story (iconic but single-sourced — handle as in §10):** Grant's aide
  **Lt. Col. Horace Porter (North)** wrote that the night before June 3 he saw veteran soldiers
  **"calmly writing their names and home addresses on slips of paper, and pinning them on the backs
  of their coats, so that their dead bodies might be recognized upon the field, and their fate made
  known to their families at home."** **[PORTER]** It is the war's most haunting image of men who
  *knew* the attack was hopeless. **BUT:** Porter's postwar memoir is the **only** source — no
  contemporary letter, diary, or newspaper corroborates it; Rhea and others note soldiers did
  pin/sew identification at **Mine Run (Nov 1863)**, so the practice was real, but the specific
  Cold Harbor scene rests on Porter alone. **[RHEA]** **Author: present it as Porter's account,
  flagged "possibly apocryphal," never as undisputed fact.**
- **The wounded left to die between the lines:** the failure of the truce negotiations meant
  hundreds of Union wounded lay in front of the works for days. An artillery officer's later
  description (quoted via [WIKI]): **"Every corpse I saw was as black as coal ... The wounded must
  have suffered horribly."** **[WIKI] — [VERIFY]** exact wording/attribution before quoting verbatim.
- **Grant's own accounting:** in his memoir Grant recorded that **"there were many dead and wounded
  men between the lines of the contending forces"** who could not be reached, and that by the time
  parties went out **all but two had died.** **[GRANT-MEM] — [VERIFY]** the "all but two" exact
  phrasing before printing it as a quotation (paraphrase is safe).
- **The scale of the killing** is the human fact: ~**12,000–13,000 Union casualties** over the
  battle, the bulk of the June 3 dead falling in the **first hour in a few fire-swept pockets** —
  the most one-sided slaughter the Army of the Potomac had suffered since **Marye's Heights at
  Fredericksburg (Dec 1862)**. **[WIKI]**

## 8. THE CAUSE — SLAVERY (ship-blocking) + the concrete hook

> **NAME THE CAUSE (locked, ship-blocking).** The section MUST, in its own prose, name **slavery**
> as the thing the war — and each side — was ultimately fought over. Omission is the default Lost
> Cause failure; "Grant the butcher" / "a tragic, pointless slaughter" framing **alone** fails the
> gate. The slaughter has to be anchored to what the men were dying *for*.

### The documented throughline
- The Confederacy fought to **preserve and extend slavery**; Lee's Army of Northern Virginia was
  defending Richmond — the capital of a slaveholding republic — and behind those Cold Harbor
  trenches, **enslaved laborers were being put to work digging Confederate fieldworks** even as
  free Black men in Union blue prepared to fight a few miles away. **[NPS-USCT]**
- Grant's whole grinding campaign had a war aim beyond capturing a city: **destroy the army that
  defended slavery.** The arithmetic the Northern press called "butchery" was, strategically, the
  pressure that would end the war — and the war, by 1864, was explicitly a war **to end slavery**
  (the Emancipation Proclamation was 16 months old; Black regiments were now in the field).

### STRONG CONCRETE HOOK — the USCT in the very pivot Cold Harbor sets up **[NPS-USCT]**
The cleanest, documented hook is the **next move** Cold Harbor forces — the swing on Petersburg —
because **United States Colored Troops were in the lead of it**, fighting for their own freedom:
- The **Overland Campaign already had Black soldiers in it** (e.g., the **23rd USCT** served in the
  campaign May–June 1864). When Grant pivoted to Petersburg, the assault was **spearheaded by a
  division of United States Colored Troops** under **Brig. Gen. Edward Hinks (North)** — roughly
  **3,500 men, many of them formerly enslaved.** **[NPS-USCT]**
- **June 15, 1864 — the opening assault on Petersburg:** USCT regiments (1st, 4th, 5th, 6th, 10th,
  22nd USCT) **overran Confederate works and captured artillery and a string of batteries** —
  Battery 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 — in a charge that crossed nearly three-quarters of a mile under fire.
  The **4th USCT took ~120 casualties at Baylor's Farm** that morning. **[NPS-USCT]** Men who had
  been *property* now took fortified guns from the army that had enslaved them.
- **The point:** the trench at Cold Harbor where thousands of white Union soldiers died, and the
  trench at Petersburg where Black Union soldiers stormed the works days later, are **the same war**
  — a war to break a slave society. The slaughter at Cold Harbor was a step on the road to the
  Petersburg siege that would end the Confederacy and, with it, slavery. *([VERIFY] the exact USCT
  regiment list and battery numbers against [NPS-USCT] before the author prints specifics; the
  load-bearing fact — a USCT division led the Petersburg assault Cold Harbor pivoted into — is solid.)*

**Author directive:** name slavery explicitly as the war's cause where the section sets its stakes;
use the USCT-at-Petersburg hook to make it concrete and forward-looking — Cold Harbor's dead and the
Black soldiers who stormed Petersburg's works are two faces of the same war aim. This satisfies the
NAME-THE-CAUSE gate with a real, documented hook, not a throat-clearing line.

## 9. Significance

- **Tactically a disaster, strategically a hinge.** The June 3 assault gained **nothing** and is
  Grant's most-criticized order — but it ended the futile phase of the campaign and forced the
  decisive move. **[ABT] [GRANT-MEM]**
- **It ended the war of maneuver and began the war of siege.** After Cold Harbor, Grant **broke off,
  disengaged on June 12, and executed the stealth crossing of the James River** — one of the most
  celebrated maneuvers of the war. Engineers laid a **~2,200-foot pontoon bridge** (Weyanoke to
  Windmill Point), and **over 100,000 men plus thousands of wagons, horses, mules, and cattle crossed
  the James largely undetected by Lee** by June 17. **[BTC-JAMES]** *(The 2,200-ft span and the
  ">100,000 men" crossing figures are from [BTC-JAMES]; treat the bridge length as approximate and
  [VERIFY] before printing exact.)*
- **It pivoted the war into the Petersburg siege.** Grant swung on **Petersburg**, the rail junction
  south of Richmond that fed the capital; the assault there (June 15–18) opened the **Siege of
  Petersburg (June 1864 – April 1865)**, which **ended with Lee's surrender at Appomattox in April
  1865.** Cold Harbor is the last open-field battle before the long entrenched endgame. **[WIKI] [BTC-JAMES]**
- **It cemented Grant's "Butcher" reputation in the North** — ~50,000 casualties in a month,
  capped by a pointless slaughter — even as it set up the maneuver that would win the war. The
  section's irony is that the most criticized day produced the move that doomed the Confederacy. **[ABT]**
