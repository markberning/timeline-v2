## Section-by-section verdict

### Opening + "The bullet that changed the math"
**CLEAR** overall. The Napoleonic setup is vivid and works for a newcomer. The smoothbore
explanation is genuinely good — the football-spiral analogy for rifling lands immediately.
The Minié ball mechanism (loose going in, tight going out) is the best explanation in the
whole draft. No complaints here.

Minor flags below.

### "What the bullet did to a body"
**CLEAR.** Terminal ballistics passage is brutal and concrete. "Lethality index" is a
borrowed term from a DoD study and needs one-word parenthetical ("a Pentagon scoring system
for weapon deadliness") — otherwise a 15-year-old reads it as jargon.

### "The myth gets a second look"
**NEEDS-GLOSS** in one spot. The two-killing-zones / parabolic arc argument is the hardest
passage in the draft. The reader is asked to visualize a bullet arc, a "safe gap in the
middle," and a second lethal zone beyond — in three sentences, without a diagram. Most
readers will follow it, but "the flatter arc of a smoothbore had no such gap" is the
sentence that loses people: the prose has not said yet that the arc of a rifled bullet is
*steeper* than a smoothbore's, it just said it loops. The mechanism needs one connecting
sentence: something like "because the rifled bullet left the barrel at a slower velocity
than a flat-flying smoothbore ball, its arc was higher and more pronounced." Without that,
the cause-effect is an unexplained leap.

### "The war the railroads decided"
**CLEAR.** The gauge explanation (different-width tracks = trains can't cross) is textbook-
clean. The Chattanooga transfer numbers are concrete. One undefined term: "railheads" (the
end of the track, where the train stops and goods are unloaded) — used twice without gloss.
Fixable with a four-word parenthetical.

### "The war run by wire"
**CLEAR.** No jargon problems. The Lincoln patent detail is charming and lands. The balloon
corps telegraph splice is vivid.

### "The weapon that came too slowly"
**NEEDS-GLOSS** in two spots. "Breech-loader" is defined in the draft — good. But "muzzle"
itself is never defined; the draft uses "muzzle-loading" and "down the muzzle" assuming the
reader knows a muzzle is the front opening of a barrel. That is not a safe assumption for a
15-year-old who may have never held a gun. One line fixes it.

"Metallic cartridges" is used once to explain why Ripley feared the logistics — a 15-year-
old doesn't know this is different from separate powder + ball. One phrase ("the self-
contained brass-and-lead rounds the repeaters needed, versus the loose powder and bullet a
musket used separately") would close it.

### "The men who dug"
**CLEAR.** "Rifle pits" is defined in parentheses — well done. The Petersburg foreshadowing
of WWI is written accessibly.

### "Everything else that was new"
**CLEAR.** Photography, balloons, mines all land cleanly. "Subterra shell" is
immediately followed by a definition, which is exactly right.

One flag: "artillery shell" is used in passing to describe the land mine, but "artillery" is
never defined anywhere in the draft. In context ("an artillery shell buried in the ground")
a newcomer can infer it means a large explosive, so this is a SHOULD-FIX, not a MUST-FIX.

### "Was it really the first modern war?"
**CLEAR.** Honest, well-argued, the framing of contested vs. settled works.

## Unexplained mechanism claims (leap audit)

1. **WHY a rifled barrel shoots farther** — The draft explains HOW rifling works (spin =
   stability), but not WHY a spinning bullet flies farther/straighter at the physics level.
   For most readers the football analogy bridges it sufficiently. SHOULD-FIX, not MUST-FIX.

2. **WHY the rifled arc is higher/more pronounced than a smoothbore arc** — This is the
   MUST-FIX mechanism gap. The Hess two-killing-zones argument rests on it. The draft says
   "a smoothbore had no such gap" but does not say why — the reader must independently know
   that a rifled bullet, while more accurate, was also slower (lower muzzle velocity) than
   a smoothbore ball, giving it a more pronounced arc. Without that sentence the cause-
   effect is invisible.

3. **WHY the defense favors the rifle over the offense** — The draft states the conclusion
   ("marching across the open toward his line is no longer brave, it is suicide") and the
   reader can follow it, but the underlying logic (defender is stationary, can take aim;
   attacker is moving, exposed for the entire 300-yard crossing that used to take 30 seconds
   now takes minutes of running) is implied, not stated. SHOULD-FIX — a single sentence
   would sharpen it significantly.

## Overwhelming passage audit

**The Hess two-killing-zones paragraph (§"The myth gets a second look")** is the single
most information-dense passage in the draft: it delivers four reasons in rapid succession
(training, habit, terrain, arc) and the fourth reason (the arc) requires the reader to
simultaneously visualize a bullet's flight path, understand that there is a "safe gap in
the middle," and then accept "the flatter arc of a smoothbore had no such gap" — all
without any diagram or connecting bridge explaining the arc difference. This is the
worst comprehension breaker in the draft. It is close to working — the mechanism is
almost there — but the missing physics link (why the rifled bullet's arc was more
pronounced) drops the reader right as the argument pays off.

No other passage rises to the LOST level. The sections on railroads and telegraphs are the
clearest in the draft.

## MUST-FIX

1. **"muzzle" undefined** — "muzzle-loading" and "down the muzzle" appear in §"The weapon
   that came too slowly" before any explanation that the muzzle is the front opening of the
   barrel. A 15-year-old who has never handled a firearm is lost.
   > Quote: "A muzzle-loading rifled musket, the Springfield or the Enfield, had to be
   > reloaded from the front after every single shot"
   Gloss needed: add "(the muzzle is the open front end of the barrel)" on first use of
   "muzzle," or rewrite the opening of that sentence: "A front-loading (muzzle-loading)
   rifled musket..."

2. **WHY the rifled bullet's arc is higher/more pronounced than a smoothbore's (the Hess
   two-killing-zones argument)** — The mechanism is the whole point of the revisionist
   correction, and the cause is never stated. The reader is told there is a safe gap in the
   middle of the arc but not why the rifled bullet arcs more steeply.
   > Quote: "The flatter arc of a smoothbore had no such gap."
   Gloss needed: one sentence before this explaining that the rifled bullet, while more
   accurate, left the barrel at a lower muzzle velocity than a smoothbore ball and therefore
   rose higher and fell further in a more pronounced arc. Without it the mechanism is an
   unexplained leap.

3. **"ordnance" / "Chief of Ordnance" undefined** — Used twice in §"The weapon that came
   too slowly" with no gloss. A 15-year-old does not know "ordnance" means military weapons
   and the department responsible for procuring them.
   > Quote: "Brigadier General James Wolfe Ripley (US/Union), the Union's Chief of Ordnance
   > from 1861 to 1863"
   Gloss needed: add parenthetical "(the army's head of weapons procurement)" or similar.

4. **"railheads" undefined** — Used twice in §"The war the railroads decided" with no gloss.
   > Quote: "armies were tethered to the railheads that fed them, the way a diver is
   > tethered to an air hose"
   Gloss needed: "(railheads — the end of the track, where the train stops and supplies are
   unloaded)" on first use. The diver-air-hose simile partially saves it, but the word itself
   is jargon.

5. **"musket" undefined on first use** — Used in the opening paragraph before any definition.
   The draft later defines the smoothbore musket thoroughly, but the word "musket" appears
   as a known quantity before the reader has been told what it is.
   > Quote: "the armies that formed up to fight the Civil War carried a way of fighting
   > that was already old... the weapon a foot soldier carried"
   The word "musket" first appears in the section header and in the second paragraph. A
   one-phrase gloss (e.g., "a musket — the standard shoulder-fired infantry weapon of the
   era") on its first use would close this. The full smoothbore explanation covers it
   retroactively, but first impressions matter.

## SHOULD-FIX

1. **"lethality index" opaque** — Reads as technical jargon from a DoD study. Add a brief
   parenthetical: "(a Pentagon-style numerical score for how many casualties a weapon
   produces per unit of time in combat)."
   > Quote: "a U.S. Department of Defense weapons study done a century later assigned the
   > rifle-musket a 'lethality index' of 154"

2. **"metallic cartridges" never distinguished from loose powder + ball** — Ripley's
   logistics objection is about a genuinely new supply chain, but the reader doesn't know
   what makes a metallic cartridge different.
   > Quote: "the logistical headache of manufacturing and shipping special metallic
   > cartridges"
   Fix: add "(the self-contained brass cases with powder and bullet pre-loaded, very
   different from the loose powder and separate ball a musket used)."

3. **WHY the defense favors a rifle over the offense** — Conclusion stated but mechanism
   only implied. A single sentence (defender stationary, can aim carefully; attacker
   exposed for the entire crossing that is now several minutes, not seconds) would sharpen
   the argument significantly.
   > Quote: "marching shoulder to shoulder across an open field toward his line is no
   > longer brave, it is suicide"

4. **Henri-Gustave Delvigne's nationality implied but not stated** — "his countryman"
   implies French but does not say it.
   > Quote: "building on the earlier work of his countryman Henri-Gustave Delvigne"
   Fix: "(Henri-Gustave Delvigne, also French)" or add "(French)" after the name.

5. **WHY a spinning bullet flies farther/straighter (physics gap)** — The football-spiral
   analogy is good, but the sentence "a spinning projectile flies straight and far instead
   of tumbling" is an assertion without a cause. A single bridging clause ("because spin
   gyroscopically stabilizes the bullet's nose-forward orientation, preventing the tumbling
   that makes a smoothbore ball veer") would close it for curious readers without slowing
   the prose.
   > Quote: "a spinning projectile flies straight and far instead of tumbling"

6. **"artillery" undefined** — Used in passing in §"Everything else" when describing the
   subterra shell.
   > Quote: "an artillery shell buried in the ground and rigged to fire when stepped on"
   Fix: "a large explosive artillery shell (the kind fired from a cannon)" — one phrase.
