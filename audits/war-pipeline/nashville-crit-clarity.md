# Nashville — NEWCOMER / CLARITY critic gate

> War content pipeline, step 4 (critic gate, CLARITY). Cold read as a zero-knowledge,
> sharp 15-year-old who has never taken a history class on the Civil War. Flags:
> undefined terms, unexplained leaps, named actors without a side-tag or scene-set,
> passages that overwhelm. CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST. MUST-FIX vs SHOULD-FIX.
> Never demands invented facts — where a fact is missing, it's flagged for the fact pack.

**VERDICT: PASS WITH REVISIONS.** Unusually clean for a battle draft — nearly every piece
of jargon is inline-defined on first use (feint, grand right wheel, redoubts, abatis,
enfilade, salient, military crest, parapet, rout are all glossed where they appear). The
reader can follow the whole thing. The real gaps are (1) a few terms used a beat *before*
they're defined, (2) two important named Confederate corps commanders (Stewart, Cheatham,
Stephen D. Lee) who never appear at all, so "Hood's left/right" floats without a face, and
(3) one geography assumption (which side of the field is "left" vs "right") that a newcomer
will lose track of. No LOST passages. One MUST-FIX (the left/right orientation), rest
SHOULD-FIX.

## TERMS — glossing audit

**CLEAR (well-defined inline on first use — keep as-is):**
- **feint** — "a deliberately loud attack meant not to break through but to fool Hood" (§2),
  and again "the false punch meant to grab Hood's attention" (§3). Defined twice, clearly.
- **grand right wheel** — "imagine a door swinging shut, hinged at the center, the far edge
  sweeping in a wide arc" (§2). Excellent, concrete gloss. The swinging-door image is reused
  in §3 so it stays anchored.
- **redoubts** — "small standalone earthwork forts, each mounting two to four cannon and
  held by roughly 150 men" (§3). CLEAR.
- **abatis** — "felled trees with sharpened branches faced outward, a barricade you have to
  climb through under fire" (§4). CLEAR.
- **enfilade / enfilading fire** — "raking down the length of a crowded line, which is the
  deadliest fire there is" (§4). CLEAR and the "deadliest fire there is" tells the reader
  why it matters.
- **salient** — "a bulge sticking out from the line — so it could be fired into from nearly
  every direction at once" (§5). CLEAR.
- **military crest vs geographical crest** — "the literal highest point... rather than... the
  slightly lower line from which you can actually see and shoot the whole slope" (§5). This
  is a genuinely tricky concept and the draft nails it.
- **rout** — "the last major Confederate field army in the West dissolving into a flood of
  men streaming south" (§5). CLEAR.
- **parapet** — "the protective top wall of the works" (§4). CLEAR.
- **USCT / U.S. Colored Troops** — "the Union army's Black regiments... Black men, free and
  formerly enslaved, in the uniform of the United States" (§4). CLEAR.
- **dismounted cavalry** — "on foot like infantry, the way Civil War cavalry usually fought
  a stand-up battle" (§3). CLEAR — good, because a newcomer assumes cavalry = always on
  horses.

**NEEDS-GLOSS (term used before, or without, definition):**

1. **"the Rock of Chickamauga" / Chickamauga (§2) — NEEDS-GLOSS, SHOULD-FIX.** The draft
   does explain the nickname well ("held a hill with a stubborn rear-guard stand that saved
   the army from total destruction... 'standing like a rock'"). But **Chickamauga itself** is
   dropped as a known place/event ("At Chickamauga in September 1863, when the rest of the
   Union army was routed"). A newcomer doesn't know Chickamauga was a major Union defeat.
   The sentence *implies* it (the army was routed), so this is borderline CLEAR — but a
   three-word gloss ("a costly Union defeat in north Georgia") would seal it. SHOULD-FIX.

2. **"the works" / "field works" (§1, used throughout) — NEEDS-GLOSS, SHOULD-FIX.** §1 says
   "heavy field works — dug-in lines and forts," which is a fine gloss — but it's tucked in
   a parenthetical mid-sentence and the bare word "works" then recurs many times ("storm
   works like that," "took works on that flank," "the strongest works on the entire line,"
   "on the works"). The first gloss covers it, but a newcomer may briefly stumble on "works"
   as a noun meaning fortifications. The §1 gloss is *probably* enough; SHOULD-FIX only if
   tightening — make the first gloss a hair more prominent.

3. **"corps" / "IV Corps," "XXIII Corps" (§3) — NEEDS-GLOSS, SHOULD-FIX.** The draft uses
   "XXIII Corps," "IV Corps" without ever telling a newcomer that a corps is a large chunk
   of an army (tens of thousands of men). A 15-year-old reads "IV Corps" as a code. The
   prose mostly survives because it pairs them with named generals and a *role* ("held in
   reserve," "pressed forward"), so the reader gets the gist. SHOULD-FIX: one early gloss
   ("a corps — a wing of the army, tens of thousands of men") the first time a corps is
   named would help, but the draft is readable without it.

4. **"effectives" (§5) — NEEDS-GLOSS, SHOULD-FIX.** "Hood reported barely 18,742 effectives
   left" and "fewer than 15,000 men." A newcomer can infer "effectives" ≈ able-bodied
   soldiers, especially next to "15,000 men," but it's an unglossed military term. One word
   ("able-bodied soldiers still able to fight") fixes it. SHOULD-FIX.

5. **"detached" (§1 Forrest, §3) — borderline CLEAR.** "had been detached toward
   Murfreesboro... to raid the railroad" — context carries it (sent away on an errand). Fine.

## NAMED ACTORS — side-tag + scene-set audit

**CLEAR — every named person gets a (North)/(South) tag on first mention per section** (the
draft confirms this in its own ledger and it checks out on a cold read). Side-tagging is not
the problem. The problem is a few people who are *named without being placed*, and several
who are *missing entirely*:

6. **MUST-FIX — the Confederate corps commanders are absent, so "Hood's left/right" has no
   one in it.** The whole battle is described as blows against "Hood's left" and "Hood's
   right," but the draft never names **who held those wings.** A newcomer keeps reading
   "Hood's left collapsed" / "the Confederate right" as abstract compass directions with no
   human owner. The fact pack carries the three corps commanders — **Stewart (South)** on
   the left (where the army broke), **Cheatham (South)** on the right (Overton Hill), and
   **Stephen D. Lee (South)** in the center. **At minimum, naming Stewart (whose left is the
   focus of §3's collapse and §5's break) and Cheatham (whose right is Overton Hill in §4)
   would give the geometry a face.** This is the single biggest clarity gap: the Union side
   has a full cast of named generals doing specific things, while the Confederate defense is
   a faceless line. *(Fact-pack note: these names ARE in the pack §2 — using them is not
   invention. Flagging for the author, not requesting new facts.)* MUST-FIX.

7. **NEEDS-GLOSS — "Hood's left" vs "the Union right": which side of the map is which?**
   (§2 MEANWHILE, §3, §5.) The draft says the feint is "On the Union left (Hood's right, to
   the east)" — good, it gives the compass bearing. But then the main blow is "the Union
   right... pivoting down onto Hood's weak left flank," and a newcomer has to hold in their
   head that *the Union's right is opposite the Confederate's left* because the two armies
   face each other. The draft does say "(Hood's right, to the east)" once, which is the key
   anchor. SHOULD-FIX: the one place it could be lost is §3, where "the far Union right" and
   "Hood's left" are used in the same breath without re-stating they're the *same end of the
   field* (the west). One reminder — "the Union right (the western end of the field, opposite
   Hood's left)" — would lock it. Because §6 (naming Stewart on the left) and this are the
   same orientation problem, fixing #6 mostly fixes this too.

8. **"the Army of Tennessee" (§1) — CLEAR.** Well-defined: "the Confederacy's main fighting
   force in the western half of the war." Good — and important, because a newcomer would
   otherwise assume "Army of Tennessee" = a Tennessee (i.e. possibly Union) army. The draft
   doesn't explicitly flag that it's *Confederate* in that first sentence, but "the
   Confederacy's main fighting force" does the job. CLEAR.

9. **"the Army of the Cumberland" — not named in the draft (fine).** The fact pack gives
   Thomas the Army of the Cumberland; the draft omits it and just calls it "his army" /
   "the army." Correct simplification for a newcomer — no fix needed.

10. **Schofield, Wood, Smith, Wilson, McArthur (§3, §5) — CLEAR but dense.** Each is
    side-tagged and given a role. The §3 paragraph routes four of them through quickly (see
    storytelling note on the traffic jam) but a clarity read survives it because each name
    has a verb attached. Wood's "IV Corps pressed forward up the Hillsboro Pike axis toward
    Montgomery Hill" is the one place a newcomer's eyes may glaze — three proper nouns (corps
    number, pike, hill) in one clause. SHOULD-FIX: simplify to the role ("pressed up the
    center").

11. **Logan (§2) — CLEAR.** "Maj. Gen. John A. Logan (North) to Nashville to take command"
    — the reader immediately understands he's the replacement. Good.

12. **Beauregard (§5) — CLEAR.** "surveying the wreck" places him; side-tagged. Fine that
    he's introduced late and briefly.

## UNEXPLAINED LEAPS / geography

13. **Nashville-as-fortress geography — CLEAR.** §1 establishes the 7-mile arc of forts, the
    Cumberland River guarding the north/east, Hood's 4-mile line to the south. A newcomer can
    build the picture: city + forts at top, river behind it, Hood's thin arc below. Good.

14. **The two hills (Shy's / Overton) — CLEAR but watch the aliases.** Both hills have two
    names and the draft handles it correctly ("Shy's Hill (then called Compton's Hill)";
    "Overton Hill — also called Peach Orchard Hill"). But across §3/§4/§5 the draft switches
    between "Shy's Hill," "Compton's Hill," "Overton Hill," and "Peach Orchard Hill" several
    times. SHOULD-FIX: after the first dual-naming, pick ONE name per hill and stick to it
    (Shy's Hill; Overton Hill) so a newcomer isn't tracking four names for two places.

15. **"the Nashville & Chattanooga Railroad" (§3) — borderline.** Named as the line Steedman
    drove across, and earlier Forrest is "tearing up track." A newcomer doesn't strictly need
    to know which railroad, and the prose doesn't over-rely on it. CLEAR enough.

16. **"five detached redoubts... Redoubt 4, then 5, then 3, then 2, then 1" (§3) — CLEAR.**
    The out-of-order numbering (4,5,3,2,1) might briefly puzzle a careful reader, but "the
    wheel rolling them up like a line of dominoes" tells them it's a spatial sequence, not a
    countdown. Fine.

17. **The drain-the-left mechanism (§4) — CLEAR and important.** "The fury of the attack
    pulled Confederate reinforcements eastward... every man Hood shifted east came off his
    already-paper-thin left." This is the causal link a newcomer needs to understand why a
    *failed* charge mattered, and the draft spells it out plainly. Well done.

## OVERWHELMING PASSAGES

18. **§3 middle paragraph ("Then, on the far Union right, the door began to swing...") —
    SHOULD-FIX.** Five named generals + multiple corps numbers + three place-names in one
    block. Not LOST (each name has a role), but it's the densest passage for a newcomer.
    Same fix as the storytelling note: keep the geometry (cavalry outer edge, infantry
    behind, wheel pivoting onto the left), thin the corps designations.

19. **§5 casualty paragraph ("Union losses came to about 3,000... Confederate losses came
    to about 6,000...") — CLEAR.** Despite being a stat block, it's readable because the
    draft immediately interprets the numbers ("that ratio is the fingerprint of an army that
    broke"). A newcomer comes out understanding *why* the prisoner count matters. Good model
    for how to do numbers.
