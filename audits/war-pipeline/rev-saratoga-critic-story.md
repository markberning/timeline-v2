# STORYTELLING CRITIC · Battles of Saratoga draft (rev-saratoga)

*Critic pass 2026-06-12, against `rev-saratoga-draft.md`. Bar = `rev-brandywine-final.md`.
Storytelling lane only; factual accuracy belongs to the fact critic. No edits made to the draft.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is the strongest draft in the Revolution vertical so far, which is the right shape for
the flagship battle. The three-section architecture holds (squeeze → two battles + the poisoned
month → convention and consequences), the pacing within sections is mostly excellent, and the
draft handles the hardest craft problem in this story — two battles eighteen days apart without
making section 2 feel like two separate battles bolted together — by running the feud as the
connective tissue between them. The Frederika journal passages are the emotional core of the
page and they land. The Boot Monument closer is close to the best single image in the vertical.
The simultaneity strands (Howe in Philadelphia, the militia surge, Clinton's raid, the French
watching) are woven in, not sealed in lanes.

What keeps it from a clean PASS: Burgoyne and Gates are the two men this battle is ABOUT, and
neither of them is a character with a want or a flaw visible from the inside. Gates is a
doctrine. Burgoyne is a plan. Fraser is the closest thing to a human being on the page, and he
dies in section 2 without section 1 having established why we should feel it. The section 1
paragraph about the Hudson crossing — the story's hinge event — announces its own significance
instead of dramatizing it. And section 3's opening buries its best hook (the silver-bullet
courier hanged the day after the surrender) three paragraphs into the sequence, behind
administrative retreat prose. These are fixable. The bones are there; the draft needs to put
faces on its commanders.

## BLOCKER

### B1. Burgoyne has no interiority — the gambler's calculus is never shown from the inside

The brief calls for "Burgoyne's gambler isolation." The draft delivers his PLAN accurately and
his OUTCOME fully, but never lets the reader inside the bet. The key moment is the Hudson
crossing, the most consequential decision of the campaign, and the draft renders it like this:

> "On September 13, Burgoyne committed the act that made everything after it inevitable. His
> army crossed to the west bank of the Hudson on a bridge of boats just north of the little
> settlement of Saratoga, and rather than leave a chain of garrisoned posts behind him, he
> deliberately cut his communications back to Ticonderoga. From that moment there were two
> possible endings, Albany or capitulation, and he said as much himself later, defending the
> campaign in Parliament."

Three problems: (1) "committed the act that made everything after it inevitable" is the
narrator announcing the significance instead of dramatizing it. (2) The June-30 quote ("This
army must not retreat") lands in the very next paragraph — but it's severed from the Sept 13
moment by a sentence that points ahead to Parliament, and used as a kicker rather than as the
key to the man's psychology. (3) We are told Burgoyne is a gambler but we never feel the wager.

The Clinton note in section 2 deepens the problem: Burgoyne digs in on "that thin promise,"
which is perfectly accurate, but the draft doesn't show us why a rational officer made that
call. The fact pack supplies the logic — Bennington and St. Leger had already eaten the margins,
his supply line was failing, a junction with Clinton was genuinely the only remaining path to
the plan working — but none of that is in the prose. The result is that Burgoyne looks not
like a gambler who calculated wrong but like a man who sat down to wait for no good reason.

**Fix:** Rewrite the Hudson-crossing paragraph so the June-30 quote OPENS it as the key to the
man, not closes it as a kicker. Add one sentence of Burgoyne's internal calculus at the Clinton
note — not sympathy, just the logic of what he was betting on. E.g., after "that thin promise
Burgoyne dug in to wait": "Albany was still possible if Clinton moved. And if Clinton moved fast
enough, Saratoga was still a campaign, not a catastrophe." The calculation that broke the army
should be visible as a calculation, not just a decision.

---

### B2. The Fraser setup in section 1 is missing — his death in section 2 hits nothing

The draft treats Fraser's death and burial as its emotional peak in section 2, and rightly so:
the Frederika journal passages are the best writing on the page, and the image of the generals
standing bareheaded while round shot falls on the burial party is genuinely powerful. But
Fraser is introduced in section 1 only as a function:

> "Simon Fraser's Advanced Corps swinging wide to the right..."

He appears again in section 1 only as the man who warns Burgoyne against punishing the McCrea
accused — one sentence, never named in the McCrea beat (the draft says "his best brigadier").
The section 2 death paragraph's "conspicuous on horseback rallying the rearguard" bio-sketch
is good but it's doing double duty for a man who should already be established.

The dossier bio is excellent ("the best soldier in Burgoyne's army... he won Hubbardton, talked
Burgoyne out of punishing Jane McCrea's accused killer to keep the Native alliance, and anchored
every advance"). That material NEEDS to land in the prose of section 1, not just in the dossier,
or the burial has no weight.

**Fix:** Name Fraser explicitly in the McCrea beat ("Simon Fraser, his best brigadier") and
expand that moment one sentence: something that tells us what kind of soldier he was — the
veteran of Louisbourg and the Plains of Abraham, the man every advance hinged on — so that when
he goes down rallying the rearguard in section 2, the reader feels the loss. The dossier bio
can carry the detail; the prose needs a single tether.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. Gates is a doctrine, not a character — the feud's dramatic stakes are told, not shown

The Gates-Arnold feud is the page's best opportunity for character work and the draft handles
it competently but at arm's length. The documented evidence layer is managed correctly:

> "Gates's report to Congress credited Freeman's Farm to 'a detachment of the army,' never
> naming Arnold, then stripped Morgan's corps out of Arnold's division. Arnold protested in
> writing; there was a furious confrontation... Gates issued him a pass to leave for Philadelphia.
> Arnold stayed in camp, with no clear command."

This is accurate and appropriately hedged. But "a furious confrontation" is telling, not
showing. The draft correctly avoids the invented tent-dialogue — but it swings too far, landing
in bureaucratic summary. The reader needs to feel WHY Arnold stayed when he had a pass to
leave, and why Gates couldn't simply remove him. That is the human drama: an army commander
who could not afford to lose his best battlefield general, and a general who could not leave
because the battle he had partly made was about to be finished without him.

**Fix:** One sentence of human motive for Arnold's staying — not invented dialogue, just the
plain inference from documented fact. Something like: "A pass to Philadelphia was also a pass
to miss the battle he had spent the summer building toward; Arnold stayed." And one sentence
on Gates's bind: he had stripped Arnold's role and couldn't give it back without conceding the
point. The feud's stalemate, not just its events, is what makes October 7 possible.

### S2. The section 3 opening buries the silver-bullet hook — retreat logistics lead instead

Section 3 opens:

> "The British army that buried Simon Fraser at sunset on October 8 began retreating that same
> night, north through cold rain to the settlement of Saratoga... It left its hospital behind,
> some 300 sick and wounded men trusted to American mercy, and most of its baggage. Frederika
> Riedesel rode the retreat in a calash with her three daughters, and spent the next six days
> in the cellar of a house under American artillery fire. Eleven cannonballs went through the
> house above her..."

Then: trap closes (paragraph 2), silver-bullet story (paragraph 3). The problem: the
silver-bullet story is the most dramatically perfect thing in the whole section — a man who
swallows a secret message, gets dosed with an emetic by the opposing governor, and is hanged
the day after the army he was riding to save surrendered — and it's arriving after two
paragraphs of retreat logistics. It should come SOONER, probably right after "On October 13
Burgoyne's council of war voted to open talks," as the cut to what the last hope actually
looked like at that moment. "One last hope was already dead, though nobody at Saratoga knew
it" is a good transition line — it just needs to arrive before the surrender talks begin, not
after.

**Fix:** Move the silver-bullet paragraph up to follow the "council of war voted to open talks"
sentence, making it the punctuation on why there was nothing left to wait for. The Frederika
cellar material is fine where it is as the texture of the siege.

### S3. The McPherson test: Howe in Philadelphia is a MEANWHILE box, not a braided strand

The draft's MEANWHILE at the end of section 1 does the work:

> "While Burgoyne crossed the Hudson, the army his plan had always quietly counted on was
> hundreds of miles away. General Howe had taken the main British force to sea..."

This is present and accurate, and it's exactly where it belongs narratively. But it sits in
a box. The Brandywine final (the bar document) handles parallel strands differently: the
Saratoga consequence of Howe's choice is woven into the Brandywine section 3 close, which
gives it structural weight. Here the Howe strand has one entry point (the S1 MEANWHILE) and
then disappears until the Clinton paragraph, which is already the too-late-too-small story.

The missing braid: the paragraph in section 1 about Burgoyne's plan's "hole in London" says
Howe went to Philadelphia and "nobody had ordered him to do otherwise" — but it doesn't name
the battle being fought at the same time, and the reader of this page may not have read
Brandywine. The simultaneity is deadlier as a fact than the draft conveys: while Burgoyne was
watching his rations shrink and his scouts go home, Washington was fighting at Brandywine and
Germantown, three days' ride to the south, and losing. That detail, even in one sentence,
makes the isolation concrete.

**Fix:** In the S1 background paragraph on the planning failure, add a single orienting clause:
not a MEANWHILE but an in-prose aside — e.g., after "nobody had ordered him to do otherwise":
"While Burgoyne's army shrank, Howe's was fighting Washington at Brandywine and Germantown."
(Brandywine pill already exists; this names the context without requiring a new pill.) This
turns the isolation from an abstract planning failure into a felt simultaneity.

### S4. The flagship weight of section 3 — the French consequence — is competently told but not felt

The France paragraph is the page's climactic beat and it reads like good historical summary:

> "The news of Saratoga crossed in about six weeks, carried from Boston by a courier named
> Jonathan Loring Austin, who reached Benjamin Franklin outside Paris on December 4. The story
> goes that Franklin called out to ask whether Philadelphia was taken, and Austin answered yes,
> but that he brought greater news: Burgoyne and his whole army were prisoners of war. Two days
> later the French foreign ministry summoned the American commissioners..."

This is correct, appropriately hedged, and covers the chain. But for the flagship battle, the
most significant single event of the war for its strategic consequences, the prose is as neutral
in energy as the Convention Army paragraph that precedes it. The Germantown final (the bar
document) nails this problem with its "Saratoga made the alliance. Germantown helped make
Saratoga believable." — a sentence with stakes.

The draft's version is: "The rebellion had become a world war, and a world war was something
Britain could lose." That's correct and clean, but it's arriving after a long fact chain and
it lands flat. The sentence before it — "London did exactly that, everything short of
independence, and it arrived too late" — is the genuine dramatic beat, because it shows
Britain conceding everything except the one thing. That sentence deserves to close the
paragraph, or be a QUOTE-formatted standalone, not be buried inside the chain.

**Fix:** Restructure the France paragraph to end on "everything short of independence, and it
arrived too late" as the dramatic closure, with "The rebellion had become a world war" as the
setup clause, not the kicker. Let the irony of Britain's too-late concession be the last word
before the pill, not a sentence that sounds like a historian's verdict.

## TRIM

### T1. Section 2 length — the author's note flags it and the trim candidates are correct

The author note identifies the three trim candidates: the turkey-call beat, the Arnold-at-
headquarters parenthetical, and the Clinton-raid paragraph. Of these, the Clinton-raid
paragraph is the most compressible:

> "Clinton, for his part, kept his word and it did not matter. He came up the Hudson on October
> 3 with about 3,000 men, stormed the Highlands forts on October 6, and sent raiders far enough
> upriver to burn Kingston, still 45 miles short of Albany: a raid, not a rescue, with no orders
> and no intention to fight through to Burgoyne."

This paragraph works and is accurate, but section 3 already tells the silver-bullet story
which covers the Clinton thread in more dramatic terms. Running the Clinton material in both
sections (as the reason Burgoyne waited AND as the silver-bullet story) doubles up. One
sentence in section 2 — "Clinton, for his part, came up the Hudson and burned a town 45 miles
short of Albany; a raid, not a rescue" — frees ~80 words without losing the beat. The fuller
telling belongs in section 3.

### T2. The turkey-call sentence — compressible

> "The story goes that Morgan rallied them with his turkey call."

Fine detail, tradition-badged correctly. But the seesaw afternoon paragraph is already the
densest in the draft, and this sentence stops the momentum right after "scattered." Integrating
or dropping it saves the pace. Suggested collapse: fold it into the scatter sentence — "they
over-pursued, ran into Fraser's main body, scattered — tradition says Morgan rallied them with
his turkey call — and what followed was a seesaw afternoon..." — or drop it entirely and let
the fragment of their recovery be implicit in the next sentence.

## NIT

### N1. "the act that made everything after it inevitable"

The word "act" is oddly clinical for what is, as the prose itself says, the moment Burgoyne
bet the army on a single outcome. "The crossing that made everything after it inevitable" is
plainer and truer, and the B1 fix would rewrite this sentence anyway.

### N2. "The battlefield's own historian"

> "(Tradition puts Arnold on horseback in the thick of the smoke that afternoon; the
> battlefield's own historian found no contemporary evidence for it.)"

"The battlefield's own historian" needs a name or a credential to carry weight. Luzader is the
fact pack's citation — "Luzader (the NPS Saratoga historian)" — and naming him in the prose,
or dropping to "the battle's most thorough historian" without the parenthetical's coyness,
would handle it. The fact critic should adjudicate whether a name is appropriate here, but the
phrase as written sounds like an unnamed authority is being invoked.

### N3. "a seesaw afternoon"

"Seesaw" appears in section 2 ("a seesaw afternoon") and the blurb ("A seesaw afternoon leaves
Burgoyne holding a field that cost twice what it paid"). The word is earned and correct. Minor:
one of the two uses could vary — "three to four hours of attack and counterattack" is already
in the prose and says the same thing with more texture.

### N4. Section 1 ends well but could end stronger

Section 1's final sentence: "One army on that river was shrinking. The other was growing." This
is the draft's clearest statement of the campaign's arithmetic, and it's correct. But the
section's actual pull-toward-section-2 moment is the Kościuszko paragraph two sentences
earlier: "Burgoyne could not force the defile, which left him one play: swing west through the
forest and try to turn the American left. That is why both battles of Saratoga would be fought
in woodland clearings northwest of the lines." That is the page's best structural line, and
burying it before the final two sentences slightly undermines it. Consider making it the last
line of the section, or the second-to-last with "One army on that river was shrinking. The
other was growing" moved immediately after "behind those lines stood some 8,500 effectives."

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES (for the record)

- **Voice ship-blockers: clean.** No em-dashes in original prose; no drama-bold; no "as we'll
  see" or "our story." "A little after noon" and "By the famous account" are the register, and
  it's the right register throughout.
- **Disputed facts handled correctly.** Arnold on Sept 19 (headquarters, not field), Murphy's
  attribution (tradition), Morgan's order (paraphrased, not quoted), the sword-exchange
  dialogue (Wilkinson, "by the famous account"), the turkey call ("the story goes"), Gates's
  "begin the game" ("is supposed to have said"), Austin's doorstep exchange ("the story goes" +
  paraphrase). All blacklist items untouched.
- **The two-battles shape is legible.** The draft does NOT treat Freeman's Farm and Bemis
  Heights as a standard two-battle page. The feud thread, the Clinton-wait thread, and the
  growing/shrinking armies thread bridge the eighteen days without making the reader feel the
  page has paused.
- **The Frederika material is exceptional.** Both the dinner-table passage and the burial
  watching-from-below passage are the right choice for human texture in section 2, and their
  placement (dinner-table at Fraser's wounding, burial at the next-morning death) earns the
  Fraser peak.
- **The Convention Army is told honestly.** "A genuinely shabby episode on the winning side"
  is exactly what the fact pack required and the draft delivers it plainly.
- **The Boot Monument is the right closing image.** "A war memorial that honors a man it
  refuses to name" closes Arnold's story on an object, not on a verdict, which is the right
  move for a story this complex. It earns flagship weight without overreaching.
- **MEANWHILEs are doing real work.** Howe-in-Philadelphia (S1) plants the isolation; Lady
  Acland's night-boat (S2) is the human counterpoint to Fraser's death and earns its place;
  Ticonderoga-abandoned (S3) is the right geographic close to the whole campaign.
- **The simultaneity braid is mostly present.** Howe is in a box (S3 is asking for a fix) but
  the militia-surge strand (McCrea → Bennington → Morgan → the growing army) and the French
  strand (battle page's half only, diplomacy handed off correctly) are woven through the whole
  draft, not siloed.

## SUMMARY FOR THE RECONCILER

**VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES**

2 BLOCKERS, 4 SHOULD-FIX, 2 TRIM, 4 NIT.

The single biggest storytelling problem: **Burgoyne has no interiority.** The gambler who bet
an army on a junction that never came is the heart of this story, and right now he is a plan
that failed. The Hudson-crossing paragraph (B1) is the key fix: it is the moment Burgoyne
commits to the wager, and it reads as logistics. B2 (Fraser setup) and S1 (Gates-Arnold feud
as drama rather than bureaucracy) are the other structural fixes. S2 (silver-bullet paragraph
moved earlier in S3), S3 (Howe simultaneity woven rather than boxed), and S4 (France
paragraph's dramatic close) are the shape fixes. Nothing here touches a fact; every suggested
rewrite recombines material already in the draft or its ledger. The fact critic should eyeball
any new sentences before integration.
