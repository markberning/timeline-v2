## ❌ MUST-FIX

### ❌ 1. Forbes quote: "being actuated by your spirits" — one word wrong
**Draft:** "I have used the freedom of giving your name to Fort Duquesne, as I hope it was in some measure **the being actuated** by your spirits that now makes us masters of the place."
**Primary source (Letters of General John Forbes, Irene Stewart ed.; confirmed by WPXI/Heinz History Center/info-ren.org):** "…as I hope it was in some measure the being actuated by your spirits that now makes us **Masters** of the place."

The verbatim text as found in primary sources reads identically to the draft *except* for capitalization of "Masters" — which is period typography and not an error. However, the draft also renders "Fort Duquesne" without the period-correct space ("Fort Du Quesne" in the original). This is minor and acceptable in modern prose, but the quote itself is **verified correct** in substance.

**Update:** On closer comparison the draft's quote is accurate word-for-word against the confirmed primary source. No correction needed on the substance. ✅ — downgrade to note only.

> **Actual ❌ #1:** The draft dates Fort Duquesne's fall as "November 24, 1758" (French blew the magazine and retreated) and states Forbes wrote his letter "three days later" (i.e., November 27). The primary source letter is confirmed dated **November 27, 1758** ✅. But the Forbes Expedition Wikipedia article and the SCWPA source state the French destroyed the fort on **November 24** and Forbes' force entered **November 25** — not November 24. The draft has Washington scouting the ruins on "November 24" (the same day of destruction), which conflicts with the sequence in primary sources: French demolition began night of November 24/morning of November 25; British entry was November 25.

**Source:** "I took possession next day [after the 25th]" — Forbes' own letter dated November 27 says the French "effectuated" the burning "on the 25th" and he took possession "next day" (the 26th). The American Battlefield Trust and SCWPA both date the French demolition November 24–25 and British entry November 25.

**Correction:** The draft says "On November 24, 1758, the French blew up the fort's magazine, burned the buildings, and retreated. George Washington personally led three scouting parties into the smoking ruins that day." This is internally inconsistent: Washington scouting the ruins *the same day* the French were blowing them up. Per Forbes' own letter, British forces entered **November 25** (the day after the demolition began), and Forbes wrote on the 27th. Change "November 24" (for the British entry/Washington scouting) to **November 25**. The demolition can remain November 24–25.

### ❌ 2. "America" vs. "Canada" in the Rhine paraphrase
**Draft:** "he had won **America** on the banks of the Rhine"
**All sources found (Wikipedia Pitt article; Britannica; American Battlefield Trust; Marianopolis Quebec History):** The universally cited formulation is "won **Canada** on the banks of the Rhine."

The ledger flagged this and correctly recommended "Canada." Web search found zero instances of the "America" variant; only "Canada" appears in secondary sources. Because this is presented as a paraphrase (not in quotation marks), "America" is not technically a fabricated quote — but it misrepresents the historical record and the factpack's own language. This must be corrected to **"Canada."**

## ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX

### ⚠️ 1. Lord Howe quote: attribution is "a British officer" — confirm first-sentence-only truncation
The draft uses only the first sentence: "In Lord Howe, the soul of General Abercromby's army seemed to expire." The full period quote (confirmed by emergingrevolutionarywar.org and Fort Ticonderoga sources) continues: "From the unhappy moment the General was deprived of his advice, neither order nor discipline was observed, and a strange kind of infatuation usurped the place of resolution."

The truncation is not wrong, but the attribution "A British officer, July 1758" is loose and period-sourced only — the factpack already flags this (⚠️#9) and the draft handles it correctly. No factual error; the caution label in the ledger is appropriate. ✅ No change required, but the author should be aware the full quote is more damning and could be used.

### ⚠️ 2. Carillon British casualty range: "1,600–2,000" — defensible but lower-bound may be conservative
The draft's range of "1,600–2,000 British killed, wounded, and missing, with more than 500 dead" is fully defensible:
- Warfare History Network (Hamilton): ~1,610 total
- Abercromby's own report: "just under 2,000, more than 500 dead"
- One source says "nearly 2,000 with upwards of 800 killed" (likely inflated)

The factpack's ⚠️#5 range is correct and the draft uses it correctly. ✅ No change required; range is the right call.

### ⚠️ 3. Treaty of Easton: draft says "thirteen nations" but the nation count is contested
The draft states "representatives of thirteen nations" and then lists the Six Nations (6) + eastern and western Lenape + Shawnee + Mingo + "a number of smaller nations." The original treaty title published by the University of Michigan lists **14 named groupings**: "Mohawks, Oneydos, Onondagas, Cayugas, Senecas, Tuscaroras, Tuteloes, Nanticokes and Conoys, Chugnuts, Delawares, Unamies, Mohickons, Minisinks, and Wapings." Historians conventionally count these as 13 nations (treating Nanticokes and Conoys as one grouping), which is the standard citation in secondary literature (Wikipedia, beavercountyindians.com). The draft's "thirteen nations" is the conventional figure and is defensible.

However, the draft's list of nations named in the text ("Mohawk, Onondaga, Oneida, Cayuga, Seneca, and Tuscarora" for the Six Nations, plus "eastern and western Lenape, the Shawnee, the Mingo, and a number of smaller nations") omits the Tuteloes, Nanticokes, Conoys, Chugnuts, Mohicans, Minisinks, Wapings, and Unamies. The draft covers this with "a number of smaller nations" — acceptable compression. No factual error. ✅
