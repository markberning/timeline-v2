### THE BULLET — rifled musket / Minié ball

**✅ Smoothbore effective range ~80–100 yards.**
Standard figure, well supported. (Wikipedia "Springfield Model 1861"; multiple sources.)

**✅ Minié ball invented 1849 by Claude-Étienne Minié, building on Delvigne.**
Confirmed: 1849 per Wikipedia "Minié ball" and "Claude-Étienne Minié." Delvigne credited for earlier expansion concept (~1826). Both elements correct.

**✅ French Army adopted (the Minié rifle) in 1857.**
The draft says "The French Army adopted it in 1857" — confirmed by Wikipedia "Minié rifle": "the French Army only officially adopted it in 1857." (A Britannica entry suggests France never adopted the Minié *ball* — this appears to conflate the 1851 British adoption of the rifle with French non-adoption of that version; France adopted its own 1857 Minié rifle. The draft's claim is accurate against the Wikipedia Minié rifle article, which is the fact pack's cited source.)

**✅ Springfield Model 1861, .58 caliber, Union's principal arm.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "Springfield Model 1861."

**✅ Pattern 1853 Enfield, .577 caliber, imported by both sides, leading Confederate import.**
Confirmed: multiple sources.

**✅ Springfield effective range 200–400 yards; reliable to 500 yards for a marksman; "mostly a matter of luck" beyond 600; Enfield sights to 900 yards.**
Confirmed verbatim against Wikipedia "Springfield Model 1861."

**✅ Traditional rifle-revolution thesis attributed to John Mahon (1961 article) and McWhiney & Jamieson, *Attack and Die* (1982).**
Confirmed: Mahon's article "Civil War Infantry Assault Tactics," *Military Affairs*, Summer 1961, pp. 57–68 (Semantic Scholar). McWhiney & Jamieson 1982 confirmed.

**✅ Hess revisionist argument (2008) presented as a counter-argument — not asserted as settled.**
Framing correct. Both the traditional thesis and Hess's correction are held in view; the mono-causal "rifle → automatic slaughter → trenches" chain is explicitly NOT presented as settled. The four Hess reasons (training, short-range preference, terrain, parabolic trajectory/"two killing zones") are accurately summarized.

**✅ Terminal ballistics: soft, heavy, low-velocity lead; shattered bone; usually necessitated amputation.**
Confirmed.

**✅ DoD lethality index 154, ~3× most lethal prior infantry weapon; ~90% of Civil War casualties from rifle/Minié bullets.**
Confirmed: HistoryNet "Civil War Guns."

**✅ Death toll: ~620,000 traditional / ~750,000 (Hacker 2011) / ~698,000 (2024 PNAS).**
Confirmed. Draft correctly presents these as a range.

**✅ Fredericksburg (Dec 1862) and Cold Harbor (1864) as bywords for frontal-assault slaughter.**
Confirmed.

### THE RAILROAD

**✅ Act of Congress January 31, 1862 authorized Lincoln to seize railroads and telegraph lines.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "United States Military Railroad."

**✅ USMRR controlled more than 2,000 miles; 419 locomotives and 6,330 cars built/acquired.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "United States Military Railroad." (Specifically: 2,105 miles of railroad, 419 engines, 6,330 cars.)

**❌ Haupt described as "formerly the chief engineer of the Pennsylvania Railroad."**
WRONG. Haupt was **general superintendent** of the Pennsylvania Railroad (1849–1851), not chief engineer. His Wikipedia article does not give him the chief engineer title for the Pennsylvania Railroad. He later served as chief engineer of the *Hoosac Tunnel* project (a separate Massachusetts tunnel project), and he was construction engineer on the Pennsylvania Railroad in 1847 before becoming general superintendent. The fact pack itself (Beat 2) says "former chief engineer of the Pennsylvania Railroad" — the error originates in the fact pack and carries into the draft.
- **Correct statement:** "formerly the general superintendent of the Pennsylvania Railroad" (or simply "formerly of the Pennsylvania Railroad").
- **Source:** Wikipedia "Herman Haupt."

**✅ Haupt's USMRR title: "Chief of Construction and Transportation" in the Virginia theater.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "United States Military Railroad."

**⚠️ Lincoln "beanpoles and cornstalks" quote — [VERIFY] resolved.**
The [VERIFY] flag is correctly applied; the quote is real and sourced to a primary document. Haupt's own *Reminiscences* (1901), p. 49, records Lincoln's words to the Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War (May 28, 1862) as: "there is nothing in it but beanpoles and cornstalks." The draft renders this as "there was nothing in it but beanpoles and cornstalks" (past tense "was" vs. primary source's present tense "is"). This is a trivial transcription variant and the draft already presents it as "reportedly said" without hard quotation marks around the full sentence — acceptable. The National Archives article title "Working Magic with Cornstalks and Beanpoles" derives from this remark, confirming attribution. The [VERIFY] is resolved: the quote is legitimate with the caveat the draft already gives. No change needed to the body prose, but the exact tense in primary source is "is" not "was" if the writer ever quotes it verbatim.

**✅ Chattanooga corps transfer: Chickamauga Sept 19–20, 1863; war council Sept 23, 1863; XI and XII Corps, ~25,000 men, ~1,200 miles, 9 railroads, complete ~Oct 8–12, ~11–12 days.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "United States Military Railroad." (The Emerging Civil War source gives "11.5 days" and "nearly 1,200 miles." Wikipedia gives ~12 days and complete by Oct 12.) The "7 days" Stanton legend is correctly flagged as the plan, not the reality.

**⚠️ Longstreet corps move: ~12,000 men, ~800 miles, ~12 days.**
The ~800-mile figure is the most widely cited number for the roundabout route through the Carolinas (Wikipedia "James Longstreet": "nearly 800-mile route"; "First Corps, Army of Northern Virginia": "775-mile route"). Some sources give 900 miles or ~1,000 miles, and the Emerging Civil War source used in the fact pack gives ~1,000 miles. The NPS source says the original direct route would have been 550 miles; the actual roundabout route was ~775–800 miles per two Wikipedia articles. The draft's "~800 miles" is defensible but the duration figure of "~12 days" requires care: lead elements arrived September 17 (8 days after departure September 9), but the last troops arrived September 25 (~16 days). "~12 days" for the main body's arrival is approximately correct. Flag as an approximate that should not be tightened.

**✅ North–South rail ratios (~3× freight cars, ~5× locomotives, ~8× iron-rail production, ~10× telegraph stations, ~24× locomotive manufacturing).**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "Confederate railroads in the American Civil War."

**✅ Confederate gauge incompatibility: 5-foot broad gauge vs. 4-foot-8½-inch standard; freight unloaded and wagon-hauled at Montgomery, Alabama.**
Confirmed.

### REPEATING RIFLES

**❌ Christopher Spencer described as "a 26-year-old Connecticut inventor."**
WRONG. Spencer was born June 20, 1833, in Manchester, Connecticut (Wikipedia "Christopher Miner Spencer"). He was **30 years old** when he demonstrated the rifle to Lincoln in August 1863. He was ~27 when he patented the design in 1860. The "26-year-old" figure does not match any documented date for Spencer.
- **Correct statement:** "a 30-year-old Connecticut inventor" (at the time of the Lincoln demonstration) or simply remove the age.
- **Source:** Wikipedia "Christopher Miner Spencer" (born June 20, 1833; demonstration August 18, 1863).

**✅ Spencer: lever-action, 7-shot, .52-caliber, tube in buttstock; more than 85,000 produced; U.S. orders 106,667.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "Spencer repeating rifle."

**✅ Henry: lever-action, 16-shot, .44 rimfire, patented 1860 by Benjamin Tyler Henry; mostly privately purchased; became the Winchester.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "Henry rifle." (16 shots = 15 in magazine + 1 in chamber; "sixteen-shot" is the accepted description in the sources.)

**⚠️ "Load on Sunday and shoot all week" — folklore framing [VERIFY resolved].**
The draft correctly presents this as folklore, not a sourced quotation from a particular soldier, and notes it floats across both the Henry and the Spencer advertising slogan. MilitaryHistoryNow confirms it is most commonly attached to the Henry, while "Load on Sunday and shoot all week" was also a Spencer slogan. Draft's handling is correct. Note: the draft elides the full phrasing "that damned Yankee rifle that they load on Sunday and shoot all week" — the fact pack has the longer version, the draft gives only the shorter "load on Sunday" portion. Both are in circulation; no error.

**⚠️ Ripley "newfangled gimcracks" — [VERIFY resolved, partially.]**
The [VERIFY] flag is correctly applied. Multiple credible popular-history sources (HistoryNet "Civil War Firepower Denied," HighCaliberHistory) attribute this phrase to Ripley, and it appears to be the standard summary phrase in the historiography. However, it has not been pinned to a specific primary document (e.g., Ripley's own written reports or congressional testimony). The draft correctly hedges it with an inline [VERIFY] and does not assert it as confirmed verbatim. The *substance* of Ripley's opposition ("encourage poor fire discipline and waste ammunition") IS sourced to Wikipedia's Ripley article. Verdict: the phrase is well-attested in the secondary literature and the draft's hedged presentation is appropriate. It can remain in quotation marks with the existing caveat, but a note to the writer is warranted that no primary-document pinning was found in this pass.

**✅ Spencer demonstrated to Lincoln August 1863, shortly after Gettysburg, on grounds near White House.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "Spencer repeating rifle"; Wikipedia "Christopher Miner Spencer" (date: August 18, 1863 per Today in Connecticut History). Location: NRA Museum gives "Washington Monument grounds"; other sources say "White House lawn." "Grounds near the White House" is the safe and accurate formulation in the draft.

**✅ Ripley removed as Chief of Ordnance September 15, 1863.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "James Wolfe Ripley."

**✅ Spencer-armed troops at Hoover's Gap, 1863.**
Confirmed: Wikipedia "Battle of Hoover's Gap"; American Battlefield Trust. Wilder's "Lightning Brigade" used Spencers. Date: June 24, 1863. Confirmed.

### BATTLEFIELD PHOTOGRAPHY

**✅ Alexander Gardner photographed Antietam two days after the battle (September 19, 1862); Brady mounted "The Dead of Antietam" exhibition, Broadway gallery, October 1862; first American battlefield so recorded.**
Confirmed: History.com; American Battlefield Trust.

**⚠️ New York Times quote: exact wording and date — [VERIFY resolved].**
**Date confirmed: October 20, 1862.** American Battlefield Trust primary source page and History.com both cite October 20, 1862.

**Wording issue:** The draft quotes the NYT as: *"Mr. Brady has done something to bring home to us the terrible reality and earnestness of the war."* The American Battlefield Trust primary source page gives: *"Mr. Brady had done something to bring home to us the terrible reality and earnestness of the war."* (past tense "had" vs. present tense "has"). Multiple secondary sources quote both variants; History.com uses "has." This is a minor transcription discrepancy across secondary sources and the primary page (ABT). The wording "terrible reality and earnestness of the **war**" in the draft vs. some sources citing "earnestness of **war**" (without "the") is also in minor variation. The "dooryards and along the streets" portion is confirmed across all sources. Given the variation in secondary-source rendering of this 1862 newspaper text, the draft should not be failed on this point alone, but the writer should be aware the ABT primary-source page gives "had" not "has."
