# Framing/Fairness Critic Gate — "The Conquest of Canada"
**Vertical:** French and Indian War  
**Draft reviewed:** `fi-conquest-draft.md`  
**Fact pack:** `fi-conquest-factpack.md`  
**Date:** 2026-06-02

---

## FIRST-CHECK RESULTS

### 1. Native nations as first-class actors with their own aims?
**PASS — strong.**  
The Iroquois neutrality at La Belle Famille is explicitly framed as a deliberate choice made for Iroquois reasons (kin-over-alliance), not as a service rendered to the British. The closing section ("The Counterweight Removed") is dedicated to the Native nations' own political calculation, names Nimwha's articulation of sovereignty, traces the Seneca war-message chain, introduces Neolin as a catalyst, and ends on Pontiac — not on a British victory lap. The draft does what few popular narratives do: it makes the Native nations' post-conquest reckoning the emotional and intellectual close of the chapter.

### 2. Human cost named: bombardment, scorched-earth, civilian toll?
**PASS — with one SHOULD-FIX (see below).**  
The bombardment figures are present and vivid: 10,000 shells, 40,000 cannonballs, 503 houses, the carcasse incendiary devices, Jean-Félix Recher's witness account of women weeping under the guns, the 1,400 stone houses and manors burned in the countryside, colonists killed, Major Scott's south-shore raiding party. The winter survivors starving and freezing alongside Murray's garrison is noted. This is the best-executed section of the three first-check criteria.

### 3. Does the Wolfe/Montcalm romance crowd out who actually suffered?
**PASS — with one SHOULD-FIX (see below).**  
The draft actively names the crowding-out risk and counters it: "The Wolfe-and-Montcalm story that history remembers is a duel of two generals; the people who actually paid that summer were the farmers whose manors were torched and the families who prayed under the shells." That sentence does the work. The generals' deaths are handled with appropriate epistemic caution (Parkman sourcing flagged; legend vs. record distinguished). The deaths themselves take roughly one paragraph each — proportionate to a chapter of this length.

## FAIR / TILTED / DISTORTED FINDINGS

### FAIR
- **Amherst's dishonors-of-war framing:** The denial of the march-out is presented neutrally — his stated reason (Native "barbarities") is given, but the draft names the flags burned rather than surrendered, which preserves the dignity and defiance of Lévis's men without editorializing against Amherst. The capitulation's slavery clause is named plainly. Both are good.
- **Montcalm vs. Vaudreuil:** The strategic disagreement is fairly laid out. Montcalm's contempt for Indigenous tactics is explicitly noted, which is historically material and properly reflects his blind spot.
- **Bougainville's absence:** Handled as a command failure that Montcalm triggered by attacking early, not as a deus-ex-machina British triumph. Correct framing.
- **Sainte-Foy as a French victory that changed nothing:** The draft is admirably clear that France won the battle and lost the campaign. No British-triumph smoothing here.
- **Article 40's worthlessness:** Named directly. "That promise was about to prove worthless." This is the right framing — a named lie, not an unfortunate misunderstanding.

### TILTED — SHOULD-FIX

**S1. The winter civilians' suffering is thin.**  
The draft mentions that "the civilians who had survived the bombardment now starved and froze alongside the garrison." One clause. The fact pack has material here — the population had just endured three months under the guns and then a brutal winter of scarcity. This doesn't need to be long, but it needs a sentence or two that names the civilian winter as its own ordeal, not a parenthetical gloss on Murray's troop-strength problem.

**S2. The Acadian exception is mentioned but not weighted.**  
The capitulation terms are summarized, and the sentence "The Acadians, the French settlers already deported in earlier years, were the conspicuous exception: Britain refused to repatriate the prisoners it held" is correct. But the Acadians' deportation (the Grand Dérangement) is one of the worst humanitarian acts of the entire war and touches tens of thousands of people. The single sentence is technically accurate and doesn't suppress the fact, but the word "conspicuous" is doing heavy lifting for what was a mass forced displacement. A brief phrase grounding the scale — "tens of thousands deported, prisoners not returned" — would keep this proportionate to the crime rather than proportionate to the length of the capitulation discussion.

**S3. French-Canadian civilian voice is present but thin beyond Recher.**  
Jean-Félix Recher's account is vivid and well-used. But outside the bombardment section, the French-Canadian civilians are largely objects (their houses burned, their parishes disarmed, their militia deserting). The "Counterweight Removed" section is entirely about Indigenous nations — rightly so — but the chapter ends without any French-Canadian perspective on what it meant to become a conquered, occupied people governed by a Protestant crown. The capitulation terms are told from the treaty-text angle. One sentence carrying a French-Canadian civilian voice on the meaning of the occupation would close that gap without adding structural weight.

### DISTORTED — MUST-FIX

**M1. The scorched-earth section stops short of naming "war crimes" framing that scholars use.**  
The draft says the scorched-earth campaign "is widely judged to have backfired, hardening neutral inhabitants into active enemies." That is true and present in the sources. But the judgment is entirely consequentialist (it didn't work strategically). Scholarly literature — including the fact pack's cited ResearchGate article on "Guerrilla Warfare and Hyperviolence in the Siege of Quebec" — frames Wolfe's deliberate destruction of civilian settlements as a form of hyperviolence and collective punishment of a civilian population, not merely a miscalculated tactic. The draft is not wrong, but the framing keeps the violence in the register of "strategic mistake" rather than "morally significant act against civilians." A single clause — something like "historians have called it systematic collective punishment, not just a miscalculated tactic" — would correct the tilt without editorializing. This is the draft's most significant framing omission: it names what happened, but anchors the moral register entirely in whether it worked.
