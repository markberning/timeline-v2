## A. "The Half King starts a war" (Jumonville Glen) — ✅ CONFIRMED

**Core facts ✅:**
- Date 28 May 1754; Fayette County, PA (between Hopwood and Farmington). ✅ (Wikipedia, *Battle of Jumonville Glen*)
- Lt. Col. George Washington, ~40 Virginians, guided by Tanaghrisson ("Half King") and **12** Mingo warriors. ✅ Wikipedia gives exactly "40 provincial troops from Virginia, plus 12 Mingo warriors" — the draft's "about a dozen" is correct.
- Ensign Joseph Coulon de Jumonville's French detachment (~35 men). ✅
- ~10 French killed, ~21 captured. ✅ Within the source range (reports vary; "10 to 12 killed," ~21–30 captured). The draft's "about ten… roughly twenty-one" is defensible and matches the pack.

**Hatchet killing — ✅ CONFIRMED AS HEDGED (this was the key check).**
The draft (¶45) presents it as widely-reported-but-contested, NOT flat fact: "By the most-repeated account… Tanaghrisson… said in French 'Thou art not yet dead, my father,' and killed him with a hatchet. But the details are disputed. Sources disagree on whether Jumonville died from a musket ball or a hatchet, and by whose hand, and the killing is reported as widely as it is contested, never as settled fact." This matches Wikipedia exactly, which carries both a "killed by a Musket-Shot in the Head" account and the tomahawk account and states "Exactly what happened next has been a subject of controversy and debate" / "several disputed and contradictory accounts." **The hedge is correct and adequately strong.**

**Tanaghrisson's motive (Fred Anderson) — ✅ CONFIRMED and properly attributed.**
Draft (¶41): "Historians such as Fred Anderson read his aim this way: he believed that a war between Britain and France would force the Ohio nations back under him…" Wikipedia attributes precisely this to Anderson: "Anderson theorizes that Tanacharison had lost influence over some of the local tribes (specifically the Delawares) and may have thought that conflict between the British and French would bring them back under his influence as allies of the British." The draft correctly frames it as a historian's reading ("read his aim this way"), not as documented inner motive. ✅
- Strategy failed ✅; he "fell ill that summer and died of pneumonia on 4 October 1754" ✅ (Wikipedia: "died of pneumonia on October 4, 1754").
- Nation: draft says "a Mingo (Ohio Iroquois) leader the British called the Half King" — the pack's recommended safe phrasing, avoiding the Catawba/Seneca over-specification. ✅

  - Minor note (not an error): draft says "about three hundred Pennsylvania provincials" — consistent with the pack's ~300; fine.

The draft (¶102) correctly traces a 1756 → 1757 → 1758 sequence with Teedyuscung as the driver. ✅

## D. Fort William Henry ~1,500-dead legend attribution — ✅ CONFIRMED

Draft (¶134): "Jonathan Carver's 1778 account claimed as many as 1,500 dead, a figure later echoed by the historian Benson Lossing, and James Fenimore Cooper's 1826 novel The Last of the Mohicans cemented the legend in the popular imagination."

Wikipedia confirms all three attributions and the precise division of credit:
- Carver **originated** the 1,500 figure ("Carver originated, without any supporting analysis or justification, the idea that as many as 1,500 people had been 'killed or made prisoner'").
- Lossing **echoed** it ("Lossing wrote that 'fifteen hundred [people] were butchered or carried into hopeless captivity'").
- Cooper's 1826 novel **cemented the legend** but did not originate the number ("his work and the sometimes-lurid descriptions… led to the belief that many more people died than actually had").

The draft correctly credits Cooper with cementing the LEGEND, not originating the number — exactly the distinction the brief asked to verify. ✅ Modern toll ~69–184 ✅.
