### Union (Army of the West)
- **Commanding general:** Brig. Gen. Nathaniel Lyon (killed in action during battle)
- **Flanking column commander:** Col. Franz Sigel
- **Post-Lyon commander:** Maj. Samuel D. Sturgis (assumed command after Lyon's death)
- **Artillery:** Capt. James Totten (Battery F)
- **Other officers:** Capt. John M. Schofield, Col. Frederick Steele, Col. Gordon Granger (rear guard cavalry)
- **Force size:** ~5,400 engaged (Wikipedia infobox gives 5,431; ABT gives ~5,400; historyofwar.org gives 5,800; ABT article gives ~7,000 total in Army of the West before the battle but ~5,400 engaged at Wilson's Creek)
- **Sigel's flanking column specifically:** ~1,100–1,200 men (civilwaronthewesternborder.org gives 1,200; ABT article gives ~1,100)

### Confederate / Missouri State Guard (Western Army)
- **Overall Confederate command:** Brig. Gen. Benjamin McCulloch (Confederate States Army; commanded Confederate and Arkansas units)
- **Missouri State Guard command:** Maj. Gen. Sterling Price (commanded MSG forces, ~7,000–8,000 men, only ~5,000 armed)
- **Arkansas state troops:** Brig. Gen. Nicholas Bartlett Pearce
- **Combined force size:** ~12,000–13,500 engaged (Wikipedia infobox: ~12,125 including ~2,000 unarmed MSG; historyofwar.org: ~13,500; historynet.com: 12,000; ABT article: ~12,000; the range reflects varying counts of armed vs. unarmed MSG)
- **Note on command structure:** McCulloch commanded the combined force by agreement but this was inherently awkward — he held a Confederate commission while Price held a Missouri State Guard commission, not a Confederate one. The two men quarreled for a month before agreeing at end of July to advance together, with McCulloch nominally in overall command. (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; ABT article)

## 2. Casualties

### Standard figures (Wikipedia infobox)
| Side | Killed | Wounded | Missing/Captured | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Union** | 285 | 873 | 186 | **1,317** (some sources round to 1,300) |
| **Confederate/MSG** | 277 | 945 | 0 | **1,222** (some sources round to 1,200) |
| **Combined** | 562 | 1,818 | 186 | **~2,539** |

### Scholarly range and source variation
- Wikipedia infobox: Union 1,317 / Confederate 1,222 / Combined ~2,539
- ABT battle facts page: Union 1,235 / Confederate 1,095 / Combined 2,330
- historyofwar.org: Union "just over 1,300" / Confederate "approximately 1,200"
- historynet.com: Union 258 killed + 873 wounded (partial); Confederate 277 killed + 945 wounded
- Contested: The exact totals vary by source; the Wikipedia infobox figures (1,317 Union / 1,222 Confederate) are the most commonly cited in academic contexts. The variation in Confederate totals reflects difficulty counting MSG casualties separately from Confederate regulars.

### Casualty rates
- Union lost approximately **24–25% of their engaged force** (wilsonscreek.com; ABT article; multiple sources)
- Confederate/MSG lost approximately **12% of their engaged force** (ABT article; wilsonscreek.com)
- On Bloody Hill specifically: over 1,700 casualties representing ~20% of combatants there (wilsonscreek.com)
- The 1st Kansas Infantry suffered the **seventh-highest casualty rate** of any Union regiment in the entire Civil War (Wikipedia)
- Wilson's Creek ranked as one of the **bloodiest battles in American history up to that point**, measured by casualty rate (ABT article)

### Lyon's death
- **Brig. Gen. Nathaniel Lyon** was the **first Union general officer killed in combat** in the Civil War (historynet.com; ABT; civilwaronthewesternborder.org; all sources agree)
- He had been wounded at least twice before the fatal shot — once in the leg, once grazed on the head (historyofwar.org; battlefields.org Bloody Hill article)
- His horse was shot out from under him earlier in the battle; he remounted to lead the fatal charge (battlefields.org Bloody Hill article)
- Killed by a bullet to the chest (heart) while leading a countercharge approximately 9:30–10:00 a.m. (historynet.com places this at ~10:00 a.m.; battlefields.org Bloody Hill gives ~9:30–10:00 a.m.)
- Price also wounded (painful side wound) during the fighting (ABT article)
- Col. Richard Weightman (a promising Missouri State Guard officer) died of wounds sustained at Wilson's Creek (historyofwar.org)

## 3. What Came Before

### Missouri's strategic importance
- Missouri was a **slaveholding border state** Lincoln could not afford to lose — it bordered Illinois and Iowa to the north, Kentucky to the east, and its rivers (the Missouri and Mississippi) were critical arteries for moving troops and supplies into the heart of the Confederacy (ABT article; wilsonscreek.com)
- Missouri sent approximately **40,000 men to the Confederate side and 60,000 to the Union side** over the course of the war (ABT article)
- The state contained critical **rail and river networks** that the Union needed to maintain (ABT article)

### Governor Claiborne Fox Jackson
- Missouri's governor in 1861; despite a **March 1861 state convention rejecting secession**, Jackson held strong Confederate sympathies and worked to take Missouri out of the Union (missouriencyclopedia.org; civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- Jackson reorganized the state militia and used it to threaten the Federal arsenal in St. Louis (ABT article)

### The St. Louis Arsenal
- A massive Federal weapons depot in St. Louis; Confederate and secessionist forces aimed to seize it to arm the state militia (ABT article; emeringcivilwar.com Lyon article)
- In January 1861, Lyon was ordered to St. Louis specifically to defend it (emergingcivilwar.com Lyon article)

### The Camp Jackson Affair — May 10, 1861
- Lyon (then a captain) learned that pro-secessionist state militia were training at Camp Jackson, a state militia encampment outside St. Louis, and that the camp had received four cannons captured by Confederates from the Federal Arsenal at Baton Rouge, Louisiana (Wikipedia Camp Jackson article)
- On May 10, 1861, Lyon led **6,500 troops** to surround Camp Jackson and forced the **700-man garrison** to surrender without firing a shot (Wikipedia Camp Jackson article)
- Lyon reportedly **disguised himself as an old blind woman** to personally scout Camp Jackson before the operation (Wikipedia Battle of Wilson's Creek)
- During the subsequent march of prisoners through St. Louis, hostile secessionist crowds gathered; after a drunk man fired into the Union ranks (fatally wounding a soldier), Lyon's men **opened fire on the mob, killing at least 28 people and wounding many more** (Wikipedia Camp Jackson; ABT article; civilwaronthewesternborder.org gives "killing 28 and wounding nearly 100")
- Sporadic violence continued for another day, with six more deaths (Wikipedia Camp Jackson)
- **Aftermath:** The incident energized secessionist sentiment and "panicked the legislature into granting Jackson near-dictatorial powers"; Jackson appointed **Sterling Price** (Mexican War hero and former governor) as major general commanding the reorganized Missouri State Guard (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; ABT article)

### The Planters' House Meeting — June 11, 1861
- Date: June 11, 1861, at the Planter's House Hotel, St. Louis (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; multiple sources)
- Attendees: Governor Jackson, Gen. Price (MSG side); Lyon (by this time promoted to brigadier general) and Frank Blair Jr. (Union side) (civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- The meeting lasted **four to five hours** (WebSearch result citing St. Louis Today article)
- Jackson proposed mutual disarmament and a neutrality agreement; Lyon rejected it (civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- Lyon delivered an ultimatum and ended the meeting; see Section 8 (Quotes) for the contested language
- **Outcome:** Jackson and Price immediately departed. Jackson's forces **burned the Osage River railroad bridge** on their way out of Jefferson City. (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; missouriencyclopedia.org)
- Lyon effectively declared war on Missouri's government

### Lyon's summer campaign
- Lyon transported forces **~145 miles upriver to Jefferson City** (Missouri's capital), securing it for the Union (historyofwar.org)
- Lyon routed Jackson's forces at the **Battle of Boonville** (June 17, 1861) (civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- Sigel moved by train to **Rolla** (the railhead) to intercept retreating secessionists, then advanced toward Springfield; he failed his intercept mission at **Carthage** (July 5, 1861), where he encountered Jackson's larger force and sustained a day-long fighting retreat (historyofwar.org; civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- By mid-July, Lyon commanded **~7,000 soldiers in Springfield** (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; ABT article)
- **McCulloch's arrival:** Brig. Gen. Ben McCulloch arrived from northwestern Arkansas with ~5,000 Confederate troops to reinforce Price — described as the **first Confederate invasion of Union territory** in the Trans-Mississippi (ABT article)
- Price's MSG and McCulloch's Confederates combined into the **Western Army**, totaling ~12,000–13,500 men; McCulloch assumed overall command (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; ABT article)

### Lyon's decision to attack
- After the August 2 **Battle/Skirmish at Dug Springs**, McCulloch positioned his army 10 miles southwest of Springfield along Wilson's Creek (civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- Lyon's strategic situation by early August 1861: supplies dwindled to "half and quarter rations"; his army was living near "the end of its logistical tether"; **short-term volunteers' enlistments were expiring within weeks**; his superior, Maj. Gen. John C. **Frémont** (Department of the West), refused reinforcements and urged retreat; the Confederates outnumbered him roughly 2:1 (historyofwar.org; ABT article)
- Rather than retreat and abandon Springfield (and Missouri), Lyon chose to **attack the Confederate camp** to weaken it sufficiently to allow safe withdrawal — or better, to break it entirely (ABT article; battlefields.org Bloody Hill)
- Lyon **confided to officers** that approving Sigel's two-pronged plan was politically necessary: the quote attributed to him is "Sigel has a great reputation, and if I fail against his advice it will give Sigel command and ruin me." [VERIFY — source cited as battlefields.org Bloody Hill article; may be secondary attribution]
- The Confederates had **also planned a surprise attack** on the Union camp for August 9 but cancelled it due to a light rain on the night of August 9 — meaning both armies were simultaneously planning to attack each other (wilsonscreek.com; historynet.com)

## 4. The Battle

### Overview of Lyon's plan
- Lyon proposed a **two-pronged dawn assault**: the main column (~4,200 men) under Lyon himself would strike the Confederate camp from the north; Sigel's flanking column (~1,100–1,200 men) would loop around to hit from the south, across the Wire Road, catching the Confederates between two fires (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; ABT article; all sources)
- The plan was **Sigel's idea**, not Lyon's; other officers objected; Lyon accepted it partly for political reasons (battlefields.org Bloody Hill; historyofwar.org)
- The operation required both columns to move in darkness and attack simultaneously at dawn to prevent the Confederates concentrating against either one individually

### Dawn attack — approximately 5:00–5:30 a.m.
- Lyon's column surprised Confederate pickets and cavalry before dawn (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; Wikipedia)
- An **acoustic anomaly** (probably the steep terrain and creek valley) prevented McCulloch and Price from hearing the initial gunfire for up to **thirty minutes** (ABT article)
- When Confederate scouts reached McCulloch, they reported the Federals were coming with supposedly **"twenty thousand men and 100 pieces of artillery"** — a wildly inflated number that initially caused confusion in the Confederate command (battlefields.org Bloody Hill article)
- **McCulloch and Price were caught off-guard, reportedly at breakfast** when word arrived (battlefields.org Bloody Hill article)
- Lyon's column drove Confederate cavalry from their positions and advanced uphill to the height later called **Bloody Hill** — a low, brush-covered plateau on the creek's east bank (all sources)
- Union artillery under Capt. **James Totten** (Battery F) opened on the Confederate camp, setting tents afire and throwing Price's men into disarray (historyofwar.org; ABT article)

### Sigel's column — initial success, then rout
- Sigel's 1,200-man force attacked from the south; his artillery barrage at the **Sharp farm** (Sharp's cornfield area) devastated Southern cavalry and camp followers — approximately 800 of 1,500 horsemen fled immediately (ABT article; battlefields.org Bloody Hill)
- Sigel captured over 100 prisoners in the opening moments and initially believed the plan was working (battlefields.org Bloody Hill)
- Sigel advanced to the **Wire Road** (the Telegraph Road connecting Springfield to Fort Smith, Arkansas), positioning his column squarely across McCulloch's line of communications (ozarkscivilwar.org / ABT battlefields article)
- Sigel **failed to post adequate skirmishers** or pickets and made no attempt to contact Lyon (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; ABT article; historynet.com)
- McCulloch assessed the broader situation and organized a surprise counterattack against Sigel using the **3rd Louisiana Infantry** and dismounted **2nd Arkansas Mounted Rifles** (ozarkscivilwar.org; ABT article)
- **The gray-uniform confusion:** Sigel's men, seeing gray-uniformed soldiers approaching, believed they were the **1st Iowa Infantry** (which wore gray uniforms early in the war) — and held their fire (Wikipedia; all sources agree on this point). The 3rd Louisiana was wearing gray; the 1st Iowa was also in gray — this was the early-war period before standard blue/gray was universal. (Wikipedia; ABT; historyofwar.org)
- McCulloch's men advanced to **40-yard range** before opening a devastating volley (battlefields.org Bloody Hill; civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- Sigel's brigade **broke and fled** in panic; Sigel lost **five of his six guns** (ABT article; historyofwar.org; historynet.com)
- Sigel **hid in a cornfield** to avoid capture, eventually making his way back to Springfield with only a single orderly (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; historynet.com gives "escaped only after six miles of pursuit")
- Casualties in Sigel's column: ~64 killed, 147 captured (battlefields.org Bloody Hill); total column losses ~130 prisoners of war noted separately (historyofwar.org)

### Bloody Hill — the main fighting, ~6:00 a.m. to ~11:30 a.m.
- With Sigel routed, the Confederate command could concentrate against Lyon's position on Bloody Hill
- **Price organized State Guard units** into defensive positions on the south slope of Bloody Hill; the fighting was primarily an uphill Confederate assault against Lyon's entrenched position (historyofwar.org; all sources)
- The **Pulaski Arkansas Battery** (Light Artillery, under Capt. William E. Woodruff Jr., ~70 men, armed with two 6-pound and two 12-pound smoothbore field guns) **unlimbered north of the Wire Road Ford** along Wilson's Creek and checked Lyon's advance at approximately 6:00 a.m., giving Price's infantry time to organize on the south slope (emergingcivilwar.com Pulaski battery article; ozarkscivilwar.org)
- The Pulaski Battery engaged in a prolonged artillery duel with Totten's Battery F; visibility was so obscured by smoke that gunners had to "give direction to their pieces by the flash" (Woodruff's own account, quoted in emergingcivilwar.com)
- The Pulaski Battery sustained three casualties including Lt. Omer Weaver (mortally wounded) (emergingcivilwar.com)
- Price's men launched **three major Confederate assaults** up Bloody Hill during the battle (Wikipedia; NPS; ABT; all sources agree on three assaults)
- Fighting was at **extremely close range** — as close as "shotgun range" or "almost point blank range before opening fire"; Price's formations were described as "three or four ranks deep" firing while lying prone, kneeling, and standing (historyofwar.org; battlefields.org Bloody Hill article)
- At one point Confederate infantry **closed within 20 feet of Federal cannons** (historynet.com)
- McCulloch eventually committed his own Confederate forces to support Price's Missourians' assaults (ABT article)
- **Command friction between Price and McCulloch** complicated the Confederate assault; the two generals quarreled about methods and command throughout the battle; McCulloch at times maneuvered his Confederate forces independently rather than in full coordination with Price (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; ABT article)
- A **Texas cavalry unit** attempted a circuitous rear charge against the Union position but was repulsed (ABT article)
- Despite their numerical superiority, Confederates struggled with **ammunition depletion** — averaging approximately **25 rounds per soldier** by the climactic phase (ABT article)
- Timeline of assaults: first assault approximately 8:00–8:30 a.m.; second assault approximately 9:00–9:30 a.m. (during which Lyon was killed); third assault approximately 10:30–11:15 a.m. (Wikipedia; NPS)

## 5. Lyon's Death

- Before the fatal moment, Lyon had **already been wounded at least twice**: a bullet grazing his head, and a wound to his right leg; his horse was shot and killed under him earlier in the battle (battlefields.org Bloody Hill; historyofwar.org; historynet.com)
- He was noted to be **"masked in blood from earlier wounds"** when he led his final charge (historyofwar.org)
- He wore **his old captain's coat** rather than a general's uniform, making him harder to identify but also less visible as a target in the conventional sense — he was everywhere on the line rallying his troops (civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- Time of death: approximately **9:30–10:00 a.m.** (all sources; some put it closer to 10:00 a.m.)
- Lyon remounted and led a charge with the **2nd Kansas Infantry** (or the 1st Iowa and 2nd Kansas, per ABT article) into a critical weakening of the Union center
- See Section 8 for his final words
- He was struck by a **bullet through the chest (heart)** and died almost immediately (historynet.com; historyofwar.org; battlefields.org Bloody Hill)
- Private **Thomas Lehmann** is cited as catching the dying general (historynet.com) [VERIFY — this specific detail appears only in one source]
- Lyon was **the first Union general officer killed in combat** in the Civil War (all sources agree)
- **The Ray House:** After the battle, Lyon's body was laid out on the family bed in the farmhouse of **John Ray**, which also served as a makeshift field hospital for Southern soldiers; the Ray House sat along the Wire Road (NPS; wilsonscreek.com)
- **Effect on the army:** Maj. **Samuel D. Sturgis** assumed command; he reorganized the Union line and **repulsed one more Confederate assault** before ordering a withdrawal (historyofwar.org; historynet.com)
- **The withdrawal:** Sturgis ordered retreat at approximately **11:30–11:45 a.m.** (Wikipedia; historynet.com); the withdrawal was described as **orderly** — Union forces fell back to Springfield, maintaining formation with rear-guard cavalry under **Gordon Granger** protecting the column's flank (historynet.com; ABT article)
- Union forces reached **Springfield at approximately 5:00 p.m.**, then retreated to **Rolla** beginning August 11 — the railhead and their supply base (Wikipedia; ABT; historyofwar.org)
- The Confederate forces did not pursue, partly due to exhaustion and critically low ammunition (ABT article; all sources)

## 7. What It Meant

### Tactical outcome
- A **Confederate tactical victory**: they held the battlefield, Lyon was killed, and Union forces withdrew (all sources agree)
- However, Confederate forces **could not and did not exploit** the victory: McCulloch refused to pursue, citing exhaustion, low ammunition, and concerns about the quality of Price's MSG relative to his supply line back to Arkansas (ABT article; civilwaronthewesternborder.org)

### The failed Confederate follow-up
- Price and McCulloch **immediately quarreled** about what to do next — McCulloch wanted to hold southwestern Missouri; Price wanted to push north toward the Missouri River (ABT article; civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- Their **fragile coalition broke apart**: McCulloch returned to Arkansas with his Confederate forces; Price led the MSG north alone (ABT article; civilwaronthewesternborder.org)
- Price's subsequent northward march led to his capture of the Union garrison at **Lexington, Missouri** (September 20, 1861), described as "the high tide of the Missouri State Guard" — but Price's 20,000 new recruits lacked logistics, and two-thirds eventually deserted when the Union mounted a new offensive (ABT article; historyofwar.org)

### Missouri's fate — the core consequence
- Missouri **never left the Union** — the Unionist provisional government retained effective state control throughout the war (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; ABT article; Wikipedia)
- However, secessionist Missouri legislators convened at **Neosho** in October 1861 and passed an ordinance of secession, establishing a rival Confederate state government; the **Confederacy admitted Missouri on November 28, 1861** — meaning Missouri had two simultaneous governments for the rest of the war (civilwaronthewesternborder.org; Wikipedia; missouriencyclopedia.org)
- This dual-government situation produced **the most vicious irregular/guerrilla warfare of any state in the Civil War** — bushwhackers, jayhawkers, and partisan violence plagued Missouri for the entire war and into Reconstruction; it "initiated three-and-a-half years of guerrilla warfare in Missouri, making it the third most-fought state in the Civil War" (wilsonscreek.com)
- The Union's control of Missouri's **river and rail networks** proved decisive in the longer war; Federal forces under Maj. Gen. Samuel Curtis defeated Price and McCulloch at **Pea Ridge, Arkansas** (March 7–8, 1862), ending the organized Confederate threat to Missouri (Wikipedia; ABT article; all sources)
- **Price's 1864 Missouri Raid** (a massive cavalry invasion) also ultimately failed (historynet.com)

### Lyon's legacy
- Historians credit **Lyon with saving Missouri for the Union** through his aggressive early actions — seizing the arsenal, capturing Camp Jackson, driving Jackson's government from the capital (wilsonscreek.com; ABT article; multiple sources)
- Lyon became an **early Union war hero**: Northern press portrayed the defeat at Wilson's Creek as a near-victory; monuments and schools were named for him (historyofwar.org; emergingcivilwar.com Lyon article)
- His aggressive, uncompromising tactics remain controversial: "Historians debate whether he was a patriotic hero or a zealous radical who unnecessarily escalated Missouri's conflict" (emergingcivilwar.com Lyon article)

### The Trans-Mississippi character
- Wilson's Creek **foreshadows the brutal, fragmented character of the Trans-Mississippi theater**: dual/disputed command structures on the Confederate side; irregular warfare; a state that fought itself; battles won but not exploited; and ultimately a war that the Confederacy could not convert into political control (ABT article; civilwaronthewesternborder.org; all sources)
- Missouri ultimately saw **more military engagements** than any other state except Virginia and Tennessee (wilsonscreek.com)

## 9. Geography for a Map

### Key locations and their spatial relationships
All distances approximate from Wilson's Creek National Battlefield historical accounts and maps.

| **Bloody Hill** | A low, broad plateau / ridge on the creek's **east bank**, north of the main Confederate camp; unnamed at the time; earned its name after the battle; elevation gain of ~100 feet above the creek bottom | This is where Lyon's main force fought; north-central part of the battlefield |
| **The Ray House (John Ray farm)** | A farmhouse on the **Wire Road**, northeast of Bloody Hill; Lyon's body was laid there after the battle; it was also used as a Confederate field hospital | Key landmark; Wire Road ran north–south past it |

| **Sharp's farm / Sharp's cornfield** | Joseph Sharp's farm, on high ground to the **south** of the main Confederate camp; where Sigel's flanking column struck in its opening attack; where the gray-uniform confusion rout occurred | South end of the battlefield; Sigel's attack zone |
| **The Ray cornfield** | Near the Ray farm; McIntosh led the 3rd Louisiana and 2nd Arkansas Mounted Rifles up the Wire Road to Ray's cornfield, where they fought Plummer's Federals at 7:30 a.m. | Between Bloody Hill and the Wire Road; mid-battlefield |
| **Edwards Farm** | Flat ground on the creek's **west bank** where Missouri State Guard divisions assembled | West side of battlefield |

### Two-pronged Union approach (compass orientation: north up)
- **Lyon's column:** Approached from the **northeast** (from Springfield direction) and attacked straight south/southwest onto Bloody Hill
- **Sigel's column:** Made a wide **clockwise loop** around the Confederate right flank, coming in from the **south/southwest** — striking at the Sharp farm area and pushing north up the Wire Road to get behind the Confederate camp
- **Springfield** is approximately **10 miles to the northeast** along the Wire Road
- **Rolla** (the railroad terminus, Lyon's supply base) is approximately **110 miles east-northeast** of Springfield — the ultimate Union destination after the battle

## 10. Contested / Uncertain Facts

### Casualty figures
- **Contested:** All casualty numbers should be treated as approximate. Wikipedia infobox (Union 1,317 / Confederate 1,222) is most commonly cited in academic contexts. ABT gives lower figures (Union 1,235 / Confederate 1,095). The gap reflects different counting methodologies for the Missouri State Guard vs. Confederate regulars and the treatment of "missing" vs. "captured." The combined total (approximately 2,300–2,540) is less disputed than the individual breakdowns.
- **Flag:** Any single precise total should note the scholarly range.

### Force sizes
- **Contested:** Confederate/MSG force size varies across sources from ~11,000 (ABT battle facts) to ~12,000 (Wikipedia infobox, ~12,125 including ~2,000 unarmed MSG) to ~13,500 (historyofwar.org, historynet.com). The variation reflects: (a) whether unarmed MSG recruits are counted; (b) uncertainty in contemporary MSG records. The figure of ~12,000 engaged with weapons is most commonly cited.
- Union force size is more consistent: ~5,400–5,431 engaged (Wikipedia infobox; ABT), though historyofwar.org gives 5,800 and the ABT narrative article gives ~7,000 for the total Army of the West pre-battle (the discrepancy is Lyon left some men in Springfield and not all were engaged).

### The gray-uniform confusion
- **Agreed core fact:** Sigel's men mistook the gray-uniformed **3rd Louisiana Infantry** for the gray-uniformed **1st Iowa Infantry** and held their fire, allowing Confederate forces to close to devastating range (all sources agree on this fundamental point)
- **Uncertain detail:** Exactly how far the Confederates closed before opening fire — sources range from "40-yard range" (battlefields.org Bloody Hill) to "point-blank range" (general) to "40 yards" (ABT). All agree it was extremely close.
- **Uncertain detail:** Whether Sigel personally ordered his men to hold fire believing the approaching troops were friendly, or whether the confusion arose spontaneously among his troops (sources vary in emphasis; the *result* — catastrophic hesitation — is not disputed)

### The Planters' House quote
- **Contested:** The exact words Lyon used at the June 11 Planters' House meeting are disputed between two secondary sources written years after the fact (Snead 1886, Confederate; Peckham 1866, Union). No contemporary document verifies either exact version. The *substance* of the ultimatum is not in dispute. (civilwaronthewesternborder.org is explicit on this)

### Time and manner of Lyon's death
- **Mostly agreed:** Time of death approximately 9:30–10:00 a.m. (small variation between sources); bullet through the chest/heart while leading a charge; had already been wounded twice; his horse had been shot earlier
- **Minor variant:** historynet.com cites Private Thomas Lehmann catching the dying general and Lyon saying "I am going" as final words. This detail appears in only one source checked; cannot be confirmed or denied from the others reviewed.

### Command structure and "overall command"
- **Agreed:** McCulloch nominally commanded the combined Western Army. The awkwardness was structural — Price held a Missouri State Guard commission (not Confederate), meaning Price's forces were not formally part of McCulloch's Confederate command; they operated as allied forces. The two men quarreled throughout and the coalition broke apart immediately after the battle. (All sources agree on this; the *degree* of dysfunction is characterized differently by different sources)

### Lyon's role in Sigel's plan
- **Minor contest:** Some sources say Lyon accepted Sigel's two-pronged plan despite objections from other officers; battlefields.org Bloody Hill suggests Lyon approved it partly for political reasons (the Sigel quote about ruining him). This is a secondary-source interpretation, not a documented primary fact.

### Whether the battle "saved Missouri"
- **Interpretive dispute:** Most sources credit Lyon's aggressive pre-battle actions (not the battle itself, which was a Confederate victory) with keeping Missouri in the Union. Some historians argue Lyon's aggressiveness at Camp Jackson *inflamed* secessionist sentiment and made the guerrilla war worse. This interpretive tension is noted in the emergingcivilwar.com article on Lyon.
