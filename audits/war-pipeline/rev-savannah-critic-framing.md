*Critic pass 2026-06-12 against `rev-savannah-draft.md` (all 303 lines, incl. FACT LEDGER
and compliance lists) and `rev-savannah-factpack.md` (sole source). Scope: fairness,

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

No blockers. The heaviest framing duties are executed well: the Chasseurs-Volontaires are
held at exactly the documented/tradition split the pack mandates; the colonial-archive

d'Estaing is not scapegoated (the clock is real and the failure is spread to the unguarded

they constitute a consistent lean: the draft over-elevates the defeat (southern-strategy
inevitability), underframes the recrimination honestly (the blame is spread in the section
prose but then the summary re-seals around d'Estaing), and carries one quiet instance of
Pulaski hero-worship in the section prose that the bio correctly hedges.

## SHOULD-FIX

**1. The dossier note seals the 24-hour failure back onto d'Estaing alone after the prose
spreads it honestly.**

The `note` field for the ALLIED side in the dossier reads:
> "It outnumbered the garrison better than two to one and was beaten anyway."

That is accurate and appropriately dry. But the dossier's BRITISH note reads:
> "A garrison that should have been too thin to hold and was not, because of two things bought in three weeks: a Royal Engineer's earthwork sprint dug by hundreds of enslaved laborers, and a relief column of about 800 men that Lieutenant Colonel John Maitland marched through the coastal creeks from Beaufort during the truce."

The structural problem: the dossier pairs a British note that names both factors of British
success (Moncrief + Maitland) against an allied note that names only the defeat's bottom
line ("beaten anyway") without naming the allied failure that let Maitland through. A reader
who reads only the dossier panel — which many mobile readers will — sees: British = two
masterstroke reasons; Allied = no explanation except being beaten. That is implicitly an
allied-failure-as-mystery framing, which tips d'Estaing toward blame by omission.

Section 2 prose correctly spreads the failure: "the failure should be spread honestly. The
allies also failed to close a water route they knew about, and d'Estaing had summoned the
town before he was ready to take it." But the dossier panel does not carry this distributed
reading at all. Readers who don't drill into the section will leave the dossier with an
unexamined "beaten two-to-one against a thin garrison" frame that implies either allied
incompetence or d'Estaing as the single cause.

FIX: Add one clause to the ALLIED dossier note naming the structural failure: e.g.,
"It outnumbered the garrison better than two to one and was beaten anyway — partly because
the hurricane clock forced every decision, partly because the one water door the garrison
needed was left unguarded between the French navy and the American army." This matches the
section-2 prose and distributes the reading honestly at the dossier level, where it needs
to stand on its own.

**2. "Into this rode Casimir Pulaski" functions as a hero beat that the section prose does
not earn by the record.**

Section 3 opens the Pulaski paragraph:
> "Into this rode Casimir Pulaski (American). He had been waiting with about 200 horse to
> charge through the breach the infantry was supposed to open, and when no breach came he
> rode forward into the smoke between the redoubts, by most accounts trying to rally the
> wavering infantry and find a gap."

The pack's instruction on Pulaski is: his charge is HIGH on the wounding; "by most
accounts" covers the rally/gap motive; no last words; the burial dispute is the section-6
beat. All of that is followed. But the framing problem is the OPENING CLAUSE "Into this
rode" — the section is performing the very hero-worship the pack is designed to prevent.
"Into this rode" is a tableau opening: it frames a man charging into carnage to be
heroic. The pack nowhere instructs a heroic tableau; it calls for "by most accounts trying
to rally the wavering infantry and find a gap" — a motive hedge, not a destiny beat.

The rest of the paragraph walks it back correctly: he is "cut down," he dies "about two
days later," and the burial becomes the section's post-battle focus. But the opening
clause does work the pack doesn't authorize: it turns the charge into a heroic entrance
rather than the chaotic, costly, unsuccessful action it was (the breach never opened; the
charge did not change the outcome; Pulaski died without accomplishing the tactical object).

FIX: Flatten the entry. "Into this rode" → "Into the same smoke rode Casimir Pulaski
(American)" still names the movement but removes the tableau. Or: "Casimir Pulaski
(American) had been waiting with about 200 horse for the breach the infantry was supposed
to open. When no breach came, he rode forward into the smoke between the redoubts —
by most accounts trying to rally the wavering infantry and find a gap. Grapeshot cut him
down." Direct statement of what happened, in sequence, without the rhetorical flourish
that makes it a tableau. The "by most accounts" hedge lands harder when it's not buried
behind a heroic opener.

**3. The outcome text re-seals the recrimination around d'Estaing rather than around the
alliance.**

The outcome's final paragraph:
> "Sir Henry Clinton called the news 'the greatest event that has happened the whole war,'
> and acted on it: with the French fleet gone he sailed south himself in December, besieged
> Charleston, and took it in May 1780 along with Lincoln and some 5,000 men, the largest
> American surrender of the war."

Preceding that, the outcome text says:
> "Franco-American relations hit their wartime low, the second straight joint operation
> after Newport to end in recrimination."

The pack's §6 says: "Franco-American relations hit their wartime low — the second straight
joint operation (after Newport 1778) to end in recrimination." The draft has this. But the
recrimination is mentioned only as a state ("hit their wartime low") without naming what
the recrimination was about. The pack has already established (Section 2) that d'Estaing
summoned in the name of "the King of France" without Lincoln and the wording was a
"lasting American grievance," and the section-3 prose says the alliance "held, but barely."
At the outcome level, the reader is left with "ended in recrimination" plus the correct
structural point (the fleet leaving enabled Clinton) but no clarity about WHAT was
recriminated and by whom.

The framing risk here is that "the second straight joint operation to end in recrimination"
naturally reads as "the French left again and the Americans complained again" — pointing
the blame at d'Estaing and France, when the pack's honest framing is mutual: d'Estaing
summoned ahead of Lincoln, the allies jointly failed to guard the water door, and the
assault plan was betrayed on the American side. The recrimination ran both ways.

FIX: Add one clause naming the bilateral nature of the breakdown: e.g., "Franco-American
relations hit their wartime low — both sides blamed the other, the Americans for being
summoned in France's name without their counsel and the French for an army they supplied
most of the blood to defend. The bruised alliance held, but barely." The Section 3 prose
already has "the bruised alliance held, but barely" — the outcome section should carry the
same framing rather than leaving the recrimination as a vague state.

## POLISH

**4. The Section 1 "Meanwhile" beat's framing of the Chasseurs' free-men-of-color status
slides briefly into a passive-voice construction that softens the colonial contradiction.**

The MEANWHILE in Section 1 reads:
> "The free men of color who filled the ranks of the Chasseurs came from a colony built on
> enslaved sugar labor, where their own status was hedged about with restrictions, marching
> now to help one set of colonists win a freedom their own home would have to fight for a
> decade later."

This is good framing overall — it names the contradiction, it's honest about "their own
home would have to fight for it." But "their own status was hedged about with restrictions"
is passive and vague in a context where the pack's framing calls for the contradictions to
be named with some specificity. "Hedged about with restrictions" is a bureaucratic phrase
for what was a legally enforced subordination in the Code Noir: free people of color in
Saint-Domingue were free but legally excluded from most civil rights, prohibited from
sitting with white colonists, from certain occupations, from marrying whites. The phrase
erases the sharpness of the contradiction these men marched into.

This is a POLISH, not a should-fix, because the broader sentence names the structural
irony correctly and the pack does not prescribe the specific language. But the reviser
should consider whether "hedged about with restrictions" is doing the contradiction's
weight justice or is softening it into something vaguer than the record warrants.
SUGGESTION: "where their own legal status sat below the white colonists they lived beside,
marching now to help one set of colonists win a freedom their own home would have to fight
for a decade later." Or any formulation that names the hierarchy specifically rather than
abstractly.
