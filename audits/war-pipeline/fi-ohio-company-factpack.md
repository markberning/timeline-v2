# FACT PACK — "The Ohio Country & the Ohio Company" (1748–1754)

**Vertical:** war (French & Indian War) · **kind:** theme (CAUSE / off-the-battlefield)
**Thesis to support with facts:** the first cause of the French & Indian War was a land grab — a Virginia real-estate syndicate's royal grant over land France claimed and Native nations actually lived on — and the Ohio nations were first-class actors with their own aims, not props.

## 1. THE OHIO COUNTRY DEFINED — and who actually lived there

- **Geography.** The Ohio Country was "a loosely defined region of colonial North America west of the Appalachian Mountains and south of Lake Erie" — the upper Ohio River valley. A 1752 trader's map put its rough bounds from the Wabash River (west) to the Thirteen Colonies (east), Lake Erie (north), the Cumberland River (south). [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*]
- **Strategic logic for France.** For New France the valley was the land bridge connecting Canada (the St. Lawrence) to the Illinois Country and Louisiana (the Mississippi). A wall of British settlement at the Forks of the Ohio would cut that link. The four French forts of 1753–54 "represented the last links in France's effort to connect its dominions in Canada with those in the Illinois Country and Louisiana." [Heinz History Center, *Constructing the French Forts of the Ohio Country*]

### The Native nations resident in the Ohio Country (mid-1700s)
The region had been emptied during the 17th-century **Beaver Wars**, when the Iroquois Confederacy drove out or absorbed rivals and held it largely as a hunting ground; Native peoples then **re-settled it from the 1720s, moving west precisely to get distance from Anglo-French colonial pressure.** [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*]

- **Shawnee** — Algonquian-speaking; "began to return to the Ohio Country in the decades that followed" earlier displacement. [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*]
- **Delaware / Lenape** — pushed west out of the Delaware/Susquehanna valleys; by 1724 they had founded the village of **Kittanning** on the Allegheny River. [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*]
- **Mingo** — Iroquois (largely Seneca/Cayuga) who had migrated south/west to the Ohio Country, semi-independent of the home Confederacy councils. (Tanaghrisson is the leading example — see §7.) [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*; *Tanacharison*]
- **Wyandot (Huron/Wendat)** — present in the region; included in the brief's roster of Ohio nations. *(Author note: I confirmed Shawnee, Delaware, Mingo, and the Iroquois/Seneca strongly in primary-leaning sources; the Wyandot presence is standard in the scholarship but I did not pull a single tight source line for them this pass. Safe to name them as resident; do not attach specific numbers/leaders to them without a source.)*
- **Migration driver (the human heart of §1).** These nations relocated westward "driven by pressure from encroaching European colonists." [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*] — i.e., they had already moved once to escape colonists, and the Ohio Company's whole purpose was to follow them west and take the new land too. This is the foreshadowing of dispossession the brief asks for, and it is sourced, not inferred.

## 2. THE OHIO COMPANY OF VIRGINIA — organization, royal grant, conditions, investors

- **Organized:** **1748**, by **Thomas Lee** and the brothers **Lawrence and Augustine Washington**, fronting Virginia (and some Maryland/British) investors' interest in the Ohio Country. [Wikipedia, *Ohio Company*]
- **The petition & royal instructions.** A petition went to **King George II**. *(NOTE — kill the common error: one secondary source says "King George I"; George I died in 1727. The monarch was **George II**. The instruction document and the Privy Council action both date to 1748/49, in George II's reign. Use George II.)* Royal **instructions dated 16 March 1748/9** went to Governor **Sir William Gooch** of Virginia to make the grant to **John Hanbury of London, merchant, and Thomas Lee**. [America's Best History timeline / virginiaplaces.org, citing the royal instructions]
- **Acreage & two-part structure.** The Crown granted **500,000 acres** in the Ohio Valley between the **Kanawha** and the **Monongahela**. Structured in two parts: **200,000 acres** up front, with a further **300,000 acres** to follow **if** the Company settled **100 families within seven years**. [Wikipedia, *Ohio Company*]
- **Conditions (the load-bearing detail).** The Company had to: **(a)** seat **100 families** on the land within **seven years**; **(b)** **build a fort and maintain a garrison at its own expense** to protect the settlement. In exchange the land was **rent- and tax-free (free of quitrent) for ten years**. [Wikipedia, *Ohio Company*; virginiaplaces.org]
- **Grant approved/issued:** after Privy Council and Board of Trade deliberation, **July 13, 1749** (some sources July 12, 1749 — one-day variance in secondary accounts). [virginiaplaces.org; American History Central] `[CONTESTED — trivial: 12 vs 13 July 1749; use "mid-July 1749" or "13 July 1749."]`
- **Investors (named shareholders):** **Thomas Lee** (organizer), **Lawrence Washington** and **Augustine Washington**, **George Washington** (the future president, a shareholder), **Robert Dinwiddie** (Lt. Governor / "Royal Governor" of Virginia — an investor; this is the conflict of interest, see §6), **George Mason** (became treasurer **1752**), **John Mercer** and his sons, **Robert Carter III**, **John Tayloe II**, and the London merchant **John Hanbury** (who led the lobbying of the Privy Council). [Wikipedia, *Ohio Company*]
- **The Company's foothold on the ground:** it built a **fortified storehouse** at **Wills Creek / Ridgeley** (in present-day WV/MD border country) **c. 1750** as a forward base. [Wikipedia, *Ohio Company*]

> **Framing fact for the thesis:** the syndicate's purpose was real estate — to win Native land cheap (or by treaty paper) and sell it dear to settlers. Its own grant *required* it to plant 100 settler families and a fort on land where Shawnee, Delaware, Mingo and Wyandot were living. The "settlement condition" and the "dispossession" are the same fact stated two ways.

## 3. THE SPECULATIVE LAND RUSH — the rivals and why the Ohio was the prize

- **The Loyal Company of Virginia** — a rival speculator syndicate organized **about the same time** as the Ohio Company; investors included **Dr. Thomas Walker**, **William Cabell**, and **Peter Jefferson** (father of Thomas Jefferson). [virginiaplaces.org / American History Central] *(Author note: I did not nail a single clean source line for the Loyal Company's exact acreage/date this pass — secondary figures circulate as 800,000 acres granted 1749. Name the Loyal Company as the Ohio Company's rival and list Walker/Cabell/Peter Jefferson; do NOT state a precise Loyal Company acreage without a tighter source.)* `[GAP — Loyal Company acreage/date unverified this pass]`
- **George Croghan** — the Pennsylvania-based Irish frontier **trader** ("king of the traders"), the British Empire's main man among the Ohio Indians. In **August 1749** Croghan purchased **200,000 acres** from the Iroquois, **excepting two square miles at the Forks of the Ohio reserved for a British fort.** Because such Indian-purchase deeds were **invalid under Pennsylvania statute** (which barred private purchases from Natives) but tolerable under Virginia practice, Croghan ended up helping **Virginia's** Ohio Company — by **1750** he and the interpreter **Andrew Montour** were guiding the Company's scout **Christopher Gist** on a tour of the Ohio Indian villages. [Wikipedia, *George Croghan*; ohiocountry.us]
- **Why the Ohio was the prize:** richest unclaimed farmland west of the mountains; the **Forks of the Ohio** (where the Allegheny + Monongahela form the Ohio) commanded river traffic in every direction — the single most valuable point in the interior for both empires (see §8).

## 4. FRANCE'S COUNTER-CLAIM — Céloron de Blainville's 1749 lead-plate expedition

- **Commander:** **Pierre-Joseph Céloron de Blainville** (also spelled Céloron de Bienville/Blainville). [Wikipedia, *Pierre Joseph Céloron de Blainville*]
- **Departed:** **June 15, 1749**, from **Montreal**. Force: **~216 French Canadians and ~55 Native Americans** (sources also give the round figure "about 270"). [Wikipedia]
- **Mission:** reassert French sovereignty over the whole Ohio watershed and **expel British traders** from it.
- **The lead plates.** At the mouths of principal tributaries the expedition **buried inscribed lead plates** (and nailed the French royal arms — a tin/copper plate — to a tree nearby) claiming the land for King Louis XV. At least **six** markers were placed in summer 1749; well-documented burials include the confluence with the Chadakoin (**July 29, 1749**), the mouth of the **Muskingum** (**August 15, 1749**), and the mouth of the **Kanawha** (**August 18, 1749**). The plates were roughly **11 in × 7.5 in** lead. [Wikipedia; West Virginia Public Broadcasting; Encyclopedia Virginia, *Céloron Plate*]
  - **Verbatim from the plate text** (renewal-of-possession formula): claiming "**the renewal of the possession we have taken of the said river Ohio and of all those which empty into it, and of all the lands on both sides as far as the sources**." [Encyclopedia Virginia / WV Public Broadcasting]
- **The reception (Native + British).** At **Logstown** Céloron found English traders and ordered them out; at **Lower Shawneetown** (~Aug 25) he again summoned and warned traders to leave. His high-handedness **offended even the Iroquois traveling with him** — some peeled off for home, **tearing the nailed plates back down as they went.** [Wikipedia, *Céloron*]
- **Outcome / Céloron's own verdict.** Expedition returned to Montreal **November 10, 1749**. The lead plates changed nothing on the ground: British traders stayed, Ohio Indians were not won over. Céloron reported to the governor that the nations of the Ohio were "**very badly disposed towards the French, and devoted entirely to the English**" (standard paraphrase of his report). *(Author note: that last is the widely-cited gist of Céloron's report; treat as paraphrase unless you want me to pull the exact translated line. The hard facts — dates, route, plates — are solid.)* `[Use as paraphrase, not as a quotation, unless re-verified]`

## 5. THE FRENCH FORT CHAIN — 1753–1754

Built to wall the British out of the Ohio and run a line from Lake Erie to the Forks. All four shared a design: a **rectangle ~75 ft × 105 ft, a bastion at each corner.** [Heinz History Center]

| Fort | Location (modern) | Construction began |
|---|---|---|
| **Fort Presque Isle** | Erie, PA (on Lake Erie) | **May 15, 1753** |
| **Fort Le Bœuf** | Waterford, PA (on French Creek / Rivière aux Bœufs) | **July 11, 1753** |
| **Fort Machault** | Franklin, PA (mouth of French Creek at the Allegheny / "Venango") | **August 1753** (work delayed for the others) |
| **Fort Duquesne** | the **Forks of the Ohio** (Pittsburgh) | **April 1754** (see §8) |

[Heinz History Center; Wikipedia, *Fort Le Boeuf*, *Fort Machault*, *Fort Duquesne*]

- **Fort Le Bœuf is the diplomatic stage** for Washington's 1753 mission (§6): the French commandant **Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre** received him there.

## 6. DINWIDDIE'S CONFLICT OF INTEREST — Washington's 1753 ultimatum mission

- **The conflict of interest (the spine of this beat).** **Robert Dinwiddie**, Lieutenant Governor of Virginia, was **himself an Ohio Company shareholder** (named investor, §2). The man dispatching an imperial ultimatum to France over the Ohio had a private financial stake in who got the Ohio. [Wikipedia, *Ohio Company*]
- **The envoy:** **George Washington**, age **21**, a major / adjutant of the Virginia militia. **Commissioned 30 October 1753** by Dinwiddie; left Williamsburg ~**31 October 1753**. [American Battlefield Trust; Founders Online, *Commission/Instructions from Robert Dinwiddie, 30 October 1753*]
- **The party:** Washington hired **Jacob Van Braam** as French interpreter (at Fredericksburg); picked up Ohio Company scout/surveyor **Christopher Gist** at Wills Creek; plus four frontier traders/baggage men, and Native escorts. [American Battlefield Trust; Mount Vernon]
- **Dinwiddie's instructions (gist, sourced):** proceed to the French forces on the Ohio, **present credentials and deliver Dinwiddie's letter to the senior French officer, demand an answer**, and **observe the French forts, forces, and intentions** en route. [Founders Online, *Instructions from Robert Dinwiddie, 30 October 1753*] *(Author note: the document is on Founders Online; I confirmed its existence and gist via the editorial apparatus. If you want an exact verbatim instruction line, ask me to re-pull document 02-01-02-0029 — my fetch of the raw text 403'd this pass.)* `[Verbatim instruction line available on request — Founders 02-01-02-0029]`
- **Arrival & meeting:** Washington's party reached **Fort Le Bœuf the evening of December 11, 1753**, escorted by **Tanaghrisson (the Half-King)** and other chiefs; met the commandant the next day. [American Battlefield Trust; NPS]
- **The French commandant:** **Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre**. His **written reply is dated "From the fort on the Rivière aux Bœufs, December 15, 1753," signed LEGARDEUR DE SAINT-PIERRE.** [explorepahistory.com, original document]
- **VERBATIM — Saint-Pierre's refusal** (the money quote of the beat):
  > "**As to the summons you send me to retire, I do not think myself obliged to obey it.**"
  He added that his orders came from his commanding general and that he would carry them out "with all the exactness and determination which can be expected from a good officer," and that he knew of nothing in the campaign "which can be construed as an act of hostility, or as contrary to the treaties between the two Crowns." [explorepahistory.com, French Commander's Reply to Gov. Dinwiddie, Dec. 15, 1753]
- **Washington left Fort Le Bœuf December 16, 1753**, reached **Williamsburg ~mid-January 1754**, and handed Dinwiddie the reply **plus his expedition journal.** [American Battlefield Trust]
- **The journal was published** — *The Journal of Major George Washington* (Williamsburg, 1754) — and made the unknown 21-year-old internationally known. [Mount Vernon; UNL Digital Commons, *The Journal of Major George Washington (1754)*]

## 7. TANAGHRISSON THE HALF-KING, LOGSTOWN, AND THE IROQUOIS-VS-RESIDENT-NATIONS PROBLEM

This is the section where the Ohio nations must read as actors with their own strategy, not props. The facts support that fully.

- **Who he was.** **Tanaghrisson / Tanacharison**, "the **Half-King**," a **Mingo** leader (Iroquois — Seneca — settled in the Ohio Country); first appears in the record at **Logstown in 1747**. The British called him the "Half King." [Wikipedia, *Tanacharison*; NPS]
  - `[CONTESTED — his origin]` The French claimed he was **born Catawba** (a Carolina nation) and taken captive/adopted by the Seneca as a child; historian **Fred Anderson** repeats the Catawba-origin claim. Treat origin as disputed; his *functioning* identity was Mingo/Seneca spokesman in the Ohio. [Wikipedia, *Tanacharison*; NPS]
  - `[CONTESTED — the title "Half King"]` Traditional reading: he was a deputy appointed by the Iroquois Grand Council to oversee the Ohio peoples. **Modern historians argue "Half King" was largely a British coinage** for a leader whose real authority over the resident Ohio nations was thinner than the British wanted to believe. Hold both. [Wikipedia, *Tanacharison*]
- **The Iroquois claim vs. the resident nations — the core tension to dramatize.** The **Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee / Six Nations)** claimed the Ohio Country **"by right of conquest"** from the Beaver Wars and treated it as theirs to negotiate away. But the people **actually living there** — Shawnee, Delaware, Mingo, Wyandot — did **not** consider the Iroquois entitled to sell their ground out from under them. The British liked dealing through the Iroquois (and through "Half King" Tanaghrisson) precisely because it produced land cessions the resident nations never agreed to. [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*, *Tanacharison*, *Logstown*]
- **The Treaty of Logstown, June 1–13, 1752.** At **Logstown** (east bank of the Ohio, ~14 mi below the Forks, near present **Ambridge, PA**), Virginia/Ohio-Company commissioners met the Six Nations. **Virginia's commissioners:** **Joshua Fry, Lunsford Lomax, James Patton.** **Ohio Company representatives:** **Christopher Gist, William Trent, William Beverley.** [Wikipedia, *Logstown*; fiwar.wikidot]
  - **The British aim:** get the Indians to **confirm the 1744 Treaty of Lancaster** (which the British read as ceding land beyond the Alleghenies) **and** consent to a Company fort and settlements.
  - **What Tanaghrisson actually conceded — and refused.** He **denied** that Lancaster (1744) had ever ceded any land **beyond the Allegheny Mountains**; the Six Nations would **not** confirm Virginia ownership of the lands the Company meant to sell. **Under pressure he did consent to a British "strong house"/fort at the mouth of the Monongahela** (the Forks) and promised the Iroquois would not molest English settlements **southeast of the Ohio** — but he did **not** sign away the Ohio nations' country. [Wikipedia, *Logstown*; fiwar.wikidot]
  - As early as **1752** Tanaghrisson had advised the Virginians to build a **"strong house" at the Forks of the Ohio.** [NPS]
- **VERBATIM — Tanaghrisson to the French commandant (recited to Washington, recorded in Washington's 1753 Journal).** This is the centerpiece quote — a resident-nation leader telling both empires the land is neither one's:
  > "**If you had come in a peaceable Manner like our Brethren the English, we shou'd not have been against your trading with us as they do, but to come, Fathers, & build great houses upon our Land, & to take it by Force, is what we cannot submit to.**" [George Washington, *The Journal of Major George Washington* (1754)]
  > "**Both you & the English are White. We live in a Country between; therefore the Land does not belong either to one or the other; but the GREAT BEING above allow'd it to be a Place of Residence for us.**" [George Washington, *Journal* (1754)]
  - Longer related passage he recited (the "silver basin" reproach to the French): "Fathers, I am come to tell you your own speeches... You, in former days, set a silver basin before us... Now, fathers, it is you who are the **disturbers in this land, by coming and building your towns; and taking it away unknown to us, and by force.**" [Washington's *Journal* (1754); also in *Writings of George Washington*]
  - **Provenance note for the author:** Tanaghrisson is "the person whom Washington quotes more than any other in his Journal." These are his words **as recorded by Washington** — i.e., filtered through a British envoy's transcription/translation; flag that lightly if you lean on them hard, but they are the genuine primary record and are exactly the Native-voice material the brief wants. [Mount Vernon]
- **Tanaghrisson's later acts (for foreshadowing only, not this section's core):** he guided Washington in 1754 and, at the **Jumonville Glen** skirmish (**May 28, 1754**) — the event that triggered open war — he is reported to have killed the wounded French officer **Joseph Coulon de Jumonville**, reportedly saying "**Tu n'es pas encore mort, mon père**" ("Thou art not yet dead, my father") `[CONTESTED — accounts of the killing are disputed and contradictory]`. He **died of pneumonia October 4, 1754** at Paxtang/Harris's Ferry (Harrisburg), PA. [Wikipedia, *Tanacharison*; NPS]

## 8. THE FORKS OF THE OHIO — the prize, the small fort, and Fort Duquesne (1754)

- **The prize.** The **Forks of the Ohio** — where the **Allegheny** and **Monongahela** join to form the **Ohio** (modern downtown **Pittsburgh**) — commanded river movement in every direction and was the keystone of the whole valley. The Ohio Company's grant specifically obliged it to build its fort here.
- **VERBATIM — Washington's own assessment of the Forks (1753 Journal),** as he scouted the site:
  > "**As I got down before the Canoe, I spent some Time in viewing the Rivers, & the Land in the Fork; which I think extremely well situated for a Fort, as it has the absolute Command of both Rivers.**" [George Washington, *The Journal of Major George Washington* (1754)]
- **The Virginia/Ohio-Company fort goes up.** On **17 February 1754**, a Virginia work party (under **Captain William Trent**, with **Ensign Edward Ward** in command on site) began a small fort at the Forks — **Fort Prince George** (a.k.a. **Trent's Fort**). [Wikipedia, *Fort Duquesne*]
- **The French seize it (spring 1754).** On **April 18, 1754**, a much larger French force of **~500 men** under **Claude-Pierre Pécaudy de Contrecœur** arrived at the Forks and forced the tiny British garrison (Ensign **Edward Ward**) to surrender without a fight. [Wikipedia, *Fort Duquesne*]
- **Fort Duquesne built.** The French tore down the British work and built **Fort Duquesne**, named for **Ange Duquesne de Menneville, Marquis Duquesne**, governor-general of New France. It became the French anchor of the upper Ohio — and the objective of Braddock's doomed 1755 march. [Wikipedia, *Fort Duquesne*]

> **The payoff line for the thesis (all sourced above):** the Ohio Company's grant ordered it to plant a fort at the Forks; France built its own fort on that exact spot first; the first shots of a world war were fired over who owned ground that Shawnee, Delaware, Mingo and Wyandot families were already living on. The real-estate venture and the war are the same story.

## 9. THE HUMAN COST / DISPOSSESSION FRAMING (all drawn from sourced facts above)

- The Ohio nations had **already migrated west once** to escape colonial encroachment (§1) — and the Ohio Company's chartered purpose was to **follow them and take the new land too** (settle 100 families + sell the rest, §2).
- Their **own aim** was not to pick a European master but to **keep both empires at arm's length and hold their ground** — stated in Tanaghrisson's own recorded words: "the Land does not belong either to one or the other... a Place of Residence for us" (§7).
- Their **leverage** was real but bounded: they could deny land cessions (Logstown 1752), expel or admit traders, and tilt the balance between the empires — but both empires were now building forts on their country regardless. The dispossession the brief asks to foreshadow is **already visible in 1754**, before the war is even fully lit.

### S1. PICKAWILLANY — Memeskia ("Old Briton" / "La Demoiselle") and the 21 June 1752 raid (the must-add)

This is the opening blow the brief was missing: France using force to keep the Ohio nations in line, and Britain unable to protect a Native trading partner it had courted. It belongs near §3 (Croghan / the trade race) and feeds §7 (the resident nations as actors) — Memeskia is the same story as Tanaghrisson, one chapter earlier and ending in death.

- **The town.** **Pickawillany** was a **Miami (Twightwee) village and trading town** on the **west bank of the Great Miami River**, near present-day **Piqua, Ohio**. It was **founded in late 1747** by **Memeskia**, who led Miami people there after breaking with the French. By **1750 it had grown to roughly 400 families** and became **the largest, most prominent center of British trade in the Ohio Country** — English traders there undersold the French and pulled neighboring nations away from the French alliance. [Wikipedia, *Pickawillany*]
- **The British trading post.** **George Croghan** (the Pennsylvania trader of §3) established a **trading post alongside the Miami village in November 1749**; a **fortified stockade ("Fort Pickawillany") followed in the fall of 1750** to protect the English goods and traders. The traders operating there were **Pennsylvanians** — which ties the raid to the PA-vs-VA claim in §S2. [Wikipedia, *Pickawillany*]
- **Memeskia — the man and the names.** **Memeskia** (Miami-Illinois *Meemeehšihkia* / *Memeskia*, meaning **"dragonfly"**; also rendered **Unemakemi**). The British called him **"Old Briton"** for his pro-British alignment; the French called him **"La Demoiselle."** He is described as **a Miami Indian and a Piankashaw (Piankeshaw) chieftain**. He had **rebelled against the French in 1747**, opposing the French fur-trade **monopoly and lower barter prices**, and **turned to British/Pennsylvania traders** for better goods and prices; he **signed a friendship treaty with George Croghan in November 1750**, cemented by Christopher Gist's visit in **February 1751**. (Standard forms to use: **Memeskia**, with "Old Briton"/"La Demoiselle"; *Unemakemi* is an alternate.) [Wikipedia, *Memeskia*; Wikipedia, *Pickawillany*]
- **The raid — date, leader, force.** On **21 June 1752**, **Charles Michel de Langlade** — a **Métis** *coureur de bois* and French-allied officer, **about 23 years old** — led the attack. **Force figure to use: ~240.** The detailed account: Langlade set out with **272 Ottawa**, reached Detroit, and **about 30 deserted**, so he **arrived at Pickawillany with roughly 240 warriors** (the round figure cited corpus-wide is **"about 240"**). The war party was **Ottawa and Ojibwe (Chippewa), with some Potawatomi**, drawn from the Great Lakes. They struck **around 9 a.m.**, when **most of the Miami men were away hunting.** [Wikipedia, *Pickawillany*; Wikipedia, *Memeskia*]
  - **Author note on the number:** use **"about 240"** (the force that actually reached the town). If you want the fuller texture, "~272 set out, ~30 deserted, ~240 arrived" is accurate. Do not write "240 Ottawa and Ojibwe" as a flat exact count — it's an approximation.
- **What happened in the town.** The raiders **killed at least 13 Miami men** and **captured several women** (sources give "four women"); **one English trader (a blacksmith) was killed**; **several British traders were captured** (figures cited: up to five captured; the traders **Thomas Burney and Andrew McBryer escaped**). The English **stockade and trading post were looted and burned.** [Wikipedia, *Pickawillany*]
- **Memeskia's death** `[CONTESTED — manner of death]`. **All sources agree Memeskia was killed in the raid.** The **manner is the contested/graphic part:** Wikipedia's *Pickawillany* article states the attackers **"seized the Miami chief Memeskia, and he was killed, boiled and eaten 'within a hundred yards of the fort' in front of his Miami men."** Wikipedia's *Memeskia* article frames it as ritual: **"Memeskia, to symbolize the victors' extreme distaste for his friendship with the English, was boiled and ritually cannibalized."** **Treatment for the author:** the killing is settled; the **boiled-and-eaten / ritual-cannibalism detail is reported by the standard sources but is a graphic claim — present it as what the sources report ("reportedly," attributed), not as bare narrated fact, and frame it as the attackers' deliberate symbolic act against his pro-British stance.** Do not embellish beyond the cited wording. [Wikipedia, *Pickawillany*; Wikipedia, *Memeskia*]
- **Aftermath / why it matters.** The town was **destroyed and burned**; the **surviving Miami abandoned Pickawillany** (relocating ~2 miles southwest, then drifting **back toward the French orbit**). The raid **drove British traders out of that part of the Ohio Country** and is treated as **directly preceding — an opening action of — the broader French and Indian War.** Its meaning for the thesis: France would **use force** to keep the Ohio nations aligned, and Britain **could not protect its Native trading partners**, even its most prominent one. [Wikipedia, *Pickawillany*; Wikipedia, *Memeskia*]

> **Placement note for the author:** Pickawillany (June 1752) and the Treaty of Logstown (June 1752, §7) happen the *same month*. The contrast is the beat: at Logstown the British are paper-negotiating a fort with the Iroquois, while at Pickawillany the French are answering the British trade push with fire and a chief's death. Both are June 1752.

### S2. THE PENNSYLVANIA-vs-VIRGINIA JURISDICTIONAL RIVALRY over the Ohio Country (should-fix)

- **Both colonies claimed the upper Ohio under overlapping sea-to-sea charters.** The dispute "arose out of ambiguities in the **1609 and 1681 charters of Virginia and Pennsylvania**." **Pennsylvania** claimed the land as a **westward extension of its colonial boundaries**; **Virginia** claimed **the entire region under the charter of the Virginia Colony.** The **crux** of the rivalry was **who would hold the Forks of the Ohio (modern Pittsburgh)** — the same prize as §8. [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*]
- **The rivalry shaped the land race.** Virginia speculators chartered the **Ohio Company** (§2); Pennsylvania's interest ran through its **traders** — **George Croghan** and the Pennsylvania men at **Pickawillany** (§S1). (The boundary stayed unsettled for decades — open Virginia-vs-Pennsylvania friction over this ground ran into the **1774–75** period and was only fixed by survey in **1784** — but for *this* section the load-bearing fact is simply that **two British colonies both claimed the Ohio**, which is why a Pennsylvania trade town and a Virginia land company were both pushing into it at once.) [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*; virginiaplaces.org, *Virginia-Pennsylvania Boundary*]
- **One-to-two sentences the author can use (sourced):** *Even on the British side the Ohio was contested ground: Virginia claimed it under its old sea-to-sea charter and Pennsylvania claimed it as a westward extension of its own — so the land race was not just Britain against France but Virginia's Ohio Company against Pennsylvania's traders, both reaching for the same Forks.* [Wikipedia, *Ohio Country*]

### S3. ANDREW MONTOUR as interpreter at the 1752 Treaty of Logstown (should-fix — CONFIRMED)

- **Confirmed: Andrew Montour was the interpreter at the June 1752 Logstown council.** The **primary treaty record** (*The Treaty of Logg's Town, 1752*, printed in *Virginia Magazine of History and Biography* 13 (1906), 154–174; UNL Early Treaties digital edition) lists **"Mr. Andrew Montour, Interpreter"** among the council participants (sessions of **May 29 / June 1, 1752**), and records the **Half-King (Tanaghrisson)** addressing him by his **Indian name Eghnisara**: *"Child, remember that you are one of our own People... you are Interpreter between us and our Brethren, which we are well pleased at."* This is the same Logstown council in §7 (Virginia commissioners Fry/Lomax/Patton; Ohio Company's Gist/Trent; the Six Nations; June 1–13, 1752 — the formal proceedings ran June 10–13). [UNL Early Treaties, *Treaty of Logstown 1752* (treaty.00004); fiwar.wikidot, *Treaty of Logstown 1752*]
- **Who he was.** **Andrew Montour** (c. 1710–1772), also known as **Sattelihu**, **Eghnisara**, and **Henry**; of **Oneida and Algonquin ancestry with a French grandfather**; a leading **interpreter/intermediary** in the Pennsylvania-Virginia backcountry. The draft already has him guiding Gist's 1750 tour (§3); this confirms he was **also the working interpreter at Logstown 1752**, and the Half-King treated him as **more than a translator — a person with standing in the council.** [UNL Early Treaties, *Treaty of Logstown 1752*; Wikipedia, *Andrew Montour*]

The confirmation comes from the **primary treaty text** (the 1906 VMHB printing / UNL Early Treaties) and the fiwar secondary summary, both of which name him explicitly as Interpreter.
