## Assessment: COVERED / THIN / MISSING

### COVERED (well)
The draft is genuinely strong across its core spine. The Wilmot Proviso vote-breakdown analysis is excellent — exactly right that it is the first moment to watch. The Compromise of 1850 section handles all five components, the three-way drama of Clay/Calhoun/Webster, and Douglas's tactical split. Kansas–Nebraska, the party collapse, Bleeding Kansas, the Caning, Dred Scott, the ideological architecture, and Harpers Ferry are all handled with appropriate weight and good narrative economy. The "Slave Power" as a unifying interpretive frame is woven in well. The handoff logic is clean.

### THIN

**4. Free Soil Party (1848)**
The draft names Free Soilers as a component of the Republican coalition (correct) but gives the party no prior existence as a political force in its own right. The Free Soil Party ran a *former president* (Van Buren) in 1848, won 10% of the popular vote — the strongest third-party performance in U.S. history to that point — and proved, four years before Kansas–Nebraska, that anti-expansion politics could organize at the national level. More importantly, it was the first party to make "Slave Power" a formal campaign concept. Without this, the Republican Party's 1854 founding looks more sudden than it was: the Free Soilers gave it its platform, much of its early leadership (Chase, Wilson), and proof of concept. The draft's silence makes the GOP seem to erupt from nothing.

**26. Economic divergence**
The draft covers "King Cotton" as a Southern confidence doctrine but gives almost nothing on the structural economic difference between the two halves. The industrializing, wage-labor North and the slave-cotton South were not just ideologically opposed — they were building incompatible economies. Northern workers had begun to articulate "free labor" ideology (the idea that wage labor was dignified and upwardly mobile, and that slave labor directly threatened it by undercutting free workers). This is the economic engine underneath the political arguments. The Panic of 1857 beat handles the symptom (Southern confidence) but not the underlying divergence. A brief pass here would ground the ideological conflict in something concrete.

**28. Kansas final endgame**
The draft ends the Bleeding Kansas section with the territory "spiraling into raid and reprisal" — accurate, but it leaves the reader wondering: what actually happened to Kansas? The Lecompton Constitution's ultimate rejection by Kansas voters (lopsidedly, roughly 6-to-1) and Kansas finally entering the Union as a free state in 1861 under the Wyandotte Constitution would give the section a satisfying factual resolution. This is genuinely secondary to the Lecompton issue below, and on its own would be SHOULD-CONSIDER — but it is entangled with item 14.

### MISSING

**14. Lecompton Constitution crisis (1857–1858)**
This is the most consequential omission. The Lecompton Constitution was the pro-slavery state constitution drafted by Kansas's proslavery territorial government and submitted to Congress by President Buchanan in February 1858, who asked Congress to admit Kansas as a slave state under it. Kansas voters rejected it in August 1858 by roughly 6-to-1, but what made Lecompton historically decisive was what happened *inside* the Democratic Party: **Stephen Douglas broke publicly with Buchanan over it.** Douglas opposed Lecompton not because he was antislavery, but because the process had violated popular sovereignty — the territorial convention had refused to submit the full constitution to a fair vote. This was Douglas's line he would not cross.

The rupture between Douglas and Buchanan over Lecompton was the **beginning of the Democratic Party split** that would produce three separate Democratic factions in the 1860 election and hand Lincoln the presidency. The draft's handoff promises the reader that "the Democrats splitting along the same North–South seam" is what set up 1860 — but never explains *when* or *how* that split began. Lecompton is the answer. Without it, the Democratic fracture looks like it materialized from nowhere at the 1860 convention, rather than being a process that started two years earlier with a specific, documented fight between the party's two most powerful figures.

Additionally, Lecompton produced one of the most revealing congressional votes of the decade: the House voted **120–112** to reject Kansas statehood under Lecompton, with Northern Democrats crossing the aisle to vote with Republicans. That kind of cross-party sectional vote — the very pattern the draft correctly identifies as the "alarm bell" — is nowhere illustrated after Wilmot (1846). Lecompton would give readers a second concrete example of the alarm bell ringing at a higher pitch, just before Harpers Ferry ends the section.

**27. Nashville Convention (1850)**
The Nashville Convention — nine slave-state delegates meeting in June 1850 to consider secession if the South's demands were refused — is the first organized, institutional secession threat in the period. The fire-eaters section (Beat 12) notes Rhett called for secession "as early as 1850," but the draft doesn't explain *in what context* that happened. The Convention is the answer: it was called by Calhoun's allies explicitly to consider leaving the Union if the Compromise of 1850 failed the South. It fizzled when moderates controlled it and most Southerners took the Compromise as a satisfactory outcome — but its existence is important for two reasons: (1) it shows secession was already being organized institutionally a decade before it happened, and (2) its failure shows why the fire-eaters were still considered cranks in 1850. The arc from Nashville fizzle (1850) → fire-eater fringe → Harpers Ferry terror-galvanized-mainstream is the arc of Southern radicalization, and it's currently missing its first data point.

The Nashville Convention is somewhat less load-bearing than Lecompton — Lecompton is essential for explaining the Democratic split; Nashville is important for explaining the arc of Southern radicalization. It is classified MISSING but is borderline SHOULD-CONSIDER. If one of the two Missing items must be cut, Nashville is the one.

## MUST-ADD (essential gaps that block the section)

### MUST-ADD 1 — Lecompton Constitution and the Buchanan–Douglas Break
**Why essential:** The draft promises the reader a Democratic split that produces 1860's four-way election, but never shows where that split started. Lecompton is the moment. Without it, the handoff to "Lincoln's Rise" is missing a causal link. The Lecompton fight also gives readers the clearest post-Wilmot example of the sectional-over-party voting pattern the draft correctly identifies as the country's core structural crisis.

**What to add:** Two to three paragraphs in or after the Bleeding Kansas section: Buchanan endorses the proslavery Lecompton Constitution (February 1858); Douglas breaks with him; the House blocks it; Kansas voters reject it 6-to-1. The significance: the Buchanan–Douglas breach is where the Democratic Party started to fracture along the same North–South seam that had already destroyed the Whigs.

### MUST-ADD 2 — Nashville Convention (1850) as the first organized secession threat
**Why essential:** The fire-eaters section correctly introduces Rhett, Yancey, and Ruffin as a fringe that had been pushing secession "as early as 1850." But the draft never grounds that claim in an event. Without Nashville, the fire-eater advocacy appears to float free, unconnected to any institutional moment. Nashville is that moment — and its failure is as important as its existence, because it explains why the South didn't leave in 1850 and why the fire-eaters remained a fringe for another decade.

**What to add:** One short paragraph, best placed near the end of the Compromise of 1850 section or at the opening of "The Architecture of the Quarrel": the Nashville Convention convened, was dominated by moderates, fizzled — but its existence showed the secession idea was already organized, not just rhetorical.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER (enriching but non-blocking)

**SC-1. Free Soil Party (1848) — one paragraph**
A brief mention of Van Buren's 1848 campaign and the Free Soil platform would make the Republican Party's 1854 founding feel like an evolution rather than a sudden appearance. The Free Soilers proved the anti-expansion coalition was viable; the Republicans scaled it. Currently the draft treats the Republican Party as born from rage at Kansas–Nebraska — true, but incomplete.

**SC-2. Economic divergence / "free labor" ideology — one paragraph**
The draft has "King Cotton" (Southern economic confidence) but no counterpart on the Northern side. The emerging "free labor" ideology — the belief that wage labor was morally superior to slave labor, and that slave labor was an active threat to free workers' ability to compete — gave Northern workers a concrete stake in stopping slavery's expansion. It is the economic foundation of the Republican coalition. A brief treatment would deepen the reader's sense of why ordinary Northern workers, not just antislavery activists, cared about Kansas.

**SC-3. The Kansas endgame — one sentence or clause**
Somewhere in the Kansas or Lecompton material, a brief note that Kansas ultimately entered the Union as a free state in 1861 would close the loop. The reader is left wondering.

**SC-4. Preston Brooks's death (January 1857)**
A minor but interesting detail already in the fact pack (Beat 8) but not in the draft: Brooks died of croup just weeks after returning to the House following his resignation and re-election. He never served out the term. This is small but adds a note of grim irony to the "hero" narrative and costs one clause.

## Fact-Pack Blind Spots

The following are items the fact pack itself is missing or underweights, meaning every downstream agent would inherit the gap:

**BP-1. Lecompton Constitution entirely absent from the fact pack.**
Beat 7 (Bleeding Kansas) mentions the Lecompton Constitution in passing, with congressional vote figures, but does not cover the Buchanan–Douglas break, its role as the first fracture of the Democratic Party, or why it matters for the 1860 split. This is a genuine pack-level omission, not just a draft-level one. A Lecompton addendum beat is needed before prose revision.

**BP-2. Nashville Convention absent from the fact pack.**
The fire-eaters section (Beat 12) references Rhett's 1850 secessionism but does not document the Nashville Convention as the institutional event behind it. Without this, any agent writing the fire-eater section would have no sourced material to work from for the Convention itself.

**BP-3. Free Soil Party 1848 underweighted.**
The fact pack mentions Free Soilers only as a component of the Republican coalition in Beat 6. It gives no sourced material about the 1848 election, Van Buren's 10% showing, or the Free Soil platform's articulation of the "Slave Power" concept. If a revising agent wants to add the Free Soil context (SC-1), there's nothing in the pack to draw from.

**BP-4. "Free labor" ideology as the Northern economic counterpart to King Cotton — absent.**
Beat 10 covers King Cotton and the Panic of 1857 from the Southern side. There is no beat covering the Northern "free labor" ideology — the idea that slave labor threatened free workers' economic dignity and mobility — which was the actual mobilizing argument the Republican Party used with Northern workers (not "slavery is evil" but "slavery threatens YOUR wages and YOUR freedom to move up"). This is the economic engine of Northern political radicalization and it is nowhere in the pack.

**BP-5. Buchanan's role underweighted throughout.**
Buchanan (Democrat, Pennsylvania) is mentioned in the fact pack only in the Lecompton Constitution sidebar in Beat 7. His role as a "doughface" (Northern man with Southern sympathies) who consistently enabled the slave power's agenda in the White House, helping accelerate the crisis rather than contain it, is a significant element of the period's political story. The fact pack's slight of Buchanan means any agent working from it would underweight the presidency as a factor in the road to war.

The draft is genuinely strong — well-structured, selective without being thin, and the ideological architecture section ("The Architecture of the Quarrel") is particularly good.
