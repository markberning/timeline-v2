# Jonesborough — STORYTELLING critic gate

> War pipeline, step 4 (storytelling critic). Judged on hook, momentum, stakes,
> human core, and whether the enormous political/emancipation weight lands.
> No invented facts demanded; where a beat needs a fact the pack lacks, it is
> flagged for the fact pack, never asserted.

**VERDICT: PASS (with 3 MUST-IMPROVE tightenings).** This is one of the strongest
drafts in the war vertical. The spine — last railroad → Hardee's stand → the line
breaks → Atlanta falls → what it decided — is built clean, the stakes are made to
*feel* enormous rather than merely asserted, and the §5 self-emancipation / Ebenezer
Creek turn lands with real gravity and is structurally bound to the battle (the
Davis throughline) instead of bolted on. The must-improves are about pace and a
couple of buried ledes, not about the bones.

---

## Beat-by-beat

### §1 the-last-railroad — the hook / the arithmetic
**GOOD.** The opening does the single hardest thing a battle piece has to do: it
makes a *railroad* feel like a throat. "An army the size of the one outside Atlanta
does not live off the land it stands on; it eats what the trains bring" is the
exact right altitude — concrete, physical, house-voice. "The railroad was the
*object*. Atlanta was the prize" is a clean load-bearing distinction that pays off
four sections later. The spelling aside (Jonesborough vs Jonesboro) is handled in
one parenthetical and gotten out of the way.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE — the cause paragraph (¶4) arrives a beat too early and a beat too
abstract.** "It is worth being plain, before the shooting starts, about what these
two armies were actually fighting over…" is the right instinct, and the payoff
("standing on the roads south of Atlanta in human form… Hold the thought") is a
genuinely good forward-hook. But landing a full slavery-stakes paragraph *before
the reader has met the battle* asks them to hold an abstraction across four
sections. It works because §5 cashes it — but the paragraph itself is the draft's
one stretch of telling-not-showing. Tighten it by ~20% so the promise is sharp and
the reader gets to the shooting faster. Do NOT cut it (it's the framing-gate seed
and the §5 setup) — compress it.

### §1 MEANWHILE box — the four months behind the town
**GOOD.** This is the right place for the campaign backstory: pulled out of the main
line so it doesn't slow the hook, but available for the reader who needs Resaca /
Kennesaw / Peachtree Creek context. The closing line ("a rail line through a 20-mile-
distant town most Northerners had never heard of") re-lands the stakes. Keep.

### §2 hardees-stand — the attack that would not be renewed
**GOOD — this is the human core of Day 1 and it lands.** The "would not make a
second one" beat is the best human moment in the first half: an army that "had
learned, in their bones, the price of running at entrenched men with rifles. They
had paid it once. They would not pay it again." That is showing the cost of the war
through the men, not through a casualty table — exactly right. The ~10:1 casualty
disparity is then delivered as the *confirmation* of what the refusal already told
us emotionally, which is the correct order (feeling first, number second).

**MUST-IMPROVE — the last paragraph (¶6) buries the single most important fact of
the whole battle.** The railroad being cut — "the last line was already severed.
Atlanta, though no one in the city knew it yet, was already lost" — is THE decisive
event of Jonesborough, and right now it's the back half of a paragraph that opens on
Hood's recall order. The reader hits the war-deciding fact mid-sentence, in a
subordinate clause, after a paragraph about a phantom fear. This is the lede of the
entire piece and it's buried. Restructure so the cut of the Macon & Western gets its
own beat and its own breath — the dramatic irony (the city is doomed and no one
knows) is enormous and the current packaging throws it away. This is the draft's
most consequential storytelling miss.

### §3 the-line-breaks — the salient gives way
**GOOD.** The salient is explained through image ("an angle that juts toward the
enemy like the prow of a ship") and then immediately weaponized (fired on / assaulted
from two directions at once), so the reader understands the geometry *and* the dread
at the same time. "Not a position taken, but a brigade taken" is a sharp,
quotable hinge. Govan captured with ~600 men, guns, and colors is the right signal
image and it's earned.

**GOOD — the Cleburne parenthetical.** The forward-flash to Franklin ("three months
later… Cleburne would be killed leading his men against another set of entrenchments")
is a small, well-placed grief note that rhymes with Day 1's lesson about charging
works. It enriches without derailing. Keep.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE — the regiment-roll in ¶4 (Estey's breakthrough) info-dumps.** "the
10th Kentucky and 74th Indiana rolling over the consolidated 6th and 7th Arkansas,
the 14th and 38th Ohio punching clean through" is six regiment numbers in one
sentence. For a zero-knowledge reader this is texture-as-noise — it slows the most
exciting moment of the section (the actual break) with a unit list they can't track.
Keep ONE vivid concrete unit detail if you want the grain of it; shed the rest into
the same sentence's verb. The break itself is the drama, not the order of battle.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE — the missed-chance ending (¶6) is correct but slightly flat.** "the
Federals dug in instead of exploiting the breakthrough… Hardee's line was broken; his
army was not destroyed. That distinction would matter for the rest of the war." This
is good, and the forward-hook to "the rest of the war" is right — but it's stated, not
felt. It's the setup for the §4 MEANWHILE ("the army that got away"), so it's allowed
to be quieter; just make sure the two don't feel redundant when read in sequence.

### §4 atlanta-is-ours — the exploding trains and the four words
**GOOD — the exploding ammunition train.** "a chain of explosions so enormous the
ground shook and the sound carried for miles into the night… a sky full of fire over
Atlanta as the army that held it walked away." This is the iconic image delivered at
full cinematic weight, and the *Gone with the Wind* nod gives a modern reader a
handhold without over-leaning on it. The pack's precise figures (5 locos / 81 cars)
are correctly withheld. Strong.

**GOOD — the stakes section (¶5–8) is where the draft earns its "decisive battle"
billing.** Setting the scene the North was living in — Petersburg stalemate, Lincoln
expecting to lose, the Aug 23 blind-memorandum — BEFORE delivering the telegram is
the correct order, and it makes the four words detonate instead of just sit there.
"The man who had expected to lose carried the country" is a clean payoff. The
election-as-an-election-about-slavery construction is genuinely the heart of the
significance and it's argued, not asserted. The reader *feels* the weight here.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE — ¶6–7 (Democratic platform / McClellan) is the densest stretch in
the piece.** The McClellan-repudiated-the-peace-plank-but-the-platform-was-the-message
nuance is accurate and worth keeping, but it's a lot of political machinery delivered
fast right after the telegram's emotional peak. Consider whether the McClellan
internal-split detail can be trimmed to one clause so the through-line (a negotiated
peace = slavery intact = the thing the war was for) stays the loudest note. Don't
lose the substance; lighten the apparatus.

**GOOD — the §4 MEANWHILE ("the army that got away").** This is the honest caveat
done right: it concedes Castel's tactical reading, then subordinates it ("a tactical
footnote under an uncontested strategic verdict") without sanding off the truth.
"Sherman won the prize. Hood saved his men to lose them somewhere else" is a strong,
fair close. Keep.

### §5 what-it-decided — the roads south, and the people on them
**GOOD — this is the best section in the draft, and the self-emancipation turn lands
with full gravity.** The move from railroad → election → "the deepest meaning of the
battle is not on the railroad and not even in the election returns. It is on the roads
south of Atlanta" is the right escalation, and it answers the question §1 planted. The
self-emancipating thousands are given agency, not pathos — "they did not merely follow;
they helped… military intelligence of people who knew the country better than any map."
"The railroad was the object. *This* was the reason" snaps the whole piece shut on its
thesis. This is exactly the §5 the pack asked for.

**GOOD — Ebenezer Creek lands with proper, stomach-turning weight, and the Davis
throughline is what makes it land.** Because the draft set Jefferson C. Davis up twice
(§3: "we will be hearing his name again… for reasons that turn the stomach"; and the
disambiguation), the Ebenezer Creek reveal is a *paid-off* horror, not a non-sequitur
atrocity. "The same officer, the same campaign, the freedom and the cruelty bound
together in one human being on one stretch of Georgia ground" refuses the comfortable
story, which is the whole point. This is the draft's emotional high and it does not
flinch. The withheld drowning count ("many drowned") is the right restraint — a fixed
number here would feel like accounting and cheapen it.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE — the closing MEANWHILE ("what the railroad really carried") slightly
re-litigates a point §5's body already made.** The body already said "The railroad was
the object. *This* was the reason." The closing box ("Two uses of one country's roads…
The thousands on the roads are not a footnote… They are the point of it") is good prose
but it's the same argument a third time. By the end the reader has been told the
railroad-vs-people contrast in the §1 cause paragraph, the §5 body, AND this box. Two
of the three is enough. Consider cutting this box OR trimming the §1 paragraph, so the
thesis hits hard twice, not softly three times.

## Cross-cutting notes

1. **Momentum is strong overall; the two drags are both "the lede is in paragraph
   position 2."** §2¶6 (railroad cut) and the front-loaded §1 cause paragraph are the
   two spots where the most important thing isn't where the eye lands first. Fix those
   two and the piece reads downhill the whole way.

2. **The thesis is stated three times** (§1 cause ¶, §5 body, §5 closing MEANWHILE).
   This is the one place the draft over-insures. Pick two.

3. **Side-tag discipline supports the storytelling, doesn't fight it** — the (North)/
   (South) tags read as clarity, not clutter, even at the density they appear, because
   the two-Davises / two-Lees disambiguation genuinely needs them.

4. **No invented-fact pressure.** Every beat above is satisfiable by tightening prose
   the draft already has; none of the must/should-improves requires a fact the pack
   doesn't carry.

---

## Punch list

**MUST-IMPROVE**
- §2¶6: un-bury the railroad cut. The single decisive fact of the battle ("Atlanta…
  was already lost") needs its own beat, not a subordinate clause behind Hood's recall.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE**
- §1¶4: compress the front-loaded cause paragraph ~20% (keep the forward-hook; lose the
  abstraction weight).
- §3¶4: shed the six-regiment roll to one vivid unit detail; let the break be the drama.
- §4¶6–7: trim the McClellan internal-split apparatus to one clause; keep the
  peace=slavery-intact through-line loudest.
- §5 closing MEANWHILE: cut or trim — the thesis is stated three times; two is stronger.
