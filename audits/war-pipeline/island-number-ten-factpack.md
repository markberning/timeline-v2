## 3. What Came Before

### The Confederate River Defense Line
- The Confederate government, aware of Gen. Winfield Scott's Anaconda Plan (seize the Mississippi, cut the Confederacy in two), built a chain of river defenses. Columbus, Kentucky, was the linchpin — heavily fortified, nicknamed "the Gibraltar of the West." [Wikipedia]
- Fort Henry (on the Tennessee River) fell to Grant on February 6, 1862; Fort Donelson fell shortly after (February 16, 1862). These twin losses made the Columbus position untenable — Union forces could advance overland from the Tennessee River to cut it off. [americanhistorycentral.com; Wikipedia]
- Gen. P.G.T. Beauregard ordered Columbus abandoned quietly. Confederate forces evacuated Columbus in late February/early March 1862, transferring much of the garrison and its enormous gun inventory southward. [Wikipedia]
- Island No. 10 and the New Madrid Bend then became the new southern anchor of the upper-Mississippi Confederate defensive line. Gen. Albert Sidney Johnston "contracted the northern extent of his defenses from Columbus, Kentucky farther downriver to the area around New Madrid." [americanhistorycentral.com]

### Island No. 10 as a Fortress
- Construction of artillery batteries on Island No. 10 and adjacent Tennessee/Kentucky shores began as early as August 1861. [americanhistorycentral.com]
- By mid-March 1862 the Confederates had 52 cannon mounted: 19 on the island itself, 22 on the Tennessee shore, 10 elsewhere. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- Additional breakdown from Wikipedia's infobox: 24 guns in 5 shore batteries above the island; 19 guns in 5 batteries on the island; 9 guns on the floating battery *New Orleans*; 14 guns at Fort Thompson (New Madrid); 7 guns at Fort Bankhead (New Madrid). [Wikipedia]
- The island's tactical strength: ships approaching from upstream had to slow to navigate the double hairpin, presenting their bows (thinnest armor) to the batteries. [Wikipedia]
- Its structural weakness: the entire Confederate position depended on a single road running south to Tiptonville, TN. Cut that road and the garrison was isolated. [Wikipedia]

### Pope's March to New Madrid
- Gen. Henry Halleck ordered Pope to take Island No. 10 in February 1862; Pope assembled the Army of the Mississippi (~23,000–25,000 men). [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com; americanhistorycentral.com]
- Pope's column marched from Commerce, Missouri on February 28, 1862. [americanhistorycentral.com]
- March 1–2: skirmishes near Sikeston and New Madrid. [americanhistorycentral.com]
- March 3: Pope began the siege of New Madrid proper. [americanhistorycentral.com]
- March 7: Pope captured Point Pleasant, further isolating New Madrid. [americanhistorycentral.com]
- March 12: Pope's heavy siege artillery arrived — this surprised Confederate commander McCown, who had not expected siege guns. [Wikipedia]
- March 13: The heavy guns opened fire on New Madrid's defenses. [americanhistorycentral.com]
- Night of March 13–14: McCown evacuated New Madrid during a heavy rainstorm, spiking some guns and abandoning both Fort Thompson (14 guns) and Fort Bankhead (7 guns), plus large stores of supplies. [Wikipedia; essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- Morning of March 14: Two Confederate deserters appeared with a white flag; Pope telegraphed: "To my utter amazement the enemy hurriedly evacuated the place last night leaving everything." [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com — Pope's telegram, March 14, 1862]
- The New Madrid capture: 33 cannon, tents, horses, mules, wagons, entrenching tools, and ammunition. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- After New Madrid, most of McCown's troops marched southeast to Island No. 10 garrison. [americanhistorycentral.com]

### The Stalemate Problem
- Foote's Western Gunboat Flotilla arrived opposite Island No. 10 on March 15. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- Pope controlled New Madrid (northwest/upstream on the Missouri bank) and had 23,000 troops — but no way to cross the river. The island's batteries controlled the crossing, and the river current was too strong to cross upstream of the guns without naval cover. [Wikipedia]
- Pope needed gunboats below the island to cover an army crossing at Tiptonville. Foote's boats were above the island. [Wikipedia]
- Foote was reluctant to run the batteries — he hoped to subdue the island by bombardment. [americanhistorycentral.com citing Wikipedia context]

## 4. The Siege and the Canal

### Foote's Bombardment
- Beginning March 15–17, 1862, Foote's ironclads and mortar rafts began a sustained bombardment. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- First significant naval action March 17: USS *Cincinnati* struck multiple times; USS *Benton* took four direct hits; the *St. Louis* had a gun explode, killing three sailors; two Confederates killed. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- **Mortar rafts:** 10–14 rafts (sources vary: Wikipedia says 11 mortar rafts each carrying one 13-inch mortar; essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com says 14 vessels with 11-inch seacoast mortars; another search result says 10 rafts with 13-inch mortars). [CONTESTED — see Section 10] Each mortar fired a 200-pound shell up to ~2 miles. [Web search result]
- The mortar unit was under Army Capt. Henry E. Maynadier. [Wikipedia]
- Result of three weeks of bombardment: largely ineffective. "High expectations that had been held for the mortars were dashed; they did very little harm to the enemy position." [Web search result quoting historyofwar.org]
- April 1: A raid by sailors and troops from the 42nd Illinois Infantry under Col. George W. Roberts spiked Battery No. 1's guns. [Wikipedia]
- April 2–3: The flotilla concentrated fire on the floating battery *New Orleans*, hitting it several times, parting its mooring lines; it drifted downstream out of action. [Wikipedia; essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com notes "April 3"]

### Pope's Canal (the engineering solution)
- **Who proposed it:** Col. Josiah W. Bissell, commanding the Engineer Regiment of the West, surveyed the route in mid-March 1862 and brought the plan to Pope. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com; civilwarmonitor.com]
- **Who built it:** Bissell and his ~600-man Engineer Regiment of the West. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- **Route:** A widened water passage connecting the Mississippi (upstream, above Island No. 10) to Wilson's Bayou, then to St. John's Bayou, bypassing the island entirely on the Missouri/Union-held side, emerging on the Mississippi below the island near New Madrid. [civilwarmonitor.com]
- **Dimensions:** ~12 miles long, 50 feet wide, 4½ feet deep. [civilwarmonitor.com; americanhistorycentral.com; essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com — all three agree on 12 miles and 50 feet wide]
- **Construction time:** 19 days; completed approximately April 4, 1862. [americanhistorycentral.com; civilwarmonitor.com; essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- **Construction method:** Crews on rafts cut trees at 8 feet above the waterline; hawsers attached to stumps were dragged by steamboat capstans to clear them; branches stripped before trees removed; submerged saws used in open-bayou sections. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com; civilwarmonitor.com]
- **Confederate awareness:** Confederate commanders knew about the canal project but believed it would fail. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- **What the canal could carry:** Transports and supply vessels only — not ironclad gunboats (too deep-drafted). It allowed four steamboats and barges to pass. [civilwarmonitor.com; Wikipedia]
- **Pope's own words on the canal:** "prosecuted with untiring energy and determination, under exposures and privations very unusual even in the history of warfare." [civilwarmonitor.com — attributed to Pope's report]
- **Controversy:** There was some dispute about who originated the canal plan — the essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com article notes "controversy existed over authorship of the plan itself." Wikipedia credits Bissell with the plan; so does the civilwarmonitor.com article. [VERIFY — not a significant contested fact, but worth noting]

### The Gunboat Runs

#### USS Carondelet (night of April 4–5, 1862)
- Cmdr. Henry Walke volunteered and convinced Foote during a March 29 conference that the run was achievable. [Web search result quoting americanhistorycentral.com]
- **Preparations:**
  - Barge planks laid over the unprotected deck; surplus chains coiled over vulnerable parts; an 11-inch hawser wound around the pilothouse; cordwood stacked around the boilers. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
  - A coal-laden barge with baled hay was lashed to the port (river) side to absorb shots. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com; Wikipedia]
  - Steam exhaust pipes rerouted aft to muffle engine noise. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
  - 23 sharpshooters from the 42nd Illinois Infantry embarked as extra crew; sailors armed with pistols, hand grenades, and cutlasses. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
  - Pilot: William R. Hoel, a 20-year river veteran. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
  - Engineers prepared to destroy the engines if the ship was in danger of capture. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- **Conditions:** Moonless night; a thunderstorm provided cover. [Wikipedia]
- **What happened during the run:**
  - The *Carondelet* was detected when accumulated soot in the smokestacks blazed up — Confederate lookouts at Battery No. 2 spotted the fire. [Wikipedia]
  - Confederate shore batteries opened fire; 47 enemy shots were fired at the vessel. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
  - Only 2 projectiles struck — both lodged in the coal barge. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
  - The *Carondelet* ran aground briefly (~50 yards from berthing) but refloated. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
  - Run took approximately 2 hours. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- **Outcome:** Completed successfully; *Carondelet* positioned below the island at New Madrid.

#### USS Pittsburg (night of April 6–7, 1862)
- Commander: Lt. Egbert Thompson. [Wikipedia]
- Date: Passed on the night of April 6 (some sources say April 6, some April 6–7; Wikipedia says April 6). [Wikipedia — "April 6"; essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com — "April 6"; americanhistorycentral.com — "April 6-7"] [VERIFY exact night — see Section 10]
- Thompson had requested a delay before proceeding. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- The *Pittsburg* passed undamaged, arriving downstream at 5 a.m. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- Both runs were conducted during thunderstorms. [Wikipedia]

#### The two gunboats at work (April 7)
- April 7: *Carondelet* and *Pittsburg* together drove off or silenced remaining Confederate shore batteries below the island. [americanhistorycentral.com]
- This opened the river crossing for Pope's army.

## 5. The Surrender (April 7–8, 1862)

### Pope's Crossing
- April 7: Using transports that had come through Bissell's canal — including steamers *Hetty Gilmore*, *Terry*, *Trio*, and *Emma* — Pope moved approximately 3,000 troops across the Mississippi to the Tennessee/eastern bank at Watson's Landing, with *Carondelet* and *Pittsburg* providing cover. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- The crossing cut the Confederate escape road at Tiptonville — the only route available to the garrison. [Wikipedia]

### The Trap Closes
- Confederate commander Mackall recognized the situation. "The game is practically up" — attributed to Mackall's assessment if Pope crossed the river. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com — [UNVERIFIED exact quotation; source does not give a primary citation]]
- Confederate forces, including those from Island No. 10 proper, attempted to retreat south toward Tiptonville. Union gunboats interfered with any escape by water. [Wikipedia; Tennessee Encyclopedia]
- Pope's men raced to Tiptonville, cutting off the retreat. [Wikipedia]

### The Surrender
- Date: April 8, 1862, approximately 2 a.m. [americanhistorycentral.com]
- Location: Near Tiptonville, Tennessee. [Wikipedia; americanhistorycentral.com]
- Mackall surrendered to Pope; terms: unconditional surrender. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- Island No. 10 itself surrendered to Flag Officer Foote and the gunboat flotilla at approximately the same time. [Wikipedia]
- Prisoners captured included three generals (including Mackall) and seven colonels. [civilwarmonths.com — [VERIFY names of the other two generals beyond Mackall]]
- For the prisoner-count dispute, see Section 2 and Section 10.

### Post-Surrender Disposition of Prisoners
- Confederate sick: shipped to St. Louis. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- Officers: sent to Fort Warren (Boston Harbor) or Columbus, Ohio. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- Enlisted men: approximately 1,500 to Chicago; 1,000 to Springfield, Illinois; remainder to Madison and Milwaukee, Wisconsin. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]

## 7. What It Meant

1. **Opened the upper Mississippi.** After the fall of Island No. 10, the river was open to Union vessels as far south as Fort Pillow, Tennessee — approximately 70 miles north of Memphis. Fort Pillow fell on June 4, 1862; Memphis fell on June 6, 1862, after the naval Battle of Memphis. [americanhistorycentral.com; Wikipedia — cascading timeline confirmed]

2. **Anaconda Plan progress.** The victory was a major step in Gen. Winfield Scott's strategy to seize the Mississippi from source to mouth, split the Confederacy, and strangle Confederate supply lines. After Island No. 10 the only remaining Confederate strongholds on the Mississippi were Fort Pillow (gone by June 4), Memphis (June 6), Vicksburg, and Port Hudson. [Wikipedia; americanhistorycentral.com]

3. **First Confederate river loss.** "The Union victory marked the first time the Confederate Army lost a position on the Mississippi River in battle." [Wikipedia — exact quote]

4. **A new tactic in warfare.** The gunboat runs introduced (or proved at scale) the tactic of steam-powered warships bypassing fixed shore batteries rather than silencing them from above — "marked the introduction of a new tactic in warfare." Later used by Farragut at New Orleans (April 1862), Port Hudson, and Mobile, and by Porter at Vicksburg. [Wikipedia]

5. **Pope's career boost — and subsequent fall.** The victory made Pope nationally famous, earned him a promotion to major general, and led directly to Lincoln calling him east to command the Army of Virginia (June 1862). That command ended in disaster at Second Bull Run (August 1862); Pope spent the rest of the war in the West dealing with the Dakota War in Minnesota. [NPS biography; battlefields.org biography]

6. **Foote's last major action.** Foote was thanked by Congress, promoted to Rear Admiral, but never recovered sufficiently to command at sea; he died in June 1863. [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]

7. **Disproportionate cost-to-gain.** Fewer than 50 Union casualties in exchange for ~4,500–5,000 prisoners (per corrected count), 109–123 heavy guns, and the opening of the upper Mississippi. Even using Pope's inflated figure of ~7,000, the ratio was extraordinary. "At a cost of fewer than 100 casualties, Pope and Foote had untied the first knot in the critical campaign to open the Mississippi." [americanhistorycentral.com — paraphrase; historynet.com — original source of "fewer than 100" formulation]

8. **P.G.T. Beauregard's verdict:** Confederate Gen. Beauregard called Island No. 10 "the poorest defense made of any fortified post during the whole course of the war." [civilwarmonths.com — [UNVERIFIED primary source; secondary citation only]]

## 9. Geography for a Map

### The River's Shape
- The Mississippi at New Madrid makes a dramatic double bend (sometimes called an "S-bend" or "hairpin"). Coming from the north/northwest, the river curves sharply right (east/southeast) into what is called New Madrid Bend or Kentucky Bend, then curves sharply back left (southwest), before heading generally south. The result is that a point on the Tennessee bank can be north of a point that is geographically upstream. **Compass bearings are highly counterintuitive here.** [Wikipedia — described as "a tight double turn"]
- Island No. 10 sits at the base of the lower of the two bends, on what is called the Tennessee side (southeast side of the bend) — although it is geographically in the river between Missouri and Tennessee/Kentucky. [Wikipedia; Tennessee Encyclopedia]

### Key Positions (described relative to each other, north up)
| Location | Position | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cairo, Illinois** | ~45 miles north (upstream) | Ohio–Mississippi confluence; Union base of operations |
| **Columbus, Kentucky** | North of Island No. 10 (upstream); on Kentucky bank | Evacuated Confederate "Gibraltar of the West"; upstream anchor of the old defense line |
| **New Madrid, Missouri** | Northwest of Island No. 10; on Missouri (west) bank | Taken by Pope March 13–14; upstream entry to the canal |
| **Island No. 10** | In the river at the Tennessee base of the lower bend; approximately at the Missouri/Tennessee border (the Kentucky border is close by to the north) | The fortified island; batteries also on adjacent Tennessee bank |
| **Kentucky/Tennessee shore batteries** | East and southeast of the island; on the Tennessee/Kentucky bank | Multiple battery positions overlooking the river approaches |
| **The canal route** | Entirely on the Missouri (west/Union) side; running east from a point upstream of the island, through swamps and bayous (Wilson's Bayou → St. John's Bayou), exiting onto the Mississippi just above New Madrid | Pope's bypass channel; ~12 miles |
| **New Madrid crossing point (Watson's Landing area)** | Downstream (south) of Island No. 10 on the Missouri bank; Pope's army crossed here to the Tennessee side | April 7 crossing using transports from the canal |
| **Tiptonville, Tennessee** | South/southeast of Island No. 10 on the Tennessee bank; downstream | The only Confederate escape road; where Pope's force cut off the garrison and Mackall surrendered |
| **Fort Pillow, Tennessee** | ~70 miles south of Island No. 10 (downstream) on the Tennessee/Mississippi bluffs | Next Confederate river strong point; fell June 4, 1862 |

### Orientation caution
Ships travelling downstream on the river are travelling generally south, but at the specific New Madrid Bend they travel east, then north (relative to Tiptonville), then south again. A downstream Union gunboat from Foote's fleet is "above" the island in river terms (upstream) but physically to the northwest on a standard map. "Above" and "below" in Civil War river usage = upstream and downstream respectively, not geographic north/south.

## 10. Contested / Uncertain Facts

### A. Prisoner Count — MAJOR CONTEST
- Pope claimed 273 officers + 6,700 enlisted = ~6,973 total. [Pope's official report, via Wikipedia and essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- Confederate strength returns show ≤5,350 present as of March 31, 1862 — making Pope's figure mathematically impossible unless you count the island garrison plus the New Madrid garrison that had already retreated. [Wikipedia]
- Wikipedia's analysis: "likely fewer than 4,500" captured. [Wikipedia]
- essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com: "approximately 3,500–4,500." [essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com]
- Mackall's own stated count: ~2,000 — generally considered an undercount, possibly including only the Tiptonville surrender and not the island itself. [americanhistorycentral.com]
- **Best defensible range for a narrative:** ~4,500–5,000; present Pope's 6,973–7,000 as his claim, note it likely overstates.
- [VERIFY against OR Vol. VIII (Series I) for Mackall's report and Pope's report — this is a key fact for any narrative.]

### B. Union Casualties — Minor discrepancy
- Wikipedia: 8 killed, 21 wounded, 3 missing = 32 total.
- americanhistorycentral.com (New Madrid–Tiptonville phase specifically): 7 killed, 14 wounded, 4 missing = 25.
- Multiple round figures cited: "fewer than 50," "fewer than 100" (the latter almost certainly includes sick/non-battle losses or is rounding broadly).
- [VERIFY against OR Vol. VIII; likely the Wikipedia infobox numbers are the more precise.]

### C. USS Pittsburg Run Date
- Wikipedia says April 6; essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com says April 6; americanhistorycentral.com says "April 6–7." Most sources agree the run was the night of April 6 (arriving downstream by early April 7). [Use: "night of April 6" or "April 6–7" — verify against OR.]

### D. Number of Mortar Rafts
- Wikipedia infobox: 11 mortar rafts, each with one 13-inch mortar.
- essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com: 14 vessels with 11-inch seacoast mortars.
- Other sources: 10 rafts.
- [VERIFY: probably 10–13 mortar boats; shell weight also described as both 200 lb and 230 lb depending on source; the Wikipedia figure of 13-inch caliber is most consistent with the "200 lb shell" claim.]

### E. Canal Length
- All main sources (Wikipedia, essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com, americanhistorycentral.com, civilwarmonitor.com) agree: ~12 miles long, 50 feet wide, 4½ feet deep. This appears well-established. [LOW CONTEST]

### F. Total Guns Captured
- americanhistorycentral.com (two independent passages): 123 pieces of heavy artillery.
- essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com: "100 heavy guns, 24 field guns (likely closer to 109 total)."
- civilwarmonths.com: 109 cannons.
- [Use 109–123 as the sourced range; verify against OR.]

### G. Pope's Rank During the Battle
- Pope was brigadier general when ordered to New Madrid; his major general promotion was dated March 21, 1862 — technically during the siege. Whether he had received/assumed that rank before the April 7–8 fighting is unclear from secondary sources. [VERIFY against biographies and OR.]

### H. The "Three generals captured" claim
- civilwarmonths.com states three generals were captured at Tiptonville. One is Mackall. The identities of the other two are not given. [VERIFY — cross-check against prisoner lists in OR Vol. VIII.]

### I. Beauregard "poorest defense" quote
- Attributed to Beauregard in civilwarmonths.com. No primary citation given. [UNVERIFIED — worth checking against OR or Beauregard's papers.]
