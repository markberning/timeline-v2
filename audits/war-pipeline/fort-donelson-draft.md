# the-rivers | The Rivers into the South

In the first winter of the Civil War, the United States went looking for a way into the South, and found two of them lying in plain sight. The Tennessee and the Cumberland are big rivers, and in early 1862 they were the closest thing the war had to open back doors. They run roughly parallel through the Confederate heartland, only about 10 miles (16 km) apart where the South had planted its forts, and both of them point like arrows from the loyal North straight down into Tennessee and beyond. The war in the East was already a slogging, head-on affair, two armies grinding into each other across Virginia. Out West, somebody noticed that you didn't have to grind. You could float.

That somebody, more than anyone, turned out to be a then-obscure brigadier general named Brig. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant (North). He had a partner in the work: Flag Officer Andrew H. Foote (North), the naval commander running a brand-new fleet of river gunboats (warships built to fight on rivers, not the open sea). And he had a green light from his superior, Maj. Gen. Henry W. Halleck (North), the cautious commander of the Department of Missouri, who authorized a campaign against the twin river forts. The idea was simple and ruthless: drive gunboats and soldiers up those two rivers, smash the forts guarding them, and you split the Confederacy and cut its supply lines in half. And the thing that whole western line existed to defend, the thing the war was finally about, was slavery. The Confederacy had broken away to keep human beings as property; its generals had strung a defensive cordon across Kentucky and Tennessee to protect that slaveholding heartland. The rivers strategy was a plan to crack that cordon open, sail up into the South, and start strangling the country built on slavery.

## The western strategy | A fort that fell to boats alone

The first door came down fast. On February 6, 1862, ten days before Donelson, Foote's gunboats steamed up the Tennessee River and reduced Fort Henry more or less by themselves, his ironclads pounding the fort until it quit before Grant's foot soldiers could even close the ring around it. An **ironclad** is exactly what it sounds like, a gunboat plated in iron armor so cannonballs bounce off instead of punching through; in 1862 these were new and frightening machines, and at Fort Henry they looked unstoppable. The Confederate garrison (the body of troops assigned to hold a fort) was around 3,400 men, and most of them got out before the trap closed. About 2,500 of them simply marched the 10-odd miles overland to the next fort, Fort Donelson, on the Cumberland, and dug in there.

So Fort Henry's fall did two things at once. It threw open the Tennessee River to Union gunboats all the way down into Alabama, and it handed the lesson of the moment to both sides: out here, the boats win. Grant decided to do it again at the second door.

## The western strategy | A twelve-mile march and a treacherous thaw

Grant didn't wait. He moved his force the roughly 12 miles (19 km) overland from Fort Henry to Fort Donelson on February 11–13, marching across the neck of land between the two rivers while Foote took the gunboats the long way around by water. On February 12 his men ran into Confederate cavalry under Col. Nathan Bedford Forrest (South) in sharp little skirmishes, and on the 13th there was some light probing at the fort's defenses. The shape of the thing was set: army on the land side from the west, gunboats coming up the river from the north.

And then the weather, which had been lying to everyone, told the truth. An unseasonably warm spell had run ahead of the battle, warm enough that soldiers threw away their overcoats on the march to lighten the load. It was a costly bit of optimism. On the night of February 13–14 a severe snowstorm rolled in and dropped the temperature to 10–12°F (–12°C), with about 3 inches of snow on the ground and a lot of men no longer carrying the coats they'd shed. Wounded soldiers left out on the field overnight simply froze where they lay. That is not a figure of speech and it is not a small detail. It is one of the documented horrors of Fort Donelson, and it had barely begun.

> The boats win. Grant decided to do it again at the second door.

_ITALIC_: At Fort Henry the gunboats had ended the fight by themselves. Grant was about to learn that the second fort had been paying attention.

### Meanwhile in the Confederate West | A line drawn across two states
The man responsible for stopping all this was Gen. Albert Sidney Johnston (South), commander of the entire Confederate Western Department, and his problem was that his line was a mile wide and an inch deep. It ran from Columbus, Kentucky, on the Mississippi, east to Bowling Green, Kentucky, anchored in the middle by the two river forts, Henry and Donelson. Johnston had roughly 12,000 men under Leonidas Polk holding Columbus and about 14,000 under William J. Hardee at Bowling Green, with the forts as the hinge between them. Johnston himself sat at Bowling Green, far from the fort about to be attacked. Pull the hinge out and the whole line had nothing to hold it together. Grant was reaching for the hinge.

# the-gunboats | The Gunboats Repulsed

On the afternoon of February 14, the gunboats came up the Cumberland to do to Fort Donelson exactly what they had done to Fort Henry, and the river fort was ready for them. This is the reversal at the heart of the whole battle: the same weapon that had won the first fort almost by itself got beaten back from the second one, and beaten back hard. Grant wasn't even there to see it. He was downriver in a meeting, which would become a pattern.

The thing the gunboats had to get past was the fort's **water batteries**, gun positions dug into the river bluff specifically to shoot at ships. Fort Donelson had two tiers of them stacked on the high west bank of the Cumberland, a lower battery down near the waterline and an upper battery above it, the heaviest gun among them a 10-inch Columbiad (a big smoothbore cannon) in the lower battery, with a rifled 64-pounder and a pair of 32-pounders up top. Sitting up on that bluff gave the Confederate gunners a brutal advantage: they could fire *down* onto the boats, plunging shot through the decks, while the river's strong downstream current and some flooding made it hard for the gunboats to hold position and aim back.

## February 14 | Closing to four hundred yards

Foote brought his whole flotilla up: four ironclads (the *St. Louis*, *Carondelet*, *Louisville*, and *Pittsburg*) and two **timberclads** behind them (older gunboats armored with thick wood instead of iron, the *Tyler* and *Conestoga*). The attack opened around 3:00 in the afternoon and lasted only about an hour and a half. Foote ran the same play that had worked at Fort Henry, closing right in to within about 400 yards (370 m) of the guns so his fire would tell.

This time it was a fatal mistake. Up close and below the batteries, his boats took the plunging fire full in the face. The numbers are almost comic if you forget there were men inside: the *St. Louis* was hit 59 times, the *Carondelet* 54 times, the *Louisville* 36 times, the *Pittsburg* 20 times. Two of the ironclads had their steering shot away, went unmanageable, and drifted helplessly downriver, and the rest of the flotilla pulled back after them. Foote himself was wounded, hit in the ankle (accounts differ on whether it was a rifle shot or a shell fragment). On the Union side, 8 sailors were killed and 44 wounded in that hour and a half. In the Confederate water batteries up on the bluff: not a single casualty.

That lopsided afternoon rewrote Grant's whole plan. Fort Henry had fallen to gunboats alone; Fort Donelson had just thrown the gunboats off the river. There would be no naval shortcut here. If the fort was coming down, the army was going to have to take it the slow, bloody way, on foot, from the land side.

> The *St. Louis* hit 59 times, the *Carondelet* 54, the *Louisville* 36, the *Pittsburg* 20. In the batteries that did it: zero casualties.

_ITALIC_: The fort had answered the one weapon everyone thought was unbeatable, and it had answered with a shrug.

### Meanwhile in the trenches | Cold enough to kill the wounded
While the gunboats were getting wrecked on the river, the land siege was settling into the same cold that had arrived the night before. The temperature had crashed to 10–12°F (–12°C) with snow on the ground, and the men shivering in the lines on both sides were the lucky ones. The wounded out in the open between the armies, soldiers who couldn't crawl back to their own side, froze to death where they had fallen. The fight on the river had a winner and a loser by 4:30 in the afternoon. The cold didn't take sides, and it didn't stop at sundown.

# the-breakout | The Escape That Wasn't

By the night of February 14, the men running Fort Donelson had done the math and didn't like the answer. The gunboats had been beaten, yes, but Grant's army was thickening on the land side by the day, the ring was closing, and at a council of war around 11:00 that night the Confederate generals agreed the fort was "probably untenable." So they made a plan to punch their way out. And then, the next morning, they executed it almost perfectly, opened a clean road to safety, and threw it away. The story of February 15 is the story of an escape that was won and then surrendered before anyone surrendered.

The plan had three parts and three generals, which was already a problem. Brig. Gen. Gideon J. Pillow (South) would lead the main assault straight south to seize Wynn's Ferry Road, the escape route running toward Nashville; Brig. Gen. Simon Bolivar Buckner (South) would hold the left as the men funneled out; and the senior commander, Brig. Gen. John B. Floyd (South), would coordinate the whole thing. A **breakout** is exactly this, a trapped force attacking outward to tear a hole in the ring around it and march its army through the gap before the enemy can close it.

## February 15 | The road thrown open

It worked. Around 5 a.m., Forrest's (South) cavalry stirred up a diversionary skirmish to the north to draw attention, and around 7 a.m. Pillow threw his full assault into Brig. Gen. John A. McClernand's (North) division on the Union right, the southern end of the line. McClernand's men bent, then broke backward; by early afternoon the Confederates had shoved the Union right back roughly 1 to 2 miles. The thing the whole plan was for had happened. The Union right was rolled up, Wynn's Ferry Road lay open, and the army's path to Nashville was clear. All they had to do now was walk out of it.

## February 15 | The order that gave it back

Instead, around 1:00 to 1:30 in the afternoon, Pillow ordered the assault troops to stop, turn around, and march back into the trenches they had just fought their way out of.

This is the most argued-over decision of the battle, and it deserves to be treated as the genuine controversy it is. Pillow's stated reasoning was that his men needed to resupply and reorganize before setting out on the breakout march, and that the road would stay open long enough for them to do it. Floyd accepted the call. Buckner protested it on the spot and was overruled. Most historians who have looked at it since have called it a catastrophic blunder, an open escape route handed back to the enemy for no good reason. Pillow himself never admitted any mistake; questioned about it afterward by Floyd, he said he was "conscious of the commission of no errors." Whether it was incompetence, a failure of nerve, or a real-time misjudgment about how long the road would hold, the men who knew best disagree, and the disagreement starts with Pillow insisting nothing went wrong at all. What is not in dispute is the result. The door the army had pried open swung shut while they stood in the trenches resting.

## February 15 | Grant comes back

Grant, true to the pattern, had not been on the field for any of this. He'd been downriver meeting with the wounded Foote to sort out resupply for the battered fleet, and he'd left no second-in-command, so his division commanders had spent the morning reacting on their own. When he rode back and saw his right broken and his officers shaken, he read the situation in a single, sharp detail: the captured Confederate soldiers were carrying full knapsacks, three days' rations packed for a march. Men loaded for a long journey aren't pressing a sustained attack, they're trying to leave. Grant grasped that the enemy was attempting escape, not victory, and that the side which moved first now would win the day. (His exact words to his staff officer Col. J.D. Webster survive in several slightly different versions, all to the effect that whoever attacked first now would be victorious and the enemy would have to hurry to get ahead of him.)

So he attacked first. He ordered counterattacks all along the line: McClernand to rally and retake the ground lost that morning, Brig. Gen. Lew Wallace (North) in the center to shift south and shore him up, and, on the left, Brig. Gen. C.F. Smith (North) to hit the Confederate earthworks the breakout had stripped of defenders. His instruction to Smith was blunt: "All has failed on our right — you must take Fort Donelson." Smith took him at his word and drove his division straight into the outer entrenchments held by the 30th Tennessee, capturing them by evening. By about 5:30 p.m. the Union had retaken most of the morning's lost ground, and the trap was back in place, tighter than before. The escape was no longer just thrown away. It was gone.

> "All has failed on our right — you must take Fort Donelson."

_ITALIC_: The Confederates had spent the morning winning their way out, and the afternoon getting walled back in.

### Meanwhile in the fort | An army that had just escaped, trapped again
For the ordinary Confederate soldier inside the perimeter, February 15 was the cruelest kind of day. By midday they had fought hard, paid in blood, and opened the way home; some of them had been close enough to the open road to see it. Then they were marched back into the same trenches, and by dark the Union line had not only re-formed but bitten off a chunk of their own outer earthworks on the left. The next morning's math, with Smith's men now lodged inside the outer defenses, looked like slaughter. Buckner would put it at a 75% casualty rate if the assault came. The men who had won their escape at dawn were, by nightfall, more trapped than when the day began.

# unconditional-surrender | Unconditional Surrender

The Confederate generals met one more time, late on the night of February 15, and this council had the air of a sinking ship. Smith's troops were dug into the outer works on the left; Buckner (South) reckoned that if they attacked in the morning from that newly won position, the defenders could lose three of every four men. The options on the table were the grim short list of a beaten army: keep fighting, try another breakout, or surrender. What happened next turned the surrender of a fort into one of the strangest exits in the war, because the two men in charge decided the one thing they would not do was be there for it.

## February 16 | The generals who left

Floyd (South) went first, and he had a personal reason to run. Before the war he'd been U.S. Secretary of War under President Buchanan, and he feared that if the North captured him he'd be prosecuted for treason or corruption. So he handed command to Pillow and got out, taking the only available steamboat and his two Virginia infantry regiments with him, slipping away early on the morning of February 16. Pillow (South), who took the command Floyd passed him, reportedly feared Northern reprisals just as much, and he didn't hold it long either. He passed command down again, to Buckner, and escaped across the Cumberland in a small boat in the dark. Two generals, one after the other, took charge of a surrendering army for just long enough to hand it to the next man and disappear.

## February 16 | Forrest rides through the water

Not everyone was willing to surrender, and one man flatly refused. Col. Nathan Bedford Forrest (South), the cavalry commander, said as much out loud: "I did not come here to surrender my command." Instead of waiting for terms, he led around 700 of his cavalrymen out of the trap the hard way, through the icy, waist-deep backwater of Lick Creek, skirting the Union left flank in the cold and the dark. They ran into no enemy on the way out, which is the quietly damning part. The escape routes were still open. Forrest's clean getaway is the proof of what the night cost the rest of the garrison: many more men could have walked out alongside him, if Pillow hadn't ordered the breakout back into the trenches the day before, and if Buckner hadn't since posted guards to stop further escape attempts. The road home was right there. Most of the army was made to stay and surrender on it.

## February 16 | An old friend asks for terms

That left Buckner, the ranking officer now by default, to do the job the other two had fled. He sent a note across to Grant asking for an armistice and a meeting to discuss surrender terms. And here the war turned personal, because Buckner and Grant were old friends. They'd been at West Point together in the 1840s and served side by side under Winfield Scott in the Mexican War, including a climb up the volcano Popocatépetl in 1847. More than that, Buckner had once rescued Grant: in 1854, when Grant had resigned from the army and ended up broke and stranded at the Astor House hotel in New York City, it was Buckner who vouched for him and covered his hotel bill until his money came through. Now the broke ex-soldier Buckner had bailed out was the general holding his army by the throat.

Grant did not let the friendship soften the terms by an inch. His reply is one of the most famous lines in American military history, and the sources agree on it word for word:

> "No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender can be accepted. I propose to move immediately upon your works."

An **unconditional surrender** means exactly that, no negotiation, no concessions, no agreement to let the defenders march away with their weapons or their pride: give up everything, or be attacked at once. Buckner, who had every reason to expect generosity from an old friend, got none. He called the terms "ungenerous and unchivalrous," but he acknowledged the "overwhelming force" arrayed against him, made a bitter nod to "the brilliant success of the Confederate arms yesterday" (Pillow's morning that had won the escape and then lost it), and accepted. The initials lined up too well for the Northern press to resist: U.S. Grant became "Unconditional Surrender Grant," and the nickname stuck.

When the two men met at the Dover Tavern, the old friendship resurfaced. Grant offered Buckner the use of his own purse, knowing he was headed for a prison camp; accounts differ on whether Buckner took the money. (The friendship outlasted the war by a long way: Buckner would serve as a pallbearer at Grant's funeral in 1885.) Grant refused to stage any formal surrender ceremony, asking, in the words attributed to him, "We have the fort, the men, the guns. Why should we go through vain forms?"

## February 16 | What it bought

The scale of what he'd just taken was staggering. Somewhere between roughly 12,000 and 13,500 Confederate soldiers surrendered at Fort Donelson (sources run as high as 15,000; the most precisely sourced count is about 12,400 captured or missing), along with some 20,000 small arms, 48 field guns, 17 heavy guns, and around 3,000 horses. It was the largest mass surrender on the North American continent up to that point in the war.

And it broke the South's whole western position in days. Johnston's (South) Columbus–Bowling Green line had hinged on these forts, and with the hinge gone the line simply collapsed. Nashville, the capital of Tennessee, was abandoned almost at once and fell on February 23, the first Confederate state capital to fall in the war. Columbus was given up by early March, and the entire western cordon was gone, the rivers now open highways carrying Union armies deep into the South. Out of it all rose Grant, lifted from obscurity to national fame in a week, promoted to major general of volunteers and second in seniority in the West only to Halleck. The North had been starving for a real victory, and Fort Donelson, the first major Union win of the war, finally gave it one.

> "No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender can be accepted."

_ITALIC_: Two generals fled, one rode out through the ice, and the man left holding the bag was the old friend who had once paid Grant's hotel bill.

### Meanwhile in the North | A country gets its first good news
For the North, Fort Donelson landed like the first warm day after a long winter. The Union had been short on victories and shorter on heroes, and here, suddenly, was both: a fort cracked open, an enemy army marched off into captivity, and a plain-spoken general whose own initials seemed to spell out his terms. Grant's name ran in every Northern newspaper. Story had it he'd been seen chewing a cigar during the fight, and a grateful public buried him in boxes of them, after which, the accounts say, he became a regular cigar smoker for life. The South lost an army and a capital. The North, at long last, got a winner.
