# FACT PACK: "Freedom Seekers & the USCT"
### Section scope: 1861–1865 — how the enslaved freed THEMSELVES (self-emancipation), the "contraband" question and the camps, and the ~180,000 Black men who then took up arms in the United States Colored Troops. The through-line is BLACK AGENCY: the enslaved acting, not being acted upon.

## BEAT 1: Self-Emancipation — The Enslaved Force the Issue

- From the war's first weeks, enslaved people fled toward Union lines in large numbers — a mass act of self-liberation, not a gift handed down. The historiographic framing (Ira Berlin and the Freedmen and Southern Society Project) calls this "self-emancipation": the enslaved made their own freedom a fact on the ground that the Union government then had to deal with.
  — Source: National Museum of African American History & Culture, "Self-Emancipation"; Encyclopedia Virginia, "Slavery during the Civil War"

- **Scale:** Between April 1861 and April 1865, an estimated ~500,000 enslaved people fled bondage and made their way toward Union lines over the course of the war. [VERIFY exact figure — see CONTESTED; sources give "around 500,000," a war-long cumulative estimate, not a precise count.]
  — Source: National Museum of African American History & Culture, "Self-Emancipation"

- This flight forced the slavery question onto a Union government that insisted, at the war's start, it was fighting only to preserve the Union — not to end slavery. Enslaved people crossing the lines made slavery an operational and political problem the army could not ignore, and so helped make emancipation a central issue of the war.
  — Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, "Slavery during the Civil War"; Civil War Monitor, "Contrabands"

- The framing point to keep front and center: the mass of runaways "belies the outdated and racist notion that enslaved African Americans simply waited for emancipation... rather, they seized almost every chance to pursue their freedom, often risking death." They were the actors.
  — Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, "Slavery during the Civil War"

## BEAT 2: "Contraband" — Butler at Fort Monroe, May 1861

- **The three men:** On the night of **May 23, 1861**, three enslaved men — **Frank Baker**, **Shepard Mallory**, and **James Townsend** — escaped by boat across Hampton Roads and reached the Union-held **Fort Monroe**, Virginia (also called Fortress Monroe). They were enslaved by **Colonel Charles King Mallory** of the Virginia (Confederate) militia.
  — Source: The Mariners' Museum, "Ben Butler and the Contrabands"; Wikipedia, "Contraband (American Civil War)"

- **Why it mattered tactically:** the three men had been forced to labor building a Confederate artillery battery at Sewell's Point — guns aimed at Fort Monroe itself. They were, literally, enemy war labor.
  — Source: The Mariners' Museum, "Ben Butler and the Contrabands"

- **The commander:** **Maj. Gen. Benjamin F. Butler (US)**, commanding at Fort Monroe. (Butler at this date was a major general of Massachusetts volunteers; a Massachusetts Democrat and lawyer, NOT an abolitionist by background — which is part of why his ruling mattered.)
  — Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, "Benjamin F. Butler (1818–1893)"

- **The demand and the refusal:** On **May 24, 1861**, Confederate **Major John Baytop Cary** rode to Fort Monroe under a flag of truce and demanded the men's return, citing the **Fugitive Slave Act**. Butler refused — reasoning that since Virginia claimed to have left the Union and was at war with the United States, he had no constitutional obligation to return fugitive slaves to a foreign enemy; and since enslaved people were legally "property" being used for the enemy's war effort, he could seize them as he would any other war material.
  — Source: The Mariners' Museum, "Ben Butler and the Contrabands"; Encyclopedia.com, "Benjamin Butler's Report on the Contrabands of War (1861)"

- **The phrase:** Butler declared the men **"contraband of war"** and put them to work for the Union. The label stuck; escaped and self-emancipated people who reached Union lines became known as "contrabands."
  — Source: The Mariners' Museum, "Ben Butler and the Contrabands"; Wikipedia, "Contraband (American Civil War)"

- **The floodgate:** Word spread fast. Butler reported a dozen escapees from Sewell's Point by May 27, then "negroes came pouring in day by day." By later in 1861 hundreds — figures of **500+ at Fort Monroe by June/July 1861** — had reached his lines.
  — Source: The Mariners' Museum, "Ben Butler and the Contrabands"; History.com, "Star of the West" context pages / Mariners' Museum

- **The word's problem (keep this honest):** "Contraband" meant *captured property* — it freed the men from their enslavers in practice while still defining them, legally and rhetorically, as a kind of seized goods rather than as people or citizens. It was a halfway status: out of slavery, not yet into freedom or rights. Worth flagging for the reader as a telling, uncomfortable term.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Contraband (American Civil War)"; Civil War Monitor, "Contrabands"

## BEAT 3: The Confiscation Acts — Congress Catches Up to the Runaways

- **First Confiscation Act — August 6, 1861.** Signed by Lincoln. It authorized the seizure of any property — explicitly including enslaved people — used to directly aid the Confederate war effort. In effect it ratified Butler's "contraband" logic into federal law, but narrowly: it covered only slaves used for Confederate military labor, and it did NOT clearly declare those people free — it stripped the enslaver's claim without settling the freed status.
  — Source: U.S. Senate, "The Confiscation Acts of 1861 and 1862"; Wikipedia, "Confiscation Act of 1861"; Freedmen & Southern Society Project (umd.edu), "The First Confiscation Act, August 6, 1861"

- **Militia Act of July 17, 1862** (adjacent enabling law): authorized the President to employ "persons of African descent" for any war service, including as soldiers, and freed such men (and their families, if owned by disloyal masters). This is the first federal statutory crack in the door to Black enlistment, before the Proclamation.
  — Source: Civil War Monitor / NMAAHC contraband pages (Militia Act of 1862); Encyclopedia Virginia

- **Second Confiscation Act — July 17, 1862.** Signed by Lincoln (after he insisted on a clarifying joint resolution). Far broader: it permitted seizure of all property of those in rebellion, and it declared that enslaved people of disloyal owners who came into Union control "shall be forever free." It treated the enslaved reaching Union lines as captives of war to be freed (commonly described as freedom taking effect after a 60-day notice period). Enforcement, however, was loose.
  — Source: U.S. Senate, "The Confiscation Acts of 1861 and 1862"; Britannica, "Confiscation Acts"; Encyclopedia.com, "First and Second Confiscation Acts (1861, 1862)"

- **Through-line for the section:** the law followed the people. The runaways acted first (1861); Congress wrote the rules afterward (Aug 1861 → July 1862); the Emancipation Proclamation (Jan 1863) then turned the trickle of policy into a war aim — and authorized Black men to enlist.
  — Source: synthesized from above; Library of Congress, "The African American Odyssey: The Civil War"

## BEAT 4: The Contraband Camps — Misery and the First Taste of Freedom

- **What they were:** As self-emancipated people poured into Union-held areas, makeshift settlements — "contraband camps" — sprang up around forts and army positions across the occupied South. They held tens of thousands over the war.
  — Source: NPS, "Living Contraband — Former Slaves in the Nation's Capital During the Civil War"; Wikipedia, "Contraband (American Civil War)"

- **Grand Contraband Camp ("Slabtown"), Hampton, Virginia:** Often called the first self-contained contraband community / the first "freedmen's" town. It grew out of the burned ruins of Hampton (the town was burned in August 1861) — residents built homes on the foundations of destroyed buildings near Fort Monroe. With Army engineers and the American Missionary Association, freedpeople rebuilt the Elizabeth City Courthouse on King Street as a school — the largest in the camp. (Mary Peake, a free Black teacher, famously taught freedpeople here under the "Emancipation Oak" at nearby Fort Monroe.)
  — Source: The Mariners' Museum, "Ben Butler and the Contrabands"

- **Freedman's Village, Arlington, Virginia:** A model contraband camp established by the U.S. Army on the seized Arlington estate of Robert E. Lee. Site selected **May 5, 1863**; village dedicated/opened **December 4, 1863**. It had ~50 duplex houses, a school, a chapel, a hospital, workshops, and a home for the aged. Residents were paid **$10/month** for labor (with **$5 deducted** to support the community) and given food and clothing; men did military/farm labor, women sewed.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Freedman's Village"; NPS, "Freedman's Village — Arlington House"

- **The misery (do not sanitize):** Camps were overcrowded and disease-ridden. At **Camp Barker** (Washington, D.C.), the death rate between June 1862 and June 1864 "approached one in seven." Smallpox, measles, dysentery, scarlet fever, and whooping cough swept the camps; children died heavily. Even the "improved" Freedman's Village averaged ~2 deaths/day; Washington's worse camps averaged ~3.5/day.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Freedman's Village"; BlackPast, "Contraband Hospital, 1862–1863"

- **The beginnings of freedom (the other half of the truth):** alongside the death, the camps were where formerly enslaved people first lived as free people — earning wages, learning to read in freedmen's schools, forming churches, reuniting families, and beginning to act as a free community. The camps were both a humanitarian catastrophe and the seedbed of free Black life in the postwar South.
  — Source: NPS, "Living Contraband"; Wikipedia, "Freedman's Village"; NMAAHC, "Self-Emancipation"

## BEAT 5: Authorizing Black Soldiers — From Trickle to Flood

- **The early, semi-official units (before formal authorization):**
  - **1st Kansas Colored Volunteers:** Organized in 1862 WITHOUT federal authorization. Fought a skirmish at **Island Mound, Missouri, October 29, 1862** — the **first time a Black regiment saw combat** in the Civil War. Officially mustered into federal service January 13, 1863.
    — Source: NPS, "First to Serve — 1st Kansas Colored Infantry Regiment"; BlackPast, "Battle of Island Mound (1862)"; Civil War on the Western Border
  - **1st Louisiana Native Guard (Union):** Organized by Gen. Butler in occupied New Orleans; **mustered September 27, 1862** — the **first Black regiment officially mustered into U.S. service**, and notable for having Black officers. Many members were free men of color from New Orleans; many others were escaped slaves enrolled with lax enforcement of the "free only" rule.
    — Source: Wikipedia, "1st Louisiana Native Guard (Union)"; HistoryNet, "Louisiana Native Guards"; NPS, "Second Louisiana Native Guard"
  - **1st South Carolina Volunteers:** The **first Union regiment organized from former slaves**. **Col. Thomas Wentworth Higginson (US)** — Massachusetts abolitionist, minister, and a backer of John Brown — took command November 24, 1862; the regiment was mustered into service by January 31, 1863. Recruits were Gullah-Geechee freedpeople from the Sea Islands of SC, GA, and FL. Later redesignated the **33rd USCT**. Higginson wrote *Army Life in a Black Regiment* (1870) about it.
    — Source: Wikipedia, "Thomas Wentworth Higginson"; NPS, "The 1st South Carolina Volunteers"; American Battlefield Trust, "Thomas Wentworth Higginson"

- **The enabling fact from the Proclamation (the ONLY Proclamation fact this section needs):** The final **Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863**, explicitly stated that freed Black men "will be received into the armed service of the United States." It turned Black enlistment from improvisation into national policy.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "United States Colored Troops"; National Archives, Emancipation Proclamation text

- **The Bureau of Colored Troops — May 22, 1863.** The War Department created the Bureau of Colored Troops by **General Order No. 143** to organize and oversee Black regiments. From this point the regiments were standardized as the **United States Colored Troops (USCT)**, with federal numbering (many existing state Black regiments were folded in and renumbered as USCT units).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "United States Colored Troops"; National Archives, "Black Soldiers in the U.S. Military During the Civil War"

- **The 54th Massachusetts** (the most famous USCT-era regiment, though raised by the state, not numbered into the USCT): authorized early 1863 under Massachusetts Gov. John Andrew; recruited free Black men from across the North (Frederick Douglass and others recruited for it). Commanded by **Col. Robert Gould Shaw (US)**.
  — Source: History.com, "The 54th Massachusetts Infantry"; American Battlefield Trust, "Fort Wagner and the 54th Massachusetts"

## BEAT 6: The Numbers

(See CONTESTED for the figures that vary by source — give ranges.)

- **Total Black men in the U.S. Army:** approximately **179,000–180,000** served over the war (≈178,000 is the most-cited count for the USCT proper). They made up roughly **10%** (about one-tenth) of the Union army's manpower.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "United States Colored Troops"; American Battlefield Trust, "United States Colored Troops"; National Archives

- **Number of USCT regiments:** **175** by war's end (1865).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "United States Colored Troops"

- **Origins:** A large share came from the slave states. Wikipedia's breakdown: **93,796 from Southern (slave) states** vs. **79,283 from Northern states** — i.e., the majority were Southern, formerly enslaved men. (The "~135,000+ from slave states" figure circulates in some popular sources; the 93,796 figure is the documented enlistment-by-state count — see CONTESTED.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "United States Colored Troops"

- **U.S. Navy:** approximately **18,000–19,000** Black men served in the Union Navy (the Navy was integrated and never barred Black sailors). ~19,000 is the common figure.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "United States Colored Troops"; National Park Service / Naval History

- **Deaths:** roughly **40,000** Black soldiers died (most-cited round figure). The **majority died of disease**, not combat. Wikipedia's tally: total deaths from all causes **68,178** for "all African Americans enrolled" (a broader denominator), of which combat deaths were about **2,751** — i.e., disease/non-combat dwarfed battlefield deaths. The mortality rate for Black troops ran roughly **35% higher** than for white Union troops, owing to worse camp conditions, fatigue/labor duty, and poorer medical care. [The "40,000" and the "68,178" come from different denominators — see CONTESTED.]
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust; Wikipedia, "United States Colored Troops"; National Archives

- **Medal of Honor:** **16 USCT soldiers** earned the Medal of Honor for actions during the war (the figure most often cited for army USCT; some tallies say 18 when Navy/other Black recipients are folded in, and ~25 Black recipients total for the war counting sailors). Use "16 USCT soldiers" with a note. (Separately, **14** of those army medals came from a SINGLE battle — New Market Heights — see Beat 8.)
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust ("14 of the USCT"); Wikipedia ("Eighteen"); see CONTESTED

## BEAT 7: The Pay Fight

- **The disparity:** Black soldiers were paid **$10 per month, minus a $3 deduction for clothing = $7 net**. White soldiers received **$13 per month PLUS a clothing allowance** (i.e., white soldiers were not docked for clothing). So the gap was not merely $13 vs $10 — it was $13-plus-clothing vs $7-after-clothing. The pay law that set this was the Militia Act of 1862, which had envisioned Black men as laborers, not combat soldiers.
  — Source: LegalClarity, "Facts About the 54th Massachusetts Regiment and Equal Pay"; National Archives, "The Fight for Equal Rights"; Massachusetts Historical Society, "The Battles of the 54th: Northern Racism and the Unequal Pay Crisis"

- **The 54th Massachusetts protest — refusing ALL pay:** The men of the 54th (and 55th) Massachusetts **refused to accept ANY pay at all** rather than accept unequal pay — for roughly **18 months**. When the Massachusetts legislature, urged by Gov. John Andrew, appropriated state money in November 1863 to make up the difference, the regiments **refused that too** — they wanted equal pay from the U.S. government as a matter of principle and equal standing, not a state subsidy that left the federal insult intact.
  — Source: LegalClarity; Massachusetts Historical Society; Massachusetts Secretary of State, "Fire and Thunder"

- **Cpl. James Henry Gooding's letter to Lincoln:** **Cpl. James Henry Gooding (US)**, 54th Massachusetts — a formerly enslaved man and former whaler out of New Bedford — wrote directly to President Lincoln on **September 28, 1863**, from Morris Island, S.C., demanding equal pay. His central line: **"Now the main question is. Are we Soldiers, or are we LABOURERS?"** Gooding was later captured, sent to **Andersonville** prison, and **died there (July 19, 1864)** — before he could collect the equal pay Congress finally granted.
  — Source: NPS, "Corporal James H. Gooding"; DocsTeach (National Archives), "Letter from Corporal Gooding to President Lincoln"; Time, "Read the Letter a Black Civil War Soldier Wrote..." (see KEY QUOTES)

- **Sgt. William Walker, executed for a pay protest:** **Sgt. William Walker** of the **3rd South Carolina Volunteers (Colored)**, a formerly enslaved man, led men of his company to **stack arms** in protest of unequal pay in **November 1863** at Jacksonville, FL, declaring **"We will not do duty any longer for seven dollars per month."** He was court-martialed, convicted of **mutiny (verdict Jan 9, 1864)**, and **executed by firing squad on February 29, 1864**. He is the starkest case of how the pay fight could cost a life.
  — Source: Zinn Education Project, "Sgt. William Walker Found Guilty of Mutiny"; Wikipedia, "3rd South Carolina Volunteer Infantry Regiment (Colored)"; Military Times, "Black Union soldiers fought a costly battle for equal pay"

- **The fix — Congress equalizes pay, 1864:** Congress passed equal-pay legislation in **June/July 1864**, making equal pay **retroactive** — to **January 1, 1864** for most, and **back to the date of enlistment** for men who could prove they had been free before the war (a galling distinction that still penalized the formerly enslaved, later largely smoothed over). The 54th finally received its full back pay in fall 1864.
  — Source: LegalClarity; National Archives; Massachusetts Historical Society

## BEAT 8: The Battles That Proved Them

### Port Hudson — May 27, 1863 (Louisiana)
- The **1st and 3rd Louisiana Native Guard** assaulted the Confederate works at Port Hudson on the Mississippi. It was the **first time Black troops fought in a major, front-line assault**. They charged repeatedly into murderous fire and took heavy losses (~the two regiments suffered on the order of several hundred casualties), and though the assault failed, it answered the doubters: Black troops would fight.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Port Hudson"; Wikipedia, "Siege of Port Hudson"; 64 Parishes

### Milliken's Bend — June 7, 1863 (Louisiana, Vicksburg Campaign)
- A small but pivotal fight. About **1,250 men** of the African Brigade — the **9th, 11th, and 13th Louisiana** and the **1st Mississippi Infantry (African Descent)**, mostly ex-slaves enlisted only days or weeks earlier — held off a Confederate attack in some of the war's fiercest **hand-to-hand combat** (bayonets, muskets used as clubs). Their stand changed Union perceptions overnight. Charles Dana relayed to Sec. of War Stanton that the Black troops' gallantry was beyond question.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust; American History Central, "Battle of Milliken's Bend"

### Fort Wagner / Battery Wagner — July 18, 1863 (Morris Island, SC)
- The **54th Massachusetts**, under **Col. Robert Gould Shaw (US)**, led the doomed twilight assault on the Confederate Battery Wagner. They breached the parapet but could not hold it. **Shaw was killed** on the parapet (shouting "Forward, 54th!") and was **buried by the Confederates in a mass grave with his Black soldiers** — meant as an insult, embraced by the North as honor ("buried with his men").
  — Source: History.com, "Robert Gould Shaw / Assault on Fort Wagner"; NPS, "The 54th Massachusetts and the Second Battle of Fort Wagner"; American Battlefield Trust
- **Casualties:** the 54th lost roughly **half** its men engaged (on the order of ~270+ killed/wounded/captured of ~600 engaged); total Union losses for the whole assault were over 1,500 to the Confederates' ~222.
  — Source: History.com; NPS — see CONTESTED on exact 54th casualty count
- **Sgt. William Harvey Carney (US)** seized the falling U.S. flag, carried it to the parapet and back despite multiple wounds, and said: **"Boys, the old flag never touched the ground."** His action is recognized as the **earliest action for which a Black soldier earned the Medal of Honor** — though the medal itself was not awarded to him until **May 23, 1900**. (Several Black soldiers received medals before Carney's 1900 award; Carney's *deed* at Fort Wagner is the earliest-dated.)
  — Source: NPS, "William H. Carney"; U.S. Army, "Meet Sgt. William Carney"; Wikipedia, "William Harvey Carney"
- **Lewis Henry Douglass**, son of Frederick Douglass, was the regiment's **sergeant major** and was in the assault (and wounded). (See Beat 9.)
  — Source: Library of Congress, "Lewis Henry Douglass"; Wikipedia, "Lewis Henry Douglass"

### The Battle of the Crater — July 30, 1864 (Petersburg, VA)
- A division of USCT under **Brig. Gen. Edward Ferrero** had been **specially trained to lead** the assault after the mine explosion at Petersburg. The day before, **Gen. Meade overruled Burnside** and pulled the USCT from the lead — fearing political blame if the (planned-to-lead) Black troops were slaughtered. An untrained white division (Ledlie's) went in first, bungled it, and piled into the crater. The USCT were finally sent in later, into chaos.
  — Source: NPS, "Battle of the Crater"; Wikipedia, "Battle of the Crater"
- When the Confederate counterattack (Mahone's men) overran the crater, **Black soldiers trying to surrender were singled out and killed** — Confederates were heard shouting orders to kill the Black troops; some USCT soldiers were bayoneted after capture. Some white officers tore off their USCT insignia to avoid being killed alongside their men. Total Union losses ~**3,798** (504 killed, 1,881 wounded, 1,413 missing/captured); a heavy share fell on Ferrero's USCT division.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of the Crater"; NPS

### New Market Heights / Chaffin's Farm — September 29, 1864 (near Richmond, VA)
- USCT regiments of the **Army of the James** (Gen. **Benjamin Butler**'s command) stormed strong Confederate works guarding Richmond. Two USCT brigades (~2,000+ men) carried the heights at brutal cost — well over **800 casualties, 130+ killed**.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Covered With Glory" / "New Market Heights"; Warfare History Network
- **Fourteen (14) Black soldiers earned the Medal of Honor** for this single battle — the **largest number of Black Medal of Honor recipients from any single Civil War engagement**. Recipients included Sgt. Maj. **Christian Fleetwood** (4th USCT), 1st Sgt. **Powhatan Beaty** (5th USCT), Sgt. Maj. **Milton Holland** (5th USCT), and 1st Sgt. **Edward Ratcliff** (38th USCT), among others.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust; National Cemetery Administration, "Medal of Honor Recipients: Battle of Chaffin's Farm"; Warfare History Network (full 14-name list available)
- An afterword that lands the theme: Butler, who in 1861 had coined "contraband," watched these same self-emancipated men and their sons carry the works at Richmond's door in 1864.
  — Source: synthesized from American Battlefield Trust New Market Heights coverage + Encyclopedia Virginia, "Benjamin F. Butler"

## BEAT 9: Fort Pillow and the No-Quarter Question (its MEANING for the USCT)

(The tactical blow-by-blow belongs to the atrocities/guerrilla section. Here: what it MEANT.)

- **Fort Pillow Massacre — April 12, 1864 (Tennessee, Mississippi River).** Confederate cavalry under **Maj. Gen. Nathan Bedford Forrest (CS)** overran the Union garrison (~600 men, roughly half of them Black USCT, plus white Tennessee Unionists). After the fort fell, **surrendering and captured soldiers were killed** — Black soldiers especially. Of ~300 Union dead, close to **200 were African American**; survival rates were starkly racial (~70% of white soldiers survived vs. ~35% of Black soldiers). Confederate losses were about 14.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"; Britannica, "Fort Pillow Massacre"; American Battlefield Trust, "The Battle of Fort Pillow"
- **"Remember Fort Pillow!"** became a **battle cry** for USCT regiments for the rest of the war — a vow to give no quarter to an enemy that had given none. It hardened, rather than broke, Black soldiers' resolve.
  — Source: Britannica; History.com, "Fort Pillow Massacre"

- **The Confederate policy behind it:** In 1862–63 the Confederate government declared it would treat captured Black soldiers NOT as prisoners of war but as **insurrectionist slaves** — to be **re-enslaved or executed**, and their white officers punished (potentially executed) for "inciting servile insurrection." This is the policy context that made surrender lethal for the USCT.
  — Source: National Archives, "Black Soldiers in the U.S. Military"; Freedmen & Southern Society Project; Slate, "Abraham Lincoln... general order to protect black POWs"

- **Lincoln's Order of Retaliation — General Order No. 252, July 30, 1863.** Lincoln ordered that for every U.S. soldier killed in violation of the laws of war, a Confederate prisoner would be executed; for every one enslaved or sold into slavery, a Confederate prisoner would be put at hard labor until the U.S. soldier was freed and treated as a proper POW. The threat **tacitly restrained** the Confederacy into treating captured Black soldiers more like POWs for much of the rest of the war — though Black captives were still treated worse than white ones, and the order was rarely actually carried out. (Fort Pillow, April 1864, shows the policy's limits held only loosely.)
  — Source: Freedmen & Southern Society Project, "President Lincoln's Order of Retaliation, July 30, 1863"; Slate; Wallbuilders (text). See KEY QUOTES for the order's wording.

## BEAT 10: Voices — Douglass and the Citizenship Argument

- **Frederick Douglass as recruiter:** Once the Proclamation opened enlistment, Douglass threw himself into recruiting Black men, especially for the **54th Massachusetts**. His most famous recruiting broadside, **"Men of Color, To Arms!"**, was published **March 21, 1863** in Rochester, New York (in *Douglass' Monthly*).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Frederick Douglass"; Frederick Douglass Heritage, "Men of Color to Arms!"; American Battlefield Trust

- **His sons served:** Two of Douglass's sons enlisted in the **54th Massachusetts** in spring 1863. **Lewis Henry Douglass** became the regiment's **sergeant major** (the highest rank then open to a Black man) and was wounded in the Fort Wagner assault. **Charles Remond Douglass** also enlisted in the 54th but, sidelined by illness (a lung condition), saw no combat and later transferred to the 5th Massachusetts Cavalry. Douglass put his own children where his argument was.
  — Source: Library of Congress, "Lewis Henry Douglass"; Wikipedia, "Lewis Henry Douglass"; National Archives, "Douglass's Sons"

- **The core argument — service = undeniable claim to citizenship:** Douglass's case was that a Black man who soldiered for the Republic earned a claim to citizenship that no one could deny. (Full quote in KEY QUOTES.) This is the section's intellectual climax: the men who freed themselves now armed themselves, and in arming themselves staked a claim to the nation. NOTE the provenance carefully — the famous "eagle on his button" line comes from a **July 6, 1863 Philadelphia speech**, NOT from the "Men of Color, To Arms!" broadside. Don't conflate them.
  — Source: Civil War Monitor, "Voice from the Past: The Right to Citizenship"; American Battlefield Trust

### 1. Self-emancipation total (~500,000)
- "Around 500,000" fled toward Union lines across the whole war (NMAAHC). It is a cumulative war-long estimate, not a census; other framings give different figures and some count only those who reached camps. **Author guidance:** use "hundreds of thousands — an estimated half a million over the course of the war," not a hard number. [VERIFY against a second scholarly source if a precise figure is wanted.]

### 2. Total Black soldiers (179,000 vs 180,000 vs 178,000)
- Most-cited army figures: **~178,000–180,000**. Wikipedia/USCT says "approximately 178,000... served during the last two years"; ABT and NPS popularize "180,000"; National Archives cites "approximately 185,000" *service records* (records ≠ individual men, since some men have multiple records). **Author guidance:** "roughly 180,000 Black men served in the Union army — about one in ten Union soldiers." Note the ~10% as solid.

### 3. Origins: 93,796 (South) vs the popular "135,000+ from slave states"
- Wikipedia's state-enlistment table: **93,796 Southern / 79,283 Northern**. The popular "more than 130,000 came from the slave/Confederate states" figure appears in some sources and likely counts border slave states (MD, KY, MO, etc.) and/or DC differently than Wikipedia's "Southern states" column. **Author guidance:** safe claim = "the majority were Southern, formerly enslaved men" and "tens of thousands came from the slave states." If using a number, prefer "more than 90,000 from the Southern states" (documented) and flag the higher figure as definition-dependent.

### 4. Deaths (40,000 vs 68,178)
- "Roughly 40,000 Black soldiers died" is the standard popular figure (ABT/NPS). Wikipedia gives **68,178 total deaths from all causes** for "all African Americans enrolled in the military" (a broader denominator, possibly including navy/laborers) with only **2,751 combat deaths**. The key, well-supported point either way: **the great majority died of disease, not battle**, and the Black mortality rate ran ~**35% higher** than white troops'. **Author guidance:** "roughly 40,000 died — most of disease, not combat; their death rate ran about a third higher than white soldiers'." Flag the 68,178 as a different (broader) count.

### 5. Medal of Honor count (16 vs 18 vs ~25)
- **16 USCT (army) soldiers** is the figure tied to the war's USCT in many sources; Wikipedia's USCT page says **"Eighteen."** Counting Black sailors and all Black recipients for the war pushes toward **~25**. The undisputed sub-fact: **14 Black soldiers won the medal in a SINGLE battle (New Market Heights, Sept 29, 1864)** — the largest single-battle group. **Author guidance:** "16 USCT soldiers earned the Medal of Honor (some counts say 18); 14 of them in one battle at New Market Heights." Flag the variance.

### 6. Navy (18,000 vs 19,000)
- Commonly "~18,000" or "~19,000." Use "roughly 18,000–19,000 Black sailors in the Union Navy."

### 7. 54th Massachusetts casualties at Fort Wagner
- The regiment lost roughly HALF its men engaged. Figures cited: ~272 (one History.com phrasing of "Shaw and 272 of his troops killed" — but that conflates the whole assault), or ~270–280 killed/wounded/captured out of ~600. **Author guidance:** "the 54th lost nearly half its men — on the order of 270–280 of about 600 engaged." Avoid a false-precise single number; the "1,515 total Union losses to 222 Confederate" figure is for the whole multi-regiment assault, not the 54th alone.

### 8. Pay equalization retroactive date
- Equal-pay act passed **June/July 1864**; retroactive to **Jan 1, 1864** for most, and to **enlistment date** for those provably free before the war. **Author guidance:** the "free-before-the-war vs enslaved" distinction is itself a telling injustice worth a sentence.

### 9. "First officially mustered" Black regiment
- **1st Louisiana Native Guard** (mustered Sept 27, 1862) = first OFFICIALLY MUSTERED into U.S. service. **1st South Carolina Volunteers** = first ORGANIZED FROM FORMER SLAVES (mustered Jan 31, 1863). **1st Kansas Colored** = first to SEE COMBAT (Island Mound, Oct 29, 1862, before federal muster). These three "firsts" are distinct — don't collapse them; each is true in its own category.

### 10. Carney's Medal of Honor — "first" is a deed-vs-award distinction
- Carney's **action** at Fort Wagner (July 18, 1863) is the **earliest-dated action** for which a Black soldier received the Medal of Honor. But the **award itself** came late — **May 23, 1900** — so other Black soldiers were physically *awarded* the medal before him. **Author guidance:** say "the first Medal of Honor *action* by a Black soldier," not "the first Black man to receive" it.

## KEY QUOTES (verbatim — verify before any change to wording)

**1. Frederick Douglass — the citizenship-through-service line (the section's thesis quote):**
> "Once let the black man get upon his person the brass letter, U.S., let him get an eagle on his button, and a musket on his shoulder and bullets in his pocket, there is no power on earth that can deny that he has earned the right to citizenship."
- **Provenance:** Speech, **July 6, 1863, National Hall, Philadelphia** (titled "Should the Negro Enlist in the Union Army?"). NOT the "Men of Color, To Arms!" broadside — do not misattribute.
  — Source: Civil War Monitor, "Voice from the Past: The Right to Citizenship"; American Battlefield Trust, "United States Colored Troops"

**2. Frederick Douglass — "Men of Color, To Arms!" (the recruiting broadside):**
> "By every consideration which binds you to your enslaved fellow-countrymen, and the peace and welfare of your country; by every aspiration which you cherish for the freedom and equality of yourselves and your children; by all the ties of blood and identity which make us one with the brave black men now fighting our battles in Louisiana and in South Carolina, I urge you to fly to arms, and smite with death the power that would bury the government and your liberty in the same hopeless grave."
- **Provenance:** Broadside / *Douglass' Monthly*, **Rochester, NY, March 21, 1863.**
  — Source: Frederick Douglass Heritage, "Men of Color to Arms!"; Political Rhetoric Archive

**3. Benjamin Butler — "contraband of war":**
- Butler labeled the three escaped men **"contraband of war."** The exact phrasing in his May 1861 report and correspondence used the property-of-war logic; the durable, quotable phrase is "contraband of war." [The single most-quoted two words; the surrounding sentence varies by source — if a full sentence is wanted, pull it from Butler's official May 24–27, 1861 report in the *Official Records* before quoting at sentence length. VERIFY full-sentence wording.]
  — Source: The Mariners' Museum, "Ben Butler and the Contrabands"; Encyclopedia.com, "Benjamin Butler's Report on the Contrabands of War (1861)"

**4. Cpl. James Henry Gooding to Lincoln (Sept 28, 1863) — the dignity question:**
> "Now the main question is. Are we Soldiers, or are we LABOURERS?"
- And, framing his appeal: "...the earnest Solicitation of my Comrades in Arms, besides the genuine interest felt by myself in the matter is my excuse, for placing before the Executive head of the Nation our Common Grievance..."
- **Provenance:** Letter, Cpl. James Henry Gooding, 54th Mass., **Morris Island, S.C., September 28, 1863**, to President Lincoln (original in the National Archives). Original spelling/capitalization preserved as shown.
  — Source: DocsTeach (National Archives); UCL "Gooding letter" transcription; Time, "Read the Letter a Black Civil War Soldier Wrote..."; *Free at Last: A Documentary History* (Berlin et al.) for the full vetted transcript. [VERIFY full letter against *Free at Last* if quoting beyond these lines.]

**5. Sgt. William Walker — the protest line that got him shot:**
> "We will not do duty any longer for seven dollars per month."
- **Provenance:** Sgt. William Walker, 3rd South Carolina Volunteers, stacking arms in protest, **November 1863**, Jacksonville, FL. Convicted of mutiny Jan 9, 1864; executed Feb 29, 1864.
  — Source: Zinn Education Project; Wikipedia, "3rd South Carolina Volunteer Infantry Regiment (Colored)"; Military Times

**6. Sgt. William Carney — the flag:**
> "Boys, the old flag never touched the ground."
- (Also rendered "Boys, I only did my duty; the old flag never touched the ground.") **Provenance:** Sgt. William H. Carney, 54th Mass., after the Fort Wagner assault, **July 18, 1863.**
  — Source: U.S. Army, "Meet Sgt. William Carney"; NPS, "William H. Carney"

**7. Lincoln — Order of Retaliation (General Order No. 252, July 30, 1863):**
> "For every soldier of the United States killed in violation of the laws of war, a rebel soldier shall be executed; and for every one enslaved by the enemy or sold into slavery, a rebel soldier shall be placed at hard labor on the public works, and continued at such labor until the other shall be released and receive the treatment due to a prisoner of war."
- **Provenance:** General Order No. 252, **July 30, 1863.**
  — Source: Freedmen & Southern Society Project (umd.edu), "President Lincoln's Order of Retaliation"; Slate

**8. Dennis/Dana on Milliken's Bend (the doubters answered):**
> "It is impossible for men to show greater gallantry than the negro troops in this fight."
- **Provenance:** Reported by Charles A. Dana (War Dept. commissioner) to Sec. of War Stanton, attributing the assessment to Gen. Elias Dennis, after **Milliken's Bend, June 7, 1863.**
  — Source: American History Central, "Battle of Milliken's Bend"

## KEY PEOPLE (pinned with side)

- **Benjamin F. Butler (US)** — Maj. Gen.; coined "contraband of war" at Fort Monroe (May 1861); raised the Louisiana Native Guards (1862); commanded the Army of the James whose USCT carried New Market Heights (1864). Massachusetts Democrat, not a prewar abolitionist.
- **Robert Gould Shaw (US)** — Col., 54th Massachusetts; killed leading the Fort Wagner assault, July 18, 1863; buried with his Black soldiers.
- **William Harvey Carney (US)** — Sgt., 54th Massachusetts; saved the flag at Fort Wagner; earliest-dated Medal of Honor action by a Black soldier (medal awarded 1900).
- **Frederick Douglass** — abolitionist; wartime recruiter ("Men of Color, To Arms!"); author of the citizenship-through-service argument. (His antebellum life belongs to "Freedom Struggle.")
- **Lewis Henry Douglass / Charles Remond Douglass (US)** — Frederick's sons; both enlisted in the 54th Mass.; Lewis = sergeant major, wounded at Fort Wagner.
- **Thomas Wentworth Higginson (US)** — Col., 1st South Carolina Volunteers (first regiment of former slaves; later 33rd USCT); wrote *Army Life in a Black Regiment*.
- **Nathan Bedford Forrest (CS)** — Maj. Gen.; commander at the Fort Pillow Massacre, April 12, 1864. (Postwar first Grand Wizard of the KKK — relevant context but likely owned by another section; mention with care.)
- **James Henry Gooding (US)** — Cpl., 54th Mass.; wrote Lincoln demanding equal pay (Sept 1863); died at Andersonville (1864).
- **William Walker (CS-enslaved → US)** — Sgt., 3rd South Carolina Volunteers; executed Feb 29, 1864 for leading a pay protest.
- **Charles King Mallory (CS)** — Confederate colonel who enslaved Baker, Mallory, and Townsend.
- **John Baytop Cary (CS)** — Confederate major who demanded the three men's return under the Fugitive Slave Act.
