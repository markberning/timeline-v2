## FIRST-CHECK (ship-blocking)

### (a) Are Native nations first-class actors with their own aims, not props? — PASS / FAIR

This is the section most at risk on this axis: a *finance* theme can easily reduce
Pontiac's War to "the thing that made the garrison look necessary." The draft does
the opposite, and does it deliberately.

- The interior is named as Native land up front, not a void to be inherited: "It was
  Native land, full of nations with their own armies, their own diplomacy, and no
  intention of being inherited like furniture" (§"An army that wouldn't go home").
- The draft explicitly refuses the prop framing before it could happen: "they are not
  a backdrop to this story. They are one of its two endings."
- An entire dedicated section, "The people the army was for," gives the nations their
  own aims, ideology, leverage, and named leadership: Neolin's spiritual revival;
  Amherst's cut-off of customary diplomatic gifts read as contempt; settler
  encroachment; "a war to defend their sovereignty, fought on their own account."
- It resists flattening the nations into one confederacy — "These were allied
  communities acting in concert, not one confederacy under a single commander, and it
  matters not to flatten them into one" — matching the fact pack's §10 caution.
- The section title "The people the army was for" is pointedly ironic: the army's job
  was suppressing a Native war for sovereignty, which quietly punctures the imperial
  "defense" rationale presented later.
- Outcome correctly framed as a stalemate, "not a Native defeat."

Native nations are first-class actors with their own aims and leverage throughout.

### (b) Is the human cost / dispossession named in the section's OWN prose? — PASS / FAIR

- The Proclamation Line is not presented as a durable safeguard: it "protected it only
  briefly and only in theory; colonists ignored the line almost immediately, and it
  was steadily eroded" — matches the fact pack's explicit dispossession caveat.
- The cost is named plainly in the closing synthesis: "a catastrophe for the nations
  of the interior, whose land the whole contest had ultimately been about and whose
  'protected' territory was overrun anyway."
- And, bluntly: "they paid the steepest price of anyone for a victory that was never
  theirs to win."

Dispossession and the human cost are named in the section's own prose, not euphemized
or omitted.

### Title-POV acknowledgment — PASS

The draft makes the required acknowledgment that the war's name centers the
British-colonial enemy's-eye view: "the very name 'French and Indian War' is the
British colonists' name, given for their enemies, as if the Native nations were
simply on the other side. In truth they fought on every side, and most of all for
themselves." Exactly the note the rule asks for.

## OTHER FRAMING FINDINGS

### 1. The triumphal opening ("greatest victory in its history") — FAIR
"Britain had just won the greatest victory in its history. France was driven off the
North American continent" could read as romanticizing conquest in isolation. It is
not: the line is immediately undercut ("And then the bill came due"), and the section
repeatedly recasts the "victory" as a catastrophe for Native nations and the seed of
Britain's own undoing ("a victory that was never theirs to win"). The triumphal note
is set up to be dismantled, not endorsed. No false glory.

### 2. Grenville's "almost reasonable" defense rationale — FAIR
The draft calls Grenville's logic "almost reasonable" (beneficiaries / army for their
defense / they should pay). This is attributed explicitly to Grenville's own reasoning
("his logic was, on its own terms, almost reasonable"), not asserted as fact, and it
has already been complicated by the preceding section showing the army's real job was
fighting a Native war for sovereignty. The imperial rationale is presented as a
point of view, then pressured — not adopted. No false balance.

### 3. Violence handling — FAIR
Casualty figures (~400 soldiers, ~2,000 settlers killed or captured) are flagged as
estimates per the fact pack, neither sanitized nor gratuitous. The draft omits British
atrocity detail (e.g., Amherst's smallpox-blanket proposal), but that is an omission
of detail outside the fact pack, not a framing distortion, and is acceptable for a
finance-theme section.

### 4. Presentism — FAIR
"No intention of being inherited like furniture" and Neolin's "bad habits" (correctly
attributed to his own teaching, not the narrator's judgment) read as voice, not
anachronistic moralizing.

### 5. Slogan / quotation discipline — FAIR (and notably careful)
"No taxation without representation" is presented as "that distilled idea" / "the cry,"
NOT as a quoted 1765 utterance — exactly the fact pack's §7 caution. The verified Otis
1764 line is used instead, and the Samuel Johnson quote carries no misleading 1765
date. This is a place a framing distortion (a fabricated founding quote) could have
crept in; it didn't.

### 6. Colonist side-tags "(British)" — FAIR (note only)
Otis, Johnson, and Oliver are tagged "(British)" per the war person-side convention.
Correct for 1764–65 subjects, if mildly ironic given the section's theme; not a
framing problem.

## SUMMARY
Both ship-blocking first-checks pass. Native nations are first-class actors with their
own sovereignty aims and leverage; the human cost and dispossession are named plainly
in the section's own prose; the title's British-colonial POV is acknowledged. No
DISTORTED findings. No false balance, no romanticized empire, no erasure of Native
agency, no presentism, no sanitized or gratuitous violence stands. This is, if
anything, a model of how to keep Native nations central inside a finance story whose
gravity would otherwise pull entirely toward the London–colonist axis.

**VERDICT: PASS. No mustFix.**
