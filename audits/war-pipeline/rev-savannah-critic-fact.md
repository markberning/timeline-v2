## BLOCKERS

**B1 · Pulaski mtDNA framing — "a grandniece" is correct but the hedge is
incomplete; the failed first run is suppressed**

> (Section 3 prose, "Pulaski's bones" passage):
> "a Georgia Southern-led team reported that the skeleton was consistent with
> Pulaski's known injuries and that its mitochondrial DNA matched a grandniece's,
> concluding the Monterey Square remains are most likely Pulaski's"

> (Fact ledger, §3 entry for Pulaski):
> "2019 Georgia Southern study — skeleton consistent with injuries + mtDNA match
> to a grandniece → 'most likely Pulaski's'"

**The issue:** The description sounds cleaner than the published record actually
is. The 2019 study involved two separate mtDNA extraction attempts. The first run
(tooth from the monument vs. femur from grandniece Teresa Witkowska) produced NO
MATCH. The team re-ran with different samples (talus bone from the monument vs.
metatarsal from Witkowska) and that second attempt did yield the shared mtDNA
profile (excluding ~99% of the population). The draft's phrasing "its mitochondrial
DNA matched a grandniece's" presents the positive result without disclosing the
failed first attempt — which is precisely the kind of methodological complexity
the pack flags as "DISPUTED at the edges" and is the source of the 2022 scholarly
challenge to the identification. Suppressing the two-attempt structure understates
the epistemic fragility of the finding and misrepresents the study in a way that
will mislead readers who encounter the 2022 rebuttal.

**Source:** Georgia Southern Newsroom (2019-03-28) + The History Blog (2019-04-08)
reconstruction of the two-attempt sequence; Live Science (2019-04-08) "DNA from
Pulaski and a maternal relative matched... excluded 99%."

**Fix:** Revise the passage to acknowledge that the identification rested on a
second mtDNA attempt after the first sample comparison did not match, and that
the positive result is why the team concluded "most likely" rather than
"definitively." Suggested replacement for the relevant sentence:

> "a 2019 Georgia Southern-led study — involving two rounds of mitochondrial DNA
> extraction, with the first sample attempt producing no match before a second
> pairing succeeded — reported the skeleton consistent with Pulaski's known
> injuries and its mtDNA profile matching a grandniece's, excluding roughly 99%
> of the population, and concluding the remains are most likely Pulaski's."

The fact ledger entry needs the same amendment.

**B2 · Vessel classification: "aboard the American brig Wasp" — the Wasp was a
privateer/merchant brig, not a Continental Navy vessel**

> (Section 3 prose, Pulaski passage):
> "He was carried off the field and taken aboard the American brig Wasp"

> (Commander bio, Pulaski card):
> "carried off the field, and died aboard ship about two days later."
> (bio itself is fine; the mislabeling is in the section prose only)

> (Dossier fact ledger):
> "grapeshot; carried off, aboard the Wasp, died ~2 days later (~Oct 11)"
> (ledger omits the vessel type — fine as written, but the prose must fix)

**The issue:** The Wasp was a South Carolina merchant brig operating as a
privateer — a privately-owned armed vessel with a letter of marque, not a
Continental Navy ship. Calling it "the American brig" without qualification
implies Continental or government naval service. Wikipedia, the New Georgia
Encyclopedia, and multiple secondary sources consistently describe it as
"the privateer brig Wasp" or "the privateer merchant brigantine Wasp."
"American brig" is not wrong in the broadest sense but is materially
misleading at this level of detail.

**Source:** New Georgia Encyclopedia, "Casimir Pulaski in Georgia"; Wikipedia
"Casimir Pulaski"; Naval History and Heritage Command records.

**Fix:** Change "aboard the American brig Wasp" to "aboard the privateer brig
Wasp" in Section 3 prose.

## SHOULD-FIX

**SF1 · Bombardment date: draft says "the night of October 3rd to 4th" — the
guns were in battery position October 3 but the bombardment opened October 4**

> (Section 2 prose):
> "on the night of October 3rd to 4th, the allies opened a bombardment of the
> town that ran for about five days"

**The issue:** Multiple sources (American History Central, britishbattles.com)
give the same picture: by October 3 the allied guns were in battery position;
the bombardment itself opened on October 4 and ran until October 8. The pack's
own §12.14 flags this as a date-split and says "the night of Oct 3-4 is safe."
That hedged formulation is defensible but it papers over the cleaner fact: the
guns fired starting October 4. Using "October 3rd to 4th" to describe when the
bombardment "opened" is slightly ambiguous and could be read as saying firing
began on the night of October 3rd, which is incorrect.

**Source:** americanhistorycentral.com: "the bombardment of Savannah started on
October 4"; britishbattles.com: "By October 3, the Allies had 53 heavy cannons
and 14 mortars in battery positions... the bombardment of Savannah started on
October 4."

**Fix:** Change to "On October 4, the allies opened a bombardment of the town
that ran for about five days." The pack acknowledges the split; "October 4" is
the cleaner and more accurate formulation. The "night of October 3–4" hedge
exists in the pack because some sources give October 3 for the start; "October 4"
is supported by both major web sources checked.

**SF2 · Summons date framing: September 16 summons issued "before his American
partner had even joined him" — technically accurate but potentially misleading;
Lincoln arrived September 16 itself**

> (Section 2 prose):
> "On September 16, 1779, before his American partner had even joined him, the
> comte d'Estaing summoned Savannah to surrender."

**The issue:** The pack (§4) and multiple sources say the summons was issued
September 16 "ahead of Lincoln's junction." Lincoln's forces arrived on or about
September 16 — the same day as the summons. The phrasing "before his American
partner had even joined him" implies Lincoln was still days away, when in fact
they were arriving essentially simultaneously. The notable grievance was that
d'Estaing issued the summons in the name of France before the joint arrival was
complete and without consulting Lincoln — not that Lincoln was days behind.

**Source:** britishbattles.com, American History Central; pack §4 "ahead of
Lincoln's junction."

**Fix:** Tighten to: "On September 16, 1779, as his American partner's forces
were still arriving, the comte d'Estaing summoned Savannah to surrender" —
or simply "On September 16, before Lincoln's army had fully joined him."

**SF3 · Moncrief's fortifications: "roughly 1,200-foot arc of redoubts" —
the 1,200 feet refers to the earthwork line, not the arc's total circumference**

> (British sides block, Moncrief bio):
> "Using 500 to 800 enslaved African Americans working up to twelve hours a day,
> he threw up a roughly 1,200-foot arc of redoubts and gun batteries"

> (Dossier note):
> "a Royal Engineer's earthwork sprint dug by hundreds of enslaved laborers"

**The issue:** The pack (§3) gives "a ~1,200-foot arc of earthworks and redoubts"
— the draft copies this. The figure of 1,200 feet appears in the pack and
secondary literature as the depth/frontage of the defensive work, not the total
perimeter of all fortifications. Some sources describe the full British defensive
line as running roughly one mile across the landward face of the town. Using
"1,200-foot arc" for the entire defensive perimeter undersells the construction.
More precisely, the 1,200-foot figure refers to the main earthwork frontage; the
full defensive perimeter including the river flanks was considerably longer.
This is a pack-inherited precision issue, not an invention, but worth flagging.

**Source:** Pack §3 carries the same figure with no qualification. Web sources
do not give a cleaner figure for the total perimeter — "~1,200-foot arc" may
simply be the standard shorthand in the secondary literature. If so, no fix
needed; flag for reviser's awareness.

**Fix (optional):** Add a qualifying phrase: "a roughly 1,200-foot arc of main
earthworks, redoubts, and gun batteries around the landward face." Or leave as-is
given the pack sourcing — this is a polish item borderline on SHOULD-FIX.

**SF4 · Maitland's distance: Beaufort described as "up the coast" without
giving distance — the draft gives the route but not the ~50-mile figure**

> (British sides block, Maitland bio):
> "sick with fever, he brought about 800 men from Beaufort through the coastal
> creeks and marshes in roughly four days"

> (Section 2, MEANWHILE block):
> "Maitland's roughly 800 men spent about four days on the inside passage"

**The issue:** The web-verified distance from Beaufort to Savannah via the
inside passage is approximately 50 miles (britishbattles.com: "a garrison at
Beaufort about 50 miles away"). The draft describes the route in detail but
never gives the reader any sense of the distance Maitland covered. This is not
a factual error but an omission that weakens the storytelling case for why the
march was remarkable — four days to cover 50 miles through tidal creeks while
sick with fever is a very different picture than "four days" unanchored.

**Source:** britishbattles.com: "Beaufort about 50 miles away."

**Fix:** Add the distance in the Maitland bio and/or the MEANWHILE block:
"brought about 800 men roughly 50 miles from Beaufort through the coastal
creeks and marshes in roughly four days."

**N2 · "about 200 cavalry and infantry" for Pulaski's legion — the draft
describes ~200 as cavalry then adds "cavalry and infantry" in the Section 1
prose**

> (Section 1): "the legion of Casimir Pulaski (American), the Polish general
> Congress had made 'Commander of the Horse,' about 200 cavalry and infantry
> drawn from Poles, Frenchmen, Germans, Irishmen, and Americans."

> (Dossier sides block): "Pulaski's legion of ~200 horse"

The dossier says "~200 horse" (cavalry only). Section 1 expands to "200 cavalry
and infantry." The pack (§3) says "Pulaski's Legion (~200 cavalry + infantry)."
The two usages in the draft are slightly inconsistent with each other (the dossier
says cavalry-only, the prose says cavalry-and-infantry). Both are defensible
per the pack — the legion was mixed but its cavalry element is the famous core.
Minor inconsistency; reviser's call.
