## NEEDS-GLOSS (should-fix, NOT comprehension-breakers)
These are genuine jargon a cold 15-year-old likely does not know, but in each case
the surrounding sentence still delivers the event/point, so none is LOST.

1. **"portage"** (line 107: "at the Niagara portage"; line 146: "bodies left on
   the portages"). Genuine jargon — a place where boats/cargo are carried overland
   between waterways — and never glossed. The reader still grasps "an ambush at a
   place near Niagara," so comprehension survives, but a three-word gloss on first
   use ("the Niagara portage, the overland carry around the falls") would close it
   and also make the closing image land harder. SHOULD-FIX.

2. **"garrison"** (used repeatedly from "A Coalition" onward). Borderline — many
   15-year-olds know it, and "its fifteen-man garrison" / "the soldiers came out"
   make it recoverable from context on first appearance. A light first-use touch
   ("its garrison — the soldiers posted there") would remove all doubt. SHOULD-FIX,
   low priority.

3. **"New France"** (line 13: "The British conquest of New France"). Used before
   it is defined, but the same sentence's Quebec/Montreal/"French Canada" content
   makes it clear this is France's North American colony. Recoverable; a glancing
   gloss on first use would tidy it. SHOULD-FIX, low priority.
