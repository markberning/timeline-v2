## SCOPE / DEDUP NOTE

This is an "Off the Battlefield" POLITICS theme: how the peace of 1763 redrew a
continent over the heads of the people who lived on most of it. Its lane: the
*end* of the wider Seven Years' War (after Canada had already fallen); the
**Treaty of Paris (10 Feb 1763)** and its territorial settlement; the
**sugar-islands-vs-Canada** debate in Britain; the scale of the result (Britain
master of the continent east of the Mississippi; New France gone; an enlarged,
newly diverse empire); the **Royal Proclamation of 7 Oct 1763** and its boundary
line; *why* the Proclamation was issued; the colonial backlash and its feeding
toward revolution; and — the framing spine — that a continent's worth of Native
homeland was disposed of by powers an ocean apart, with **no Native nation a
party** to the treaty.

## MUST-COVER BEATS (arc = the pressure → the settlement → the line → the consequence)

1. **The global frame** — the FI War was the North American front of a world war; Canada had fallen by 1760 but the Seven Years' War ground on (Caribbean, India, Philippines, Europe); Spain's late entry (1762) cost it Havana and Manila; exhaustion brought everyone to terms.
2. **The Treaty of Paris (10 Feb 1763)** — the core territorial swap; France out of mainland North America; the secret prior cession of Louisiana to Spain (Fontainebleau, 1762); Spain trades Florida for Havana + Manila; what France *kept*.
3. **Sugar vs. Canada** — the real British debate that the wealth of Guadeloupe/Martinique might outweigh all of Canada; Voltaire's "a few acres of snow"; Britain's choice and why.
4. **The scale** — Britain now dominant east of the Mississippi; New France gone; a vastly enlarged, newly diverse empire to govern (French Catholic Canadians, the Floridas).
5. **The Royal Proclamation (7 Oct 1763)** — the line down the Appalachian crest; the West "reserved for the Indians"; settlement and private land-purchase forbidden west of it; new royal governments (Quebec, East & West Florida, Grenada).
6. **Why** — partly to calm/stabilize relations with Native nations during and after Pontiac's War; partly Crown control of the pace and profit of westward expansion.
7. **The backlash** — speculators (Ohio Company, Washington) and frontier settlers furious at being walled out of land they'd fought for; the line ignored almost at once, unenforceable, and a grievance on the road to revolution.
8. **The Native dimension (framing spine)** — the treaty disposed of immense Native homelands with no Native nation present or consenting (the same silence as the lead plates / royal land grant earlier in the war); the Proclamation Line was a promise the empire would not keep; name the dispossession plainly.

## BEAT 1: The Global Frame — A World War Outlives Its North American Front

- **One front of a world war.** The French and Indian War (the name in the British colonies) was the North American theatre of the **Seven Years' War**, a global conflict fought in the Caribbean, India, the Philippines, West Africa, and across Europe.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Treaty of Paris (1763)"; history.state.gov Milestones, "Treaty of Paris, 1763"

- **Canada fell first; the war went on.** Quebec fell in **1759**, Montreal in **1760** — New France was effectively conquered — but the wider war continued for two more years until the powers were exhausted.
  — Source: history.state.gov Milestones; Wikipedia, "Treaty of Paris (1763)" (dedup: details owned by `fi-conquest`)

- **Spain enters late (and loses big).** Spain (Bourbon, ruled by a branch of the same family as France's Bourbons) signed the **Third Family Compact with France in August 1761**. In December 1761 Spain embargoed British trade, seized British goods, and expelled British merchants; **Britain declared war on Spain on 4 January 1762.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Anglo-Spanish War (1762–1763)"

- **Havana and Manila.** In **August 1762 a British expedition captured Havana** (Cuba); about a month later, in **late 1762, Britain captured Manila** (the Philippines). Spain's entry on France's side thus cost it two of its richest colonial ports to British fleets — which is exactly what Spain bought back at the peace table by giving up Florida.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Anglo-Spanish War (1762–1763)"; Wikipedia, "Siege of Havana"

- **Britain's dominant negotiating hand.** By the time talks opened, Britain had taken from France (over the war) Canada, Guadeloupe, Martinique, Dominica, Pondicherry (India), Senegal, and Belle Île, and from Spain Havana and Manila — leaving Britain holding the strong cards.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Anglo-Spanish War (1762–1763)" / "Great Britain in the Seven Years' War"

## BEAT 2: The Treaty of Paris — 10 February 1763

- **Date, place, signatories.** Signed **10 February 1763** in Paris. Parties: **Great Britain, France, and Spain** (with **Portugal** in agreement).
  Negotiators: **Britain** — John Russell, 4th Duke of Bedford; **France** — César Gabriel de Choiseul, Duke of Praslin; **Spain** — Jerónimo Grimaldi, 1st Duke of Grimaldi. (The architect on the French side overall was foreign minister **Étienne-François, Duc de Choiseul.**)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Treaty of Paris (1763)"; Avalon Project (Yale Law), treaty text

- **France out of mainland North America east of the Mississippi.** France ceded to Britain **Canada with all its dependencies** plus all territory **east of the Mississippi River**, EXCEPT the city of New Orleans.
  - **Article IV (verbatim):** France cedes *"Canada, with all its dependencies, as well as the island of Cape Breton"* to Britain "in full right."
  - **Article VII (verbatim):** the boundary is fixed as *"a line drawn along the middle of the River Mississippi, from its source to the river Iberville,"* and *"the town and port of New Orleans and the island in which it is situated, shall remain to France,"* with navigation of the Mississippi guaranteed equally to both nations along its whole length.
  — Source: Avalon Project (Yale Law), "Treaty of Paris 1763," Arts. IV & VII

- **The secret prior deal: Louisiana to Spain (Fontainebleau, 1762).** Before the public peace, France had already **secretly ceded Louisiana — everything west of the Mississippi, plus New Orleans — to Spain** in the **Treaty of Fontainebleau, 1762.** So the New Orleans that the Paris treaty left "to France" was in fact already promised to Spain; France was getting out, just by two doors.
  **[NOTE for author]** Spain did not actually take physical possession of New Orleans/Louisiana until **1769** (the changeover was slow and later sparked unrest among the French inhabitants). Useful nuance; not essential.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Treaty of Paris (1763)"; Britannica, "Treaty of Paris (1763)"

- **Spain trades Florida for its captured ports.** Spain ceded **Florida** to Britain; in exchange Britain restored **Havana/Cuba** (and the Philippines/Manila) to Spain.
  - **Article XIX (verbatim sense):** Britain restores **Cuba and Havana** to Spain.
  - **Article XX (verbatim):** Spain cedes *"Florida, with Fort St. Augustin, and the Bay of Pensacola, as well as all that Spain possesses on the continent of North America"* — i.e. all Spanish holdings east/southeast of the Mississippi.
  — Source: Avalon Project (Yale Law), Arts. XIX & XX; history.state.gov Milestones

- **What France KEPT.** France retained the wealthy Caribbean **sugar islands Guadeloupe and Martinique** (plus Saint Lucia and Gorée in West Africa), its **Indian trading factories** (Chandernagore, Pondicherry — restored, but stripped of fortification rights), its **Newfoundland fishing rights**, and the small islands of **Saint Pierre and Miquelon** as a fishing base.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Treaty of Paris (1763)"; Avalon Project

- **Religious / emigration terms for Canada.**
  - **Article IV (verbatim):** *"His Britannick Majesty, on his side, agrees to grant the liberty of the Catholick religion to the inhabitants of Canada."* French inhabitants were also given **18 months** to emigrate if they chose.
  — Source: Avalon Project, Art. IV; history.state.gov Milestones

## BEAT 3: "Sugar Islands vs. Canada" — The Debate in Britain

- **A real argument.** During the negotiations there was a genuine debate over whether Britain should keep **Canada** or the **sugar island of Guadeloupe** (Britain had captured both and intended to hand some conquests back to avoid provoking a fresh war). The economic case for the sugar was strong.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Treaty of Paris (1763)" / "A few acres of snow"

- **The economic figures.** Guadeloupe was producing on the order of **£6 million a year** in sugar; it was said that **Guadeloupe alone produced more sugar than all of Britain's existing Caribbean islands combined.** Canada, by contrast, was seen by many as a costly near-wilderness yielding mainly **codfish and beaver/furs.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Treaty of Paris (1763)"; Wikipedia, "A few acres of snow"

- **Voltaire's famous dismissal (verbatim, with source).** In *Candide* (written 1758, published **1759**, Ch. 23), Voltaire has a character say the two nations *"are at war about **a few acres of snow** somewhere around Canada, and… are spending on this beautiful war more than all Canada is worth."*
  - **Original French:** *"pour quelques arpents de neige vers le Canada."*
  — Source: Wikipedia, "A few acres of snow" (quotes *Candide*, Ch. 23)

- **Britain's choice and why.** Britain chose to **keep Canada and return Guadeloupe to France.** The reasoning was strategic rather than commercial: holding Canada **removed the French military threat from the back of the Thirteen Colonies** and secured the continental interior, whereas the sugar islands, however rich, would have kept Britain entangled with a still-dangerous French Caribbean. (Choiseul, for France, was content: he prized the defensible, profitable islands over the drain of Canada.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Treaty of Paris (1763)" / "A few acres of snow"; history.state.gov Milestones
  **[CONTESTED — degree, not fact]** Whether the sugar was "really" worth more than Canada was argued at the time and is still debated; the *fact of the debate* and the headline figures above are solid. Frame as "some in London argued," not as settled accounting.

## BEAT 4: The Scale of the Result

- **Britain, master of the east.** The treaty made Britain **dominant in North America east of the Mississippi** and **ended New France** as a power on the continent. The French military threat to the colonies was gone.
  — Source: history.state.gov Milestones; Wikipedia, "Treaty of Paris (1763)"

- **A bigger, more diverse empire to govern.** Britain now had to administer a **French, Catholic population in Canada** (hence the religious-liberty guarantee) and the **newly acquired Floridas** (organized as two colonies, see Beat 5) — a far more varied empire than before.
  — Source: history.state.gov Milestones

- **The seed of revolution (forward handoff — keep brief).** The war had been "enormously expensive"; Britain financed it on debt and then sought to recoup costs through colonial taxation and to manage the frontier cheaply. The State Department's own summary: the treaty *"ultimately encouraged dissension between Anglo-American colonists and the British Government because their interests in North America no longer coincided."* (Hand off to the war-finance / road-to-revolution thread; do not narrate the Stamp Act etc. here.)
  — Source: history.state.gov Milestones (verbatim phrase quoted)

## BEAT 5: The Royal Proclamation of 7 October 1763

- **Date and issuer.** Issued **7 October 1763** by **King George III**, in the wake of the Treaty of Paris.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"; Avalon Project (Yale Law), Proclamation text

- **The Proclamation Line.** It drew a boundary roughly along the **crest of the Appalachian Mountains** (tracking the Eastern Continental Divide), running from Georgia in the south up through the colonies. **West of the line was closed to colonial settlement.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"

- **The West reserved "for the Indians" (verbatim).** *"We do further declare it to be Our Royal Will and Pleasure… to reserve under our Sovereignty, Protection, and Dominion, for the use of the said Indians, all the Lands and Territories not included within the Limits of Our said Three new Governments…"*
  — Source: Avalon Project (Yale Law), Royal Proclamation of 1763

- **No grants beyond the line (verbatim).** *"…no Governor or Commander in Chief in any of our Colonies… do presume, upon any Pretence whatever, to grant Warrants of Survey, or pass any Patents for Lands beyond the Bounds of their respective Governments…"*
  — Source: Avalon Project, Royal Proclamation of 1763

- **No private purchase of Native land (verbatim).** *"…no private Person do presume to make any purchase from the said Indians of any Lands reserved to the said Indians… but that… the same shall be Purchased only for Us, in our Name, at some public Meeting or Assembly of the said Indians…"* — i.e. only the **Crown**, at a **public council**, could buy Native land.
  — Source: Avalon Project, Royal Proclamation of 1763

- **New royal governments organized.** The Proclamation set up government for the new acquisitions: the **Province of Quebec**, **East Florida** and **West Florida**, and **Grenada** (with the ceded Caribbean islands).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"

## BEAT 6: Why the Proclamation Was Issued

- **To calm and stabilize Native nations — Pontiac's War was raging.** The Proclamation was issued partly to reassure Native nations, **during and after Pontiac's War (1763–66)**, that the Crown would protect their lands from the flood of settlers. (Dedup: Pontiac's War is owned by `fi-pontiacs-war`; name it as the pressure, don't re-narrate it.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"; Canadian Encyclopedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"

- **To control (and profit from) expansion under the Crown.** It was also a tool of **imperial control**: the Crown — not land companies or individual colonies — would manage the pace, the purchases, and the orderly settlement of the West.
  — Source: history.state.gov Milestones; Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"

- **First formal recognition of Native land title.** The Proclamation is significant as **the first formal acknowledgment in British colonial law of Native American land title** — barring patents on land claimed by a tribe until that title was extinguished by Crown purchase or treaty. (It is recognized in Canada's **Constitution Act, 1982** as a foundation of Aboriginal rights.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"; Canadian Encyclopedia
  **[CONTESTED — meaning]** Historian **Colin Calloway** notes "scholars disagree on whether the proclamation recognized or undermined tribal sovereignty." Some historians warn against reading it as an unblemished charter, since settlers and even colonial officials violated it almost at once. Frame the recognition as real-on-paper but immediately undercut in practice.
  — Source: web summary citing Calloway; Canadian Encyclopedia

## BEAT 7: The Colonial Backlash

- **Furious speculators and settlers.** The line enraged the very colonists — **land speculators and frontier settlers** — who had fought partly to win the western land now placed off-limits. Land companies like the **Ohio Company** had claims west of the line; veterans expected land bounties there.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"; history.state.gov Milestones

- **George Washington — speculator and skeptic.** Washington had been **promised land bounties for his French and Indian War service** (the 1754 Dinwiddie proclamation eventually yielded large western grants; figures up to ~20,000 acres are cited in summaries — see contested note) and treated the Proclamation Line as a temporary nuisance to be quietly evaded.
  - **Verbatim, Washington to William Crawford, 17 September 1767 (original spelling):**
    > "I can never look upon that Proclamation in any other light (but this I say between ourselves) than as a temporary expedien[t] to quiet the Minds of the Indians & must fall of course in a few years…"
    > "any Person therefore who neglects the present oppertunity of hunting ou[t] good Lands & in some measure Marking & distinguishing them for their own (in order to keep others from settling them) will never regain it."
    > "I woud recommend it to you to keep this whole matter a profound Secret… and the [Scheme] snugly carried on by you under the pretence of hunting other Game which you may I presume effectually do at the same time you are in pursuit of Land."
  — Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, primary document, "Letter from George Washington to William Crawford (September 17, 1767)"; Founders Online, Washington Papers 02-08-02-0020
  **[CONTESTED — the acreage]** The "20,000 acres for FI War service" figure appears in a web summary; the bounty story is real but the round number should be treated as approximate. **Prefer to write it as "land bounties promised for his war service" rather than a hard acreage** unless the author re-verifies a specific figure. The *quotes above are verbatim and verified.*

- **Ignored almost at once; unenforceable.** Settlers crossed the line almost immediately; the Crown lacked the troops and will to police a mountain-long frontier. The line was repeatedly pushed west by later treaties — **Fort Stanwix (1768)**, **Hard Labour (1768)**, **Lochaber (1770)** — which moved the boundary to open more Native land to purchase.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"

- **A grievance on the road to revolution.** Walling colonists out of land they believed they'd won became **one more colonial grievance** feeding toward the Revolution — a tangible sign that imperial and colonial interests had diverged.
  — Source: history.state.gov Milestones; Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"

## BEAT 8: The Native Dimension — A Continent Signed Away (FRAMING SPINE)

- **No Native nation was a party to the treaty.** As the European powers negotiated at Paris, they **did not consult Native leaders or weigh Native interests.** A continent's worth of homeland east of the Mississippi was transferred between empires — by men an ocean away — with **no Native nation present or consenting.** This is the same silence as the French lead plates buried along the Ohio and the British royal land grants earlier in the war: Native land disposed of on paper by outsiders.
  — Source: web summary, Royal Proclamation entries; Canadian Encyclopedia; (cross-ref `fi-native-alliances`, `fi-ohio-spark` lead-plates context)

- **The nations were not absent from the land — only from the table.** The peoples whose homelands were thus "transferred" included, in the Great Lakes / Ohio Country, the **Odawa (Ottawa), Ojibwe (Chippewa), Potawatomi, Wyandot (Huron), Shawnee, Lenape (Delaware), Mingo (Ohio Seneca), Miami, Kickapoo,** and others — the very coalition that, under the Odawa leader **Pontiac (Obwandiyag)**, rose in **Pontiac's War** in spring 1763 and seized roughly **ten British forts** in the Great Lakes region. The Proclamation was the Crown's hasty answer to that war.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Pontiac's War"; Canadian Encyclopedia (use correct nation spellings; dedup with `fi-pontiacs-war`)
  **[AUTHOR NOTE on names]** Use the nations' own names where natural: **Odawa/Ottawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Lenape/Delaware, Wyandot, Mingo (Seneca).** "Pontiac" is the common name; **Obwandiyag** is the Odawa name. Don't over-stuff the list — name a representative few.

- **A promise drawn on a map by an empire that would not keep it.** The Proclamation Line *was* a real promise to Native nations — and it was broken almost immediately, by settlers, by speculators like Washington who openly called it a "temporary expedient" certain to "fall… in a few years," and by the Crown's own later treaties pushing the line west. **Name the dispossession plainly:** the West was reserved for Native nations in October 1763 and was being taken from them within a decade.
  — Source: Washington–Crawford letter (above); Wikipedia, "Royal Proclamation of 1763"; history.state.gov Milestones
