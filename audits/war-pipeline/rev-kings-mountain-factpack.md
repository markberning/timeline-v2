# FACT PACK — Battle of Kings Mountain, October 7, 1780

*American Revolution (southern campaign). ONLY source for the battle page. THE "FIRST CIVIL WAR" FLAGSHIP: the only British soldier on the field was Major Patrick Ferguson — every other man, on both sides, was an American colonist. A self-mustered frontier army with elected colonels ran down the king's Loyalist militia on a wooded ridge, killed its commander, kept killing after the white flags went up, and hanged nine prisoners a week later. This page does NOT sanitize the post-surrender killing or the hangings — they ARE the thesis. Confidence tags: HIGH / MED / DISPUTED; HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags inline.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **Battle of Kings Mountain, October 7, 1780** — a wooded ridge in (then) the New Acquisition District, today York County, South Carolina, about 1.5-2 miles south of the North Carolina line and ~9 miles south of the modern town of Kings Mountain, NC. Coordinates ~35.140,-81.377 (Kings Mountain National Military Park, Blacksburg, SC). [HIGH]
- **Result: decisive Patriot victory** — Ferguson's command annihilated in about an hour: killed, wounded, or captured to the last man. [HIGH]
- **The thesis fact [HIGH — the page's reason to exist]:** of the roughly 2,000 men on the field, **the only British soldier was Major Patrick Ferguson himself.** His ~100 red-coated "Provincials" were American Loyalists from New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut; his ~900-1,000 militia were Carolina Loyalists; the ~900 men who stormed the hill were American frontier militia. A battle remembered as a famous American victory over "the British" was, almost to a man, Americans killing Americans.
  - **PRECISE WORDING (locked with the civil-war theme page, see Sec. 12):** say "the only British **soldier** on the field" or "the only Briton in arms." Do NOT say "no British units" (the Provincials were on the British establishment) and do NOT say "the only British-**born** man on the field" — that stricter claim is false: Ferguson's own adjutant, Capt. Alexander Chesney, was born in County Antrim, Ireland (emigrated 1772), and both armies were full of Ulster- and Britain-born immigrants. [HIGH on Chesney's Irish birth — his published journal; the distinction matters for the gate]
- **Signature elements:** (1) the self-summoned army — no Congress, no Continental officers, colonels elected/self-appointed, mustered at **Sycamore Shoals** beyond the mountains; (2) **rifle vs. musket** on a wooded slope — treed riflemen against volley-and-bayonet; (3) **Ferguson's death** — shot from his horse, dragged by a stirrup, his body riddled; (4) **the surrender problem** — "Tarleton's quarter!" shouted as men with white flags were shot down, officers physically stopping it; (5) **the nine hangings at Bickerstaff's** a week later. [HIGH on all five as events; evidence levels per section]
- **Why it mattered [HIGH]:** Cornwallis abandoned his first invasion of North Carolina and fell back to Winnsboro, SC; backcountry Loyalists never again rallied in force to a British column — the Loyalist-manpower theory underpinning Britain's whole southern strategy died on the hill. Clinton later called it the first link in the chain that lost America (Sec. 8).

## 2. BACKGROUND
### After Camden
- **Strategic situation [HIGH]:** Charleston fell May 12, 1780 (~5,000 Americans surrendered — the war's largest capitulation); Tarleton's Legion cut down Buford's Virginians at the **Waxhaws** (May 29), birthing the cry "Tarleton's quarter" for no-quarter killing; Gates's southern army was destroyed at **Camden** (August 16). Organized Continental resistance in the South was effectively gone. In September Cornwallis began his move north into North Carolina, occupying **Charlotte** (Sept 26).
- **Ferguson's role [HIGH]:** Maj. Patrick Ferguson, appointed **Inspector of Militia** for South Carolina (May 22, 1780), had spent the summer raising and drilling Loyalist militia in the backcountry. As Cornwallis advanced, Ferguson's corps (~1,000-1,100: his "American Volunteers" provincial detachment + Carolina Loyalist militia) operated as the **left/western flank guard** of the invasion, sweeping through the foothills toward Gilbert Town (near modern Rutherfordton, NC).
- **The Loyalist-manpower theory [HIGH]:** Britain's southern strategy assumed the backcountry was full of king's friends who would hold conquered ground as militia while the regulars moved on. Ferguson was that theory's working instrument — which is what made his annihilation strategy-killing rather than just a lost detachment.

### The threat — HANDLE-WITH-CARE, the documentation chain is weaker than the legend
- **The canonical story [told everywhere, including our own spine]:** Ferguson paroled a captured Patriot, **Samuel Phillips** (a distant kinsman of Isaac Shelby), to carry a verbal message over the mountains: *"if they did not desist from their opposition to the British arms, and take protection under his standard, he would march his army over the mountains, hang their leaders, and lay their country waste with fire and sword."*
- **The actual chain [HIGH on the chain itself]:** the threat's first appearance in the record is **Isaac Shelby's own pamphlet of 1823** — 43 years after the fact, written by the man it glorifies. Every later version (Draper 1881 → 20th-c. histories → NPS) reproduces Shelby's language. A 2024 Journal of the American Revolution study (Caldwell, "Isaac Shelby, Patrick Ferguson, and Fire & Sword") found **no contemporary documentation**: no 1780 newspaper, none of 300+ surviving participant accounts, and none of Ferguson's own proclamations (his Sept 9, 1780 proclamation offers protection and amnesty, not destruction) carries the threat. Caldwell's verdict: "more folklore than truth" — possibly Shelby's memory fusing Ferguson's real proclamations with the real brutality of Tarleton, Wemyss, and Brown elsewhere that summer. [HIGH that this is the scholarly state of play]
- **SAFE FRAMING for the page (the threat is load-bearing — tell it at evidence level):** the story of the threat comes from Shelby, told decades later; it is how the over-mountain men themselves explained their march, and the march is fact. Write: "Shelby said afterward that Ferguson sent a paroled prisoner over the mountains with a threat: desist, or he would come over, hang the leaders, and lay the country waste with fire and sword. Whether Ferguson ever said it, the men of the Watauga acted as if he had." Do NOT print the threat as a verbatim documented Ferguson utterance. **CROSS-PAGE NOTE:** the shipped spine (`rev-world-war` §9) states "Ferguson had threatened... fire and sword" as flat fact — this page should carry the evidence level, and the spine sentence is arguably defensible as compressed tradition, but flag to the coordinator (Sec. 12).
- **What IS documented from Ferguson's pen — the Denard's Ford proclamation, Oct 1, 1780 [HIGH, text survives]:** to the NC Loyalists, days before the battle: *"Unless you wish to be eat up by an inundation of barbarians... I say, if you wish to be pinioned, robbed, and murdered, and see your wives and daughters, in four days, abused by the dregs of mankind — in short, if you wish or deserve to live, and bear the name of men, grasp your arms in a moment and run to camp. The Back Water men have crossed the mountains; McDowell, Hampton, Shelby and Cleveland are at their head, so that you know what you have to depend upon. If you choose to be degraded forever and ever by a set of mongrels, say so at once, and let your women turn their backs upon you, and look out for real men to protect them."* This is the verifiable voice — "barbarians," "dregs of mankind," "mongrels" — and it is better material than the legend. [HIGH; widely reproduced from the original]

### The over-mountain settlements — the F&I/causes tie
- **Who they were [HIGH]:** the Watauga, Nolichucky, and Holston settlements (today's northeast Tennessee and southwest Virginia) sat **west of the Proclamation Line of 1763** — the boundary Britain drew after the French and Indian War forbidding settlement beyond the Appalachian crest. The settlers were there in defiance of imperial policy from the start: the **Watauga Association** (1772) wrote its own compact of self-government and leased (then bought) its land directly from the Cherokee. **Sycamore Shoals** itself was where the 1775 Transylvania Purchase was negotiated. These were communities that had already, for a decade, governed and defended themselves without Britain — the Revolution's causes arc made flesh. [HIGH; tie to the F&I/Proclamation material in the causes chapters]
- **Refuse the frontier-superman framing [standing instruction]:** the 19th-century telling (Draper's filial piety, Theodore Roosevelt's *Winning of the West*) makes every man a buckskin Boone, nature's own soldier. Reality check the page should carry lightly: they were farmers and stockmen, many of them recent arrivals; their settlements were squatter colonies on Cherokee land, and the same army's leaders (Sevier) spent that December burning Cherokee towns (Boyd's Creek campaign) — the "freedom" they marched for included the freedom to take the land they stood on. One honest beat, not a lecture. [HIGH on Boyd's Creek Dec 1780 and the encroachment context]
- **The muster, Sycamore Shoals, September 25, 1780 [HIGH]:** ~1,000-1,100 men: **William Campbell** with ~400 Virginians (Washington County, VA), **Isaac Shelby** with ~240 (Sullivan County), **John Sevier** with ~240 (Washington County, the Watauga/Nolichucky men), plus ~160 Burke County, NC refugees under **Charles McDowell** who had fled over the mountains after earlier fighting. Sevier and Shelby financed powder and supplies on personal credit from entry-taker **John Adair**, who handed over the public land-office money (~$12,000-13,000) — Adair's recorded reply is tradition-level but beloved (Sec. 8). A guard remained against the Cherokee. The Rev. **Samuel Doak** is said to have preached them off with "the Sword of the Lord and of Gideon" — see APOCRYPHA (the sermon's text is a much-later reconstruction). [muster facts HIGH; Doak text DISPUTED]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
### Strengths (dossier stats)
- **Patriot:** ~900-940 picked, mounted riflemen made the final attack (selected on the night of Oct 5-6 at Green River/Cowpens from a larger pool; the whole gathered force had been ~1,400-1,800+ with footmen). [HIGH on ~900 attackers; pool figures vary]
- **Loyalist:** ~1,000-1,125 total: Ferguson + ~70-120 Provincial regulars ("American Volunteers" — detachments of the King's American Regiment, Queen's Rangers, New Jersey Volunteers origin, i.e., northern Loyalists in red coats) + ~900-1,000 Carolina Loyalist militia. [HIGH on composition; totals vary across good sources — say "about eleven hundred"]
- **Duration:** about 65 minutes (participant timing; "about an hour" is safe). Started roughly 3:00 p.m. [HIGH]

### The Patriot colonels — an army with no general
- **Command arrangement [HIGH]:** no Continental commission on the field. The colonels governed by **council**; on Oct 2, at Shelby's suggestion (to dodge a North Carolina seniority squabble), they made **William Campbell** — the senior *Virginia* colonel, thus neutral in NC disputes — "officer of the day" to execute the council's plans, while Charles McDowell rode off to ask Gates for a general officer. An elected, improvised command for a self-mustered army: use it, it's the civil-war-thesis texture.
- **William Campbell (1745-1781) [HIGH]:** Washington County, Virginia militia colonel, a 6'6" Scots-Irish Presbyterian famous for hanging Tories on the Holston; brother-in-law of Patrick Henry. Nominal commander at Kings Mountain; his regiment took the brunt (bore the highest Patriot casualties). Died of illness in 1781 weeks before Yorktown. **No reliably verified life portrait found this session — leave img blank rather than use a later imagining.**
- **Isaac Shelby (1750-1826) [HIGH]:** Sullivan County (now TN) colonel, Maryland-born; the muster's prime mover with Sevier. His 1823 pamphlet is both a key source and a self-interested one (the fire-and-sword chain, a postwar feud with Campbell's reputation). Twice governor of Kentucky; Kentucky counties and towns across the South bear his name.
- **John Sevier (1745-1815) [HIGH]:** Washington County (Watauga/Nolichucky) colonel, Virginia-born land speculator and Indian fighter, "Nolichucky Jack"; later six-term first governor of Tennessee. Led the Watauga men up the southwest slope. (His December 1780 Cherokee campaign is the honest counterweight beat.)
- **Benjamin Cleveland (1738-1806) [HIGH]:** Wilkes County, NC colonel, ~300 lbs, already notorious for summary hangings of Tories; joined with ~350 men (with Maj. Joseph Winston) at Quaker Meadows. At Bickerstaff's he was a driving force of the trials.
- **Joseph McDowell (1756-1801) [MED-HIGH]:** led the Burke County men in the battle in place of his elder brother Charles (who had gone to find a general — the family command is sometimes confused; say "the McDowells' Burke County men" if unsure).
- **James Williams (1740-1780) [HIGH]:** South Carolina militia colonel (Ninety Six district), commanding a separate SC contingent (with Lacey and Hill's men, who joined Oct 6). **Mortally wounded near the battle's end — shot as the white flags were going up — died October 8.** The senior Patriot fatality.
- **Also on the field [HIGH]:** Col. **Edward Lacey** and Col. **William Hill** (SC); Lt. Col. **Frederick Hambright** (wounded) and Maj. **William Chronicle** (killed in the first assault) leading the Lincoln County "South Fork boys"; Maj. **Joseph Winston** (Surry County, NC). The **1815 Chronicle marker** at the battlefield is among the oldest battlefield monuments in the United States [MED-HIGH].

### The Loyalist side
- **Patrick Ferguson (1744-1780) [HIGH]:** born June 4, 1744, at Pitfour, Aberdeenshire — son of a Scottish judge (Lord Pitfour), nephew of Lord Elibank, raised among Edinburgh Enlightenment company. Cornet in the Scots Greys at 15; captain in the 70th Foot in the West Indies; **inventor of the Ferguson rifle** (1776) — a breech-loading flintlock a trained man could fire 4-6 times a minute lying down, demonstrated before the king's officers at Woolwich in pouring rain. Led an experimental rifle corps at **Brandywine** (Sept 11, 1777), where a musket ball shattered his **right elbow**, permanently crippling the arm — he retrained himself to fence and write left-handed; the rifle corps was broken up while he convalesced. (The famous "the man who didn't shoot Washington" episode is real as FERGUSON'S OWN account — see APOCRYPHA for the identity problem.) Raids at Chestnut Neck/Little Egg Harbor 1778; major, 71st Foot, Oct 1779; siege of Charleston 1780; then Inspector of Militia. At 36, the youngest-serving British major... [strike superlatives — just "36 years old"]. Killed at Kings Mountain — the only British soldier on the field, commanding a thousand Americans. Owned a slave plantation ("Castara") in Tobago bought in 1770 [HIGH — use only if the page wants the honest-texture beat; do not force].
- **Capt. Abraham de Peyster (1753-1798) [HIGH]:** New York Loyalist (King's American Regiment), Ferguson's second-in-command; inherited the command when Ferguson fell and raised the white flag. From one of Manhattan's oldest Dutch families — a useful one-line emblem that the "British" officer corps here was American too. Survived; exiled to New Brunswick.
- **Capt. Alexander Chesney (1755-1843) [HIGH]:** Ferguson's adjutant, Irish-born South Carolina Loyalist farmer whose journal is a prime eyewitness source; captured at the battle. (Also the man whose existence breaks the "only British-born" overclaim.)

## 4. THE PURSUIT (Sept 25 - Oct 7)
- **Sept 25 [HIGH]:** muster at Sycamore Shoals on the Watauga (~1,000-1,100).
- **Sept 26-27 [HIGH]:** the column climbs the Blue Ridge crest through **Yellow Mountain Gap** (~4,600 ft, Roan Highlands — among the highest ground any Revolutionary army crossed), snow "shoe-mouth deep" on top in late September. Two men with Loyalist sympathies (named in tradition as **James Crawford and Samuel Chambers**) deserted and carried warning to Ferguson — so the hunt was no secret. [HIGH on the desertion/warning; the names MED, via Draper-line tradition]
- **Sept 30 [HIGH]:** junction at **Quaker Meadows** (the McDowells' plantation, near modern Morganton, NC) with Cleveland and Winston's ~350 Wilkes/Surry men; force now ~1,400.
- **Oct 1-4 [HIGH]:** Ferguson, warned, pulled out of **Gilbert Town** (issuing the Denard's Ford "dregs of mankind" proclamation Oct 1) and angled southeast, asking Cornwallis for reinforcement; the Patriots reached the abandoned Gilbert Town camp Oct 4. Ferguson's request to Cornwallis and Tarleton's non-arrival (Tarleton ill with fever) meant no help came. [HIGH on the pullback/request; "Cornwallis never answered in time" — keep simple]
- **Oct 6, Cowpens [HIGH]:** the pursuers reached the **Cowpens** (the same pasture ground of the January 1781 battle — nice forward echo, one clause max), were joined by ~400 South Carolinians under Lacey, Hill, and Williams plus Hambright/Chronicle's South Fork men, learned from spies that Ferguson was ~30 miles east making for Charlotte, and **picked ~900 of the best-mounted, best-armed men** to ride through the night. It rained all night; the men wrapped rifle locks in blankets and hunting shirts. They crossed the rain-swollen Broad River at **Cherokee Ford** near dawn and rode on through drizzle.
- **Ferguson stands [HIGH on the dispatch; motive MED]:** rather than run the last 35 miles to Cornwallis, Ferguson camped on Kings Mountain's ridge Oct 6 and wrote Cornwallis: *"I arrived today at Kings Mountain & have taken a post where I do not think I can be forced by a stronger enemy than that against us."* Why he stopped is argued — confidence in the position, honor, waiting for reinforcements; don't psychologize beyond "he chose to stand."
- **The "God Almighty" boast:** the famous line that Ferguson "defied God Almighty and all the rebels out of hell to overcome him" is Draper-era tradition from Patriot recollection, not a Ferguson document — see APOCRYPHA. The Oct 6 dispatch above is the verifiable version of the same hubris; PREFER IT.

## 5. THE BATTLE (~3:00-4:05 p.m., October 7)
- **The ground [HIGH]:** a stony, wooded ridge ~600 yards long, rising ~60 feet above the surrounding plateau — slopes timbered with hardwoods and boulders, the narrow crest more open where the camp and wagons stood. The trees and rocks that Ferguson trusted to mark his perimeter were exactly the cover rifle-armed attackers needed. James Collins (16-year-old Patriot, memoir 1859): the mountain "was covered with flame on every side" — see Quotes for his usable lines.
- **The encirclement [HIGH]:** the Patriots dismounted ~a mile off, tied horses, and moved in **eight or so detachments to ring the ridge's base** — Campbell's Virginians and Shelby's men taking the "heel," Sevier, McDowell, Winston, Hambright/Chronicle, Cleveland, Lacey, and Williams's parties closing the sides and toe. The orders as remembered: every man "his own officer" once engaged.
- **Rifle vs. musket [HIGH as dynamics]:** the Loyalists fought in close order with smoothbore muskets and bayonets (the militia's "bayonets" largely hunting knives whittled to fit the muzzle [MED-HIGH — commonly attested improvisation]); the attackers fought tree to tree with long rifles, accurate to 2-3 times musket range, and carried no bayonets at all. Three times bayonet charges swept attackers down the slopes — and each time the riflemen flowed back up behind the trees. Firing DOWNHILL, the Loyalists chronically overshot; the rifle fire coming up was murderous. Volley war lost to aimed war on that ground. [the overshooting point is standard in the literature]
- **Ferguson on the ridge [HIGH on whistle; MED on shirt]:** Ferguson commanded from horseback, his crippled right arm useless, directing charges with a **silver whistle** (the detail is in participant tradition via Draper and universally retold — tag "men remembered"); accounts have him in a **checked hunting shirt or duster over his uniform**, which marked him out [MED — same tradition layer]. He cut down at least one white flag raised by his own men [MED — attested in accounts; handle as "is said to have"].
- **Ferguson's death [HIGH on the event; details tradition-graded]:** near the end, Ferguson with a few officers tried to cut through the closing ring. Rifle fire blew him from the saddle — **his body was found with multiple wounds (commonly "six or seven balls")** — and one foot hung in a stirrup so his horse dragged him into the Patriot line [HIGH that he was shot from his horse and dragged; the wound-count varies]. Several riflemen claimed the shot ever after; tradition names Sevier's man Robert Young and his rifle "Sweet Lips" — APOCRYPHA, do not adjudicate. The body was stripped of its possessions; Patriot accounts themselves record desecration before he was buried [MED — keep to "stripped where he lay" unless the framing gate wants the uglier tradition explicitly hedged].
- **The surrender problem — DO NOT SANITIZE [HIGH]:** command fell to de Peyster, who raised a white flag; it was shot down — men kept firing into the huddled mass on the crest. Survivors and participants recorded the cries: **"Tarleton's quarter!" and "Give them Buford's play!"** — the Waxhaws come home. Some attackers had not understood what a white flag meant; many others understood perfectly. Campbell ran along the line shouting (as participants remembered it) **"For God's sake, don't shoot! It is murder to kill them now!"** and ordering prisoners to sit; Sevier and Shelby likewise drove their men off. De Peyster, from horseback, protested to Campbell that it was "damned unfair" [MED — participant tradition via Draper]. The killing took minutes to stop, and Loyalist wounded were bayoneted and shot where they lay in the interim. Also: Col. Williams was mortally hit in these final minutes, which fed a fresh surge of revenge firing [MED on the causal link; HIGH on his mortal wounding at battle's end].
- **Kin against kin [MED — tradition via Draper/Wilkinson, flag as such]:** the **Goforth brothers** of the South Carolina backcountry fought on opposite sides; tradition has brothers Preston (Patriot) and John (Loyalist) both dying on the hill, by some tellings at each other's hands; Gen. James Wilkinson's memoirs relate two expert-rifleman brothers seen "to present at each other, to fire and fall at the same instant." Use ONE such beat, attributed ("neighborhood tradition held..."), never as established fact. The captured were guarded by men who knew their names and farms — that much is structural fact. [HIGH on the neighbors-knew-neighbors reality; the named brother stories are tradition]
- **Overnight [HIGH]:** the victors and ~700 prisoners camped among the dead and the crying wounded; Loyalist wounded largely lay untreated where they fell (one Loyalist surgeon, Uzal Johnson, worked the field). Collins's memoir of the next morning carries the scene (Quotes).

## 6. CASUALTIES (dossier ranges)
- **Loyalist [HIGH on scale; exact sets vary]:** the commonly used set (Draper-derived, used by ABT/NPS and already by our spine): **~157 killed, ~163 too badly wounded to move (left on the field), ~698 captured** — effectively 100% of the force. Wikipedia and some modern counts give ~668 captured; JAR's prisoner study counts 716 marched off (648 militia + 68 provincials). Use "about 1,100 killed, wounded, or taken — the entire force" and the common set as "commonly given."
- **Patriot [HIGH]:** **~28-29 killed, ~62 wounded.** Campbell's Virginians suffered most; Col. James Williams mortally wounded (d. Oct 8), Maj. William Chronicle killed, Lt. Col. Hambright wounded.
- The wounded Loyalists left on the field: many died there; local Loyalist families came for the bodies (Collins: the wives and children "came in, in great numbers... a melancholy sight indeed!"). Wolves notoriously haunted the half-buried field for years — Collins again [MED-HIGH; his memoir's famous grim coda — verify exact wording at draft time if quoted directly].

## 7. AFTERMATH
### The retreat with the prisoners [HIGH, JAR "Fruits of Victory" is the spine]
- The victors left the morning of Oct 8, fearing Tarleton (and with militia enlistments evaporating), driving ~700 prisoners north on starvation rations — prisoners got their first food (a raw corn ear apiece) after ~two days; guards by Loyalist account sold corn and even water to prisoners for Continental paper. [HIGH via Allaire/Johnson diaries + Moravian records]
### Bickerstaff's — the hangings, October 14 [HIGH; this is the civil-war ugliness, tell it precisely]
- At **Bickerstaff's (Biggerstaff's) Old Fields**, Rutherford County, NC (near Gilbert Town), Patriot officers convened a court under color of a **North Carolina statute allowing two magistrates to summon a jury and try criminal cases** — several of the militia officers were sitting magistrates. The charges were civil-war charges, not battlefield ones: "breaking open houses, killing the men, turning the women and children out of doors, and burning the houses" — plus parole-breaking and desertion. [HIGH on the court and charge language via JAR/Draper; the statute detail MED-HIGH]
- **30-36 prisoners were tried and condemned in some number (commonly: ~36 tried, 12 condemned); NINE were hanged** from a great oak by torchlight, **three at a time**, the night of Oct 14: Col. **Ambrose Mills**, Capt. **James Chitwood**, Capt. **Walter Gilkey**, Capt. Grimes, Lt. Lafferty, John McFall, John Bibby, Augustine Hobbs, Robert Wilson [names MED-HIGH via JAR]. After the ninth, the executions stopped — by the usual account **Shelby intervened**; competing explanations (a mother's plea to the officers, vengeance satisfied to match Loyalist hangings of Patriots at Camden/Ninety Six/Augusta, word that Tarleton was near) are unresolved — don't pick one as fact. [HIGH on 9 hanged/Oct 14/the halt; motive for the halt DISPUTED]
- **The justification offered:** retaliation for Patriots hanged by the British at Camden, Ninety Six, and Augusta — the cycle, stated plainly, is the page's civil-war engine: Camden/Ninety Six/Augusta → Kings Mountain's "Tarleton's quarter" → the Bickerstaff nine → (forward echo only) Pyle's Massacre and the Bloody Scout. [HIGH as the actors' own framing]
- **The Baldwin escape [MED — single-thread, charming]:** a condemned man named Baldwin escaped when his younger brother, embracing him in farewell, cut his bindings with a hidden knife. "The story goes" framing.
- **Allaire's verdict [MED-HIGH]:** Loyalist Lt. **Anthony Allaire's** diary (printed with Draper) records the condemned dying defiant — Mills, Wilson, and Chitwood "died like Romans" — and seethes at rebel "mock jury" justice. Verify exact wording against the printed diary at draft time if quoted.
### The death march [HIGH via JAR]
- Of ~700 marched off Oct 8: ~100 escaped in one rainy night's march alone (Oct 15); ~300 remained at the Moravian town of **Bethabara** by Oct 23 (penned "like cattle in a small fenced off space... nearly starved" — Moravian diary); **only ~130 reached the Continental camp at Hillsborough** (Nov 23); ~60 remained by January 1781. Most escapees simply went home — or re-enlisted under the king. The victorious army itself had largely dissolved homeward within two weeks: militia war on both sides.
### Strategic consequences [HIGH]
- **Cornwallis abandoned the invasion:** he evacuated Charlotte (~Oct 14) and fell back to **Winnsboro, SC**, for the winter; the first British invasion of North Carolina was over. The backcountry Loyalists, having watched the king's militia annihilated and its survivors hanged, **never again turned out in force** — Loyalist recruitment in the Carolinas never recovered. The southern strategy now had to be won by regulars alone, which is the road to Cowpens, Guilford Courthouse, and Yorktown.
- Greene took the southern command in December (Charlotte, Dec 2-3); Morgan rode with him; Cowpens followed in January — forward pointers only, one clause each.

## 9. APOCRYPHA BLACKLIST / HANDLE-WITH-CARE LEDGER
1. **The "fire and sword" threat** — first recorded by Shelby himself, 1823; zero contemporary corroboration (JAR 2024). Tell it AS Shelby's story (it is genuinely how the march was remembered and justified); never as a documented Ferguson order. The Denard's Ford proclamation is the documented Ferguson voice — prefer it.
2. **"God Almighty and all the rebels out of hell"** — Draper-era tradition from Patriot recollection; no Ferguson document. If used: "the boast his enemies remembered." The Oct 6 dispatch is the verifiable equivalent.
3. **The Doak sermon ("Sword of the Lord and of Gideon")** — that Doak was the settlements' preacher and that a departure service is plausible tradition; the circulating sermon TEXT is a 19th/20th-century reconstruction with no contemporary source. At most one hedged clause; do not quote the text.
4. **Virginia Sal and Virginia Paul** — the "two mistresses"/camp women: pure tradition, first gathered in letters ~a century later (Draper said the tale had circulated "for fifty years"). Red-haired Virginia Sal killed in the battle and buried with (or near) Ferguson; Virginia Paul surviving and variously embroidered (pointing Ferguson out, riding off to Cornwallis's army). The second grave at the cairn is real-ish folklore attached to a real monument. If used at all: one sentence, explicitly as legend. [DISPUTED throughout]
5. **Who shot Ferguson / "Sweet Lips"** — many claimants; the Robert Young rifle-named-Sweet-Lips story is Tennessee tradition. Don't adjudicate; "no one knows whose ball did it" is the honest line.
6. **The white/checked shirt and the silver whistle** — participant tradition via Draper, retold everywhere including NPS. Usable with "men remembered" framing [MED]; not battlefield-document fact.
7. **Ferguson's corpse desecrated / buried in a raw oxhide** — stripped: well attested; the uglier desecration details and the oxhide are tradition-layer [MED]. The page may say "stripped, and buried where he fell, wrapped — the story says — in a raw beef hide."
8. **"The man who didn't shoot Washington" (Brandywine)** — Ferguson's OWN account of declining to shoot "an unoffending individual, who was acquitting himself very coolly of his duty" is genuine (his letter); that the officer WAS Washington is unprovable and disputed (Pulaski is a candidate; the hussar-escorted officer was identified to Ferguson afterward by hearsay). Frame: Ferguson believed, or came to believe, he had spared Washington. [HIGH on Ferguson's letter existing; identity DISPUTED]
9. **"Every man a Boone" frontier-superman framing** — Draper/Roosevelt romance. Refuse: farmers and herdsmen, a self-made army, brave and brutal, squatting on Cherokee land. No noble-savage-of-the-frontier prose.
10. **Casualty/strength precision** — every set (1,125 vs ~1,000; 698 vs 668 vs 716 captured) is a choice among sources; use "about" and the commonly-given set, never false precision.
11. **"Turning point of the Revolution" superlative** — Jefferson/Clinton/Roosevelt verdicts are quotable AS verdicts; the page should claim concretely what is documented (Cornwallis's withdrawal, the death of Loyalist recruitment) and let the quotes carry the superlative.
12. **The 1930 sesquicentennial** — President Hoover spoke at the battlefield Oct 7, 1930 to a vast crowd (estimates ~70,000+) [MED on crowd figure]; the **Ferguson grave marker** was placed around the same era (its inscription is a courtesy of American citizens to a brave fallen enemy) and the **cairn** grew by the tradition of visitors adding stones [MED on the stone-adding tradition; HIGH that the marked grave + cairn exist at the park today]. Verify the marker's exact inscription/date if the page uses it beyond a closing image caption.

## 12. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)
- **Date:** October 7, 1780 · **Where:** Kings Mountain, South Carolina (York County, just south of the NC line)
- **Forces:** ~900 Patriot frontier militia (picked, mounted riflemen) vs ~1,100 Loyalists under Ferguson (~100 Provincial regulars + ~1,000 militia) — every man on both sides American except Ferguson
- **Duration:** about 65 minutes
- **Casualties:** Loyalist ~157 killed, ~163 badly wounded, ~700 captured (the whole force); Patriot ~28 killed, ~62 wounded
- **Winner line material:** Patriot victory, total — the king's Loyalist militia in the Carolinas annihilated by an army no government sent, on a field where the only British soldier present was the dead commander. Cornwallis turned back; backcountry Loyalism never recovered; Clinton called it the first link in the chain that ended at Yorktown.
