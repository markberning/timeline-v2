# FACT PACK — "The War at Sea" (1775–1783)

*American Revolution vertical, war-years Off-the-Battlefield theme chapter (route slug `war-at-sea`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against live-web sources (NHHC/history.navy.mil, NPS, founders.archives.gov, Journal of the American Revolution, American Battlefield Trust, museum scholarship); confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges and "flag" guidance for contested specifics. SCOPE NOTE: the French ALLIANCE diplomacy (Franklin in Paris, the treaty, the money) belongs to the sibling `french-alliance` chapter — this chapter takes the French FLEET as an instrument, not the alliance story. The peace belongs to `treaty-of-paris`; Yorktown's land siege belongs to its own chapter. This chapter OWNS the Battle of the Chesapeake.*

**Orientation.** In 1775 Britain owned the sea. The Royal Navy was the largest and most experienced fleet on earth — roughly 270 ships including over a hundred ships of the line — and the thirteen colonies had exactly none. Sea power meant Britain could land an army anywhere on a 1,000-mile coastline, supply it indefinitely, and strangle American trade with blockade. The rebels could not win at sea, and the war's central naval fact is that they didn't have to: they only had to make the ocean expensive for Britain and stay alive until someone with a real fleet joined in. The American sea war was therefore three different stories at once. There was a tiny official navy — Washington's converted fishing schooners in fall 1775, then the Continental Navy (founded October 13, 1775), whose thirteen-frigate program ended almost entirely in capture and scuttling and whose honest record is a nuisance and a flag, not a fleet. There was the REAL American sea war: privateering — legalized private warfare under letters of marque, thousands of commissions, on the order of 1,500–2,300+ British vessels taken, insurance rates climbing in London — a business that stung Britain commercially while draining the navy's own manpower. And there was one captain, John Paul Jones, who took the war to Britain's coast and turned a sinking ship off Flamborough Head into a legend. Against those run the dark ledger (the prison ships of Wallabout Bay, where more Americans died than in all the war's battles) and the decisive instrument: the French navy, whose victory at the Chesapeake on September 5, 1781 — a battle with no American ships present — sealed Yorktown and effectively the war.

## 1. THE ROYAL NAVY'S OCEAN (the starting fact)

- **The Royal Navy in 1775 was the world's largest and most experienced navy.** It had beaten both the French and Spanish fleets as recently as the Seven Years' War (1756–1763). (HIGH — American Battlefield Trust, NHHC)
- **Size at the war's start:** commonly given as **~270 vessels of all sizes** — one standard breakdown: **131 ships of the line + 139 smaller craft** in 1775. Use "about 270" or "more than 250." (HIGH on the ~270 order; MED on the exact 131/139 split — sources round differently)
- **Growth:** by **1783** the fleet had swelled to roughly **468 ships** (the war went global after 1778 and Britain built furiously); of these only about **100 — mainly frigates and lighter vessels — were committed in American waters**, because the heavy ships were needed against France and Spain in Europe and the West Indies. (MED — Encyclopedia.com/standard naval histories; use "roughly" phrasing)
- **The colonies' navy in 1775: zero.** No warships, no naval administration, no dockyards capable of building ships of the line. (HIGH)
- **Ship-of-the-line / ship rating in plain language:** the battleships of the age were "ships of the line" — vessels big enough (64–100+ guns, two or three gun decks) to stand in the line of battle. Third-rates of ~74 guns were the workhorse. Frigates (~24–44 guns, one gun deck) were the cruisers — scouts, raiders, convoy escorts. The Continental Navy never owned a single ship of the line that got to sea under its flag. (HIGH — note: the 74-gun *America* was launched Nov 1782 but given to France before ever serving the US; mention only if useful)
- **What sea power MEANT (the mechanism — explain, don't just assert):** (HIGH)
  - **Mobility:** Britain could move armies anywhere on the coast faster than Washington could march to meet them — it evacuated Boston (Mar 1776) and appeared at New York (summer 1776), took Philadelphia by sea via the Chesapeake (1777), shifted the war south to Savannah (1778) and Charleston (1780) — all by ship.
  - **Supply:** the British army in America was fed, armed, and paid across 3,000 miles of ocean; sea control made an otherwise impossible logistics problem manageable.
  - **Blockade:** the navy could close American ports, choke trade, and strangle the rebel economy — though the coastline's sheer length (roughly 1,000 miles of usable coast, countless inlets) meant the blockade leaked, badly, especially once the fleet was stretched thin by the French war after 1778.
- **The strategic equation for the rebels (the chapter's spine):** the Americans could never contest command of the sea, and never needed to. Their realistic naval goals: raid British commerce (make the war expensive), run supplies in (gunpowder, arms — most of the Continental Army's early gunpowder arrived by sea), embarrass Britain where possible, and hold on until a European navy changed the math. That is exactly what happened. (HIGH — framing consistent with NHHC / standard scholarship)

## 2. WASHINGTON'S SCHOONERS, THEN A NAVY ON PAPER (1775–1783)

### Washington's schooners — the "first navy" (fall 1775)
- **The improvisation:** besieging Boston in 1775, Washington had no powder and watched British supply ships sail unmolested into Boston harbor. His answer: arm small New England fishing schooners, man them with soldiers who had been fishermen (heavily from **Marblehead, Massachusetts** — John Glover's regiment), and send them after unarmed/lightly armed supply vessels. (HIGH)
- **Hannah** — the first vessel: a schooner taken into Continental service at Washington's direction, **commissioned/sailed early September 1775** (set sail September 5 from Beverly; commissioning usually dated September 2), commanded by **Captain Nicholson Broughton**; took a prize within days. Often called the first armed vessel in Continental pay/service. (HIGH on the spine; MED on "first navy" title — see HANDLE-WITH-CARE: the title is contested between Hannah partisans, Rhode Island, and Machias)
- **The fleet grew to a handful of schooners** (commonly counted as **6–8 vessels** over 1775–77, e.g. *Lee, Franklin, Warren, Lynch, Harrison, Washington*), operating out of Beverly/Marblehead and Plymouth. (MED on exact count — sources count differently; say "a half-dozen converted schooners")
- **The Lee and the Nancy — the jackpot (November 28, 1775).** The 74-ton schooner **Lee** (chartered Oct 1775, fitted out at Marblehead, commissioned Oct 28 under **Captain John Manley**, crewed by soldiers detached from Washington's regiments) took the **250-ton, unarmed British ordnance brig *Nancy*** near Boston harbor at dusk on **November 28, 1775**. *Nancy* mistook *Lee* for a pilot boat and actually signaled for assistance; Manley sent across a boat of men with weapons concealed. (HIGH — NHHC DANFS "Lee I")
  - **The cargo** — exactly what the army outside Boston could not make for itself: **about 2,000 muskets, ~100,000 flints, ~30,000 round shot, ~30 tons of musket shot, barrels of powder, and a 13-inch brass mortar** — military stores the Americans could hardly have gotten any other way. (HIGH on the items; MED on exact quantities — round them: "two thousand muskets, a hundred thousand flints, a thirteen-inch mortar") The mortar was hauled to the lines at Cambridge in celebration (Israel Putnam reportedly christened it "Congress" — MED, colorful but secondary-sourced; flag or drop).
  - Framing: one lucky capture by a converted fishing boat re-armed an army. That asymmetry — tiny investment, outsized return when it works — previews the whole American sea war. (HIGH as framing)
- **Record:** Washington's schooners took dozens of prizes (commonly cited ~35–55 over 1775–77 depending on what's counted) before being absorbed/wound down. Keep loose: "dozens of prizes." (MED — counts vary by source and date range)

### The Continental Navy — born October 13, 1775
- **Founding:** the Continental Congress voted on **October 13, 1775** to fit out **two armed vessels** to cruise against British supply ships (these became *Andrew Doria* and *Cabot* in most accounts of the first purchases). October 13 is the US Navy's official birthday. (HIGH)
- The decision was contested — many in Congress thought challenging the Royal Navy insane. Samuel Chase of Maryland called it "the maddest idea in the world" (widely quoted; MED on exact wording — verify before quoting verbatim, often given as "It is the maddest idea in the world to think of building an American fleet"). (MED)
- **Naval Committee → administration:** a seven-member Naval Committee (Oct 1775), succeeded by the Marine Committee; the **Continental Marines** were established **November 10, 1775**. (HIGH; Marines = one passing line at most, they belong to Nassau below)
- **The thirteen frigates (authorized December 13, 1775):** Congress ordered **thirteen frigates** — five of 32 guns, five of 28, three of 24 — absurdly, to be built in three months. (HIGH on the program; the three-month timetable is in the resolution — HIGH)
- **The frigates' fate — the program was a disaster.** (HIGH — per Continental Navy records/Wikipedia summary of DANFS data; verify names if used)
  - Only **8 of the 13 ever got to sea**. Four (**Washington, Effingham, Congress, Montgomery**) were **scuttled or burned in 1777, before ever sailing**, to keep them from the advancing British.
  - Of the eight that sailed: **Hancock** captured July 1777 (taken into the Royal Navy as HMS *Iris* — which later, painfully, helped capture the Continental frigate *Trumbull* in 1781); **Delaware** captured Sept 1777; **Randolph** blew up in action with HMS *Yarmouth*, March 1778 (with the loss of over 300 men — among the worst single-ship death tolls of the age; MED on exact toll, commonly 301–311); **Raleigh** run aground and taken 1778; **Warren** burned at Penobscot 1779 (§5); **Providence** and **Boston** captured at the fall of Charleston, May 1780; **Trumbull** taken August 1781.
  - **Bottom line: NONE of the thirteen survived in Continental service to the end of the war.** (HIGH) The frigate **Alliance** — the navy's last ship, sold in 1785 — was a LATER ship (launched 1778), NOT one of the thirteen. Don't conflate. (HIGH)
- **Overall Continental Navy numbers:** roughly **60–65 vessels** served in Continental service across the whole war (new-built, converted, chartered, captured); peak strength ~**31 ships in 1777**; it captured on the order of **~200 British vessels** all war. By war's end almost nothing remained; Congress sold the last ship (*Alliance*) in **1785** and the United States had **no navy at all until 1794**. (MED on the ~200 figure; HIGH on the rest)
- **The honest record:** the Continental Navy never fought a fleet action after Nassau, never broke a blockade, never seriously threatened the Royal Navy. What it actually did: carried diplomats and dispatches (it took Franklin to France), raided commerce, raised the flag in European waters, and produced the war's naval legend (Jones). A nuisance and a flag, not a fleet. (HIGH as assessment — consistent with NHHC framing)

### Esek Hopkins and the Nassau raid (March 1776) — the one fleet operation
- **Esek Hopkins** of Rhode Island, the Continental Navy's first (and only) commander in chief, took the first squadron — **eight small converted merchantmen** (*Alfred* (flagship), *Columbus*, *Andrew Doria*, *Cabot*, *Providence*, *Hornet*, *Wasp*, *Fly*) — to sea from Cape Henlopen, Delaware, **February 17, 1776**. (HIGH)
- Instead of patrolling the Chesapeake/Carolina coast as ordered, Hopkins sailed for the Bahamas to seize British military stores. (HIGH — his orders had an escape clause but Congress later treated it as disobedience; MED on how to characterize, see below)
- **The raid (March 3–4, 1776):** about **230–270 marines and sailors** (commonly: 234 marines + ~50 sailors) under Marine **Captain Samuel Nicholas** landed on New Providence Island — **the Continental Marines' first amphibious operation** — took Fort Montagu without a shot, then Nassau's Fort Nassau the next day, also without resistance. (HIGH)
- **The haul:** **~88 cannon, ~15 mortars**, thousands of round shot and other ordnance — but most of the **gunpowder** (the actual point of the trip) had been spirited away by ship the night before (the governor sent ~150+ barrels out through the unguarded harbor). (HIGH on cannon/mortars order-of-magnitude; MED exact counts; HIGH that the powder mostly escaped)
- **The trip home soured everything:** off Block Island (April 6, 1776) the whole squadron failed to take the lone 20-gun **HMS Glasgow**, which mauled *Cabot* and escaped. One frigate against a fleet, and the fleet lost the exchange. (HIGH)
- **Aftermath:** Hopkins was censured by Congress (Aug 1776), suspended (1777), and **dismissed January 2, 1778**. The Continental Navy never again operated as a fleet. (HIGH)
- Framing: the navy's ONE fleet operation of the entire war was a supply raid on an almost undefended island — a success that still ended in censure and court-martials. Tells the reader the scale honestly. (HIGH as framing)

## 3. PRIVATEERS — THE REAL AMERICAN SEA WAR

### The instrument: a letter of marque (define precisely)
- **A letter of marque (privateering commission)** was a government license that authorized a privately owned, privately armed vessel to attack and seize enemy ships in wartime. With the paper, a captured crew were prisoners of war and the seizure was lawful "prize"; without it, the identical act was piracy, a hanging offense. (HIGH)
- **How the machine worked, step by step:** (HIGH on the mechanics)
  1. Investors (often syndicates of merchants) fitted out and armed a vessel and applied for a commission — from the **Continental Congress** (which formalized privateering **March 23, 1776** and approved commission/bond forms in early April) or from an **individual state**. Owners posted a **monetary bond** for good conduct (commissions specified rules: enemy vessels only, prisoners treated as POWs).
  2. The privateer cruised for enemy merchantmen. Most captures involved little or no fighting — the typical prize was an unarmed or lightly armed trader that struck her colors when overhauled.
  3. A captured ship was sailed into port by a "prize crew" and condemned in a **prize court** (admiralty court), which ruled the capture lawful and ordered ship + cargo auctioned.
  4. Proceeds were split by pre-agreed contract — roughly **half to the owners/investors, half to officers and crew by shares** (exact splits varied by articles; don't assert one formula). A single rich prize could make a sailor more than years of navy pay. (HIGH on the structure; MED on any specific split — keep it "roughly half to the owners, the rest in shares")
- **Terminology nuance (optional, one parenthetical at most):** strictly, a "privateer" cruised to hunt prizes, while a "letter-of-marque ship" was a trader licensed to take prizes if opportunity offered; usage blurred then and now. (MED — fine to omit)

### The scale — honest ranges (numbers vary WILDLY; never assert one figure)
- **Commissions:** the Continental Congress issued about **1,700 letters of marque** (per-voyage basis; documentation incomplete — NPS), and the **states issued hundreds more**; total commissioned vessels are commonly given as **~2,000+** (some vessels held multiple/successive commissions, inflating counts). (MED — use "roughly 1,700 Continental commissions, plus state commissions; perhaps two thousand vessels all told")
- **Prizes taken — THE most contested number. Give a range:**
  - Low/conservative (NPS): privateers "captured or destroyed about **600** British ships."
  - Middle (common in scholarship, e.g. Robert Patton): about **2,300** prizes.
  - High (Lloyd's of London-derived records, cited by USMM and others): nearly **3,000** British vessels (some Lloyd's-based figures: 2,208 or 3,087, with ~900 recaptured/ransomed).
  - **Safe formulation:** "somewhere between several hundred and three thousand British vessels, depending on who was counting — the honest answer is well over a thousand, several times what the Continental Navy took (~200)." (DISPUTED — flag in text with a range; the 600 vs 2,300+ gap is partly "captured or destroyed and CONDEMNED" vs "all reported captures")
- **Manpower:** estimates of Americans who served on privateers run to tens of thousands across the war (a frequently cited figure: **~55,000 seamen** total; at points in 1777–81 there were probably more Americans afloat hunting prizes than serving in Washington's Continental Army). Use "tens of thousands"; the 55,000 is a loose cumulative estimate. (MED — flag as estimate)
- **Scale comparison for the reader:** at peak the Continental Navy had ~31 ships; the privateer fleet numbered in the hundreds at any one time. The privateers WERE the American sea war. (HIGH as framing)

### The economics (why privateering outcompeted the navy)
- **For investors** it was a speculative industry — a war lottery. Merchant syndicates in Salem, Beverly, Newburyport, Boston, Providence, Philadelphia, Baltimore bought shares in cruises; fortunes were made (and lost — see the honest caveat). Robert Morris's line captures the investor math: **"One arrival will pay for two, three or four losses."** (HIGH; quote verified in scholarship on Morris/privateering — MED on exact verbatim, see VERIFIED QUOTES)
- **For sailors** the pitch was unbeatable: cruises of **2–3 months** (vs. a year's navy enlistment), looser discipline, and a share of prize money that could dwarf navy wages. The Continental Navy chronically failed to man its ships because privateers paid better; navy captains in port watched crews evaporate to the privateer wharves. (HIGH)
- **For ports** it became the wartime economy: Salem and the Massachusetts North Shore above all; also Philadelphia, Baltimore, the Connecticut ports, Providence. (HIGH)
- James Warren to John Adams on the new fortunes: **"Fellows who would have cleaned my shoes five years ago have amassed fortunes"** (the social-upheaval beat; MED on exact verbatim — see VERIFIED QUOTES). (MED)

### The strategic effect (what it actually did to Britain)
- **It raised Britain's costs.** Marine insurance premiums in the London market climbed steeply once American cruisers got loose — contract/transport insurance rates that ran near peacetime norms rose to the tens of percent (one documented series: army-contractor shipping rates ~13% in March 1776 rising to ~32% by that summer; rates to the West Indies notoriously high after 1777 when privateers swarmed the Caribbean). **Use the direction and roughness, not a single precise figure.** (MED — figures exist but vary by route/year; flag "insurance rates roughly doubled and worse on some routes")
- **It hurt merchants and made noise in Parliament.** British merchants' petitions complained of losses; goods prices rose; the government had to convoy. Parliament's countermeasure was the **"Pirate Act" of 1777** — captured American privateersmen could be held without trial, denied POW status/exchange (this feeds directly into the prison-ship section, §6). (HIGH on the act and merchant pressure; MED on any specific petition wording)
- **It diverted Royal Navy effort** to convoy and trade protection that would otherwise have tightened the American blockade. (HIGH as standard assessment)
- **What it did NOT do:** privateering never threatened British sea CONTROL — no privateer could fight a frigate, let alone a ship of the line. It was economic harassment, brilliantly scaled, not naval power. (HIGH)

### The honest caveat (state it plainly)
- **Privateering was a business.** Some of it wore patriotism thinly; cruises chased profit, not strategy. (HIGH)
- **It drained the war effort's manpower:** the Continental Navy could not man its frigates in part BECAUSE the privateers paid better, and even Washington's army lost coastal recruits to the wharves. Contemporaries said so loudly. (HIGH)
- **It was dangerous as any battlefield:** thousands of captured privateersmen ended in British prisons and prison ships — most of the Wallabout Bay dead (§6) were privateersmen, not navy or army men. Whole ports were bled: Gloucester, Mass. reportedly lost every one of its two dozen privateers and a huge share of its adult men; Newburyport lost ~1,000 men. (MED on the port-level figures — single-source-ish, use "reportedly"; HIGH that most prison-ship dead were privateersmen)

## 4. JOHN PAUL JONES — THE LEGEND, TOLD HONESTLY

### The documented arc (before Flamborough Head)
- **Who he was:** born **John Paul**, gardener's son, Kirkcudbrightshire, **Scotland, 1747**; went to sea at ~13; rose to merchant captain. He added "Jones" after killing a mutinous sailor at Tobago (1773) — he claimed self-defense — and fleeing to America rather than stand trial there. A Scot fighting Britain, with a shadow over his name. (HIGH on the spine; the Tobago killing's self-defense claim is his own — frame as "he said")
- **Sloop *Providence* (1776):** commissioned a Continental lieutenant Dec 1775 (aboard *Alfred*; he was at Nassau); as captain of the 12-gun sloop **Providence** (1776) he took or destroyed over a dozen prizes on one cruise (commonly given as 16 vessels taken/destroyed incl. fishing fleet damage at Canso). One of the navy's few unambiguous performers. (HIGH on the cruise's success; MED on the exact 16)
- ***Ranger* to Europe (Nov 1777):** the 18-gun sloop-of-war **Ranger**, Portsmouth-built, carried Jones to France; on **February 14, 1778** in Quiberon Bay the French admiral La Motte-Picquet's fleet returned *Ranger*'s salute — widely called the **first foreign salute to the Stars and Stripes** by a foreign power (MED — competing "first salute" claims exist, e.g. St. Eustatius to the *Andrew Doria*'s Continental colors Nov 1776; phrase carefully or omit).
- **The Whitehaven raid (night of April 22–23, 1778):** Jones took two boats (~30 men) into **Whitehaven, England** — the port he'd sailed from as a boy — intending to burn the shipping (200–400 vessels lay in the tidal harbor). The raid mostly fizzled: lanterns ran out of fuel (the men famously stopped at a pub), a crewman slipped away and roused the town, the guns of the batteries were spiked but only **one ship was set alight** and the fire was doused. Material damage trivial; psychological effect real — **the first hostile landing on English soil in over a century**, and panic + insurance jitters along the coast followed. (HIGH on the raid + fizzle; MED on the pub detail (memoir-sourced, colorful — flag) and on the "first since…" superlative — phrase as "the first enemy raid on an English port in living memory / in over a century")
- **St. Mary's Isle, the SAME day (April 23, 1778) — the botched kidnapping, told plainly:** Jones crossed the Solway Firth to seize the **Earl of Selkirk** (Dunbar Douglas, 4th Earl) from his estate near Kirkcudbright — the plan was to trade him for American sailors held by Britain. The earl wasn't home. **The "raid" netted nothing but the family silver:** Jones, to appease his prize-hungry crew, let them take the Selkirk plate (Lady Selkirk, pregnant, handled the intrusion with composure); the officers took the silver and left. It was a kidnapping attempt that failed and ended in petty looting — say so plainly. (HIGH)
  - **The apology and the buyback (verified):** Jones wrote Lady Selkirk a long, florid letter of apology (from Brest, **May 8, 1778**), vowing to buy the plate back from his own crew at his own expense and return it. **He did** — the silver was eventually returned to the Selkirks (years later, post-war; the family tradition that the teapot still held its tea leaves is charming but anecdotal — MED, flag or drop). The episode is peak Jones: piratical act, chivalric letter. (HIGH on letter + eventual return; MED on return-date specifics — keep vague: "years later")
- **HMS *Drake* (April 24, 1778):** off Carrickfergus (Belfast Lough), *Ranger* fought and took the Royal Navy sloop-of-war **Drake** (~20 guns) in about an hour; *Drake*'s captain was mortally wounded. One of the first British warships taken in fair fight by a Continental vessel, and paraded into Brest as proof Americans could do it. (HIGH; "first" claims hedged — *Lexington* and others took smaller RN vessels earlier)

### Bonhomme Richard vs. Serapis — Flamborough Head, September 23, 1779
- **The ship:** France gave Jones a worn-out East Indiaman, **Duc de Duras**, which he converted (~40–42 guns, including six old 18-pounders in the gunroom) and renamed ***Bonhomme Richard*** — "Poor Richard," for Franklin. (HIGH)
- **The squadron:** *Bonhomme Richard* (Jones), the American frigate **Alliance** (under the unstable French captain **Pierre Landais**), French frigate **Pallas**, brig **Vengeance** — a joint squadron under American colors, cruising clockwise around the British Isles taking prizes. (HIGH)
- **The encounter:** late afternoon **September 23, 1779**, off **Flamborough Head, Yorkshire**, the squadron intercepted a **41+ ship Baltic convoy** (naval stores) escorted by **HMS Serapis** (Captain **Richard Pearson**; a new 44-gun two-decker) and the 20-gun **Countess of Scarborough**. The convoy escaped inshore; Pearson placed *Serapis* between the enemy and his charges. (HIGH)
- **The battle (~7 p.m. to ~10:30 p.m., moonlight, crowds reportedly watching from the headland):** (HIGH on the spine)
  - *Serapis* outgunned and outsailed *Richard*. Early in the action **two of Jones's old 18-pounders burst**, killing their crews and wrecking the gunroom battery — after that *Richard* could not win a gunnery duel. (HIGH)
  - Jones's only chance was to grapple. The ships fouled, and Jones **lashed them together** ("the bow of the Richard against the Serapis' stern"-then-alongside sequence; keep simple: he made them one burning raft). For hours they fired into each other muzzle-to-muzzle; *Serapis*'s 18-pounders gutted *Richard*'s lower decks below the waterline while American sailors and marines cleared *Serapis*'s upper deck with musketry and grenades from the tops. (HIGH)
  - **Landais's *Alliance* sailed around the locked pair and fired broadsides that hit BOTH ships** — repeatedly hulling *Bonhomme Richard*. Whether madness, incompetence, or malice was never settled (Landais was later court-martialed out of the navy over separate conduct). State it plainly: the American consort shot into her own flagship. (HIGH on the firing into *Richard*; characterization of motive DISPUTED)
  - **The turning point:** seaman **William Hamilton** crawled out a yard with a bucket of grenades and dropped one through *Serapis*'s open hatch onto loose powder cartridges; the explosion flashed down the gun deck, killing/burning dozens of British gunners. (HIGH that the grenade-detonation happened; MED on Hamilton's name being load-bearing — well attested in standard accounts, "reportedly" is safe)
  - With *Richard* sinking under him — pumps failing, fires near the magazine, prisoners released to man the pumps, his own gunner crying quarter — Jones refused to strike. **Pearson struck his colors around 10:30 p.m.** *Serapis*'s mainmast fell shortly after. (HIGH)
- **Casualties:** appalling for the age — roughly **half of each crew**; *Bonhomme Richard* ~150–170 killed and wounded; *Serapis* over a hundred (49+ dead by the first count, "many more" by later ones). Use "about half the men on each ship." (MED on precise figures — sources vary; the "half of each crew" framing is standard)
- **The victor's ship sank.** Jones's crew fought the flooding for a day and a half; ***Bonhomme Richard* went down on the morning of September 25, 1779.** Jones sailed the captured *Serapis* to the neutral Dutch anchorage at the **Texel** (arrived October 3). He won the battle and lost his ship — the legend rests on exactly that. (HIGH)
- **Pearson, the honest coda:** Pearson had saved his entire convoy — the strategic point of his fight — and was **knighted** for it. Jones's quip on hearing it ("Should I have the good fortune to fall in with him again, I'll make a lord of him") is widely quoted but weakly sourced — anecdotal; badge it or drop it. (HIGH on the knighthood; the quip MED/anecdote)

### "I have not yet begun to fight" — the documentation chain (HANDLE-WITH-CARE, but tellable)
- **The famous sentence is NOT contemporary.** It first appears in print in **1825** — 46 years after the battle, 33 after Jones's death — when *Richard*'s first lieutenant **Richard Dale** supplied his recollection to **John Henry Sherburne's biography** (*Life and Character of the Chevalier John Paul Jones*, New York, 1825). Dale, who WAS on deck beside Jones, recalled that when Pearson hailed to ask if the *Richard* had struck, Jones answered: **"I have not yet begun to fight!"** (HIGH on this provenance)
- **What IS documented from 1779:** Jones's own official report to Franklin (**October 3, 1779**) says only that the English commodore "asked me if I demanded quarters," and that he "answered him in the most determined negative," after which "they renewed the battle with redoubled fury." (HIGH — Founders Online, Franklin Papers vol. 30; quote the report's phrasing, not a reconstructed shout)
- **The rival versions:** Jones's own later account renders it as roughly "I have not yet thought of it, but I am determined to make you strike"; a contemporary newspaper/crewman version circulating within weeks gives the saltier **"I may sink, but I'm damned if I strike"**; Benjamin Rush recorded yet another from Jones's own dinner-table telling. (MED on each variant's exact wording — the multiplicity is the point)
- **How to tell it:** narrate the defiance as fact (he refused quarter — that's in his own hand), give the famous line WITH its badge ("the words every American schoolchild would learn were first written down 46 years later, by his lieutenant; what Jones himself reported was an answer 'in the most determined negative'"). Never print the famous line unbadged. (REQUIRED handling)

### After the battle (one line each)
- Jones never again fought a major action under the American flag; he lobbied in Paris, was feted and medaled (Congress's gold medal, 1787), served briefly and unhappily as a **rear admiral in Catherine the Great's Russian navy** (1788–89, Black Sea, against the Turks; the appointment collapsed in intrigue and an accusation of assaulting a girl — scandal never resolved; DON'T sanitize but one clause suffices), and **died alone in Paris, July 18, 1792, aged 45**. (HIGH)
- Forward line: his lost grave was found in 1905; an American squadron carried him home, and he lies now in a marble crypt under the **U.S. Naval Academy chapel** at Annapolis — the navy that barely existed in his lifetime made him its founding saint. (HIGH; crypt completed 1913 — keep loose: "reinterred at Annapolis, 1906; entombed in the chapel crypt")

## 5. PENOBSCOT, 1779 — THE WORST DAY (told plainly)

- **The setup:** in **June 1779** a British force (~700 men under Brigadier General **Francis McLean**, plus three Royal Navy sloops under Captain **Henry Mowat**) occupied the peninsula of **Majabigwaduce** on Penobscot Bay (today's **Castine, Maine** — then part of Massachusetts) and began building **Fort George**, planting a Loyalist province ("New Ireland") on the Maine coast. (HIGH)
- **The response — a STATE expedition:** **Massachusetts** (not Congress) mounted the largest American naval expedition of the war to evict them: about **19 armed ships and ~24 transports** — a mix of 3 Continental Navy vessels (including the 32-gun Continental frigate **Warren**, the flagship), Massachusetts and New Hampshire state ships, and **chartered privateers** — carrying roughly **1,000+ militiamen**. Commanders: **Commodore Dudley Saltonstall** (naval), **Brigadier General Solomon Lovell** (land), and **Lieutenant Colonel Paul Revere** commanding the artillery train. (HIGH on the structure; MED on exact ship/man counts — sources give 19+24 ≈ 43–44 vessels total and 1,000–1,200 militia; use "more than forty vessels")
- **The indecision:** the fleet arrived **late July 1779**. The marines and militia stormed a bluff and took ground (July 28); the British fort was half-built, its walls chest-high. Then — for **two-plus weeks** — Lovell wouldn't assault without Saltonstall first destroying the British sloops, and Saltonstall wouldn't take his ships in while the fort's guns stood. Councils of war; letters; nothing. (HIGH — the paralysis is the documented heart of the story; Saltonstall's reported retort about not risking his ships in that "damned hole" is anecdotal — MED, badge or drop)
- **The collapse (August 13–14, 1779):** a Royal Navy relief squadron under **Sir George Collier** (seven warships led by the 64-gun *Raisonnable*) sailed in. The American fleet didn't fight; it **fled up the Penobscot River**, where, trapped, the Americans **burned and scuttled their own ships — effectively the entire fleet, more than forty vessels lost** (burned, blown up, scuttled, or captured). Survivors walked home through the Maine woods. Roughly **500** American casualties (killed, wounded, captured/missing) against trivial British loss. (HIGH on total loss of the fleet + the self-destruction; MED on the ~500 figure)
- **The bill:** the expedition bankrupted Massachusetts' war finances (the state later billed Congress; figures in the millions of pounds/dollars vary — keep qualitative: "it nearly broke the state's treasury"). (MED on figures — qualitative only)
- **The verdict on the commanders:** (HIGH)
  - **Saltonstall** was court-martialed and **dismissed from the Continental Navy** — the scapegoat-in-chief, though the failure was collective.
  - **Paul Revere** — yes, that Paul Revere — was accused (by an army captain, Thomas Carnes, among others) of disobedience and unsoldierly conduct during the retreat (the specific charges centered on refusing an order about a boat and leaving for Boston without orders). He was relieved of his militia command, **demanded a full court-martial to clear his name, and was acquitted in February 1782** — the court found the charges unsupported. State both halves plainly: the disgrace was real at the time; so was the acquittal. (HIGH)
- **The superlative (use with attribution):** Penobscot is **routinely called the worst American naval disaster until Pearl Harbor (1941)** — by vessel count it remains arguably the largest single loss of US shipping in any one operation. Phrase as "often called"; it's a comparison historians make, not a measured statistic. (MED — attributed framing)
- Framing: Penobscot is the Continental/state navy story in miniature — brave landings, divided command, no doctrine, and the Royal Navy arriving like weather. It also explains why the navy's reputation never recovered: in the same year as Flamborough Head, America's biggest fleet destroyed itself without a battle. (HIGH as framing)

## 6. THE PRISON SHIPS — THE DARK LEDGER

- **The system:** Britain held captured American seamen (overwhelmingly **privateersmen** — the Pirate Act of 1777 denied them ordinary POW exchange for years) in rotting, decommissioned hulks anchored in **Wallabout Bay**, off the Brooklyn shore of New York's East River. More than a dozen hulks served over the war; the infamous one was **HMS Jersey** — a 1736-built 64-gun ship of the line, hulked (masts and guns stripped) and moored as a prison from **1779/1780** to the war's end. Prisoners called her "Hell" or "the hulk." (HIGH; MED on the exact year she converted from hospital ship to main prison hulk — 1779 vs 1780 varies; "by 1780" is safe)
- **Conditions — one honest beat, no wallowing:** ~**1,000 or more men at a time** packed below decks; dysentery, smallpox, yellow fever, typhus; starvation rations (and quotas skimmed by contractors); water from a foul cask; the dead hauled up each morning after the guards' reported nightly cry — **"Rebels, turn out your dead!"** — and buried in shallow sand on the Brooklyn shore. Several bodies a day was normal by survivor accounts (commonly "five to ten a night" at the worst — MED, memoir-based). The British offered release to any prisoner who would enlist in the Royal Navy/Army; the overwhelming majority refused — which is the quiet heroism of the story. (HIGH on conditions/diseases/burials and the enlistment offer; the nightly cry is from survivor memoirs — MED, badge "survivors recalled")
- **The toll — ranges and basis (HANDLE-WITH-CARE):**
  - **The traditional figure: ~11,000–11,500 dead** on the Jersey and the Wallabout hulks. Its basis is early-19th-century estimation (postwar claims and the 1808 reinterment campaign), NOT a body count — no death register survives complete. (HIGH that this is the traditional figure and that its basis is soft)
  - **Modern scholarship:** **Edwin G. Burrows** (*Forgotten Patriots*, 2008 — the standard study) estimated **~25,000–30,000+ Americans were held prisoner** in and around New York and that **at least ~17,500 may have died in British captivity overall** (all prisons: ships, sugar houses, churches), while granting the evidence is fragmentary. Ranges commonly given for the prison ships alone run **7,000–12,000**. **Use: "somewhere between about 7,000 and 12,000 on the ships — by the traditional count about 11,000 — and more in the city's prisons."** (MED-HIGH — give the range, name the uncertainty)
  - **The comparison — verified and attributable:** about **6,800 Americans died in BATTLE** in the entire war; deaths in British captivity (Burrows: ≥17,500; prison-ship traditional count alone ~11,000) **exceeded all American battle deaths — roughly twice over by Burrows's estimate.** Attribute the comparison ("historians estimate…", "by Edwin Burrows's count…"); it survives even on the lowest serious prisoner-death figures. (HIGH on the ~6,800 battle-death baseline and on the direction of the comparison; MED on any exact multiple)
- **The shore and the monument (one closing line):** for decades after the war, **bones washed out of the Wallabout sand** with every tide and construction dig; collected remains were crypted in 1808 and finally beneath the **Prison Ship Martyrs' Monument** (Fort Greene Park, Brooklyn, dedicated **1908** — a 149-ft Doric column by Stanford White's firm), which still stands over what the harbor did to them. (HIGH)
- Framing: this section is the cost-ledger of the privateering boom in §3 — the same sailors who chased prize shares ended here when the odds ran out. Connect them explicitly. (HIGH as framing)

## 7. THE FRENCH NAVY — THE DECISIVE INSTRUMENT

*(Scope: the FLEET and what it changed. How France came into the war — the treaties, Franklin, the money — is the `french-alliance` chapter. One sentence of handoff, no more.)*

- **What changed in 1778:** France's entry (followed by Spain, 1779, and war with the Dutch, 1780) turned a colonial police action into a **world war Britain had to fight without a single ally**. The Royal Navy now had to cover the English Channel (a real invasion scare in 1779), the Mediterranean (Gibraltar/Minorca), the West Indies (the sugar islands — economically dearer to both empires than the thirteen colonies), India, AND America. The American blockade — never airtight — **stretched thin and tore.** Supplies, French troops (Rochambeau, 1780), and French fleets crossed essentially at will. (HIGH)
- **The pattern before 1781 (one beat, honest):** the first French interventions disappointed — Admiral **d'Estaing** missed chances at New York and Newport (1778) and failed bloodily at Savannah (1779). The lesson the allies finally applied in 1781: naval superiority only matters if it shows up at the decisive point at the decisive moment, in overwhelming local strength. (HIGH)
- **The 1781 setup (compressed — Yorktown's land story lives elsewhere):** Cornwallis's army fortified **Yorktown, Virginia**, on the York River, expecting the navy to supply or extract it. Admiral **François-Joseph Paul, comte de Grasse**, brought his **entire West Indies fleet — 28 ships of the line — north from Saint-Domingue (Haiti)**, gambling the Caribbean convoys, and anchored inside the Chesapeake (Lynnhaven Bay) on August 30 with 3,000+ troops to join Lafayette. **Admiral de Barras's** smaller squadron (**8 ships of the line + transports carrying the French siege artillery**) sailed separately from Newport. The British fleet under **Rear Admiral Thomas Graves** (19 ships of the line, with Hood's ships joined from the Indies) sailed from New York to break it up. (HIGH)

### The Battle of the Chesapeake (a.k.a. the Virginia Capes), September 5, 1781 — THIS CHAPTER OWNS IT
- **The stakes, stated plainly:** if Graves controls the bay mouth, Cornwallis is supplied or rescued and Yorktown never happens. If de Grasse holds it, Cornwallis is sealed in a trap with French siege guns coming. **No American ship took part. The decisive naval battle of the American Revolution was fought entirely between Britain and France.** (HIGH)
- **The morning:** Graves arrived off the capes early on **September 5** to find de Grasse already anchored inside. Caught with ~90 officers and ~1,800 men ashore on boat duty, **de Grasse sortied anyway with 24 of his 28 ships of the line**, shorthanded, beating out against the tide past Cape Henry rather than be trapped at anchor. Graves — though he had the wind and the chance to strike the strung-out French van — formed his conventional line and waited for his line to come parallel. (HIGH)
- **Line-of-battle mechanics in plain language (the reader needs this once):** fleets fought in single file — a "line of battle" — so every ship's broadside bore and no friend blocked a friendly gun. Doctrine (the Royal Navy's *Fighting Instructions*) prized keeping the line intact; signals were flags, easily misread; turning a 2,000-ton wooden ship took minutes and sea-room. Battles between matched lines were usually indecisive by design — which is exactly what Britain could NOT afford here, because a draw left France holding the bay. (HIGH)
- **The fight (~4 p.m. to sunset, about two hours of firing):** only the leading and center ships ever truly engaged; the rear divisions (Hood's) never got into action — Graves flew **contradictory signals ("line ahead" and "close action" together)**, and Hood, reading the line signal as governing, held his station while the vans pounded each other. British van ships were mauled aloft (French gunnery aimed at rigging). (HIGH on the signal confusion and partial engagement; the Graves-vs-Hood blame fight ran for years — characterize as confusion, don't adjudicate)
- **Casualties/damage:** British ~**90 killed, 246 wounded**, five ships badly damaged — one, **HMS Terrible (74)**, so leaky she was **scuttled/burned days later**; French ~**200–220 killed and wounded**, two ships damaged. Modest numbers for a world-deciding battle — the point is what the battle DECIDED, not what it destroyed. (HIGH — Wikipedia/standard accounts: British 90/246; French ~209–220)
- **The decisive week AFTER the gunfire:** the fleets drifted south within sight of each other for days, Graves's van too cut-up to renew action. Then de Grasse turned back — and when both raced for the bay, **the French got there first (September 13), finding Barras's Newport squadron already inside with the siege guns.** De Grasse now had **~35–36 ships of the line** in the Chesapeake. Graves, outnumbered and battered, **returned to New York to refit.** (HIGH)
- **Why it sealed Yorktown:** Cornwallis's supply line, reinforcement, and evacuation all required the bay. After September 13 he had none of the three. The French fleet blockaded the York River while Washington's and Rochambeau's armies (and Barras's siege artillery) closed the land side. Britain's relief fleet finally sailed from New York **October 19, 1781 — the day Cornwallis surrendered.** (HIGH)
- **The verdict (attributable):** tactically a mauling draw, "a tactical victory for the French by no clearcut margin, but a strategic victory… that sealed the principal outcome of the war" (historian Russell Weigley's much-quoted formulation). Washington's own verdict is the chapter's thesis — see VERIFIED QUOTES (the November 15, 1781 letter to Lafayette: "without a decisive naval force we can do nothing definitive…"). (HIGH)

### Gibraltar and the global pin (ONE beat — context only)
- While the Chesapeake decided America, most of the Royal Navy was somewhere else: the **Great Siege of Gibraltar (June 1779 – February 1783)** — the longest siege British forces have ever endured — pinned huge fleets to periodic relief convoys (Rodney 1780, Darby 1781, Howe 1782, the last against the famous floating-batteries assault of September 1782, which the garrison destroyed); meanwhile fleets fought over the West Indies (until Rodney's redeeming victory at the **Saintes, April 1782**, where de Grasse himself was captured), Minorca fell (1782), and squadrons dueled off India (Suffren vs. Hughes). The point for THIS chapter: **Britain didn't lose the sea war — it lost the sea at one place, for one month, and that was enough.** Yorktown happened in the gap. (HIGH on the events; the closing formulation is framing — keep it, it's the honest verdict; MED on "longest siege endured by British armed forces" — standard claim, attribute as "usually called")

## 8. THE CLOSE — THE HONEST VERDICT (and one forward line)

- **The ledger, stated flat:** America never built a navy that mattered. The Continental Navy lost or burned essentially everything it ever floated — all thirteen original frigates gone by 1781, the last ship sold in 1785. What America actually had: **privateers that stung** (hundreds of vessels, four-digit prize counts, London insurance rates climbing), **one captain who became a legend** by refusing to surrender a sinking ship, and **an ally whose fleet won the decisive sea battle** of the war without a single American vessel present. (HIGH — every clause anchored above)
- **The strategic moral (the chapter's takeaway):** the rebels never contested command of the sea; they made British sea power expensive, survived it, and borrowed a navy for the one month that counted. (HIGH as framing)
- **Forward line:** when the United States finally built a real navy — six frigates authorized in **1794** (the Naval Act, against the Barbary corsairs; one of them the USS *Constitution*) — it built them remembering all of this. The US Navy counts its birthday from October 13, 1775; its actual fleet begins in 1794. (HIGH; keep to one line — it's a different story)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **"I have not yet begun to fight" is a 46-years-later recollection, NOT a contemporary record.** First in print 1825 (Richard Dale, via Sherburne's biography). What Jones HIMSELF reported (Oct 3, 1779, to Franklin) is that he answered a demand for quarter "in the most determined negative." Rival period-adjacent versions exist ("I may sink, but I'm damned if I strike" — newspaper/crewman; "I have not yet thought of it, but I am determined to make you strike" — Jones's own later telling; Rush's dinner-party variant). **Telling rule: narrate the refusal as fact (it is), give the famous sentence WITH its badge, never print it unbadged.** The battle is fully tellable on documented material alone.
2. **The prison-ship death toll is an estimate range, and the famous comparison needs attribution.** Traditional figure ~11,000–11,500 (basis: early-1800s estimation + the 1808 reinterment campaign, no surviving complete death register). Modern ranges for the ships: ~7,000–12,000; Edwin Burrows (*Forgotten Patriots*, 2008) puts ALL American deaths in British captivity at ≥17,500 vs. ~6,800 American battle deaths. **The "more died on the prison ships than in all the war's battles" line is defensible but must be attributed and ranged** — by Burrows's wider captivity count it's roughly two-to-one; on the lowest ship-only figures it still clears the battle-death total. Never print "11,000" as a hard count.
3. **Privateer numbers are wildly inflated and double-counted in popular sources.** Commissions: ~1,700 Continental + hundreds state (vessels often recommissioned per voyage → "2,000+ privateers" counts vessels-with-paper, not distinct hulls). Prizes: NPS says ~600 captured/destroyed (condemned-and-counted standard); common scholarship ~2,300; Lloyd's-derived counts run toward 3,000 (with ~900 recaptured/ransomed). **Use ranges with the honest gloss ("depending on who was counting"), and always pair the boom with its costs** (manpower drain from the navy, profit motive, Gloucester/Newburyport losses, the prison ships).
4. **No Jones hagiography.** The Selkirk raid was a **botched kidnapping that ended in looting the family silver** — the apology letter and the personally-financed buyback/return are verified and make the story humane, but tell the act plainly first. Whitehaven was a fizzle (one ship singed) whose power was psychological. The Tobago killing (self-defense per Jones), the Russian-navy scandal, and dying alone in Paris stay in — one clause each. He was a prickly, glory-hungry, genuinely brilliant fighting captain; both halves true.
5. **Revere's Penobscot disgrace is factual — and so is the acquittal.** He was accused of disobedience/unsoldierly conduct in the retreat, relieved of his militia command, **demanded the court-martial himself, and was acquitted February 1782.** Tell both beats; neither a smear nor a cover-up. (And Penobscot's "worst naval disaster until Pearl Harbor" is an attributed commonplace, not a statistic — "often called.")
6. **Don't promote the Continental Navy.** Its honest record: ~60 ships ever, peak ~31, ~200 prizes, one fleet operation (a raid on a near-undefended island), all 13 original frigates lost, the flag shown in Europe, Franklin ferried, one immortal single-ship action. "A nuisance and a flag, not a fleet" is the calibrated verdict. Resist navy-birthday romance — the real US fleet dates from 1794 (one forward line only).
7. **The Chesapeake must stay a FRENCH battle.** No American ships were present — that absence IS the story's point; don't smuggle in American agency. Equally, don't caricature Graves: the signal confusion ("line ahead" + "close action" flying together, Hood holding station) and the Fighting-Instructions doctrine explain the British failure better than personal cowardice; the Graves/Hood blame war was never settled — present as confusion, don't adjudicate.
8. **Scope fences:** the French ALLIANCE (Franklin, treaties, money, motives) = `french-alliance` chapter — here the fleet simply arrives as an instrument with one handoff clause. The peace = `treaty-of-paris`. Yorktown's land siege = its own chapter; this chapter delivers Cornwallis sealed, then stops. Gibraltar/West Indies/India get ONE context beat (where the Royal Navy was pinned), not a tour.
9. **Insurance-rate figures are directional, not precise.** Documented series exist (army-contractor shipping ~13% → ~32% over 1776; Caribbean routes notoriously worse after 1777) but vary by route/year/source. Phrase as "rates climbed steeply / roughly doubled and worse on some routes"; never one clean percentage for "British insurance."
10. **Numeric fields to keep loose:** Royal Navy 1775 (~270 total; 131-of-the-line split is one source's accounting); Nancy cargo quantities (round them); Washington's-fleet prize count (~35–55, "dozens"); Nassau haul (~88 cannon/15 mortars, "nearly a hundred cannon"); Flamborough casualties ("about half of each crew"); Penobscot vessels (19+24 ≈ "more than forty, all lost") and ~500 casualties; *Jersey* daily deaths (memoir-based); 55,000 privateersmen (loose cumulative estimate).
