## MUST-ADD (Essential, Blocking)

These gaps are substantively significant enough that the section is incomplete without addressing them.

### MUST-ADD 1 — The States' Rights Internal Contradiction in the Confederate Constitution

**The single most important omission in this draft.**

The draft makes a powerful case that the Confederate Constitution was explicitly about slavery. But it misses the most incisive analytical point: the constitution that claimed to be built on "states' rights" simultaneously and explicitly FORBADE individual Confederate states from abolishing or even weakening slavery (Art. I §9(4): "no law denying or impairing the right of property in negro slaves"). A state that wanted to phase out slavery on its own had no constitutional right to do so. The territories clause (Art. IV §3(3)) went further: no territorial population could choose against slavery — it was mandatory in every new territory the Confederacy acquired. This is not a small irony. It is the definitive refutation of the "states' rights" reading from within the document itself. The Confederacy protected one right — the enslavers' right to own people — and used that protection to override any state's right to do otherwise.

This point requires only 2–3 sentences and can be added to the Confederate Constitution section ("In Their Own Words") without structural change. The fact pack does not flag this explicitly (it is a blind spot — see below), but all clauses needed to make the argument are already quoted verbatim in the pack.

**Why blocking:** The draft's "states' rights" demolition is currently based only on what the constitution said; it does not make the stronger point that "states' rights" is contradicted on the face of the document itself by the clauses the draft already quotes. This is the next analytical step the reader needs to see.

### MUST-ADD 2 — Texas Declaration of Secession

The draft uses Mississippi, South Carolina, and Georgia to establish the slavery-cause case. The fact pack also contains Texas's declaration (Feb 1, 1861) — equally explicit, and with different rhetoric: Texas grounded Confederate identity in racial hierarchy as "settled truth" ("held and regarded as an inferior and dependent race"). Using four declarations instead of three is not a comprehensiveness nicety; it was four states that issued formal declarations, and using all four strengthens the evidentiary weight, especially when the draft explicitly says "four official documents" in the closing. Currently the draft counts only three (it says "four states" but quotes only SC, MS, GA — a factual mismatch that also appears to be an error).

The Texas passage is brief and in the fact pack ready to use. The fix is small.

**Why blocking:** The draft explicitly claims "four states. four official documents" in paragraph 91 but then provides only three. This is an internal inconsistency that undermines the evidentiary argument at the moment it lands.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER (Non-Blocking, but Strengthens the Section)

These are gaps or thin spots that would improve the piece but are not essential.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER A — Confederate Constitution's Non-Slavery Structural Differences

The draft treats the Confederate Constitution as if its only meaningful changes from the US Constitution were the slavery clauses. But the Confederate framers also: eliminated "general welfare" from the taxing power, banned protective tariffs explicitly, gave the president a single 6-year non-renewable term and a line-item veto, and opened the preamble with "each State acting in its sovereign and independent character" (vs. the US "We the People" / "more perfect Union" language). These differences matter for one reason: they show what the secessionists' actual economic grievances looked like in law. Acknowledging them — briefly — would make the slavery argument stronger, not weaker, because the reader can see that when the framers wanted to protect something economically, they wrote it in explicitly (as they did for slavery). The structural "states' rights" provisions are real but narrow; the slavery protections are absolute and unamendable.

This is enrichment, not a correction. A single paragraph.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER B — The Provisional vs. Permanent Constitution Distinction Needs One More Beat

The draft notes Feb 8 (provisional) and March 11 (permanent) but doesn't explain what changed between them. The Cornerstone Speech was delivered after the permanent constitution was adopted — Stephens was explicitly celebrating what the permanent document enshrined. A single sentence connecting the permanent constitution's adoption to Stephens's speech would tighten the chronological logic.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER C — The "Unfinished Capitol" Visual

The draft mentions Lincoln stood on "the steps of the unfinished U.S. Capitol." This is historically accurate (the Capitol dome was under construction in 1861 and was famously completed during the war at Lincoln's direction) and is a vivid detail that could be given one more sentence — Lincoln delivering his inaugural in front of a half-built dome, a country half-built itself. The draft alludes to it but doesn't exploit it. Minor.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER D — International Slave Trade Clause as Additional Irony

The Confederate Constitution banned the international slave trade (Art. I §9(1)) — a fact the draft omits entirely. This is counterintuitive: a nation founded to perpetuate slavery banned importing new enslaved people from Africa. The reason was economic protectionism — existing slaveholders didn't want the value of their "property" diluted by cheap imports — but it also shows the Confederate framers protecting the commodity market in human beings, not some abstract liberty. A single sentence noting this ban and its reason would sharpen the economic-realism strand the draft already develops through the Mississippi declaration's "four billions of money" framing.

## Fact-Pack Blind Spots

These are gaps in the fact pack that the draft correctly did not assert (or correctly flagged as unverified) but that a future authoring pass should address:

1. **Texas declaration not carried forward from fact pack into draft.** The pack has the Texas verbatim passages; the draft omits them without noting the omission. This feeds MUST-ADD 2 above.

2. **The states' rights internal contradiction.** The pack quotes all four clauses needed to make the argument (Art. I §9(4), IV §2(1), IV §3(3)) but never draws the conclusion that these clauses simultaneously prevent states from exercising the very right the Confederacy claimed to be defending. The fact-pack assembled the evidence; the analytical conclusion is missing.

3. **Anderson's personal background.** The fact pack does NOT confirm that Anderson was Kentucky-born or a former enslaver. The draft self-flags this ([VERIFY] in the fact ledger) and is correct to do so. Both details should be sourced or cut. Anderson was from Kentucky (this is historically accurate and verifiable from other sources) but the "former enslaver" claim needs sourcing before use.

4. **Delaware's DuPont gunpowder works.** Not in the fact pack. Delaware's strategic value is understated in both the pack and draft — the DuPont works eventually supplied ~half of Union gunpowder. Worth adding to the fact pack for the authoring revision.

5. **Confederate Constitution preamble language.** The pack quotes the slavery clauses verbatim but does not quote the preamble's "each State acting in its sovereign and independent character" language, which is the clearest textual example of the states' rights framing — and therefore the clearest target for the internal-contradiction argument. The Avalon Project text has this; it should be in the pack.

6. **6-year single presidential term / line-item veto / tariff ban / general welfare removal.** None of these Confederate Constitutional differences appear in the fact pack. They are not essential to the slavery-cause argument, but they belong in a complete treatment of "what the two constitutions actually said."

7. **Star of the West (Jan 9, 1861).** In the pack but correctly scoped to the sibling section. No action needed here.

## Summary Assessment

The draft is comprehensive and accurate on its core argument. It covers every major beat in this section's lane with care and good evidence. The verbatim primary-source work is the section's strongest feature.

**MUST-ADD count: 2**

1. The Confederate Constitution's internal "states' rights" contradiction (the constitution explicitly forbade individual states from abolishing slavery — the states' rights argument is demolished by the document's own clauses, not just by what the document added)
2. The Texas declaration (the draft claims "four states, four documents" but only quotes three; Texas is already in the fact pack)

**Single most important omission:** MUST-ADD 1 — the internal contradiction in the "states' rights" claim. The draft builds a powerful case that the Confederate Constitution was explicitly about slavery. It stops one step short of the decisive blow: the same constitution prevented any Confederate state from choosing to end slavery on its own, and mandated slavery in every future territory. "States' rights" named one right — the right to enslave — and then used that right to override all others. This is the argument that closes the door on the Lost Cause reading from within the Confederacy's own founding document, and the draft's quoted clauses are all the evidence needed to make it.
