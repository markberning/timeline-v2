# Clarity / Newcomer Critic — "War Finance & the Road to Taxation"

**Section:** French and Indian War, off-the-battlefield theme (kind=theme, POLITICS)
**Read cold as:** a sharp 15-year-old with zero prior knowledge.
**Verdict: PASS** — no LOST (genuine comprehension-breaker) items. A handful of SHOULD-FIX polish items below; none block understanding.

This draft is unusually disciplined about glossing. Almost every piece of genuine
finance/legal jargon is defined inline at first use (bonds, interest, national debt,
servicing the debt, regulars, hogshead, excise officers, legal tender, specie/hard
money, vice-admiralty courts, internal vs. external/direct tax, virtual representation,
sophistry, Sons of Liberty, non-importation). The story arc — one war debt lighting
two fuses — is laid out plainly and is followable end to end. The Native-nations
section is treated as actors with their own aims, not backdrop, and reads clearly.

No over-glossing of common English was found; the gloss density is appropriate.

## Section-by-section

### Intro + "The cost of winning"
- **CLEAR.** "bonds," "interest," "national debt," "servicing the debt" all glossed
  inline at first use. The £75M → £130M / £132.6M debt story and the "interest ate
  more than half the budget" point are stated plainly. No jargon left bare.

### "An army that wouldn't go home"
- **CLEAR** on the army itself — "regulars (full-time professional soldiers)" glossed.
  The colonial fear of a peacetime standing army (the tyranny association) is explained
  in plain terms.
- **SHOULD-FIX — "the Bute ministry" introduced cold (line 24).** First mention is
  "the Bute ministry decided in early 1763." A zero-knowledge reader doesn't know who
  or what Bute is — prime minister? when? It recurs ("Lord Bute's ministry," "Bute was
  hanged in effigy") and is only retroactively reconstructable from "His successor,
  George Grenville" (line 58) and Grenville "took over" (line 67). Not a comprehension-
  breaker — the *ministry's actions* are clear regardless, and the reader can back-fill
  that Bute was the prior PM — but a four-word ID on first use ("the Bute ministry — the
  government of the day") would remove the small stumble. Status: NEEDS-GLOSS (minor).

### "The people the army was for" (Pontiac's War)
- **CLEAR.** Pontiac ("The Odawa war leader Pontiac"), Neolin ("'the Delaware Prophet,'
  a Lenape spiritual leader"), and General Jeffery Amherst (identified, British context
  obvious) are all properly scene-set. The diplomatic-gifts mechanism is explained well
  ("understood as the necessary courtesy of diplomacy, not charity").
- **NOTE (not a fix) — the nation-name list (line 35) is the draft's one dense
  passage.** ~13 nation names in a single sentence, grouped by region. This is heavy for
  a cold reader, but it is deliberate (the prose explicitly refuses to "flatten them into
  one"), it is geographically organized, and the reader does NOT need to retain the list
  to follow the story. It reads as a roll-call, not a wall of plot the reader must track.
  Not LOST, not a required fix. Leave as is.

### "Why not just tax England?" (cider riots)
- **CLEAR.** "Cyder Act," "hogshead (a large barrel)," "excise officers (the
  government's tax collectors)" all glossed. The "letting itself into the kitchen"
  warrantless-search point is vivid and clear.
- **SHOULD-FIX — "hanged in effigy" never glossed (lines 58, 121; caption 62).** A sharp
  15-year-old may not know "in effigy" means a dummy/likeness, not the person. Context
  (and the satirical-print caption) carries it, and it never blocks the sentence's
  meaning, so this is polish only. Status: NEEDS-GLOSS (minor) — a parenthetical on first
  use ("hanged in effigy — a stuffed likeness strung up in the street") would close it.

### "Grenville's reasoning"
- **CLEAR.** Grenville is fully scene-set here ("took over as head of the Treasury and
  prime minister in April 1763"). The reasoning, the £200,000 cost vs. ~£78,000 program,
  and the no-vote-in-Parliament fault line are all stated in plain language. The Sugar
  Act preamble quote is set up clearly.

### "The first reach into colonial pockets" (Sugar & Currency Acts)
- **CLEAR.** "molasses (the thick syrup left over from refining sugar...)," "vice-
  admiralty courts" (glossed inline with the no-jury / presumed-guilty mechanics), and
  "legal tender (currency a creditor is legally required to accept...)" all glossed. The
  "tax cut that was actually a tax increase" hinge and the hard-coin scarcity squeeze are
  explained well.
- **SHOULD-FIX (very minor) — "royal assent" used unglossed (lines 86, 134; also 121).**
  "given royal assent on 19 April 1764." For a cold reader this is inferable as "the king
  signed it into law," especially paired with passage dates, so it is not blocking. One
  light gloss on first use would tidy it. Status: NEEDS-GLOSS (minor).

### "The Stamp Act"
- **CLEAR.** The internal-vs-external/direct-tax distinction — the whole spine of the
  section — is glossed thoroughly and correctly. "paid in specie, in gold and silver
  coin" ties cleanly back to the Currency Act vise. Strong, followable.

### "No taxation without representation"
- **CLEAR.** The principle is glossed in plain terms ("a free people... can be taxed only
  by a body they have voted for"). James Otis Jr. (British) — side-tagged, scene-set as
  "the Massachusetts lawyer." Samuel Johnson (British) — side-tagged, identified as "The
  writer." "virtual representation" glossed; "sophistry" immediately re-glossed ("a
  clever evasion dressed up as a principle"). No bare jargon.

### "The colonies answer"
- **CLEAR.** Stamp Act Congress scene-set ("twenty-seven delegates from nine colonies...
  the first time the colonies had come together"). "Sons of Liberty, networks of
  organized protest and intimidation" glossed. "non-importation agreements, refusing to
  buy British goods" glossed inline. Andrew Oliver (British) side-tagged and identified.

### "Repeal, and the principle Britain kept"
- **CLEAR** on the substance — repeal vs. the Declaratory Act's "in all cases
  whatsoever," and why that settled nothing, is explained plainly.
- **NOTE — Benjamin Franklin (line 128) appears with no side-tag and no scene-set on
  first mention.** Under the war pipeline's loosened rule, marquee figures are exempt
  from the side-tag requirement, and Franklin is a marquee-tier name; more importantly,
  his allegiance (a colonist arguing for repeal) is made instantly obvious by the very
  quote attributed to him ("as we are not represented there"). Not flagged as a fix — no
  comprehension gap. ("the Examination" / "examined before the House of Commons" reads
  clearly as "questioned," reinforced by "answering 174 questions.")

### "One victory, two fuses"
- **CLEAR.** The throughline synthesis lands. The Albany Plan / "Join, or Die" callback
  (Franklin "had stood at Albany and proposed that the colonies bind themselves together")
  is self-contained — a reader who never heard of Albany gets enough here. The two-fuses
  frame is the clearest statement of the section's spine.
