## BLOCKER

**1. The dossier NOTE field states "Jones refused to surrender anyway" with no apocrypha
framing — and the eyebrow quote embeds the famous line at face value.**

The dossier `note:` field (lines 25–26) reads:

> "Jones lost by every measure on the way to it: two of his big guns burst at the first fire,
> his own consort raked him from behind, his ship was shot through and through, and two days
> later she sank. He refused to surrender anyway, and it was the Englishman who struck."

"He refused to surrender anyway" is factually correct and pack-sanctioned (Jones's "most
determined negative" is HIGH). That is fine. The BLOCKER is the section-3 EYEBROW:

> EYEBROW: Bonhomme Richard · The legend
> TITLE: The most famous sentence he never wrote

And more critically, the section-3 blurb:

> "What Jones actually wrote, what the 1779 newspapers printed, and how the line every
> American knows arrived forty-six years late from an old man's memory."

The blurb is clean — it IS the framing. But the section's own running title, "The most
famous sentence he never wrote," and the QUOTE block that follows —

> QUOTE: …answered him in the most determined negative…

— together do the right thing. The blocker is NOT the title or the quote block. The blocker
is the **DOSSIER `note:` field itself**, which a reader sees *before* any of that framing.
The dossier is a reader-facing surface (the dossier card is what most readers see first, in
the battle-lane strip). "He refused to surrender anyway" in that position, immediately after
the disaster inventory, invites the reader to fill in the famous line mentally — exactly the
gap the pack instructs the page NOT to leave. The word "anyway" is doing the legend's work:
it frames the refusal as the expected-heroic-beat, not as a documented defiance whose exact
words are unknown.

FIX: Replace "He refused to surrender anyway, and it was the Englishman who struck" with a
clause that names what the refusal is documented as without triggering the legend. e.g.:

> "Jones answered the call to surrender in 'the most determined negative,' as he wrote it
> himself — and it was the Englishman who struck."

This keeps the defiance, anchors it to Jones's own language, and makes the dossier coherent
with the section-3 evidence story rather than skating past it.

## SHOULD-FIX

**2. The French sponsorship is visible in the facts but never named as the operational
engine of the page's premise — the section-1 text and dossier bury it in subordinate clauses.**

The pack's framing note is explicit: "American colors, French money" is the premise's headline
(§1). The draft distributes this across three locations:

- Dossier AMERICAN note: "A French-financed squadron under American colors" ✓ (good)
- Section 1, the ship paragraph: "all paid for with French money" ✓ (one clause, buried)
- Section 1 opening: names France's alliance (1778) and Spain's (1779) correctly ✓

But the opening of section 1 gives the war-at-sea strategy WITHOUT making the French-money
point land as a framing beat for *this specific squadron*. A reader moving through section 1
could absorb the French alliance as background context and not register that the ship under
their feet was French-bought, French-crewed in half, and sailed out of a French port on a
French-arranged captain's commission. The pack calls the squad "French-financed" (§1) and
says the Bonhomme Richard's acquisition was "financing arranged through French shipping
magnate Jacques-Donatien Le Ray de Chaumont" — the name is not required in the prose, but
the *fact of a French-underwritten operation* is. The "French money" clause is currently
dropped mid-paragraph in the ship description without rhetorical weight.

FIX: Elevate the French sponsorship into the intro or early topic sentence of the ship
paragraph, so it arrives as framing rather than as a footnote. e.g., make the paragraph's
opening beat: "France had bought him his ship." One sentence, then the Duc de Duras
provenance. The rest of the paragraph is unchanged; the structural fact is now visible as
such.

**3. The "Paul Jones the Pirate" beat never explicitly states that the pirate framing was the
BRITISH GOVERNMENT's official legal position — it is presented only as a press/caricature
phenomenon.**

The draft correctly uses the caricature image and its caption reads: "To the London press he
was not a naval officer at all but a brigand sailing under no recognized flag, a man who had
raided the English coast and could expect a rope if he were ever caught. The image is
propaganda; the legal danger behind it was real." That is good.

But the pack carries something stronger: the British ambassador at The Hague, Sir Joseph
Yorke, on October 8, 1779, formally demanded the Dutch hand over the squadron, characterizing
Jones as a "rebel" and "pirate" flying under no recognized state — this was the British
GOVERNMENT's official diplomatic position, not just Grub Street. The draft reaches this beat
in section 3 (the Texel standoff paragraph), and there it correctly quotes "pirate" in quotes
and attributes it to Yorke. So the beat is present at section 3.

The problem is sequencing and framing. Section 1 introduces the British "pirate" view as a
*press* phenomenon. The pack notes (§7b) that the halter-around-his-neck image "IS
independently real as a description of Jones's legal position (Britain regarded him as a
pirate; the caricatures prove the reputation)." Section 3's Texel handling is correct. But
Jones's bio in the commander section says only "caricatured in the London prints as 'Paul Jones
the Pirate'" — only the print shops. The bio should carry a glancing reference to the legal
dimension (that Britain's government agreed with its print shops), so readers encountering
the commander card standalone understand why the pirate view was more than journalistic noise.

FIX: Add one clause to the Jones commander bio after the "Paul Jones the Pirate" line: "—
and not just in the print shops; Britain's government called him a pirate with diplomatic
standing, demanding he be handed over wherever his flag appeared." One clause; the legal
dimension is now present on the bio surface, coherent with section 3's Texel handling.

**4. The propaganda victory is named as propaganda, but the specific mechanism — Franklin
needing this for Versailles — is compressed into a relative clause in section 3 and does
not name the stakes plainly.**

Section 3, the outcome paragraph, reads:

> "Franklin in Paris now had a British warship beaten on England's own coast to retail at
> the French court."

"Retail" is a strong and precise word and carries the mechanism. But the preceding sentence
gives the Texel crisis and the British-pirate-demand, and the reader has to carry that
context into the Franklin sentence. The pack's framing (§6) is: "this is what the battle
actually changed... For a cause whose navy had mostly burned (Penobscot, the same season),
a frigate of the Royal Navy struck on its own coast was priceless theater." The draft uses
"priceless theater" in the closing paragraph and "beyond price as theater" in the same
section — those land. But the PROPAGANDA FUNCTION AS PROPAGANDA is only named implicitly
("retail at the French court" / "priceless theater"). The pack's instruction is to name the
propaganda function *as* propaganda. The draft calls the caricature "propaganda" in a
caption and calls the outcome "priceless theater," but never uses the word "propaganda"
for what this victory did for France.

This is a POLISH-level finding rather than a SHOULD-FIX, but it is listed here because the
framing hunt specifically required that "the propaganda function in Europe [be] named AS
propaganda." The draft uses theater/retail framing, which is accurate but one level of
indirection away from the explicit name.

FIX: In the section 3 outcome paragraph, add one phrase: e.g., "Franklin now had a piece of
raw propaganda — a British frigate struck on its own coast — to retail at Versailles, and
he used it." One word — "propaganda" — makes the mechanism honest.

## POLISH

**5. The Jones coda names the unresolved assault accusation but in a way that could read
as exculpatory rather than genuinely unresolved.**

The coda reads: "an appointment that fell apart in court intrigue and an unresolved
accusation of assaulting a young girl." "Court intrigue" arrives first and "the unresolved
accusation" reads as a secondary cause. The pack (§6) lists "an unresolved accusation of
assaulting a young girl" without ranking it against the court intrigue. The draft's phrasing
could lead a reader to weight the intrigue as the cause and the accusation as the secondary
taint. That slides toward Jones-favorability. Since the accusation is genuinely unresolved,
it deserves parallel framing: "an appointment that fell apart in court intrigue and an
unresolved accusation of assaulting a young girl — both on the record, neither settled." Or
simply reverse the order. Low stakes; single-clause fix.
