## the-breadbasket · The Shenandoah · The breadbasket worth burning

### Why the Valley was the war’s grocery store — and its back door

Start with the name, because it’s the first confusing thing about this battle. Officially, on the War Department’s books, this is the **Battle of Opequon** — named for **Opequon Creek**, the stream the Union army splashed across to reach the field. Almost nobody calls it that. To everyone who tells the story it’s the **Third Battle of Winchester**, and the “third” is the grim part: the town of **Winchester, Virginia** sat on so many roads, and changed hands so many times, that armies fought *three* separate named battles over it across the war (First Winchester in 1862, Second in 1863, and this one, the third and by far the largest). So you get one fight with two names — a formal one hardly anyone uses, and a common one that quietly tells you the town had been bled three times.

To understand why anyone was fighting over Winchester at all, you have to understand the ground it sits in: the **Shenandoah Valley**, the long farm corridor running down the western edge of Virginia between two mountain ranges. The Valley did two things for the Confederacy, and both made it priceless. First, it was a larder. Its prosperous farms grew the grain and raised the livestock and put up the hay that fed **Lee’s Army of Northern Virginia** — the main Confederate army, dug in around Richmond and Petersburg to the east. The Valley fed that army so reliably that the period gave it a nickname: **“the breadbasket of the Confederacy.”** Second, the Valley was a covered highway pointed straight at the North. Because of the way the land drains, going *north* in the Valley is going *“down”* it — and an army marching down the Valley came out near Washington, screened by mountains the whole way, while a Union army marching the other direction threatened nothing that mattered. That lopsided geography made the Valley a favorite Confederate invasion chute: Stonewall Jackson used it in 1862, and in the summer of 1864 **Lt. Gen. Jubal A. Early (South)** used it to march a small army all the way to the outskirts of Washington itself.

Now name the thing under the thing, because the breadbasket is the *object* and not the *reason*. The Confederacy existed to preserve slavery — eleven states had broken from the United States rather than accept any limit on the enslavement of Black people, and built their economy, their society, and their armies on it. The Shenandoah’s farms were part of that economy: this was a slave society (enslaved people made up roughly a fifth of the Valley’s population in 1860), and enslaved and free Black laborers worked the very fields whose grain and cattle were loaded up and sent east to feed Lee’s soldiers. So when Union general-in-chief Ulysses S. Grant looked at the Valley in 1864, he didn’t just see an invasion route to plug. He saw the material engine of a slaveholding rebellion, and he decided to break it. He sent **Maj. Gen. Philip H. Sheridan (North)** into the Valley with orders that went past beating Early’s army: he was to *strip the place* — burn the barns and mills, drive off the herds, take the breadbasket out of the war for good. That campaign of deliberate destruction became known as **“The Burning,”** and Third Winchester was the door it had to come through first.

> **MEANWHILE — what changing hands meant to the enslaved.** For the soldiers, Winchester trading owners was a strategic fact. For the town’s Black population — the 1860 census counted hundreds of enslaved and hundreds of free Black people there — it was the literal difference between freedom and re-enslavement. When Union troops held the town, enslaved people from the surrounding countryside walked in to free themselves; a Confederate diarist groused that the freedom of “the servants” was among the most irritating things about the Yankee occupation. When Confederate troops retook the town — in 1862, in 1863, again in 1864 — they captured local enslaved people and handed them back to the men who claimed to own them. The town didn’t just change hands. For some people it changed everything, and then changed it back, three times over.

## the-spy · The intelligence · The message in the tin foil

### How an enslaved man told Sheridan when to strike

By the middle of September 1864, Sheridan (North) had a big army in the lower Valley and a problem: he didn’t know how strong Early (South) really was, and Early — the Confederate commander, leading the **Army of the Valley** — was spread out and shifting his divisions around Winchester. Attacking a concentrated enemy in good ground is a bloody business; attacking him while he’s dispersed is a chance. Sheridan needed to know which situation he was looking at. The answer came to him by the bravest and most improbable courier of the campaign — and it is the war’s cause made flesh on this exact field.

There was a Unionist living in Winchester named **Rebecca Wright (North)**, a Quaker schoolteacher whose loyalty the Union general **George Crook (North)** could vouch for. Sheridan wanted to get a message to her and a message back, through enemy-held country, without it ever being found on the messenger. The man who carried it was **Thomas Laws** — an enslaved Black man, of Clarke County, Virginia, who held a pass that let him cross between the lines to sell produce. Union scouts recruited him, and Laws agreed to do a thing that could have gotten him killed on the spot: he carried Sheridan’s message into Winchester and Rebecca Wright’s reply back out, the paper concealed in tin foil and held *in his mouth*, so that a search of his pockets and his cart would turn up nothing.

Wright’s reply was the hinge of the whole battle. She told Sheridan that Early had just been *weakened* — that a Confederate division and its artillery had been detached and sent away from the Valley toward Lee. That was exactly the intelligence Sheridan had been missing. Early was not the concentrated army Sheridan feared; he had given up a chunk of his strength and was strung out around Winchester. With that paper out of Thomas Laws’s mouth and on his desk, Sheridan stopped wondering and started planning. He would attack on **September 19, 1864**, and try to destroy the Army of the Valley before it could pull itself back together.

Hold onto who Laws was, because it’s the whole argument of the war in one man. He was, by the law of the society Early’s army was fighting to defend, *property* — a thing owned, not a person with rights. And he risked his life to hand the Union the information it needed to win. The Valley was a slave-worked breadbasket; the man who helped break the army defending it was himself enslaved, betting his life on the side that would free him. That is the cause of the war, standing on the Winchester road with a scrap of foil in his cheek.

> **MEANWHILE — the men and the matchup.** The two armies were badly mismatched on paper, and the sources genuinely disagree about by how much, so take the figures as a range: at the point of contact Sheridan’s army outnumbered Early’s by better than two to one — on the order of 40,000 engaged against roughly 15,000. (Larger present-for-duty totals get quoted too, but the engaged figures are the ones that tell the story.) Yet a two-to-one edge is the kind of advantage you can squander, and Sheridan very nearly did — for the most mundane reason imaginable, which the next section is about. The Confederate side, for its part, was thick with names worth a second glance: Early’s corps commander **Maj. Gen. John C. Breckinridge (South)** had been Vice President of the United States only a few years earlier, before he went south. The man who told the army when to strike was enslaved; one of the men it struck had sat a heartbeat from the American presidency. The war was strange that way.

## the-canyon · The morning · The traffic jam that nearly lost the day

### Why a narrow ravine almost beat a whole army

Here is the part of the story that sounds like a comedy until you remember men died of it. Sheridan’s plan depended on speed — hit Early before he could concentrate — and his army’s approach to Winchester from the east ran through a bottleneck that turned speed into a snarl.

It started well. Around two in the morning, **Brig. Gen. James H. Wilson’s cavalry (North)** — *cavalry* being the mounted, fast-moving troops who scout and screen and seize ground ahead of the foot soldiers — pushed out from the east, secured the crossing of Opequon Creek, and rode up the **Berryville Pike** (the road running west from the town of Berryville into Winchester) to grab the high ground beyond. So far, perfectly. The trouble was the road itself. West of the creek the Berryville Pike threaded the **Berryville Canyon** — a narrow ravine, a couple of miles long, that was the *only* practical way for Sheridan’s infantry (his foot soldiers, the bulk of his army) to get from the creek to the open ground east of town. One road, one canyon, a whole army that needed to pour through it.

And it choked. **Maj. Gen. Horatio G. Wright’s VI Corps (North)** — a *corps* being one of the big building-block formations an army is divided into, tens of thousands of men under one general — reached the canyon first, around five in the morning. But instead of shoving its fighting men through, Wright’s corps pushed its *wagons and artillery* into the defile ahead of the infantry. Then it got worse: ambulances carrying wounded men *back out* of the canyon met the wagons grinding their way *in*, head to head, in a ravine with no room to pass. The result was a monumental traffic jam — a clot of wagons, guns, and ambulances plugging the one road the army had to use. **Maj. Gen. William H. Emory’s XIX Corps (North)** — Sheridan’s second big infantry corps, queued up behind Wright — couldn’t even *enter* the canyon until around nine in the morning. Sheridan’s attack, the surprise blow against a scattered enemy, was delayed by more than two hours.

Those two hours were the difference between a pounce and a brawl. While the Union army stood jammed nose-to-tail in a ditch, Early (South) used the gift of time to do the one thing Sheridan had attacked to *prevent*: he pulled his dispersed divisions together in front of Winchester. The army Wilson had caught spread thin was, by the time the Union infantry finally cleared the canyon, concentrated and waiting. A clogged road had erased the whole advantage Thomas Laws had bought. The easy victory was gone before a real shot of the main battle had been fired; what remained would have to be taken the hard way, head-on, against an enemy who was now ready.

## the-flank · The afternoon · Russell, Rodes, and the hammer from the north

### A gap, two dead generals, and the cavalry that broke the line

The main assault finally went in late in the morning — Wright’s VI Corps and Emory’s XIX Corps (both North) attacking together, side by side, east of Winchester. And almost at once the head-on fight showed why head-on fights are murder.

The two Union corps advanced over uneven ground, and as they pushed forward a **gap** — a hole in the line where two formations drift apart — tore open between them. A gap in a battle line is a doorway, and the Confederates went straight through it. **Maj. Gen. Robert E. Rodes (South)**, one of Lee’s finest division commanders, and **Maj. Gen. John B. Gordon (South)** drove a counterattack into the seam, and the Union center buckled and fell back in disorder. For a stretch of that afternoon, the battle Sheridan was supposed to win comfortably was coming apart in the middle.

It was saved by a man who died saving it. **Brig. Gen. David A. Russell (North)**, a division commander in Wright’s corps, brought up his reserves to plug the gap — and as he ordered the counterattack that would close the doorway, a bursting shell killed him. His men, with **Brig. Gen. Emory Upton (North)** among the brigade commanders, drove on anyway and restored the line. Sources call that the turning point of the fight: the moment the Union center stopped collapsing and started holding. And in the very same churn of fighting, on the other side of the gap, Rodes (South) was killed leading his counterattack — struck down, reportedly while urging his men on, in the early afternoon. Two generals dead in the same patch of ground inside an hour, one closing the gap and one trying to keep it open. That was the texture of this battle: close, and expensive at the very top. Before the day was done it had killed two Confederate generals (Rodes and **Brig. Gen. Archibald C. Godwin (South)**, also cut down by a bursting shell) and one Union general (Russell), and wounded several more on each side — an unusually heavy harvest of senior officers for a single day, and the surest sign of how hard the men had to be driven and how close the thing was.

Then came the blow that ended it, and it came from the right. All through the fight Sheridan had been keeping pieces in reserve, and around three in the afternoon he turned them loose against Early’s *left flank* — the northern end of the Confederate line, the open shoulder of an army where, if you can get around it, you’re no longer pushing the enemy back but rolling him up sideways. **Brig. Gen. George Crook’s Army of West Virginia (North)** — the reserve corps Sheridan had been holding — went in against Gordon’s left. At the same moment Sheridan’s massed cavalry came sweeping down onto that same flank from the north and northeast: **Brig. Gen. Wesley Merritt (North)** and **Brig. Gen. William W. Averell’s (North)** horsemen drove straight into the open end of Early’s line. *This* is why the cavalry on the right was decisive — not because horsemen are glamorous, but because they were the mobile force massed exactly where the Confederate army had no flank to spare, and they hit it while Crook’s infantry hit it too. Infantry pressing from the east, cavalry and more infantry caving in the north: it was a hammer and an anvil, and Early’s army was the thing between them. Late in the afternoon **Brig. Gen. George A. Custer (North)** led a massed cavalry charge straight at the Confederate forts north of town, and Early’s line — pressed in front, turned on the flank, overrun on the north — came apart.

## whirling-through-winchester · The rout · Whirling through the town

### The collapse, the streets, and what one day in the Valley won

Early’s (South) army broke and ran south, and it ran the worst possible way: not in an orderly fighting retreat but in a near-rout, divisions tangling together, men streaming back *through the streets of Winchester itself* and out the **Valley Pike** — the hard-surfaced turnpike running north–south through the town that was Early’s only good road out toward Newtown and Fisher’s Hill. A Confederate soldier remembered it as the most disorderly retreat he ever saw, the road choked with fugitives from every command, men of broken units mixed together and just *going*.

It is from this collapse that the battle’s most famous line comes. The image that stuck, repeated ever after on the Union side, was that Sheridan’s men had sent Early’s army **“whirling through Winchester”** — the enemy spun out of the town and down the pike like something flung. It’s a wonderful phrase, and the sources can’t agree who actually said it or wrote it — Sheridan himself, his chief of staff, in a telegram to one place or another — so take it as what it plainly is: the way the *winning side* described the day, the picture of an army whirled out of a town it had held that morning. Whoever first set it down, it’s the truest four words about how September 19 ended.

Early got away. He still had an army, battered as it was, and he would turn and fight again at Fisher’s Hill three days later. But what happened at Winchester had already done the larger work. This was the largest and one of the bloodiest battles ever fought in the Shenandoah — more than 54,000 men engaged, over 8,600 of them killed, wounded, or missing by dark. The Union lost roughly 5,000; the Confederates somewhere in the neighborhood of 3,600 to 4,000. But the raw totals undersell it, because the armies weren’t the same size. Sheridan lost about an eighth of the men he put in. Early lost better than a quarter of his — and Early’s smaller army could not replace those men or those dead generals. That disproportion is the whole significance in a number: this one day began a run of losses for the Army of the Valley from which it would never recover.

What followed proves the point. Third Winchester was the first of three hammer-blows Sheridan landed in a single month — Winchester on September 19, **Fisher’s Hill** on the 22nd, **Cedar Creek** on October 19 — that together destroyed Early’s army as a fighting force and cleared the lower Valley for good. And with the Valley cleared, Sheridan carried out the order he’d come to fulfill: The Burning. His men went systematically through the breadbasket torching barns and mills and crops and driving off the herds, so that the corridor could never again feed or shelter a Confederate army. His own campaign tally of what was destroyed ran into the thousands of cattle and sheep and hogs and horses, the hundreds of thousands of bushels of grain, the dozens of mills and the hundreds of barns. The slave-worked larder that had fed Lee’s soldiers stopped feeding them.

And the timing made it matter beyond the Valley. The victory came seven weeks before the 1864 presidential election, landing on top of Sherman’s capture of Atlanta at the start of the month. A war that Northern voters had begun to fear was stalemated suddenly looked, in headline after headline, like a war visibly being won — and that shift helped carry Lincoln to reelection, which meant the war would be fought to its finish and slavery to its end. Winchester made Sheridan a national name and took the Shenandoah Valley out of the war. The breadbasket was the object. Slavery was the reason it was worth burning. And the man who told Sheridan when to swing the first blow was an enslaved man with a message in his mouth.
