## 1. SLAVERY-NAMED CHECK (first, always) — **PASS**

**Does the section name slavery as the cause in its OWN prose? YES.**

The word "slavery" / "slaves" appears repeatedly and load-bearingly, in the section's
own voice, not buried in a ledger or a link:

- §1 Meanwhile: "...a Union whose stated purpose was, within the month, about to openly
  become the destruction of slavery — **the institution Lee's army existed to defend**."
  (Names the cause AND names what the Confederate army was fighting for.)
- §3 Meanwhile (Lee's Hill): "...from that hill, **a slaveholding republic's army** was
  watching a numerically superior enemy destroy itself..."
- §5 prose (the cause hook): "the war's stated purpose openly became **the destruction
  of slavery** in the rebelling states... the cause they served was formally redefined
  as **the abolition of slavery** — while the army that slaughtered them on the heights
  was **defending the slaveholding Confederacy** that emancipation was about to threaten."
- §5 prose also lands the explicit moral-clarity sentence: "it is worth being plain
  about what each side was standing for."
- §5 OUTCOME verdict: "...turning the war openly into a war against slavery."

The Emancipation Proclamation hook (took effect Jan 1, 1863, ~3 weeks later) is present
and correctly used as the framing spine in §1 and §5. The section does NOT commit the
Lost Cause omission. Slavery-named gate: **PASS.**

## 2. FINDINGS

### A. Marye's Heights "turkey shoot" pathos — **FAIR**
The directive flagged the risk that the slaughter pathos tips into romanticizing
Confederate nobility. It does not. The draft keeps the sympathy pointed at the *victims*
(Union dead/wounded) and frames the Confederate position as a *killing machine*, not as
gallantry: "a massacre with a name," "the failure was... the whole idea that men could
cross that ground and live," Alexander's "a chicken could not live on that field." The
defenders are described as sheltered and unstrained ("held it without strain"), which is
the opposite of romanticizing their valor. No tilt.

### B. The Kirkland "Angel of Marye's Heights" legend — **FAIR (model handling)**
This is the highest-risk romanticization vector in the whole battle and the draft handles
it almost exactly right. It does NOT assert the legend as fact. §4 explicitly interrupts:
"Here honesty has to interrupt the legend," names it as Kershaw's 1880 account "roughly
seventeen years after the battle," notes Kirkland was dead by then, notes "no contemporary
accounts," and lands "possibly true, possibly embellished — and not as a verified fact."
The FIGURE caption note even requires "contested 1880 account, not established fact." This
is the model anti-romanticization treatment. No framing distortion.

> Note (for fact-checker, not a framing fix): the *content* of the Kirkland claim is fully
> flagged [VERIFY] in the ledger; framing-wise it is correctly de-romanticized.

### C. Pelham + Lee "glorious courage in one so young" quote — **FAIR, borderline-watch**
§3 narrates Pelham's two-gun stand admiringly ("nearly stopped the whole thing by
himself") and includes Lee's "It is glorious to see such courage in one so young." This
is genuine Confederate-valor material. It stays FAIR because: (1) it is balanced
immediately by the carnage of Meade's broken breakthrough and the slaughter at the wall;
(2) it is a single beat, not a romanticizing through-line; (3) the larger §3 Meanwhile
re-grounds the same Lee in "a slaveholding republic's army." Admiration for an individual
soldier's skill is not Lost Cause distortion as long as it doesn't ennoble the *cause* —
and here it doesn't. Leave as-is; flag only if the fact-checker also dings the quote.

### D. Lee "war is so terrible / lest we grow too fond of it" — **FAIR (well-defused)**
Highest-romance quote in the Lee canon, and the draft pre-empts it: explicitly flags it as
"a post-war recollection that the man supposed to have heard it never mentioned," says
"read as tradition, not transcript," then deliberately deflates the romance — "The
unromantic fact underneath it is plainer: from that hill, a slaveholding republic's army
was watching a numerically superior enemy destroy itself." This converts the single most
Lost-Cause-prone line in the battle into an anti-romantic frame. Strong handling.

### E. Confederate "high tide of confidence" Meanwhile (§5) — **FAIR**
The closing Meanwhile celebrates Lee's army's confidence ("could beat anything the North
sent at it") but immediately frames that confidence as "dangerous over-confidence" and a
"faith in his army's invincibility" that gets men killed at Gettysburg ("the arithmetic of
Marye's Heights would come back the other way"). Confederate confidence is set up to be
punished, not admired. No tilt.

### F. The town sack / "Vandals" — **FAIR (notably even-handed against the North)**
§2 does NOT sanitize the Union looting: "something the army has never been entirely proud
of," "soldiers' rampage," "the town itself was gutted," Lee "likened them to Vandals." This
is the draft holding the Union to account, the opposite of pro-Union tilt. Good — and it
avoids the inverse distortion (excusing Federal destruction). The "$170,000 raised to
rebuild" detail is [VERIFY] but framing-neutral.

### G. Human reality — **FAIR (neither sanitized nor gratuitous)**
The frozen-field night is handled with restraint and honesty: wounded crying for water,
dead stripped of boots/uniforms by Confederate soldiers ("the un-romantic texture of the
slaughter, men robbing the dead for boots while the wounded begged for water"). This is
the correct register — it does not sanitize Confederate behavior (the stripping of the dead
is named, not hidden), and it is not gore-for-gore's-sake. The ~918 stripped dead count is
documented-feeling and purposeful. Balanced.

### H. Both-sides balance — **FAIR, no false equivalence**
The draft repeatedly refuses false balance: §1 and §5 explicitly contrast what each side
"stood for" (Union → destruction of slavery; Confederacy → defending slavery). It credits
Confederate tactical skill and Union folly honestly (Burnside's "whole idea" indicted,
Franklin's failure to reinforce indicted) WITHOUT moral both-sidesing. Tactical credit ≠
moral credit, and the draft keeps that line clean.

### I. Presentism / euphemism check — **FAIR**
No euphemism for slavery ("the institution Lee's army existed to defend," "slaveholding
Confederacy," "abolition of slavery" — all plain). No presentist editorializing beyond the
warranted moral framing, which is grounded in the contemporary fact of the Proclamation.
The "courage of the men behind the wall was real and the position was magnificent, but it
is worth being plain about what each side was standing for" sentence is the right move —
it concedes the soldiers' courage (accurate, humane) without laundering the cause.

---

## 3. VERDICT

**Overall framing verdict: FAIR.**

This draft is unusually well-armored against Lost Cause distortion. The two highest-risk
romanticization vectors the directive named — the Marye's Heights pathos and the Kirkland
legend — are both handled correctly: the slaughter pathos points at the victims and indicts
the *idea* of the assault rather than ennobling the defenders, and Kirkland is explicitly
framed as a contested 1880 account, not fact. The two most Lost-Cause-prone Lee quotes
("glorious courage," "war is so terrible") are both deliberately re-grounded in "a
slaveholding republic's army." Slavery is named as the cause in the section's own prose in
three separate places, and the Emancipation hook is the framing spine. No soft-pedaling, no
false both-sides balance, no euphemism, no Confederate ennoblement of the *cause*, human
reality neither sanitized nor gratuitous.

### MUST-FIX (ship-blocking)
**None.** The slavery-named gate passes and no framing distortion rises to ship-blocking.

### SHOULD-FIX (non-blocking, framing-only)
1. **(minor) §3 Pelham beat — watch, don't necessarily cut.** Lee's "glorious courage in
   one so young" is the one spot where individual-valor admiration runs longest before the
   counter-weight lands. It is FAIR as written, but if the fact-checker independently flags
   the quote's wording/provenance, drop the quote rather than keep it — the Pelham stand
   reads fine without Lee's editorial blessing on it. Purely defensive; no action needed if
   the quote survives fact-check.
2. **(very minor) §5 "the position was magnificent."** "Magnificent" is the most laudatory
   adjective applied to anything Confederate in the draft. It is immediately balanced ("but
   it is worth being plain about what each side was standing for"), so it stays FAIR. If a
   lighter word is wanted ("formidable" / "deadly"), it would remove the last sliver of
   romance, but this is optional polish, not a distortion.

No framing changes are required to ship. Items above are optional hardening.
