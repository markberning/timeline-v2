# FRAMING CRITIC — Battle of Quebec (1775) draft

Critic lane: fairness, emphasis, interpretive honesty.
Draft: `audits/war-pipeline/rev-quebec-draft.md` · Pack: `audits/war-pipeline/rev-quebec-factpack.md`

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

No blockers. The draft's framing discipline is strong across all six judged axes: the defeat
is told as a defeat, Carleton's generalship gets the same respect as American daring, the
Canadians are actors (on both sides) rather than scenery, the smallpox-warfare allegation is
absent, and every tradition-grade story carries its evidence badge in the prose. Two
should-fixes (one unbadged caption surface, one over-scoped near-success claim) and a few
nits.

## BLOCKER

None.

## SHOULD-FIX

1. **Battle-card caption is the one Trumbull-image surface without the imagined-scene caveat.**
   Image #2 (the card): "Clemens's 1798 engraving after Trumbull's painting of Montgomery's
   death." The hero caption carries the full caveat ("Trumbull was not there; he staged the
   scene deliberately as a martyrdom") and the Section 3 inline carries the phenomenon framing
   ("as the early republic chose to remember it"). The card carries neither, and on the battles
   lane the card is the FIRST surface a reader meets — they may tap through without ever
   reading the hero caption. A caption is a gated surface; "after Trumbull's painting" implies
   derivative art but does not say the scene is invented. The pack (§8.1) requires the
   later-imagining caveat on the Trumbull image, and the card reproduces that exact scene.
   FIX (one clause): "Clemens's 1798 engraving after John Trumbull's imagined scene of
   Montgomery's death, painted eleven years after the battle." Same fix wherever the card
   caption is reused.

2. **"It came closer to working than the odds said it should" over-scopes the pack's
   near-success claim.** Section 1: "It was a calculation, made by capable men who were out of
   alternatives, and it came closer to working than the odds said it should." The pack backs
   near-success only at one end: uncertainty #8 says "It nearly worked at the Sault-au-Matelot
   end." The draft's own Section 2 shows the southern half of the plan dead in minutes and the
   feints ignored — so the global "the assault came close" slightly inflates American
   performance in a battle the pack calls a decisive defeat. This is exactly the kind of
   gentle spin the defeat-honesty bar exists for.
   FIX: scope the clause — "...and at the Sault-au-Matelot end it came closer to working than
   the odds said it should." (Or move the near-success observation into Section 2 where the
   stall-and-trap sequence earns it.)

## NIT

1. **Meta-narration sentence arguing with historiography.** Section 1: "The assault on Quebec
   sometimes gets told as a doomed romantic gesture, and it was not." The framing work it does
   is correct and pack-mandated (no doom-music, uncertainty #8), but it does that work by
   narrating ABOUT the story instead of telling it — the corpus voice rule
   (`memory/feedback_no_meta_narration`) territory. The next sentence ("It was a calculation,
   made by capable men who were out of alternatives") already carries the whole load.
   FIX: cut the "sometimes gets told" clause; open the beat at "It was a calculation..."

2. **"the cause's first martyred general" (Montgomery dossier bio).** Pack-sanctioned phrase
   (§3 bio uses it verbatim), and "the cause's" does attribute the martyrdom to the
   Revolution rather than the draft. Fine to keep; if revising the bio anyway, "the cause's
   first martyr" reads even more clearly as reported phenomenon. No change required.

3. **"first national memorial the United States ever commissioned" appears twice in Section 3**
   (the prose paragraph and the FIG caption directly below it). Not a framing error — both
   are pack-HIGH — but the repetition reads like the draft pressing the martyr-cult
   significance. Trim one (keep the prose, lighten the caption).

## WHAT THE DRAFT GETS RIGHT (for the reviser — do not regress these)

- **Defeat honesty.** "Decisive British victory · the first major American defeat of the war"
  in the verdict; "collapsed in minutes at one end and in a trap at the other"; the spring
  retreat told as "something close to a rout"; "Canada stayed British, the fourteenth colony
  never materialized." No spin anywhere on the outcome.
- **The fourteenth colony as American assumption, not destiny denied.** "surely, the thinking
  ran, its people would rise" is attributed to the 1775 American mind, and Section 3 answers
  it with Canadian agency: "The city was substantially defended by Canadians, and that fact is
  itself the answer to the question the invasion had been sent to ask." That sentence is the
  framing centerpiece and it is exactly right — a Canadian reader gets Canada staying British
  as a choice grounded in interest, not a tragedy.
- **Both-sides honesty.** Carleton gets "his plan was simple and correct," "Patience was the
  generalship," the Montcalm-lesson MEANWHILE voiced as interpretation per the pack, and the
  honorable-burial beat said straight ("a genuinely respectful gesture toward a fallen enemy,"
  pack-mandated). The American side's desperation is told as the enlistment-cliff arithmetic
  ("Attack before New Year's, or watch the army dissolve"), not romance.
- **Canadians on BOTH sides, named.** Chabot and Picard command the picket that kills
  Montgomery; Voyer's militia holds the second barricade; Livingston's 200 Canadian volunteers
  attack with the Americans; the habitants paragraph lands on "It was interest, fairly
  reckoned on all sides, and it held." Nobody is scenery.
- **Smallpox.** The Carleton allegation is wholly absent (uncertainty #7 honored); disease
  carries its real weight ("broke the American army faster than the British ever did,"
  "broken by smallpox and silver, not bayonets").
- **Every legend badged in the prose, none resolved by fiat.** "Push on, brave boys" =
  "Tradition fills in... the words come only from later accounts"; McQuarters = "Tradition
  likewise names... the record only says the defenders fired"; Morgan's lashes = his own tale,
  "No record of the sentence survives; Morgan showed the scars." Hero caption carries the
  staged-martyrdom caveat; Carleton portrait captioned "traditionally identified as."
- **Superlatives all pack-backed.** "American Hannibal" framed as period press (MED, framed as
  required); "one of the most admired soldiers in America," "conducted the defense exactly
  right," "he beat the invasion with patience," "the single best period document" — all
  verbatim pack-sanctioned. Carleton's inflated dispatch tally framed as "his own reckoning...
  ran somewhat higher than the settled count." Martyrdom cult reported as phenomenon ("The
  martyr-image did real work"), never adopted as the draft's voice.
