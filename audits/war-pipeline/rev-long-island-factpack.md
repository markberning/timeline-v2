# FACT PACK — Battle of Long Island (Battle of Brooklyn), August 27, 1776

*American Revolution. ONLY source for the battle page. The first major battle after independence was declared, the largest battle of the war by troops engaged, a heavy American defeat — redeemed by the night evacuation across the East River. Use ranges; American prisoner figures vary widely. Sources verified 2026-06-10: Wikipedia (battle + campaign articles, cross-checked), American Battlefield Trust, NPS ("The Maryland 400"), Founders Online, Maryland State Archives "Finding the Maryland 400" project, JAR/allthingsliberty, Commons API (every image checked live).*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- [HIGH] Main battle: **August 27, 1776**, in Brooklyn (then rural Kings County on the western end of Long Island), New York. Wider operation: Aug 22 (Gravesend Bay landing) → Aug 29–30 (night evacuation to Manhattan). Also called the **Battle of Brooklyn** / Battle of Brooklyn Heights — modern Brooklyn covers the whole battlefield.
- [HIGH] The FIRST major battle fought after the Declaration of Independence (July 4, 1776), and the LARGEST battle of the entire Revolutionary War by total troops engaged and deployed in the campaign.
- [HANDLE-WITH-CARE] "Largest battle of the war" is true by total forces in the theatre (~30,000 on the field, ~40,000+ in the campaign); do NOT inflate to "largest battle in American history to that point" without the "of the war" scope.
- [HIGH] Result: decisive British victory. Clinton's night flanking march through the unguarded Jamaica Pass rolled up the American forward line on the Heights of Guan; ~1,000+ Americans captured including two generals — but Howe halted before the Brooklyn fortifications, and Washington's army escaped across the East River on the night of Aug 29–30, preserving the army (often called "America's Dunkirk" in modern writing — a modern label, not period).

## 2. BACKGROUND — why New York
- [HIGH] Boston fell March 17, 1776: Washington's artillery on Dorchester Heights (Ticonderoga guns) made the town untenable; Howe evacuated to Halifax, Nova Scotia, to refit — not home. Both sides knew the next move was New York.
- [HIGH] Why New York: (1) a deep, sheltered harbor — an ideal Royal Navy base; (2) the Hudson River corridor — control it (with a second force coming down from Canada) and you sever New England, the rebellion's engine, from the colonies to the south; (3) a population believed far more Loyalist than New England's. Strategy endorsed in London (Germain).
- [HIGH] Washington anticipated this and moved the Continental Army from Boston to New York in spring 1776 (Washington arrived April 13). Charles Lee had begun the defenses earlier in 1776, declaring the city indefensible against naval power but worth making costly. The Americans fortified Manhattan AND Brooklyn Heights — the high ground commanding New York City across the East River (the Bunker Hill lesson: whoever holds the heights commands the town).
- [HIGH] The British buildup, June–August 1776: Gen. William Howe arrived off Sandy Hook June 25/29 and landed unopposed on **Staten Island July 2–3** (the same days Congress voted and declared independence) with ~9,000+ troops and ~130 ships. His brother **Vice Adm. Lord Richard Howe** arrived July 12 with ~150 more ships. Through July–August: Clinton's force back from the failed Charleston attack, plus hired German ("Hessian") regiments.
- [HIGH, ranges] The armada's scale by mid-August: **~32,000 troops** (including ~8,000–9,000 Hessians) plus **~10,000 sailors**, on a fleet of **~400 ships** — commonly itemized as ~30 dedicated warships/men-of-war plus ~300–400 transports; some counts give "73 warships" by counting all armed vessels. **The largest expeditionary force Great Britain had ever sent overseas to that point**, and the largest fleet ever seen in American waters. [HANDLE-WITH-CARE: keep ship counts as "~400 ships, several dozen of them warships"; the 30 vs 73 warship split is a counting-method artifact. The superlative "largest of the eighteenth century" appears in good sources (McCullough et al.) but phrase as "largest Britain had ever sent abroad."]
- [HIGH] Private Daniel McCurtin, a Maryland rifleman watching from Manhattan as the first fleet came in (June 29), wrote that the bay looked like "a wood of pine trees trimmed" and "I thought all London was afloat." (See Quotes.)
- [HIGH] The Howe brothers were ALSO peace commissioners, empowered to issue pardons. July overtures failed partly on protocol — Washington's staff refused letters addressed "George Washington, Esq., etc., etc." that denied his rank. The Declaration was read to the army in New York July 9; that night crowds pulled down the gilded equestrian statue of George III on Bowling Green (famously melted into musket balls).
- [HIGH] American defensive problem: ~19,000–23,000 mostly raw Continentals and militia defending an archipelago against total naval superiority. Washington split the army between Manhattan and Brooklyn — he could not be sure where the blow would land even after the British landed on Long Island (he suspected a feint for days, holding troops in Manhattan).
- [HIGH] Command shuffle that helped doom the defense: **Nathanael Greene**, who had built and knew the Brooklyn works, fell seriously ill (a raging fever, commonly given as malaria) ~Aug 15–20 and was evacuated to Manhattan; **John Sullivan** (just back from the failed Canadian campaign) took over Aug 20; **Israel Putnam** superseded him in overall Long Island command Aug 24 — three days before the battle, on ground neither knew well. Sullivan kept command of the forward line on the ridge.

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
- **American** (~9,000–10,000 on Long Island by Aug 27; only ~3,000–4,000 on the forward ridge line): Washington commander-in-chief (crossed between Manhattan and Brooklyn; on the Brooklyn side during the battle); Putnam commanding Long Island from the Brooklyn lines; **Sullivan** the center-left on the wooded ridge (Flatbush/Battle Pass and Bedford Pass); **Lord Stirling** the right (the Gowanus Road by the shore, with Smallwood's Maryland and Haslet's Delaware regiments — the army's best-trained troops, though their colonels were absent that day at a court-martial in Manhattan; Maj. Mordecai Gist led the Marylanders). Col. Samuel Miles's Pennsylvania riflemen east of Bedford Pass. The Jamaica Pass — easternmost, 3 miles from the main line — was watched by just **five mounted militia officers**. [HIGH]
- **British/Hessian** (~20,000 landed on Long Island): Gen. **William Howe** commanding; **Henry Clinton** led the van of the flanking column (the plan was his); **Lord Cornwallis** and **Lord Percy** in the column with Howe — ~10,000 men and 14 guns on the night march. **Maj. Gen. James Grant** with ~5,000 on the British left (the shore road diversion against Stirling). Lt. Gen. **Leopold Philip von Heister** with the Hessian center (~4,000–5,000) facing Battle Pass. [HIGH]
- [HIGH] Quality gap: British regulars and professional Hessians vs. an American army largely of new levies and militia; many American units had never been under fire.

## 4. THE BATTLE
**The landing (Aug 22–26)**
- [HIGH] Aug 22: covered by warships, an advance of ~4,000 under Clinton and Cornwallis crossed the Narrows from Staten Island and landed **unopposed at Gravesend Bay** (~8:00 a.m.); by noon ~15,000 men and 40 guns were ashore. Col. Edward Hand's Pennsylvania riflemen skirmished and fell back, burning grain stores. Cornwallis pushed up to Flatbush village. Aug 25: von Heister's ~5,000 Hessians crossed, bringing the force to ~20,000.
- [HIGH] Between the landing beaches and the American works at Brooklyn ran the **Heights of Guan** (Gowanus Heights) — a wooded glacial ridge, passable for an army only at the passes: the **Gowanus Road** along the shore (west), **Flatbush Pass** (center; later "Battle Pass"), **Bedford Pass**, and the **Jamaica Pass** far to the east.

**Clinton's plan and the night march (Aug 26–27)**
- [HIGH] Clinton — who had grown up in New York (his father had been royal governor) — learned from local Loyalists that the Jamaica Pass was essentially unguarded and proposed the wide night flanking march; Howe adopted it. Grant on the shore and the Hessians at Flatbush would demonstrate against the American front while the main army walked around its left.
- [HIGH] ~9:00 p.m. Aug 26: the column of ~10,000 set out from Flatlands — Clinton's van, then Cornwallis, Howe, Percy — moving northeast by back roads, guided by Loyalist farmers. Around 2–3 a.m. they reached **Howard's Halfway House** (William Howard's tavern near the pass); Howe compelled the tavern keeper **William Howard and his son William Jr.** to guide the column by the Rockaway Foot Path around/through the Jamaica Pass. [HANDLE-WITH-CARE: the tavern story rests on the Howard family's own much-retold account (the son's recollection written down decades later, incl. Howe reportedly saying Howard "must" guide them); the core fact of coerced local guides at the pass is in standard histories — tell it as the Howards' story, not as gospel dialogue.]
- [HIGH] The five mounted militia officers watching the pass were captured without a shot (they mistook the British for Americans). The column passed the ridge by dawn and reached Bedford, behind Sullivan's line, ~8:30–9:00 a.m. Aug 27. **Two signal guns ~9:00 a.m.** told Grant and the Hessians to attack in earnest. The Americans never detected a 10,000-man army marching across their front all night. [MED on the detail that Sullivan later claimed he had personally paid the mounted patrol — appears in reputable retellings; keep light or drop.]

**The collapse of the center and left**
- [HIGH] Fighting had actually opened around midnight–1:00 a.m. on the shore road near the **Red Lion Inn**, where Grant's advance ran into American pickets. Putnam sent Stirling with ~1,600 to block the shore road; from dawn Stirling's line on the slopes (Marylanders, Delawares, others) stood up to Grant in open-field European style for hours — Grant, under orders to pin not press, obliged.
- [HIGH] At the 9:00 a.m. signal, von Heister's Hessians attacked Battle Pass frontally while British light infantry and grenadiers rolled in from Bedford — Sullivan's rear. The center broke; fighting in the woods turned hand-to-hand (clubbed muskets, bayonets); fleeing companies ran a gauntlet back to the Brooklyn lines. **Sullivan was captured** (by tradition, taken by three Hessian grenadiers in a cornfield — [MED] on the cornfield detail).
- [HIGH] Atrocity/no-quarter material: contemporary British and Hessian letters boast of bayoneting rebels who tried to surrender, and American camps filled with stories that the Hessians gave no quarter (see Quotes). Handle plainly; both panic and propaganda amplified it, but period British letters themselves supply the worst lines.

**Stirling, the Maryland 400, and the Old Stone House**
- [HIGH] By late morning Stirling was nearly surrounded: Grant in front, Hessians on his right, and Cornwallis's reserve now astride the Gowanus Road BEHIND him at the **Vechte–Cortelyou farmhouse** (today's Old Stone House replica). Stirling ordered the bulk of his force to escape across the Gowanus marsh and Brouwer's mill creek (~80 yards of water; some men drowned), while he personally led a rear guard of **~260–270 men of Smallwood's Maryland battalion** under Maj. Mordecai Gist in repeated counterattacks against Cornwallis at the house.
- [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — the "Maryland 400" label]: the name is traditional; the actual rear guard was ~260–270 men. The traditional count of "256 killed" comes from Smallwood's report of **256 killed AND missing combined** (the original casualty list is lost) — Maryland State Archives research shows many of the "killed" were actually captured. Modern best statement: the rear guard lost roughly two-thirds of its men killed, wounded, or captured; only Gist and a handful (~9–12) cut their way back to the American lines that day.
- [MED] Number of charges: accounts vary from two attacks (period-derived) up to the traditional six; say "again and again" / "repeated charges," not a precise count.
- [MED] Washington, watching from the Cobble Hill fort inside the lines, reportedly cried "Good God, what brave fellows I must this day lose!" — contemporary but anonymous and secondhand (see Quotes). Tag as reported, never as verbatim Washington.
- [HIGH] Stirling, refusing to surrender to the British, broke through and surrendered his sword to the Hessian commander von Heister. (His own Aug 29 letter to Washington from captivity, on Founders Online, narrates the fight.)
- [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — the mass grave]: the tradition that the Maryland dead lie in a mass grave on Third Avenue between 7th and 8th Streets traces only to an 1867/1869 local-tradition claim (Thomas Field); a 1957 survey and a 2017 archaeological dig found **no human remains**. Period accounts suggest many dead were barely buried where they fell, with bodies still visible in the fields months later. The burial site is genuinely unknown — say so.
- [HIGH] Thomas W. Field (1869 historian, NOT a participant and NOT Cadwalader) called the stand "an hour more precious to liberty than any other in history" (wording varies slightly across quotations of Field — verify against the 1869 text before quoting verbatim; the attribution to Field is solid).

**Howe halts; two days in the trapped camp**
- [HIGH] By early–mid afternoon the survivors were inside the Brooklyn lines with their backs to the East River. Clinton and Cornwallis wanted to storm the works at once; **Howe halted** and began formal siege approaches. His stated reasons: the lines were strong (months in the building, unlike Bunker Hill's overnight redoubt), his troops had marched and fought all night and day, and the lines "must fall" cheaply by regular approaches. Criticized by subordinates and in Parliament ever after as the war's great lost chance. The shadow of Bunker Hill on Howe is standard historiography [HIGH as historians' interpretation — frame as such].
- [HIGH] Aug 28–29: a cold nor'easter — driving rain, flooded trenches, men without tents or dry powder; British saps creeping closer (within ~600 yards); the Royal Navy kept out of the East River by the same northeast wind. Washington fed another ~1,200 men over from Manhattan (Aug 28), then grasped the trap: if the wind shifted, British frigates in the East River would seal ~9,500 men in Brooklyn and the war could end there.

**The night evacuation (Aug 29–30)**
- [HIGH] Aug 29, ~4:00 p.m.: council of war voted unanimously to withdraw to Manhattan. Every usable boat on the East River and Hudson was collected by order. Troops were told they were being relieved at night, not evacuated — the ruse held.
- [HIGH] From ~9–11 p.m., regiments slipped by detachments down to the **Brooklyn ferry landing**, wagon wheels muffled, all talk forbidden. The boats were manned by **Col. John Glover's 14th Continental Regiment (the Marblehead, Mass. mariners)** with Hutchinson's 27th Massachusetts (Salem fishermen) — sailors rowing a whole army, with gear, horses, and guns, across roughly a mile of tidal river, repeatedly, in the dark. Adverse wind early in the night gave way around 11 p.m.; ebb-tide and rain covered the noise.
- [HIGH] Toward dawn, with much of the rear guard still in the lines, a **dense fog settled over Brooklyn** and lingered past sunrise, hiding the last crossings; Benjamin Tallmadge (then a young officer in the rear guard, later Washington's spymaster) left the classic eyewitness description (see Quotes). One near-disaster: a garbled order pulled Mifflin's rear guard out of the lines hours early; they were turned around and the gap closed before the British noticed [HIGH — in Tallmadge and standard accounts].
- [HIGH] By ~7:00 a.m. Aug 30, ~9,000–9,500 men with their field artillery, horses, provisions, and equipment were in Manhattan. Tradition holds Washington crossed in one of the last boats [MED for "the very last boat" — say "among the last"]. A few heavy cannon mired in the mud were abandoned.
- [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — "without losing a single life"]: true of the evacuation itself in all standard accounts (no one was killed in the crossing); some accounts note 3–4 stragglers who stayed behind (to plunder, by tradition) were captured. Phrase: "without the loss of a single life — the British woke on the 30th to empty trenches."

## 5. CASUALTIES (ranges — prisoner figures vary widely)
- [HIGH] **British/Hessian: ~370–400 total.** British ~61–64 killed, ~290–293 wounded, ~30 missing; Hessians 5 killed, 26 wounded. (ABT rounds to 388.)
- [DISPUTED — document the range] **American: ~1,000–2,000 total, most of them prisoners.** Killed ~200–300; wounded ~650–800 (counts overlap with the captured); **captured ~870–1,100+** including Generals Sullivan and Stirling (and Brig. Gen. Nathaniel Woodhull of the NY militia, taken separately Aug 28 east of the lines). Howe's official claim of ~3,300 total American losses is inflated; American returns were chaotic because so many missing later straggled in. Safe dossier line: "~300 killed; ~1,000 or more captured" with the wounded folded into a ~2,000 total-casualties ceiling.
- [HIGH] Fate of the prisoners: the Long Island captives became the first large cohort of the New York prison-ship and sugar-house system; a large share died of disease and starvation in captivity (the ~11,500 prison-ship dead figure often cited is for the WHOLE WAR at Wallabout Bay — never attach it to this one battle).

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- [HIGH] Morale crisis: militia deserted in droves. Washington to Hancock, Sept 2, 1776: "Our situation is truly distressing..." (see Quotes — fully verified, Founders Online).
- [HIGH] **The Staten Island Peace Conference, Sept 11, 1776**: the Howes paroled the captured Sullivan to Philadelphia with an invitation to talk; John Adams privately called Sullivan a "decoy duck" (see Quotes). Adams, Franklin, and Edward Rutledge met Lord Howe at the Billopp (Conference) House on Staten Island; three hours, no common ground — Howe could not treat with them as representatives of independent states, and they would not renounce independence. The war went on.
- [HIGH] The dominoes of 1776: Kip's Bay landing Sept 15 and the British occupation of New York City (held SEVEN YEARS, until Evacuation Day, Nov 25, 1783); Harlem Heights Sept 16; the Great Fire Sept 21; White Plains Oct 28; the catastrophic surrender of Fort Washington Nov 16 (~2,800 more captured); the retreat through New Jersey — and then Trenton, Dec 26. Long Island opened the darkest stretch of the war for the Revolution; the army's survival on Aug 29–30 is why there was a war left to turn around.
- [HIGH] Stirling was exchanged within weeks (for royal governor Browne of Montfort) and fought on until his death in service in 1783; Sullivan was exchanged and served through the war; Greene's illness spared him association with the defeat; Putnam's field reputation never recovered (interpretive — frame lightly).

## 7. LOYALIST PERSPECTIVE / LONG ISLAND UNDER OCCUPATION
- [HIGH] Western Long Island was fertile ground for the Crown: Kings County's old Dutch farming towns and Queens County were notably Loyalist-leaning (Queens had voted overwhelmingly against sending deputies to the provincial congress in 1775); local Loyalist intelligence handed Clinton the Jamaica Pass, and Loyalist farmers guided the night column. [Frame: this was also a civil war — neighbors guided the army that morning.]
- [HIGH] After the battle, Long Island and New York City became THE Loyalist haven of the continent — thousands of displaced Loyalists flooded in, and the city stayed British to 1783. **Oliver DeLancey** was commissioned brigadier and raised DeLancey's Brigade (three battalions of ~500), recruited heavily from Long Island Loyalists.
- [HIGH] The other side of the occupation: seven years of requisitions, billeting, and woodcutting fell on Long Island farms (Loyalist and Whig alike); many eastern (Suffolk) Patriots fled across the Sound to Connecticut, and the Sound became a raiding zone (whaleboat warfare); Setauket later hosted Washington's Culper spy ring.
- [HANDLE-WITH-CARE] **Nathaniel Woodhull**: the NY militia brigadier (and president of the NY Provincial Congress) was captured Aug 28 near Jamaica while rounding up cattle, was badly cut up by his captors, and died of his wounds Sept 20. The famous story that he was struck down for answering "God save us all" when ordered to say "God save the King" is legend, first recorded much later — tell it only AS legend if at all.
- [NOTE] No significant Native American combat role at this battle (unlike the F&I packs) — do not force one. The Loyalist/civil-war thread is this page's "other side."

## 9. COMMANDER BIO MATERIAL (dossier cards — 60–90 words each; portraits verified live on Commons API 2026-06-10)
**AMERICAN**
- **George Washington** (1732–1799), commander-in-chief. The Virginia planter and French & Indian War veteran had just driven the British from Boston without a major battle; Long Island was his first full-scale battlefield test commanding the army — and he was outgeneraled, caught with his force split and his left flank open. He redeemed the defeat with the decision his reputation rests on as much as any victory: the silent night evacuation of 9,000 men that saved the army, and the Revolution, to fight on. Portrait: **File:George Washington by Peale 1776.jpg** (Charles Willson Peale, painted 1776 — same year; PD).
- **Israel Putnam** (1718–1790), Long Island commander. "Old Put," the Connecticut farmer whose French & Indian War exploits (and Bunker Hill fame) made him a folk hero, took over the Long Island defenses on August 24 — three days before the battle, on ground he barely knew. The forward line was his to watch; the Jamaica Pass, his open back door, was covered by five mounted officers. His field reputation never fully recovered. Portrait: **File:Israel putnam portrait.jpg** (Fabronius lithograph, 1864; PD) — [FLAG: a 19th-century print, not a life portrait; caption as such].
- **William Alexander, Lord Stirling** (1726–1783), commanded the American right. A wealthy New Jersey gentleman who claimed a Scottish earldom (the House of Lords refused it; the Americans used the title anyway), Stirling stood up to Grant's diversion all morning in parade-ground style, then led ~270 Marylanders in repeated charges against Cornwallis to cover his trapped command's escape. Refusing to hand his sword to the British, he surrendered to the Hessian von Heister. Exchanged, he served until his death in 1783. Portrait: **NONE authentic** — no life portrait of Stirling is known; Commons holds only posthumous engravings (e.g., **File:William Alexander, Lord Stirling.png**, Harper & Brothers, 1905, PD) — if used, caption as a later imagined engraving.
- **John Sullivan** (1740–1795), commanded the forward center. A New Hampshire lawyer and son of Irish immigrants, just back from the collapse of the American invasion of Canada, Sullivan held the wooded ridge at Battle Pass — until 10,000 British troops appeared behind him. Captured in the rout, he was paroled to Philadelphia carrying Lord Howe's peace feeler; John Adams sneered that he was a "decoy duck." Exchanged that fall, he fought on through the war and later governed New Hampshire. Portrait: **File:Major General John Sullivan MET MM2147.jpg** (Thomas Hart print, London, 1776; Met, CC0) — [FLAG: from the 1776 London print series of largely SPECULATIVE likenesses of American generals; caption as a period print, not a true likeness].

**BRITISH**
- **Gen. William Howe** (1729–1814), British commander-in-chief in America. A combat legend since Quebec 1759 — he led the climb to the Plains of Abraham — and the man who paid for Bunker Hill's frontal assaults, Howe executed the war's most elegant flanking victory at Brooklyn, then halted before the American works rather than storm them, choosing siege spades over bayonets. With his brother Admiral Lord Richard Howe he was also a peace commissioner — conqueror and negotiator in one. Critics never forgave the halt. Portrait: **File:WilliamHowe1777ColorMezzotint.jpeg** (Corbutt/Purcell mezzotint, 1777; PD) — [FLAG: from the same speculative London print series; it is nonetheless the standard Howe image — caption as a period mezzotint].
- **Gen. Henry Clinton** (1730–1795), author of the flanking plan. Raised partly in New York, where his father had been royal governor, Clinton knew the ground and pressed the idea Howe adopted: a night march around the unguarded Jamaica Pass. He led the van himself. Prickly, brilliant on paper, and forever at odds with Howe, he would succeed him as commander-in-chief in 1778 — and preside over the war's loss. Portrait: **File:SirHenryClinton.jpg** (John Smart miniature, 1777; PD — authentic likeness).
- **Lt. Gen. Charles, Lord Cornwallis** (1738–1805), led the reserve of the flanking column. An aristocrat who had voted against taxing America in the House of Lords, then crossed the Atlantic to fight her, Cornwallis seized the Gowanus Road at the Old Stone House and stood at the point where the Maryland charges broke. He urged storming the Brooklyn lines; Howe refused. Five years later he handed his army over at Yorktown — then rebuilt his career governing India. Portrait: **File:Lord Cornwallis.jpg** (Thomas Gainsborough, 1783; PD — authentic).
- **Maj. Gen. James Grant** (1720–1806), commanded the diversion on the British left. A Scottish veteran captured at Fort Duquesne in 1758 and later governor of East Florida, Grant told the House of Commons in 1775 that with 5,000 regulars he could march from one end of America to the other. At Brooklyn he led almost exactly 5,000 — under orders only to pin Stirling's line, which he did all morning while the real blow landed behind it. Americans loathed him; his name made the fight personal. Portrait: **File:Major Gen. James Grant, Colonel of the 55th Foot, 1720-1806 (NYPL Hades-254503-429729).jpg** (NYPL period print; PD). [Avoid File:James-grant-1720-1806.jpg — it is a John Kay CARICATURE, 1798.]

## 10. DOSSIER STATS
- **Date/duration**: main battle, dawn–early afternoon, Aug 27, 1776 (first shots near the Red Lion Inn ~1 a.m.; signal guns ~9 a.m.; Stirling's surrender early afternoon). Full operation Aug 22–30 (landing → evacuation).
- **Forces**: British/Hessian ~20,000 landed on Long Island (of ~32,000 in the theatre); American ~9,000–10,000 on Long Island, only ~3,000–4,000 in the forward fighting. [HIGH, as ranges]
- **Casualties**: British/Hessian ~370–400 (~63–69 killed). American ~1,000–2,000 total: ~200–300 killed, ~650–800 wounded, **~1,000–1,100 captured** (incl. Generals Sullivan and Stirling; Woodhull taken Aug 28). [Captured figure DISPUTED — always a range.]
- **Winner line**: Decisive British victory — the war's biggest battle, won by a night march around an unguarded pass; the beaten army escaped by water two nights later without losing a man in the crossing.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES (the author MUST keep these soft)
1. **American prisoners**: ~870–1,100+; Howe's 3,300 total-loss claim inflated; returns chaotic. Always a range.
2. **Maryland 400**: rear guard was ~260–270, not 400; "256 killed" actually = Smallwood's killed-AND-missing combined (list lost); many later proved captured. Charges counted anywhere from 2 to 6 — say "repeated."
3. **The mass grave**: Third Avenue site is 1860s local tradition; 1957 survey and 2017 dig found nothing; burial site unknown.
4. **Washington's "brave fellows" line**: contemporary but anonymous/secondhand (Force's *American Archives*, letter of Sept 1, 1776) — reported speech only.
5. **Howard's tavern story**: core (coerced local guides) solid; dialogue and details are family tradition recorded decades later.
6. **Fleet/army superlatives**: ~400 ships / ~32,000 troops / ~10,000 sailors solid; warship subcount varies (30 vs 73) by definition; "largest expeditionary force Britain had ever sent abroad" is the safe superlative.
7. **"Not a single life lost" in the evacuation**: standard and essentially true of the crossing; a handful of stragglers captured. Washington "in the last boat" is tradition — "among the last."
8. **Sullivan's capture** (cornfield, three grenadiers) — traditional details; his capture itself is certain. His claim that the five Jamaica Pass officers were a patrol he had personally arranged/paid — his own later self-defense, treat skeptically.
9. **Why Howe halted**: his stated reasons are on record; "Bunker Hill scarred him" is historians' interpretation — frame as such.
10. **Woodhull's "God save us all"** — legend, first recorded much later.
11. **Field's "hour more precious" line**: attribution (Thomas W. Field, 1869) solid; exact wording varies across quotations — check the 1869 text before quoting verbatim; never attribute to Cadwalader or any participant.
12. **D'Andrea painting license**: Commons-tagged PD via the National Guard, but contractor work-for-hire status undocumented — prefer 18th–19th-c. art for prime slots.
