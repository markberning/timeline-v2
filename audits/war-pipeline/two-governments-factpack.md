### (A) THE CORNERSTONE SPEECH
**Alexander H. Stephens, Confederate Vice President**  
**Delivered:** Athenaeum, Savannah, Georgia, March 21, 1861  
**Recorded:** Published in the *Southern Confederacy* (Atlanta), March 25, 1861  
**Source:** American Battlefield Trust (battlefields.org); Wikipedia "Cornerstone Speech" article; multiple academic reprints

#### Transcription note
The speech was extemporaneous (not prepared text). Stephens later claimed reporters' notes were "very imperfect," but historians and scholars consider his post-war disclaimers implausible given the consistency of multiple contemporary records. The text below is the accepted scholarly version.

#### Key Verbatim Passages

**On slavery as the immediate cause of secession:**
> "The new Constitution has put at rest forever all the agitating questions relating to our peculiar institution—African slavery as it exists among us—the proper status of the negro in our form of civilization. **This was the immediate cause of the late rupture and present revolution.**"

**On the Founding Fathers' error:**
> "Those ideas, however, were fundamentally wrong. They rested upon the assumption of the equality of races. This was an error."

**THE CORNERSTONE PASSAGE (the central verbatim anchor):**
> "Our new government is founded upon exactly the opposite idea; its foundations are laid, its corner-stone rests, upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery subordination to the superior race is his natural and normal condition. This, our new government, is the first, in the history of the world, based upon this great physical, philosophical, and moral truth."

**On biblical imagery of the cornerstone:**
> "Our confederacy is founded upon principles in strict conformity with these laws. This stone which was rejected by the first builders 'is become the chief of the corner'—the real 'corner-stone'—in our new edifice."

**On the Confederacy's claim to natural law:**
> "It is the first government ever instituted upon the principles in strict conformity to nature, and the ordination of Providence, in furnishing the materials of human society."

#### Punctuation note
Some sources render the cornerstone passage as "slavery, subordination to the superior race" (with comma); others as "slavery subordination to the superior race" (without). The original newspaper printing (Southern Confederacy, March 25, 1861) is the closest primary source; most scholarly editions include the comma. The author should use the comma version as it is more commonly accepted: "slavery, subordination to the superior race, is his natural and normal condition."

### (B) THE CONFEDERATE CONSTITUTION
**Adopted by the Confederate Provisional Congress:** March 11, 1861, Montgomery, Alabama  
**Source:** Avalon Project, Yale Law School (avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/csa_csa.asp)

The Confederate Constitution used the word "slaves" and "negro slaves" explicitly throughout — a deliberate departure from the U.S. Constitution, which euphemized enslaved people as "persons held to service or labor" and never used the word "slavery."

#### Slavery clauses verbatim, by article and section:

**Article I, Section 2(3) — representation:**
> "...three-fifths of all slaves..." counted for apportionment of representatives and direct taxes.
> *(Note: mirrors the U.S. Constitution's Three-Fifths Clause but uses the word "slaves" openly.)*

**Article I, Section 9(1) — slave trade:**
> "The importation of negroes of the African race from any foreign country other than the slaveholding States or Territories of the United States of America, is hereby forbidden..."
> *(Confederate Constitution banned the international slave trade — to protect the value of existing enslaved people — while protecting the domestic trade explicitly.)*

**Article I, Section 9(4) — the critical property clause:**
> "No bill of attainder, ex post facto law, or law denying or impairing **the right of property in negro slaves** shall be passed."
> *(This clause appears nowhere in the U.S. Constitution. It made the protection of slavery a permanent, unamendable constitutional guarantee.)*

**Article IV, Section 2(1) — right of transit with enslaved people:**
> "The citizens of each State shall be entitled to all the privileges and immunities of citizens in the several States; and shall have the right of transit and sojourn in any State of this Confederacy, with their slaves and other property; and **the right of property in said slaves shall not be thereby impaired**."

**Article IV, Section 2(3) — fugitive slave clause:**
> "No slave or other person held to service or labor in any State or Territory of the Confederate States, under the laws thereof, escaping or lawfully carried into another, shall, in consequence of any law or regulation therein, be discharged from such service or labor; but shall be delivered up on claim of the party to whom such slave belongs..."

**Article IV, Section 3(3) — slavery in territories (the key expansion clause):**
> "In all such territory the institution of negro slavery, as it now exists in the Confederate States, shall be recognized and protected be Congress and by the Territorial government; and the inhabitants of the several Confederate States and Territories shall have the right to take to such Territory any slaves lawfully held by them in any of the States or Territories of the Confederate States."
> *(This was the central political dispute of the 1850s — whether slavery could expand into new territories. The Confederate Constitution answered it permanently: yes, always, forever, protected by law.)*

### (C) SECESSION DECLARATIONS — STATES IN THEIR OWN WORDS

All sourced from the Avalon Project, Yale Law School, unless otherwise noted.

#### South Carolina — December 24, 1860
**Source:** avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/csa_scarsec.asp

Key verbatim passages:

**On slavery as a constitutional right:**
> "The right of property in slaves was recognized by giving to free persons distinct political rights, by giving them the right to represent, and burthening them with direct taxes for three-fifths of their slaves; by authorizing the importation of slaves for twenty years; and by stipulating for the rendition of fugitives from labor."

**On Northern states' violations — the central grievance:**
> "fourteen of the States have deliberately refused, for years past, to fulfill their constitutional obligations... [and] have enacted laws which either nullify the Acts of Congress or render useless any attempt to execute them."

**On slave insurrection being fomented:**
> "They have encouraged and assisted thousands of our slaves to leave their homes; and those who remain, have been incited by emissaries, books and pictures to servile insurrection."

**On Lincoln's election as the breaking point:**
South Carolina cited Lincoln's stated belief that "Government cannot endure permanently half slave, half free" as the proximate cause, arguing his election meant the federal government would be wielded against slavery.

---

#### Mississippi — January 9, 1861
**Source:** avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/csa_missec.asp

**The famous opening — verbatim:**
> "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery—the greatest material interest of the world."

**Continuation (verbatim):**
> "Its labor supplies the product which constitutes by far the largest and most important portions of commerce of the earth. These products are peculiar to the climate verging on the tropical regions, and by an imperious law of nature, none but the black race can bear exposure to the tropical sun."

**On the forced choice:**
> "There was no choice left us but submission to the mandates of abolition, or a dissolution of the Union."

**On economic stakes:**
> The declaration warns of "loss of property worth four billions of money" — referring to the value of enslaved people as property — if abolition succeeded.

---

#### Georgia — January 19, 1861
**Source:** avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/csa_geosec.asp

**On the central complaint:**
> "For the last ten years we have had numerous and serious causes of complaint against our non-slave-holding confederate States with reference to the subject of African slavery."

**On the Republican Party's purpose:**
> "It is admitted to be an anti-slavery party... By anti-slavery it is made a power in the state."

**On the territorial question as the key:**
> "The prohibition of slavery in the Territories is the cardinal principle of this organization."

**On the existential framing:**
> "Because their avowed purpose is to subvert our society and subject us not only to the loss of our property but the destruction of ourselves, our wives, and our children."

---

#### Texas — February 1, 1861
**Source:** avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/csa_texsec.asp

**On Texas's founding identity:**
> "She was received as a commonwealth holding, maintaining and protecting the institution known as negro slavery—the servitude of the African to the white race within her limits."

**On racial hierarchy as settled truth:**
> "...the African race had no agency in their establishment; that they were rightfully held and regarded as an inferior and dependent race, and in that condition only could their existence in this form be tolerable or advantageous."

**On what the North demanded:**
> "They demand the abolition of negro slavery throughout the confederacy, the recognition of political equality between the white and negro races."

## BEAT 1: THE CONFEDERATE GOVERNMENT FORMS IN MONTGOMERY

- **February 4, 1861:** Delegates from six Deep South states (South Carolina, Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana) convene in Montgomery, Alabama to form a provisional government. Texas, which seceded February 1, sends delegates shortly after.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Provisional Congress of the Confederate States"; NPS timeline

- **February 8, 1861:** Provisional Constitution of the Confederate States adopted.
  - Source: Zinn Education Project; Provisional Congress Wikipedia

- **February 9, 1861:** Jefferson Davis of Mississippi unanimously elected provisional president by the delegates. Alexander H. Stephens of Georgia elected provisional vice president.
  - Source: multiple (EBSCO Research Starters; Wikipedia; NPS)

- **February 11, 1861:** Stephens takes oath of office as VP (his 49th birthday).
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Alexander H. Stephens"

- **February 18, 1861:** Jefferson Davis inaugurated as provisional president at the Alabama State Capitol, Montgomery, at 1:00 p.m.
  - Source: Encyclopedia of Alabama; EBSCO Research Starters; multiple

- **March 11, 1861:** Permanent Confederate Constitution adopted. (See framing section above for slavery clauses.)

- **Capital move:** On May 8, 1861, the Confederate Congress voted to relocate the capital to Richmond, Virginia. Davis and the executive branch moved by train on May 27, 1861. The move reflected Virginia's strategic and industrial importance — Virginia's industrial output nearly equaled that of all other Confederate states combined — and the desire to bind the crucial upper South to the new nation.
  - Source: Richmond in the American Civil War, Wikipedia; Washington Post "House Divided" blog

## BEAT 2: JEFFERSON DAVIS — BIOGRAPHY AND CONTRAST WITH LINCOLN

**Full name:** Jefferson Finis Davis  
**Born:** June 3, 1808, Fairview, Kentucky  
**Died:** December 6, 1889, New Orleans, Louisiana  
**Source:** Wikipedia, "Jefferson Davis"; American Battlefield Trust

**Education:** Saint Thomas College (Kentucky); Transylvania University (Kentucky); U.S. Military Academy, West Point (graduated 1828, 23rd of 33 in his class)

**Military career:**
- U.S. Army lieutenant, 1828–1835; served under General Zachary Taylor (later his father-in-law briefly)
- Black Hawk War (1832)
- Mexican-American War: Colonel, 1st Mississippi Rifles; distinguished himself at the Battle of Buena Vista (1847)

**Political career:**
- U.S. House of Representatives (Mississippi), 1845–1846
- U.S. Senator (Mississippi), 1847–1851, then 1857–1861 (resigned upon Mississippi's secession)
- U.S. Secretary of War under President Franklin Pierce, 1853–1857 (modernized the Army, championed camels as desert transport, surveyed railroad routes West — i.e., a genuinely accomplished administrator)

**Plantation owner:** Developed Brierfield Plantation in Mississippi; owned approximately 113 enslaved people at peak.  
**Source:** Wikipedia, "Jefferson Davis"

**The contrast with Lincoln:**
- Davis was the experienced soldier-statesman: West Point graduate, decorated combat veteran, former cabinet secretary, U.S. Senator. He was the obvious choice to Confederates, expected to be a steady, capable wartime leader.
- Lincoln was the underestimated outsider: one-term congressman, railroad lawyer, no military experience beyond a brief militia stint in the Black Hawk War. His own party had preferred others. Much of the country — North and South — underestimated him.
- [NOTE FOR AUTHOR: This contrast is rich for storytelling — the man everyone thought would win the war vs. the man no one expected to lead it.]

**Davis's inaugural tone (February 18, 1861):**
- Grounded secession in the Declaration of Independence: "it is the right of the people to alter or abolish governments whenever they become destructive" and "the right solemnly proclaimed at the birth of the States...undeniably recognize in the people the power to resume the authority delegated."
- Rejected the word "revolution": "it is by abuse of language that their act has been denominated a revolution."
- Notably contained **no explicit mention of slavery** — framed the entire project in constitutional self-government language.
- Mixed conciliation ("anxious to cultivate peace and commerce with all nations") with threat ("we must prepare to meet the emergency and to maintain, by the final arbitrament of the sword").
- Key phrase: "We have entered upon the career of independence, and it must be inflexibly pursued."
- Note: Davis reportedly wrote the entire speech the night before and delivered it in about 15 minutes. Contemporary accounts describe it as measured, not fiery.
  - Source: Rice University, Papers of Jefferson Davis; Civil War Monitor; Constitution Center

## BEAT 3: LINCOLN'S FIRST INAUGURAL ADDRESS

**Date:** March 4, 1861  
**Location:** U.S. Capitol, Washington, D.C.  
**Source:** Avalon Project, Yale (avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/lincoln1.asp); Wikipedia

At the time of Lincoln's inauguration, **seven states had already seceded** and the Confederate Provisional Government was already 13 days old.

#### Key verbatim passages:

**On not interfering with slavery where it existed:**
> "I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists."

**On secession's illegality — holding federal property:**
> "I hold that in contemplation of universal law and of the Constitution the Union of these States is perpetual."

> "The power confided to me will be used to hold, occupy, and possess the property and places belonging to the Government."

**On who bears responsibility for war:**
> "In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war."

**The famous closing — "better angels" (verbatim, full passage):**
> "I am loath to close. We are not enemies, but friends. We must not be enemies. Though passion may have strained it must not break our bonds of affection. The mystic chords of memory, stretching from every battlefield and patriot grave to every living heart and hearthstone all over this broad land, will yet swell the chorus of the Union, when again touched, as surely they will be, by the better angels of our nature."

**Note on the "better angels" phrase:** This closing was substantially revised at the suggestion of Secretary of State-designate William H. Seward, who proposed "the guardian angel of the nation." Lincoln transformed Seward's pedestrian prose into the final musical version. The closing was deliberately conciliatory — Lincoln was still hoping the upper South slave states could be kept from secession.  
Source: Abraham Lincoln's First Inaugural Address, Wikipedia; Constitution Center

## BEAT 4: FORT SUMTER — THE TRIGGER

**Location:** Charleston Harbor, South Carolina (man-made island fort)  
**Source:** American Battlefield Trust (battlefields.org/learn/civil-war/battles/fort-sumter); Battle of Fort Sumter, Wikipedia

#### The supply standoff:
- Major Robert Anderson (Union) commanded approximately **85 men** at Fort Sumter.
- Anderson had relocated his garrison from the indefensible Fort Moultrie to Fort Sumter on December 26, 1860 — a bold, unilateral move that immediately raised tensions.
- **January 5–9, 1861:** The *Star of the West*, a merchant vessel carrying ~200 reinforcements and provisions, was fired upon by Citadel cadets as it attempted to enter Charleston Harbor on January 9, forcing it to turn back. This was the first Confederate act of military force — weeks before Fort Sumter.
- By April 1861, Anderson's garrison was critically low on food; he informed Washington he would be forced to evacuate within days without resupply.

#### The Confederate decision:
- **March 1, 1861:** Jefferson Davis ordered P.G.T. Beauregard to take command of Confederate forces around Charleston.
- **April 4, 1861:** Lincoln informed Southern delegates he would attempt to resupply the garrison (food only, no reinforcements or weapons — a deliberate de-escalation attempt).
- **April 9, 1861:** Davis and his cabinet decided to "strike a blow" before the resupply fleet arrived.
- **April 10, 1861:** Beauregard's aides formally demanded Anderson's surrender. Anderson refused but told them (off the record) he would be starved out in a few days. The Confederates decided to fire anyway.

#### The bombardment:
- **April 12, 1861, 3:30 a.m.:** Beauregard sends final notice: he will open fire in one hour.
- **April 12, 1861, 4:30 a.m.:** First Confederate shot — a flaming mortar round — signals the start of the bombardment. (Traditional accounts say this was fired by Confederate officer Roger Pryor or Captain George James; the details are disputed.)
- Anderson's garrison returned fire around 7:00 a.m.
- Bombardment lasted approximately **34 hours**.
- Confederate forces: approximately 500–6,000 troops; 43 guns and mortars positioned around the harbor.
- The Union garrison was vastly outgunned.

#### Surrender:
- **April 13, 1861, 2:30 p.m.:** Major Anderson surrendered Fort Sumter to Beauregard.
- The garrison evacuated on **April 14**.

#### Casualties — the key fact:
- **Zero Union or Confederate soldiers were killed during the bombardment itself.** (Over 3,000 shells were fired.)
- **One Union soldier killed, one mortally wounded:** During the 100-gun salute to the U.S. flag before evacuation, a cannon cartridge exploded prematurely. **Private Daniel Hough** of the 1st U.S. Artillery was killed instantly; Private Edward Galloway was mortally wounded and died days later. Hough is considered the first military fatality of the Civil War.
  - Source: American Battlefield Trust; Battle of Fort Sumter, Wikipedia

**Note for author:** The fact that a 34-hour artillery bombardment produced no combat deaths — and the only death happened during the surrender ceremony — is one of the war's great ironies. The war "began" without killing anyone; the carnage would come later.

#### P.G.T. Beauregard:
- Pierre Gustave Toutant Beauregard, Confederate Brigadier General; a Louisiana Creole and West Point graduate. Before the war, he had been superintendent of West Point. His former instructor there: Major Robert Anderson, the man he was now shelling.
  - Source: American Battlefield Trust; Battle of Fort Sumter, Wikipedia

#### Lincoln's response:
- **April 15, 1861:** Lincoln issued a proclamation calling for **75,000 state militia volunteers** for 90 days to suppress the rebellion.
- Verbatim from the proclamation: "Now, therefore, I, Abraham Lincoln, President of the United States in virtue of the power in me vested by the Constitution and the laws, have thought fit to call forth, and hereby do call forth, the militia of the several States of the Union, to the aggregate number of 75,000, in order to suppress said combinations and to cause the laws to be duly executed."
  - Source: U.S. Senate archives; Visit the Capitol; Proclamation 80, Wikipedia

## BEAT 5: THE UPPER SOUTH SECEDES — AFTER SUMTER AND THE MILITIA CALL

Lincoln's call for 75,000 militia — asking the slave states to provide troops to fight other slave states — was the tipping point for the upper South. They had been watching cautiously; now they had to choose.

- **Virginia — April 17, 1861:** Virginia's secession convention voted to secede, two days after Lincoln's militia call. Ratified by popular vote May 23, 1861; formally joined the Confederacy June 19, 1861. Virginia becoming Confederate immediately made Richmond the obvious new Confederate capital.

- **Arkansas — May 6, 1861**

- **North Carolina — May 20, 1861**

- **Tennessee — June 8, 1861** (popular referendum; East Tennessee largely Unionist — the internal division would become a significant subplot)

Source: Civil War on the Western Border; Confederate States of America, Wikipedia

**Total at the end:** 11 Confederate states.

## BEAT 6: ROBERT E. LEE — THE PIVOT

**Key verifiable facts:**
- Lee was a Colonel in the U.S. Army, serving as superintendent of West Point before the war. He was widely considered one of the finest officers in the Army. General-in-Chief Winfield Scott regarded him as indispensable.
- Lee personally opposed secession — he called it "nothing but revolution" in private letters — and opposed slavery in principle (though he was an enslaver himself, having inherited enslaved people from his father-in-law's estate).

**Timeline:**
- **April 17, 1861:** Virginia votes to secede.
- **April 18, 1861:** Francis Preston Blair (acting at the direction of President Lincoln and Secretary of War Simon Cameron) meets with Lee and formally offers him field command of the Union Army. Lee declines.
  - Lee's own words on his refusal: "I declined the offer he made me to take command of the army that was to be brought into the field, stating as candidly and as courteously as I could, that though opposed to secession and deprecating war, I could take no part in an invasion of the Southern States."
- **April 18, 1861** (same day): Lee also meets with General-in-Chief Winfield Scott and informs him of his position.
- **April 20, 1861:** Lee resigns his commission from the U.S. Army, writing to Secretary of War Simon Cameron and to General Winfield Scott.
  - From Lee's letter to Scott: "Since my interview with you on the 18th instant I have felt that I ought not longer to retain my commission in the Army."
  - Source: History.com; NPS (Arlington House); Lee Family Archive

**The Virginia pull:** Lee's decision followed his home state. He explained he could not bear arms against Virginia, his family, and his home — even though he knew the decision was potentially catastrophic. He took command of Virginia's state forces shortly after, and later of Confederate Army of Northern Virginia.

## BEAT 7: THE BORDER STATES

Four slave states that did NOT secede: **Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri**.

Collectively they held: substantial white population, key rivers (Ohio, Mississippi, Potomac, Missouri), railroad networks, significant industrial capacity, and — critically — they nearly surrounded Washington, D.C. Lincoln understood that losing any of them could end the war before it began.

### Delaware
- Smallest and least contested of the four.
- By 1860, only **~1,800 enslaved people** in the state (about 1.6% of population); 20,000 free Black residents.
- Slavery had nearly died out economically; Delaware was integrated into the Northern economy.
- The state legislature voted **unanimously** against secession.
- Confederate sympathizers existed (particularly in southern Delaware) but never came close to controlling state government.
  - Source: Britannica; WHYY; Border States, Wikipedia

### Maryland
**Strategically the most critical:** Washington, D.C. is surrounded by Maryland on three sides. If Maryland seceded, the capital would be an island inside Confederate territory.

**Baltimore riot — April 19, 1861:**
- Massachusetts and Pennsylvania soldiers passing through Baltimore to reach Washington were attacked by pro-Confederate mobs. **4 Union soldiers and 12 civilians were killed** — the first Civil War deaths on the ground (before any formal battle; the Fort Sumter death was April 14).
- Baltimore's mayor ordered railroad bridges burned to prevent more troops from passing through.

**Lincoln's hard response:**
- **April 27, 1861:** Lincoln suspended the writ of habeas corpus along the military corridor from Washington to Philadelphia — the first such suspension in American history.
- Pro-Confederate agitators and militia leaders were arrested without trial, including John Merryman.
- Chief Justice Roger B. Taney (of Dred Scott fame) ruled in *Ex parte Merryman* (June 4, 1861) that Lincoln's suspension was unconstitutional. Lincoln ignored the ruling.
- **April 29, 1861:** Maryland legislature voted **53–13 against secession** — but under significant federal pressure, with Union troops already occupying key points.
- **September 17, 1861:** Federal government arrested approximately **one-third of the Maryland General Assembly** to prevent them from voting on secession.
  - Source: Baltimore riot of 1861, Wikipedia; Maryland in the American Civil War, Wikipedia; NPS (Fort McHenry)

### Kentucky
- Kentucky declared official **neutrality on May 20, 1861** — uniquely, refusing to provide troops to either side. Both Lincoln and Davis (both born in Kentucky) initially respected this.
- Kentucky was Lincoln's birth state. Both presidents were born in Kentucky within less than 100 miles of each other.
- Lincoln walked a careful line, not pressing Kentucky while quietly arming Unionist home guards.
- By September 1861, when Confederate forces crossed into Kentucky (violating neutrality), the legislature voted to side with the Union and ordered Confederate troops expelled.

**The "Kentucky quote":**
- The famous line "I hope to have God on my side, but I must have Kentucky" is **[LIKELY APOCRYPHAL — see CONTESTED section below]**.
- The VERIFIED, sourced Lincoln statement on Kentucky's importance:
  > "I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game. Kentucky gone, we can not hold Missouri, nor, as I think, Maryland. These all against us, and the job on our hands is too large for us."
  - **Source:** Lincoln to Orville Browning, September 22, 1861. *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln*, Vol. 4. (Teaching American History; University of Michigan Library Digital Collections)

### Missouri
- Missouri had the most violent internal conflict of any border state — essentially its own civil war within the war.
- Governor Claiborne Fox Jackson: publicly supported the Union while privately supporting secession.
- **Camp Jackson Affair, May 10, 1861:** Union commander Brigadier General Nathaniel Lyon (with regiments largely composed of pro-Union German immigrant volunteers) surrounded and captured a state militia unit training at Camp Jackson outside St. Louis. The militia had been covertly armed with Confederate cannons seized from a federal arsenal.
  - As Lyon marched his prisoners through St. Louis, a crowd attacked. After a drunk man fired on the soldiers, Lyon's forces opened fire on the mob, killing approximately **28 civilians**.
  - The Camp Jackson affair secured St. Louis for the Union and Missouri's critical rail and river network — but inflamed Southern sympathizers and pushed Missouri toward guerrilla war.
  - Source: Camp Jackson Affair, Wikipedia; NPS; Civil War on the Western Border

## CONTESTED / FLAGGED ITEMS

### [LIKELY APOCRYPHAL] "I hope to have God on my side, but I must have Kentucky"
- **Status:** No verified primary source. Multiple sources describe Lincoln as "reputed" to have said this. No letter, diary, or contemporary account has been identified containing this exact wording.
- **What IS verified:** Lincoln wrote to Orville Browning on September 22, 1861: "I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game." (*Collected Works*, Vol. 4) — this conveys the same idea and IS sourced.
- **Recommendation for author:** Use the Browning letter quote; footnote or omit the "God on my side" version, or explicitly flag it as apocryphal.

### [NOTE] Cornerstone Speech transcription
- The speech was extemporaneous; no official transcript exists. The accepted text derives from the *Southern Confederacy* (Atlanta) March 25, 1861, plus Stephens's own later memoir.
- Stephens post-war denied or downplayed the most extreme portions; historians universally reject his self-serving revisionism. The text as quoted is the scholarly standard.
- Minor punctuation variants exist (comma before "subordination"). Most reliable editions include the comma.

### [VERIFY] First Confederate shot at Fort Sumter
- Who fired the first shot? Accounts differ. Roger Pryor (Confederate officer) is sometimes credited but reportedly declined when the moment came. Captain George James (commanding Fort Johnson) is credited in some accounts with the signal mortar shot. The NPS attributes the first shot to the Confederate mortar batteries without naming an individual definitively. The author should not attribute the first shot to a specific named individual without checking a more authoritative source.

### [NOTE] Deaths at Fort Sumter — two victims, not one
- Often summarized as "one Union soldier died." More precisely: **Private Daniel Hough** was killed outright; **Private Edward Galloway** was mortally wounded in the same explosion and died shortly after. The American Battlefield Trust and Wikipedia both confirm two casualties from the salute accident.

### [NOTE] Lee's resignation date vs. Virginia's secession date
- Virginia's secession convention voted April 17. Lee resigned April 20. The offer from Blair was April 18. This three-day sequence matters for narrative: Virginia seceded, Lee was offered Union command (and declined), then resigned — not simultaneously.

### [NOTE] Davis as "experienced" — nuance
- Davis was widely expected to be a strong leader, but his actual wartime performance is contested by historians. The "everyone expected Davis to win" framing is accurate for Spring 1861 — but the author should avoid implying this expectation was validated. Save the Davis-vs.-Lincoln leadership comparison for later sections.

### [NOTE] Confederate Constitution Art. I §9(4) — "no bill shall be passed"
- The Avalon Project text reads: "No bill of attainder, ex post facto law, or law denying or impairing the right of property in negro slaves shall be passed." This is the verbatim text. Some reprints add a "No" before "law denying" for clarity — confirm against the Avalon source before publication.

### [NOTE] Maryland legislature arrests — timing
- The September 17, 1861 arrests of Maryland legislators came months after the spring crisis. For the "Two Governments" section (which covers spring 1861), the key Maryland facts are: Baltimore riot (April 19), habeas corpus suspension (April 27), and the April 29 legislature vote against secession. The September arrests are a follow-on development; the author may want to note they occurred but keep the spring facts primary.
