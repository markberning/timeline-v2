# FACT PACK — "The Acadian Expulsion" (1755–1764)
### French and Indian War, off-the-battlefield theme · kind=theme (AFTERMATH)

**Scope note for the author:** Write ONLY from facts below. Each item carries its source.
Items flagged `[CONTESTED]` carry the scholarly range — do not pick a single number where a

**Terminology / spelling (use consistently):**
- The event has three accepted names: the **Expulsion of the Acadians**, the **Grand Dérangement**
  ("the Great Upheaval"), and the **Acadian Deportation**.
- **Acadia** (the colony) = **Acadie** in French; became British **Nova Scotia** in 1713.
- **Mi'kmaq** (the people / plural and adjective; older spelling "Micmac" appears in sources and quotes).
  Their homeland = **Mi'kma'ki**. Singular person = Mi'kmaw.
- **Île Saint-Jean** = present-day Prince Edward Island. **Île Royale** = Cape Breton (Louisbourg).
- Use accents: Grand-Pré, Dérangement, Beauséjour, Le Loutre, Évangéline.

## 1. WHO THE ACADIANS WERE

- **French-speaking Catholic settlers** in the region of **Acadia** — mainland Nova Scotia and
  parts of present-day New Brunswick and Prince Edward Island.
  [Wikipedia, *Expulsion of the Acadians*]
- France established its first settlement in **Mi'kma'ki in 1604**; the founding settlement was
  **Port-Royal** (founded 1605). Acadian families largely came from **Poitou** in western France.
  [Salut Canada / landscapeofgrandpre.ca; Wikipedia]
- They were **farmers**, famous for reclaiming the tidal salt marshes of the **Bay of Fundy** with
  earthen **dykes** and **aboiteaux**. An aboiteau is a wooden sluice built into the base of a dyke,
  fitted with a swinging door (clapet) that lets fresh rainwater/river water drain out at low tide
  but shuts to block salt seawater at high tide — so the marsh behind it desalinates over a few years
  and becomes rich farmland and pasture.
  [acadian.org, *Acadian Aboiteau (Dyke)*; Wikipedia, *Aboiteau*]
- **Aboiteau farming began near Port-Royal in the 1630s.** By 1755, roughly **13,000 acres (≈5,300 ha)**
  of Bay of Fundy salt marsh had been reclaimed this way for pasture and intensive crop production.
  [novascotia.ca, *Maritime Dykelands*; landscapeofgrandpre.ca]
- As the population grew, Acadians spread from Port-Royal (renamed **Annapolis Royal** by the British)
  to new marshland settlements at **Chignecto / Beaubassin** and the **Minas Basin** (**Grand-Pré**).
  [acadian.org; Wikipedia]
- **Population on the eve of the expulsion `[CONTESTED — range]`:** sources give roughly **13,000–14,100**
  Acadians in the region in 1755. (Father Le Loutre's War article cites ~12,000 Acadians c.1749;
  the Expulsion article cites ~14,100 by 1755.)
  [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*; Wikipedia, *Expulsion of the Acadians*]

## 2. THE POLITICAL BIND — TREATY OF UTRECHT (1713) AND THE OATH

- The **1713 Peace / Treaty of Utrecht** ended the War of the Spanish Succession and **ceded Acadia
  to Great Britain**; it became the British colony of **Nova Scotia**. The Acadians were allowed to
  keep their lands and stay. They thereby became British subjects.
  [Wikipedia; acadien.novascotia.ca timeline; cbc.ca]
- Britain repeatedly demanded an **unconditional oath of allegiance** to the British Crown. The
  Acadians refused it, fearing it would force them to **bear arms against France** (and, in their own
  framing, against their **Mi'kmaq** neighbors). They instead sought to swear a **conditional oath of
  neutrality** — to be peaceful British subjects but not take up arms. They called themselves the
  **"neutral French" / "French Neutrals."**
  [Wikipedia; cbc.ca, *The oath of allegiance*; acadian.org, *Oaths of Acadian*]
- A conditional/neutrality arrangement was at one point accepted by Governor **Richard Philipps**.
  For roughly **four decades** this uneasy neutrality held.
  [acadien.novascotia.ca timeline; cbc.ca]
- This is the bind to dramatize: caught between two empires, the Acadians tried to be loyal to
  neither army — peaceful subjects of Britain, but unwilling to march against France or the Mi'kmaq.

## 3. THE MI'KMAQ DIMENSION — FIRST-CLASS ACTORS, NOT BACKDROP

The Mi'kmaq were the region's Native nation, allied to France, fighting their **own war** against
British encroachment — **Father Le Loutre's War (1749–1755)**, also called the Anglo-Mi'kmaq War or
the Mi'kmaq War. Their aims were their own: defending Mi'kma'ki from British settlement.

- **Dates:** 21 June 1749 – 16 June 1755. At outbreak, roughly **2,500 Mi'kmaq** and ~12,000 Acadians
  lived in the region. [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*]
- **The trigger — Halifax/Chebucto (1749):** Governor **Edward Cornwallis** arrived with 13 transports
  and founded **Halifax** on **21 June 1749**, at **Jipugtug (Chebucto)** — land the British had earlier
  promised the Mi'kmaq they would not settle. The British settled **3,229 people** there in the first
  years, more than the entire Mi'kmaq population — seen as a direct threat to "the traditional occupiers."
  [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*]
- **Mi'kmaq grievance stated formally:** on **24 September 1749**, the Mi'kmaq protested through
  Father Maillard, "proclaiming their ownership of the land, and expressing their opposition to the
  British actions in settling at Halifax." [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*]
- **Mi'kmaq actions (their own war, their own initiative):**
  - 19 Aug 1749 — Mi'kmaq seized a vessel and took 20 prisoners at Canso.
  - 30 Sept 1749 — raid on **Dartmouth**, 4 British settlers killed.
  [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*]
- **Named Mi'kmaq leaders (use correct names):**
  - **Chief Jean-Baptiste Cope** — negotiated the **Treaty of 1752** with the British.
  - **Chief Étienne Bâtard** — organized the ambush/killing of Edward How (October 1750).
  - **Chief Paul Laurent** — listed as a commander.
  [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*]
- **Jean-Louis Le Loutre** was a **French Catholic missionary priest** (not Mi'kmaq) who worked
  alongside and supplied the Mi'kmaq and Acadian resistance — British codename "Moses"; historian
  Micheline Johnson called him "the soul of the Acadian resistance." (He also paid Mi'kmaq fighters
  for British scalps.) Be careful: Le Loutre is an ally/instigator, **not** a Mi'kmaq leader; do not
  let him stand in for Mi'kmaq agency. [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*]
- **British brutality runs both ways — name it:** Cornwallis issued an **extirpation proclamation
  (2 October 1749)** authorizing colonists to "annoy, distress, take or destroy the Savage commonly
  called Micmac," with a bounty of **10 guineas per scalp**, raised to **50 guineas (21 June 1750)**.
  [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*]
- **Charles Lawrence** succeeded Cornwallis and continued aggressive settlement (founding Lunenburg
  1753, Lawrencetown 1754) — the same official who would order the Acadian deportation two years later.
  [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*]
- The war ended with the **fall of Fort Beauséjour (June 1755)** — which is also the trigger for the
  expulsion (next section). The defeat broke organized French–Mi'kmaq–Acadian resistance.

## 4. THE TRIGGER — FORT BEAUSÉJOUR, THE OATH, THE DECISION (June–August 1755)

- **Fort Beauséjour** (a French fort built on the Chignecto Isthmus to contain British expansion into
  present-day New Brunswick) was besieged **3–16 June 1755** and **surrendered on or about 16 June 1755**
  to British forces under **Lt. Col. Robert Monckton**. `[Battle dates: siege 3–16 June; some sources
  date the fall 4 June / 16 June — say "June 1755."]`
  [Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*; Wikipedia, *Expulsion of the Acadians*]
- **Some Acadians were found among the fort's defenders** — this gave Lieutenant Governor
  **Charles Lawrence** the pretext he wanted to doubt Acadian neutrality.
  [Wikipedia; subject brief corroborated by Parks Canada Fort Beauséjour]
- **July 1755, Halifax:** Lawrence summoned Acadian delegates and pressed them to take the
  **unqualified (unconditional) oath of allegiance**. They again refused. Lawrence **imprisoned the
  delegates**. [Wikipedia, *Expulsion of the Acadians*]
- **28 July 1755:** **Charles Lawrence and the Nova Scotia Council** formally decided to **deport** the
  Acadians. [Wikipedia; multiple]
- **Charles Lawrence** — title: **Lieutenant Governor of Nova Scotia** (acting governor). He is the
  central British author of the policy. Use "Lieutenant Governor Charles Lawrence."

### Verbatim — Lawrence's deportation orders (Halifax, 11 August 1755)
Instructions to Major Handfield, commanding the garrison at Annapolis Royal (one of several parallel
orders to field officers). Speaker: **Charles Lawrence**, 11 August 1755. [acadian.org, *The Deportation Orders*]

> "That the Inhabitants may not have it in their power to return to this Province nor to join in
> strengthening the French of Canada in Louisbourg; it is resolved that they shall be dispersed among
> his Majesty's Colonies upon the Continent of America."

> "You will use all the means proper and necessary for collecting the people together so as to get them
> on board. If you find that fair means will not do with them, you must proceed by the most vigorous
> measures possible, not only in compelling them to embark, but in depriving those who shall escape of
> all means of shelter or support by burning their houses and destroying everything that may afford
> them the means of subsistence in the country."

- Lawrence's stated plan was that **whole families should travel in the same vessel** — a promise
  **frequently broken in practice** (families were split across ships). [acadian.org]
- `[CONTESTED / use with care]` A Lawrence memorandum (9 August 1755) is quoted as
  "take an Eye for an Eye a Tooth for a Tooth and in short life for Life." This appears on the
  Massachusetts Commonwealth Museum exhibit attributed to Lawrence but is harsher/less commonly cited
  than the Handfield instructions; if used, attribute carefully or omit. [sec.state.ma.us, Commonwealth Museum]

## 5. THE GRAND DÉRANGEMENT ITSELF (autumn 1755 – 1764)

- The deportation order to officers went out **10–11 August 1755**; the roundups began in the
  **autumn of 1755** and the expulsion continued in waves through **1764**.
  [Wikipedia; acadien.novascotia.ca timeline]
- **Grand-Pré, 5 September 1755:** **Lieutenant Colonel John Winslow** (a New England provincial
  officer, under Lawrence's orders) summoned the **men and boys of Grand-Pré into the parish church**
  and read them the deportation order — homes and property forfeit, the people to be removed.
  [Wikipedia; sec.state.ma.us; Nova Scotia Archives, *Journal of Colonel John Winslow*]

### Verbatim — John Winslow's address at Grand-Pré, 5 September 1755
Speaker: **Lt. Col. John Winslow**, reading to the assembled Acadian men in the Grand-Pré church.
Source: Winslow's own journal (orig. manuscript, Massachusetts Historical Society; transcribed in
*Collections of the Nova Scotia Historical Society*). [sec.state.ma.us, Commonwealth Museum; Nova Scotia Archives]

> "The Part of Duty I am now upon is what tho necessary is very disagreeable to my natural make & temper…"

> "That your land & tennements cattle of all kinds and live stock of all sortes are forfitted to the
> Crown with all other your effects saving your money & household goods and you your selves to be
> removed from this his Province."
*(Note the original 1755 spelling — quote it as-is, or paraphrase; do not "modernize" silently and
present as a quote.)*

### Verbatim — Winslow's journal on the embarkation (8 October 1755)
Speaker: **John Winslow**, journal entry. [sec.state.ma.us, Commonwealth Museum]

> "began to embarke the inhabitants who went verry [solemnly] and unwillingly, the women in great
> distress carrying their children in their arms…"
*(Source renders "solentarily"/"solemnly" — use the sense "unwillingly, in great distress"; this is
the single best human-cost quote in the pack.)*

- **Method:** British troops rounded up families at gunpoint, **burned their houses, barns, and crops**
  (explicitly so they could not return — see Lawrence's order above), and **packed them onto crowded
  transport ships** (Lawrence's orders allotted roughly **2 persons per ton** of vessel capacity).
  Families were separated. [acadian.org; Wikipedia]

- **Numbers deported `[CONTESTED — give the range]`:**
  - Roughly **10,000+** Acadians deported overall is the standard summary figure; Wikipedia's tally
    gives **~11,500 deported** out of ~14,100 (with **~2,600** eluding capture and remaining by the
    1764 census).
  - Wikipedia's destination breakdown of the **first wave (1755, to the Thirteen Colonies)** —
    Massachusetts 2,000; Virginia 1,100; Maryland 1,000; Connecticut 700; Pennsylvania 500;
    North Carolina 500; South Carolina 500; Georgia 400; New York 250 (≈6,950).
  - **Second wave (after 1758):** Britain **866**; France **~3,500**.
  Author: say "**more than 10,000**" or "roughly 11,000–11,500 over 1755–1764," not a false-precise single number.
  [Wikipedia, *Expulsion of the Acadians*]

## 6. THE SCATTERING AND THE DEATH TOLL

- **Destinations:** the **Thirteen Colonies** down the seaboard (where Acadians were often
  **unwelcome / refused entry** — e.g. ships turned away, exiles held in poverty); **Britain**; and
  **France**. After 1758 the British shipped exiles **directly to France** rather than the colonies.
  [Wikipedia; Wikipedia, *Ile Saint-Jean campaign*]
- **Overall deaths `[CONTESTED — range]`:** Wikipedia's summary tally gives **~5,000 dead** of
  disease, starvation, drowning, and exposure. Standard popular summaries say "**thousands** died."
  Use "thousands" or "as many as ~5,000"; flag that exact totals are uncertain. [Wikipedia]

### The 1758 Île Saint-Jean campaign — the deadliest phase
- After the **British took Louisbourg (surrendered 26 July 1758)**, the British deported the
  inhabitants of **Île Saint-Jean** (present-day PEI) — **more than 3,000 people** — shipping them to
  France. This was the **deadliest** phase of the whole expulsion: **over half** of those deported
  from Île Saint-Jean died of **shipwreck or disease**. [Wikipedia, *Ile Saint-Jean campaign*]

### The ship disasters of December 1758 (the human-cost set-piece)
On 20 October 1758, a convoy left Île Saint-Jean for France. Three of the transports sank in the
North Atlantic in December 1758. [Wikipedia, *Duke William (ship)*; *Violet (ship)*; *Ile Saint-Jean campaign*]
- **Duke William** — left with **over 360 Acadians**; sank ~**13 December 1758**, about 20 leagues
  (≈97 km) off the coast of France. Captain **William Nichols** survived. `[CONTESTED death count:
  "over 360" / 364 died.]`
- **Violet** — carried **over 280 Acadians**; sank ~**12 December 1758** with all aboard.
  `[CONTESTED: 280–400 lost; one source gives 280.]`
- **Ruby** — carried ~**310 Acadians**; also sank; ~**213 died** (one source). `[CONTESTED]`
- **Combined, nearly 1,000 Acadians died** when these three ships sank. [Wikipedia]
- **Human face:** aboard *Duke William*, the elder **Noël Doiron**, his wife Marie, five of their
  children with spouses, and over thirty grandchildren — **about 120 family members** — were lost.
  [Wikipedia, *Duke William (ship)*]

## 7. AFTERMATH AND LEGACY — THE DIASPORA, THE RETURN, THE CAJUNS

- **Return permitted (1764):** on **11 July 1764** the British government issued an order-in-council
  **permitting Acadians to return** to British territories — but **in small, isolated groups** and
  **only on taking the unqualified oath of allegiance**. Acadians trickled back, resettling around
  mainland Nova Scotia, Cape Breton, and the Maritimes (in scattered new communities, not their old
  marsh farms — those had been given to incoming British/New England settlers). `[~2,600 had eluded
  deportation and remained by the 1764 census.]` [Wikipedia, *Expulsion of the Acadians*]
- **Louisiana / the Cajuns:**
  - A first small group (~20, escaped from New York) reached **Louisiana in April 1764**.
  - In **February 1765**, ~**193** exiles from Halifax, led by **Joseph Broussard ("Beausoleil")**,
    landed at New Orleans.
  - The largest single migration came in **1785**, when Spain (which had acquired Louisiana) paid to
    transport ~**1,600** Acadians who had been repatriated to France. Across **1765–1785 roughly
    3,000** Acadians came to Louisiana from France. Spain welcomed them as fellow Catholics and granted
    them land (e.g. the Attakapas District). [acadianmemorial.org; 64parishes.org; wheninyourstate.com]
  - There, **"Acadien" → "Cadien" → anglicized "Cajun."** The Acadians became the founding core of
    Louisiana's Cajun culture. [Wikipedia; 64parishes.org]
- **Cultural memory — Longfellow's *Evangeline*:** in **1847** the American poet
  **Henry Wadsworth Longfellow** published the narrative poem ***Evangeline: A Tale of Acadie***,
  about a fictional Acadian woman separated from her betrothed by the deportation. It became hugely
  popular and is the work that fixed the expulsion in broad public memory. [Wikipedia]

## 8. FRAMING — HOW SCHOLARS NAME IT (handle squarely, neither sanitize nor sensationalize)

Name it plainly: a **forced deportation / expulsion of a civilian population** — the clearest
atrocity of the war, falling on noncombatant farming families. There is a live scholarly debate over
the strongest label; present it honestly rather than asserting one verdict:

- **John Mack Faragher** (*A Great and Noble Scheme*, 2005) compared the expulsion to **ethnic
  cleansing**, pointing to anti-Catholic laws barring Acadians/Catholics from office, voting, and land.
- **John Grenier** objected that Faragher "overstates his case; his focus on the *grand dérangement*
  as an early example of ethnic cleansing carries too much present-day emotional weight."
- Acadian historian **Maurice Basque**: "the term 'genocide' … does not apply at all to the
  Grand Dérangement."
- Historian **A. J. B. Johnston**: "to my mind, you can't just transfer concepts between centuries."
- **Most historians** also note the British **military rationale**: cutting supply and intelligence to
  Louisbourg, Québec, and France's Indigenous allies, and removing a population that might rise behind
  British lines.
[All four positions: Wikipedia, *Expulsion of the Acadians*, "Characterization" section, quoting the
named historians.]

**Author guidance:** the suffering — burned farms, separated families, drowned ships, exiles refused
entry up and down the coast — is the heart of the story and must be carried in the prose, not buried
in a number. State the forced-removal-of-civilians fact directly. You may note that scholars debate
"ethnic cleansing"/"genocide" as labels; do NOT assert either as settled fact, and do NOT use the
debate to soften the documented reality of the removal.

## SUPPLEMENT — the Mi'kmaq war's resolution (1755–1761) (added in reconcile)

**Why this supplement exists:** a comprehensiveness critic flagged that the section gives the
Mi'kmaq the last word but ends their thread at 1755 ("the war wound down only when the French
fort fell in the summer of 1755"). That is an **overstatement**. Acadian/Mi'kmaq partisan
resistance *continued* for years after Fort Beauséjour fell, and the Anglo-Mi'kmaq War did not
actually end until the **Halifax / Peace and Friendship Treaties of 1760–1761**. This supplement
records the verified facts so the author can resolve the thread instead of leaving it dangling.

### S1. Resistance CONTINUED after Fort Beauséjour fell (June 1755) — it did not end in 1755
- After the fall of Fort Beauséjour, a body of Acadians stayed behind under **Charles Deschamps
  de Boishébert** (a French officer) and the Acadian militia leader **Joseph Broussard, "Beausoleil"**
  and kept fighting the British (notably around Fort Cumberland — the renamed Beauséjour).
  [Parks Canada, *The great upheaval — Fort Beauséjour / Fort Cumberland NHS*; Wikipedia, *Joseph Broussard*]
- **Broussard ("Beausoleil")** led an armed Acadian resistance through the expulsion, "leading
  assaults against the British on several occasions **between 1755 and 1758**" as part of the forces
  of **Boishébert**. He took up a converted privateer schooner and fought in the guerrilla war waged
  jointly by the Acadian resistance, the French, the **Mi'kmaq**, the **Wolastoqiyik (Maliseet)**, and
  the **W8banakiak (Abenaki)** against the British.
  [Parks Canada, *Joseph Broussard dit Beausoleil National Historic Person*; Dictionary of Canadian
  Biography, *Brossard (Broussard), dit Beausoleil, Joseph*; Wikipedia, *Joseph Broussard*]
- **Broussard's arc into 1758–1762:** he had been jailed at Fort Lawrence in fall 1755 but escaped
  with ~80 other Acadian prisoners; armed a ship in 1758; **his ship was seized in November 1758**,
  after which he fell back to **Miramichi** (Boishébert's base) and kept up the resistance. He was
  later imprisoned at **Fort Edward (1762)**, moved to Halifax, and released in **1764** — after
  which he led the exile group to Louisiana (cf. §7: ~193 to New Orleans, Feb 1765).
  `[CONTESTED — a wounding "in 1758" appears in some Parks Canada / secondary summaries but is NOT
  in the Wikipedia biography; the 1758 ship-seizure-then-Miramichi sequence is the better-sourced
  fact. State the years-long resistance; do not assert a specific wounding.]`
  [Wikipedia, *Joseph Broussard*; Dictionary of Canadian Biography]
- **Bottom line for the author:** organized field resistance was *broken* (not ended) at Beauséjour;
  Acadian/Mi'kmaq/Maliseet/Abenaki partisan and privateer warfare persisted roughly **1755–1758/1761**.

### S2. The actual resolution — the Halifax / Peace and Friendship Treaties of 1760–1761
- The Anglo-Mi'kmaq conflict was resolved not by a battle but by a series of **peace treaties signed
  at Halifax in 1760–1761** between the British and the **Mi'kmaq** (and the **Maliseet /
  Wolastoqiyik** and **Passamaquoddy**). These are the **Halifax Treaties**, also grouped as the
  **Peace and Friendship Treaties** / "**Treaties of 1760–1761**." One tally counts **11** such
  treaties signed 1760–1761. [Wikipedia, *Peace and Friendship Treaties*; Wikipedia, *Burying the
  Hatchet ceremony (Nova Scotia)*; Crown–Indigenous Relations Canada, *Treaty Texts — 1760–61*]
- **The "Burying the Hatchet" ceremony — 25 June 1761:** held at Lieutenant Governor **Jonathan
  Belcher's** garden (Governor's Farm, on present-day Spring Garden Road, Halifax). The Mi'kmaq chiefs
  washed the war paint from their bodies as a token that hostilities had ended; the hatchet was
  buried. At least four Mi'kmaq chiefs signed: **Jeannot Peguidalouet** (Cape Breton),
  **Claude Atouach** (Shediac), **Joseph Sabecholouet** (Miramichi), and **Aikon Ashabuc**
  (Pokemouche). `[CONTESTED date: 25 June 1761 is standard; a minority of accounts give 8 July 1761.]`
  [Wikipedia, *Burying the Hatchet ceremony (Nova Scotia)*]
- **The treaties ceded NO land — CONFIRMED.** They were **peace-and-friendship treaties, not

### S3. CORRECTION the author should apply to the draft
- The draft line that the Mi'kmaq war "**wound down only when the French fort [Beauséjour] fell in
  the summer of 1755**" is an **overstatement** and should be revised. What actually happened:
  - The fall of Beauséjour (June 1755) **broke organized French–Mi'kmaq–Acadian resistance** but did
    **not end the war**.
  - **Partisan/privateer resistance continued** under **Boishébert** and **Broussard ("Beausoleil")**,
    with the Mi'kmaq, Maliseet, and Abenaki, into **1758** and beyond.
  - The Anglo-Mi'kmaq War actually **ended with the Halifax / Peace and Friendship Treaties of
    1760–1761**, ceremonially sealed at the **Burying the Hatchet ceremony, 25 June 1761** — treaties
    that **ceded no land** and are **still binding law** (R v. Marshall, 1999).
  - This lets the section give the Mi'kmaq the last word *honestly*: they did not simply vanish in
    1755; they fought on and ended their own war on negotiated terms whose rights survive in court today.

### S4. Death-toll context (for §6 — the ~5,000 figure sits at the LOW end)
- The pack's §6 cites **~5,000** Acadian deaths (Wikipedia summary tally). For range awareness:
  **John Mack Faragher** (*A Great and Noble Scheme*, 2005) estimates the expulsion cost about
  **10,000 lives**, with secondary summaries citing **~55% mortality** of the deported population.
  So **~5,000 is the LOW end** of the scholarly range, not a consensus total. The draft's hedged
  "thousands, as many as ~5,000, exact unknown" remains fine — this is context, not a required change;
  the author may optionally note "up to ~10,000 by some estimates (Faragher)."
