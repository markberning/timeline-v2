## SECTION: the-regulars-are-coming-out
EYEBROW: Lexington & Concord · The night before
TITLE: The regulars are coming out

In the spring of 1775 Boston was an occupied city. After years of escalating quarrel between Britain and its American colonies, the town was garrisoned by regiments of regulars (the king's professional, full-time soldiers) and a force of Royal Marines, all under General Thomas Gage (British), and the occupation was exactly the problem it was meant to solve. Outside Boston, the Massachusetts countryside had been organizing for months for the day the troops marched out of it. Every town kept a militia, the part-time citizen soldiery of farmers and tradesmen mustered into companies under their own officers, and the towns had built a designated rapid-response tier on top of it: the minute companies, roughly a quarter to a third of the militia rolls, picked men expected to turn out at a minute's warning. None of this was spontaneous. It was a system, drilled and re-drilled, waiting for a trigger.

PILL: /war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/boston-massacre | An army in Boston: the Townshend Acts & the Massacre

The trigger was a warehouse town. The Provincial Congress (the assembly the Massachusetts rebels had set up in defiance of Gage's government) had been stockpiling arms and supplies at Concord, about 18 miles from Boston, and Gage's spies knew it in detail. On the night of April 18 he handed Lieutenant Colonel Francis Smith (British), a heavy, methodical, thirty-year professional of the 10th Foot, written orders to take about 700 men drawn from the garrison's grenadier and light infantry companies (the two picked "flank companies" every regiment kept: the grenadiers its biggest men, for assaults; the light infantry its fastest, for skirmishing and flank work) and march on the stockpile.

QUOTE: with the utmost expedition and Secrecy to Concord, where you will seize and distroy all Artillery, Ammunition, Provisions, Tents, Small Arms, and all Military Stores whatever

The spelling is Gage's own. So is the restraint: the same orders told Smith, "But you will take care that the Soldiers do not plunder the Inhabitants, or hurt private property." Gage even specified the method, down to the detail: knock the trunnions (the stub axles a cannon barrel pivots on) off each gun, shake the powder and flour into the river, burn the tents. He attached a map of Concord with the supply caches marked on it, because his spies really were that good. On paper it was a police raid: stores, not people, and no first shot.

The secret leaked before the boats were in the water. Dr. Joseph Warren, the Boston physician at the center of the rebels' intelligence in the city, had a source close to the very top of the British command, and on April 18 he consulted that source before dispatching his riders. Gage later said he had told the full plan to only one person besides his second in command. Who Warren's informant was has never been established. The famous candidate is Gage's own American-born wife, Margaret Kemble Gage, and Gage did send her to England that summer; but the evidence is entirely circumstantial, several specialists call the spy story a myth, and it remains an unproven suspicion, nothing more. The leak is a fact. The leaker is a blank.

Warren sent two riders by two routes: William Dawes overland through the town gate at Boston Neck, and Paul Revere across the water by the Charlestown crossing. The two lanterns hung that night in the Old North Church steeple were a pre-arranged backup signal in case neither rider got out, not the message itself. And nobody shouted that the British were coming, because everyone in Massachusetts was still British; the warning that ran ahead of the column that night was that the regulars were coming out. From Lexington the word stopped depending on any one man. It propagated through the pre-built system, fresh riders, church bells, signal guns, drums, town to town through the dark. Revere himself was captured in Lincoln and released on foot; it was Dr. Samuel Prescott who carried the alarm the last miles into Concord. The town's minister, the Reverend William Emerson, recorded it in his diary: "This morning, between 1 and 2 o'clock, we were alarmed by the ringing of the bell." That system, not one horseman, is what put thousands of armed men on the road by morning.

Concord, in truth, had not needed the midnight warning. Revere had ridden out with an earlier alert on April 8, and the Provincial Congress's stores had been dispersing for days, carted off, buried, hidden farm by farm. The regulars were marching all night to raid a warehouse that had been quietly emptying for the better part of two weeks.

The raid itself began badly. The companies mustered on the Back Bay beach of Boston Common around 10 p.m., rowed across the Charles to Lechmere Point in East Cambridge about an hour later, waded ashore through the marsh, and then stood soaked in the dark for hours waiting on provisions. The column did not actually march until around 2 a.m., its secrecy already dead. Somewhere on the road, hearing the alarm bells ringing ahead of him in the night, Smith did two smart things early. He sent Major John Pitcairn (British), a Royal Marines officer whom even Bostonians respected as one of the fairer men of the occupation, ahead at speed with six light infantry companies. And he sent a rider back to Gage requesting reinforcements, hours before a shot had been fired. That message would produce Percy's brigade, and it would save the column's life.

MEANWHILE: Massachusetts | A system, not a minuteman | The legend remembers one rider and a countryside of farmers grabbing muskets on impulse. The reality was better organized than the raid it defeated: a standing alarm network of riders, bells, and signal guns, and town militia companies with officers, muster rolls, and a rapid-response tier that had been drilling for months. The regulars marched all night into a machine that had been built for exactly this, and it was running before their boats touched the Cambridge shore.

## SECTION: the-green-the-bridge-and-the-road
EYEBROW: Lexington & Concord · April 19
TITLE: The green, the bridge, and the road

Pitcairn's advance reached Lexington a little before 5 a.m. and found about 77 militiamen drawn up in parade-ground lines on the town green under Captain John Parker (American), a 45-year-old farmer, a French and Indian War veteran, and a man dying of tuberculosis. He had not formed them across the Concord road. They stood off to the side of it, a presence rather than a barricade, and not an ambush. Six days later Parker swore a deposition (a formal statement made under oath) about his intentions.

QUOTE: concluded not to be discovered, nor meddle or make with said Regular Troops (if they should approach) unless they should insult or molest us; and upon their sudden Approach, I immediately ordered our Militia to disperse and not to fire

Mounted officers, commonly said to include Pitcairn, rode forward shouting at the militia to disperse ("Disperse, you rebels!" in the colonial accounts). The men were filing off, though not grounding their arms, when a shot came from somewhere. Nobody has ever established who fired it: a regular, a militiaman, an onlooker, an accident. Some regulars swore it came from behind a hedge or a tavern corner; both sides later swore the other began it; and a captured British officer, Lieutenant Edward Gould, gave the most honest contemporary answer, deposing that he could not say which side fired first. What followed is certain. The regulars fired volleys and charged with bayonets, and their officers needed some minutes to beat the firing back down. Eight Americans were killed and ten wounded, against one regular lightly wounded. Jonathan Harrington dragged himself to his own doorstep and died at his wife's feet. It took minutes.

The column reached Concord around 7 a.m. The local militia, too few yet to fight, withdrew across the North Bridge over the Concord River to a hill beyond it and watched the town fill with regulars while companies from neighboring towns marched in to join them. Below, the grenadiers searched the locations marked on Gage's map and found the leftovers of a stockpile two weeks gone: three 24-pounder siege guns too heavy to have been moved, which lost their trunnions; gun carriages and tools, burned; about 500 pounds of musket balls dumped in the millpond (the town later fished most back out); flour and salt fish staved open and dumped. A detachment sent 2 miles to Colonel James Barrett's farm, where the map promised heavy weapons, found nothing of consequence: cannon lay plowed into the furrows under the searchers' boots. The officers in town mostly behaved correctly, paying for what they took, but the burning gun carriages set the town house alight by accident, and although regulars joined the bucket line that doused it, the militia across the river saw only the smoke. Concluding the town was burning, they formed up and advanced.

FIG: /war-img/rev-lexington-north-bridge.jpg

About 400 militiamen came down in column on the 90 to 100 regulars at the bridge, who fell back across it and fumbled into a bad formation. Here there is no mystery about the first shot: the regulars fired first, scattered shots and then a volley, killing Captain Isaac Davis of Acton, whose company led, and Abner Hosmer. Major John Buttrick of Concord ordered the reply, by tradition crying "Fire, fellow soldiers, for God's sake, fire!" The volley killed three regulars and wounded nine, four of the eight officers among them, and broke them; they ran for town. It was the first ordered American volley against the king's troops, and that volley, not Lexington, is what "the shot heard round the world" commemorates. The phrase is not contemporary: Emerson wrote it in 1837, for the hymn sung at the dedication of the bridge monument.

In the minutes after the fight, a wounded regular trying to rise was struck in the head with a hatchet by a young Concord man (tradition names Ammi White) and died. The killing is real, and it grew monstrous in the telling: by the time the story reached Boston the man had been scalped and mutilated, a version that entered Gage's official account. The Provincial Congress answered with sworn statements from the men who buried the bodies: "neither of those persons were scalped, nor their ears cut off." One ugly blow had become a propaganda atrocity running in both directions, and some historians read the regulars' rage that afternoon partly through the rumor.

Smith then sat in Concord for about two hours, gathering wounded and hiring carriages, while the hills kept filling. The march for Boston began around noon, and the real battle began with it. At Meriam's Corner, a mile east, where a small bridge forced the flanking parties (light infantry detachments sweeping the fields wide of the road to keep ambushers off the column) to fold back in, the gathered companies opened fire; two regulars died. The pattern held for 16 miles. At a double bend in the Lincoln woods later named the Bloody Angle, fire from both sides of the road killed about eight men. At the Lincoln and Lexington town line, Parker's reformed company, the morning's survivors, ambushed the column from a wooded rise: Parker's Revenge, known from a single account told 50 years later but fixed on the ground by archaeologists who recovered the musket balls in the 2010s. Somewhere in this stretch of road (sources split on the exact spot) Smith was shot in the thigh, and at Fiske Hill discipline began to fail outright; Pitcairn's horse threw him and bolted into the American lines with his pistols, and the retreat was nearing a rout.

Percy saved it. The relief brigade, perhaps 1,000 to 1,400 men with two 6-pounders (field guns firing a six-pound ball), met the column east of Lexington green between 2 and 3 p.m. Smith's exhausted men collapsed inside its shelter while the cannon, the first artillery fire of the war, broke up the militia massing in pursuit, and Percy's men torched several houses that snipers were using or could use. Then the march resumed behind a rotating rearguard and strong flanking parties sweeping the walls and houses, and those sweeps were where the day's ugliness lived: houses cleared with bayonets, then looted.

FIG: /war-img/rev-lexington-percy-rescue.jpg

The worst came at Menotomy (now Arlington), between about 4:30 and 5:30 p.m., where fresh companies from Essex County waited in a town the road ran straight through. The fight went house-to-house and hand-to-hand, and it cost roughly 25 American and 40 British dead, half the British dead of the entire day. At Jason Russell's house, Russell, 58 and lame, was killed defending his own doorstep along with 11 other Americans shot or bayoneted in the house and yard, the bloodiest single spot of April 19. Samuel Whittemore, in his late 70s, killed three regulars before being shot, bayoneted, and left for dead; he recovered and lived into his nineties. Unarmed men in the houses and taverns died too. The plundering that came with the house-clearing is in the British officers' own diaries: Lieutenant John Barker of the 4th Foot wrote that "The plundering was shameful" and, of the cleared houses, "All that were found in the houses were put to death." Lieutenant Frederick Mackenzie of the 23rd set down how the afternoon's two crimes fed each other.

QUOTE: Many houses were plundered... I have no doubt this inflamed the Rebels... Some soldiers who stayed too long in the houses were killed in the very act of plundering.

Gage's written orders had forbidden exactly this. The honest ledger of the road is even-handed and grim: the regulars plundered and killed in the houses, the militia shot men from ambush all day, and both sides' propaganda inflated both.

At Cambridge, Percy made the day's smartest call, swerving off the expected route over the Great Bridge, where an ambush had been prepared, and making for Charlestown instead. The column reached Charlestown Neck (the narrow strip joining the peninsula to the mainland) at dusk, around 7:30 p.m., under the covering guns of the warship HMS Somerset, whose presence ended the pursuit. The militia ring of skirmishers that had hung on the column all afternoon broke off at the Neck. But the militia did not go home. They camped in a ring around Boston.

MEANWHILE: The road | The flankers' war | The battle was decided a hundred yards either side of the road. Where the light infantry flanking parties could sweep wide through the fields, the ambushes behind the walls died before they fired; where the terrain pinched the flankers back into the column, the column bled. After dawn the militia never once stood in the open against the regulars, and never needed to.

## SECTION: a-siege-by-morning
EYEBROW: Lexington & Concord · What it meant
TITLE: A siege by morning

The returns told the story of the road. The British counted 273 casualties, 73 killed, 174 wounded, and 26 missing, out of the roughly 1,700 to 1,900 men engaged once Percy's brigade is included: about one man in seven. The Americans lost around 95, 49 of them killed, about 40 wounded, and a handful missing, out of the 3,500 to 4,000 who fought in relays across the day. And the distribution mattered as much as the totals. The green and the bridge, the two famous places, account for a few dozen of those casualties between them. Nearly everything else happened on the 16 miles home, and the single worst town was Menotomy, which no one had planned to fight over at all.

The bitter joke of it was that Smith's regulars had done their job. They had executed Gage's written orders almost to the letter: marched to Concord, destroyed what stores they could find, and mostly spared the inhabitants and their property until the road home made a battlefield of every house along it. It did not matter. The stores had been gone for two weeks, 273 men were down, and the army that had marched out to prevent a war marched back into a siege.

The British officers understood immediately what they had met. Percy, who had watched the militia work all afternoon, men taking real risks to fire from close range, companies relieving each other town by town, wrote a letter to a fellow general the very next day, and it is the most honest British verdict on April 19.

QUOTE: Whoever looks upon them as an irregular mob will find himself much mistaken. They have men amongst them who know very well what they are about...

He went on: the rebels were "determined to go through with it," and the insurrection would not "turn out so despicable as it is perhaps imagined at home." Smith, nursing his thigh wound, read the same day the other way, sneering in his report to Gage that "Notwithstanding the enemy's numbers, they did not make one gallant attempt during so long an action." Both verdicts landed on Gage's desk within days. Percy's was the one that aged well.

The other battle began before the gunsmoke cleared, and it was fought with paper. Both sides understood within hours that who fired first at Lexington mattered as much as who won the road, and the dueling sworn statements that resulted are why the question can never be honestly resolved: they were produced as weapons, not as evidence. Within days the Provincial Congress had collected roughly a hundred depositions, expressly assembled to prove the regulars fired first, and had an account on a fast schooner to Britain; the American version ran in London newspapers nearly two weeks before Gage's official report arrived. Gage's counter-narrative swore the rebels began it, and carried the scalping rumor from the North Bridge along with it. Strip the instruments away and what remains is the one honest line from the green: a captured British officer who was there could not say which side fired first.

PILL: /war-revolution/outbreak | The war begins: the whole arc of 1775

What could not be spun was the siege. The militia companies that broke off at Charlestown Neck on the evening of the 19th did not disband; they camped, and more kept coming, militia converging from four colonies, until the lines ran in an arc from Roxbury to Chelsea and Boston was a garrison town cut off by land. Loose command of the gathering settled on General Artemas Ward the next day. The siege of Boston would last from April 19, 1775, to March 17, 1776, and its first great battle came just two months in, when the army outside the town met the regulars again on the hills above Charlestown at Bunker Hill. Pitcairn, the marine major whose advance companies had walked onto Lexington green at dawn, died there, leading his marines up Breed's Hill. Parker, too sick to fight by June, died of his tuberculosis that September, age 46.

Memory went to work on the day almost as fast as the propaganda had. The boulder on Lexington green that has Parker saying "Stand your ground; don't fire unless fired upon" carries a much-later recollection, not anything he is known to have said; his actual sworn deposition, dispersal and no firing, is better testimony anyway. The statue by the green is an idealized young minuteman, not a portrait; no likeness of Parker exists. And Emerson's shot that rang round the world belongs to the bridge, sixty-two years after the fact. The day itself needs none of it. About 700 professional soldiers went out to confiscate a rebellion's hardware, an organized countryside fought them home from behind its stone walls, and by morning the king's army in America was surrounded in its own headquarters town.

MEANWHILE: London | The race for the story | The Provincial Congress put its hundred depositions on a fast schooner because it understood the war's second front from the first morning: the version of April 19 that reached Britain first would frame everything after. The American account, regulars firing first on dispersing farmers, beat Gage's official report to the London papers by nearly two weeks. Neither side's sworn paper settles who actually fired, and it was never meant to. It was ammunition.
