# Framing / Fairness Critic — French and Indian War: "Years of Disaster"

**Draft:** `fi-disaster-draft.md` | **Fact pack:** `fi-disaster-factpack.md`

---

## FIRST-CHECK RESULT

**1. Native nations as first-class actors with their OWN war-aims — PASS**

The draft keeps Native nations as agents throughout. The Braddock-Shingas exchange is given its full political weight: Shingas's explicit land-security question and Braddock's "No Savage Should Inherit the Land" refusal are treated as the decisive strategic turning point that flipped the Ohio nations, not a sideshow. Pontiac and Shingas are named participants at Monongahela. The Mohawk-Caughnawaga split — same people on opposite sides — is called out. The Great Lakes warriors' presence at Fort William Henry is given their own logic: they came for captives and scalps by their own war-customs, not as French auxiliaries. Theyanoguin's death is grieved as a strategic as well as human loss. The Iroquois alliance is described as conditional, earned and lost on military performance. **This is first-class-actor treatment throughout. No flag-on-a-European-army problem here.**

**2. Human cost: Acadian Expulsion AND Fort William Henry killings — PASS**

The Acadian section is the draft's strongest passage. It names the death toll (roughly 5,000), the 53% death rate on the 1758 wave, the Mi'kmaq dimension, the full moral-status debate (ethnic cleansing / genocide), the 2003 Royal Proclamation. It states plainly: "Britain uprooted a long-settled people who had broken no treaty, scattered them across an ocean, and thousands of them died." The Fort William Henry killing is named, described in concrete detail (wounded prisoners scalped, women and children seized), and Monro's fate is given its human weight.

**3. Fort William Henry: myth corrected AND Native logic explained — PASS**

The draft explicitly debunks the 1,500 figure, traces it to Cooper/Parkman, gives Ian Steele's 69–184 range with percentage context, and explains the warriors' own logic (unmet war-prize expectations, broken agreements) without excusing the killing. The smallpox aftermath — devastating the victors' own nations — is included. **All three first-check items pass.**

## FINDINGS

### FAIR

- **Braddock's refusal is framed as the cause, not a backdrop.** The text says directly: "That refusal decided which way the Ohio nations would jump." British decision-making is treated as the driver of Native alignment, not as incidental color.
- **Mohawk aims are named, not assumed.** Johnson's personal credit — language, customs, relationship with Molly Brant, the word "Warraghiggey" — is the explained mechanism of the Mohawk alliance; the draft does not present the alliance as automatic British-loyalty.
- **Acadian resistance is rendered coherent**, not stubborn or disloyal: the oath would have violated their faith, ended their treaty guarantee, endangered their Mi'kmaq relationships, and risked conscription. Their refusal is presented as rational.
- **Montcalm is not exonerated on Fort William Henry.** The draft notes he gave no alternative compensation to his allies and could not restrain the outbreak, attributing the violence to a structural failure of the surrender terms combined with warriors' own agency — consistent with the Steele reading. He is credited for physical courage in intervening, but not absolved of responsibility for the setup.
- **Both-sides glibness is absent.** The draft does not suggest France and Britain were equally positioned or that the outcome was neutral. France is winning because it fights better AND because Native nations are making rational choices France has earned; Britain is losing because it alienated potential allies and couldn't coordinate.

### TILTED (SHOULD-FIX)

**T1. Great Lakes nations' war-aims at Fort William Henry are explained, but only in the surrender context.**

The draft explains *why warriors felt betrayed at the surrender* (captive/scalp customs, broken expectations) but does not earlier state why these nations crossed hundreds of miles to be there in the first place — their own strategic interest in keeping the British out of the Great Lakes trade sphere. The fact pack has this material (section 8). One sentence at the assembly of Montcalm's force naming what the Great Lakes nations came for — beyond Montcalm's war — would complete the first-class-actor treatment for this section.

**T2. The Mohawk at Fort Edward during the siege are mentioned but their perspective is absent.**

The draft notes Johnson and ~150 Mohawk sat at Fort Edward and "never came." This is presented entirely as Webb's failure. But the Mohawk at Fort Edward had just watched Theyanoguin die at Lake George two years earlier. Their presence and non-intervention is more fraught than the draft suggests — were they held back by Webb, or were they also calculating their own exposure? A brief acknowledgment that this is not only a British command failure avoids presenting the Mohawk as passive bystanders to a British decision.

**T3. The Acadian section never names the Mi'kmaq as a people with their own relationship to this land.**

The Mi'kmaq are mentioned once in passing as "longtime neighbors and allies" of the Acadians (in the oath-refusal explanation). But the Mi'kmaq were the original inhabitants of Acadie, had a treaty relationship with France, and resisted the expulsion actively. Their erasure from the Acadian section — they are referenced only in relation to Acadian interests, not their own — is a fairness gap. One sentence naming them as a people with their own stake in this country would fix it.

**T4. Pitt's "fresh resources" conclusion is told from London's POV only.**

The "Meanwhile" card and the closing paragraph describe Pitt's turn entirely as a British metropolitan story: money, commanders, strategy. There is no signal that what Pitt "threw at North America" would cost the Native nations who had been winning. The reader is left with a Britain-rising frame that will feel imbalanced when the next chapter delivers French defeat and Native dispossession. A single phrase acknowledging that the Pitt surge is also the beginning of the end for the nations currently holding the upper hand would prepare the reader without front-loading analysis that belongs in the next chapter.

### DISTORTED — none found

No romanticization of violence, no presentism, no sanitization of death tolls, no "both empires equally right" false balance. The Acadian section is the war's most emotionally direct passage in the draft and it reads as honest, not gratuitous.
