## MUST-FIX (comprehension breakers — gate-blocking)

1. **"knots" undefined** ("Some could make 13 to 19 knots, genuinely fast for the era" — "The cat-and-mouse off Nassau"). A 15-year-old almost certainly does not know a knot is a unit of speed (~1.15 mph). The reader is told this is "fast" but has no unit to attach the number to. Suggested fix: "13 to 19 knots (roughly 15 to 22 miles per hour), genuinely fast for the era."

2. **"frigate" never defined** (used at "steam frigate *Merrimack*" in "Mallory's gamble," then again for *Minnesota* and *Congress* in "March 8"). The draft uses "frigate" three times as though it is a known category. A reader has no idea whether a frigate is big or small, fast or slow, important or expendable — which undercuts the stakes when they are sunk or endangered. Suggested fix: on first use ("steam frigate *Merrimack*"), add a gloss: "a large steam-powered warship" or "one of the navy's large warships."

3. **[VERIFY] authoring note visible in published prose** ("A cheesebox on a raft"). The inline flag "[VERIFY: Lincoln 'It strikes me there's something in it' is a widely-repeated anecdote...]" is an authoring artifact that will appear to readers if the draft is integrated as-is. Either confirm the quote and replace the flag with a clean attribution, or cut the quote and the flag together. Either way, the flag must not appear in the published section.

## SHOULD-FIX (quote + suggested gloss)

1. **Farragut and Grant lack side tags on first mention** ("When David Farragut (Union) captured New Orleans... and Ulysses Grant (Union) took Vicksburg..." — "The snake nobody wanted" vindication para). House rules require every named person on a side to be tagged on first mention per chapter. Neither Farragut nor Grant has appeared before this point. Add "(Union)" after each name.
   *(Note: the draft is self-aware here — it says in the FACT LEDGER "Grant (Union)" — but the tag is missing from the actual prose.)*

2. **"War of 1812" and "Mexican-American War" used as credential anchors without any gloss** ("a hero of the War of 1812 and the Mexican-American War" — "The snake nobody wanted"). A 15-year-old may have heard of these, but a one-clause anchor would land the credential: "the War of 1812 (the last war with Britain)" and "the Mexican-American War (the 1840s conflict that won the U.S. the Southwest)."

3. **"Flag Officer" not glossed** ("Flag Officer Franklin Buchanan (Confederate)" — "March 8"). A 15-year-old will not know this rank. Suggested fix: "Flag Officer Franklin Buchanan (Confederate) — roughly the equivalent of an admiral, the officer commanding the squadron."

4. **"executive officer" not glossed** ("his executive officer, Lieutenant Catesby ap Roger Jones" — "March 8"; again in "March 9"). Suggested fix: add "his second-in-command" on first use.

5. **"solid shot" not glossed** ("her solid shot simply bounced off the *Virginia*'s sloped iron sides like pebbles off a stove" — "March 8"). The image is vivid but the term is period jargon. A 15-year-old won't know a "solid shot" is the most powerful round the *Cumberland* could fire — which is exactly what makes the bounce shocking. Suggested fix: "solid shot — plain iron cannonballs, the heaviest rounds she had."

6. **"smoothbore cannon" not glossed** ("two 11-inch Dahlgren smoothbore cannon" — "A cheesebox on a raft"). Drop the technical term or add a brief gloss: "smoothbore, meaning the barrel had no spiral grooves, making these guns powerful but shorter-range than rifled guns."

7. **"diplomatic" strangulation mentioned before it is explained** ("cutting the South off from the world economically and diplomatically" — "The snake nobody wanted"). The diplomatic angle (Europe staying out because it needed cotton) isn't explained until two sections later ("King Cotton"). A one-clause forward bridge would help: "cutting the South off from the world economically and diplomatically — meaning they were betting they could keep Europe from even recognizing them as a country."

8. **Fort Fisher not glossed** ("guarded by the guns of Fort Fisher" — "The cat-and-mouse off Nassau"). Suggested gloss: "Fort Fisher, a Confederate coastal fortress that made Wilmington the hardest port on the coast to blockade."

9. **Samuel Pook has no explicit side tag** ("designed by naval constructor Samuel Pook" — "Both ships gone by Christmas"). He is inferred as Union from context (Eads, a Union contractor, hired him), but house rules require explicit tagging. Suggested fix: "Samuel Pook (Union)."
