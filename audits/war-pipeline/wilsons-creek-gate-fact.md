## Verdict: PASS (with one MUST-FIX and several SHOULD-FIX items)

The draft is well-grounded. The fact ledger in the draft is unusually thorough and self-aware about its uncertainties. No fabrications found. One claim is verifiably wrong (the "six-mile night march" is a misattribution of a different distance); the other flagged items are handled correctly or need minor softening.

## MUST-FIX

### ❌ 1. "Six-mile night march" — WRONG attribution

**Draft says:** "sent the halves on a **six-mile night march** to hit the sprawling Confederate camp from two directions at once at dawn"

**What the sources show:** The "six miles" figure does not describe the night approach march. It describes the distance Rebel horsemen *chased Sigel* during his retreat after the rout (historynet.com: "Rebel horsemen chased him for six miles"). The night approach march has no confirmed mileage in the primary or secondary literature reviewed; sources describe Wilson's Creek as "~10 miles southwest of Springfield" and say Lyon "halted around 1 a.m. with the campfire glow visible" — but no source gives a march distance for the approach. The pack's own footnote correctly flags this: "pack does not give an exact march distance for the night approach; 'six-mile' is inferred from the pursuit-distance detail." That inference is wrong — the pursuit-distance figure belongs to Sigel's *retreat*, not Lyon's *advance*.

**Correction:** Remove or replace "six-mile." Options:
- "sent the halves on a night march to hit the sprawling Confederate camp from two directions at once at dawn" (simply drop the distance)
- "sent the halves on an all-night march" (the army left Springfield ~8 p.m. Aug 9 and halted around 1 a.m.)

**Sources:** historynet.com ("Rebel horsemen chased him for six miles"); essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com; nps.gov/wicr (no march-distance figure in any NPS account reviewed); warfarehistorynetwork.com (no six-mile approach figure); wilsonscreek.com (no six-mile approach figure).

## SHOULD-FIX

### ⚠️ 2. "Most of Price's recruits deserted" — softened below source

**Draft says:** "his new recruits had no logistics behind them and most of them melted away when the Union pushed back"

**What sources show:** ABT article says "two-thirds of Price's men deserted" (confirmed independently). The draft uses "most" (a soft paraphrase), which is technically consistent with two-thirds but understates a specific and striking number.

**Recommendation:** Strengthen to match the source: "roughly two-thirds of his new recruits melted away when the Union pushed back." This is more accurate *and* more vivid.

**Status:** Not wrong, but weaker than the source warrants. SHOULD-FIX.

---

### ⚠️ 3. "Ninety days" enlistment — ACCEPTABLE but worth a note

**Draft says:** "His short-term volunteers — men who had signed on for ninety days — were weeks from the end of their enlistments and entitled to go home."

**What sources show:** This is accurate. Lincoln's April 15, 1861 call was explicitly for 75,000 volunteers to serve three months (90 days). The 1st Iowa Infantry, present at Wilson's Creek, mustered out August 20 — ten days after the battle, directly because its 90-day term expired. The warfarehistorynetwork.com source uses "three-month enlistments" and the essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com source specifies Lincoln called for "90-days." The pack was overly cautious here; "ninety days" is the correct term, not just an inference.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The draft correctly uses "ninety days." No fix needed.

---

### ⚠️ 4. "This means war" / Planters' House quote — handling CORRECT

**Draft says:** The exact words "have come down to us in two different versions, both written years later by men who took opposite sides, and no document from the day itself records what he actually said." Pull-quote attributed: "as remembered by a Confederate aide writing in 1886; the exact wording is disputed, but the ultimatum was real."

**What sources show:** The quote and its contested nature are confirmed. civilwaronthewesternborder.org is explicit that both versions are secondary. The Snead (1886) Confederate version and Peckham (1866) Union version are real and diverge. The substance (Lyon delivered an ultimatum, declared war, sent them out) is agreed by all sources.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The draft's handling (framed as remembered/secondary, uncertainty stated in the attribution) is correct and appropriate.

---

### ⚠️ 5. Lyon's last words ("Come on, my brave boys…") — handling CORRECT, one nuance

**Draft says:** "He is remembered to have called out as he went, 'Come on, my brave boys, I will lead you! Forward!' — words that appear on the marker at the site and have been repeated in account after account, though they come down to us as a remembered, reported line rather than anything written at the time."

**What sources show:** The quote appears in multiple secondary sources (historynet.com, historyofwar.org, NPS Civil War series sec. 11, hmdb.org marker record). The hmdb.org marker itself (University Club of Springfield, 1928) does not actually quote these words — it commemorates Lyon's death without quoting last words. However, a separate NPS/hmdb historical marker at the Bloody Hill stop *does* carry the "Come on, my brave boys" text per the NPS Bloody Hill page and the NPS Civil War series.

One additional nuance: historynet.com and at least one other source also record Lyon's actual dying words as "I am killed" (or "Lehmann, I am going"), distinct from his battle cry just before being shot. The draft does not confuse these — it says "he called out as he went" (before the fatal bullet), not as his last breath. That framing is accurate.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The draft's framing ("remembered to have called out," "marker at the site," "remembered/reported line, not written at the time") is accurate and appropriately hedged.

### ⚠️ 6. Pearce "field-glasses" quote — SOURCE IS PRIMARY

**Draft says:** `"We watched the retreating enemy through our field-glasses, and were glad to see him go." — Brig. Gen. Nicholas Bartlett Pearce, on watching the Union army withdraw.`

**Fact pack flags:** "[VERIFY — secondary source; no primary citation given in the article.]"

**What sources show:** The NPS Wilson's Creek website publishes "General N.B. Pearce's Account of the Battle" as a primary source document. The NPS fetch confirms: "Yes, Pearce uses both phrases. Here is his exact quote: 'We watched the retreating enemy through our field-glasses, and were glad to see him go.'" This appears in Pearce's own account. The Missouri Encyclopedia also attributes the quote to Pearce. The historyofwar.org secondary attribution is corroborated by the NPS primary.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED and upgraded — stronger than the pack flagged. The quote is Pearce's own words in his account, not merely a secondary attribution. No fix needed; the draft can treat this with confidence.

### ⚠️ 7. Casualty figures and rates — CONFIRMED (range approach correct)

**Draft says:** "Union losses around 1,317 and Confederate around 1,222 [Wikipedia/academic], while the American Battlefield Trust gives somewhat lower figures, roughly 1,235 Union and 1,095 Confederate…Union lost something like a quarter of every man he brought to the field — roughly 24 to 25 percent…Confederates, despite winning, lost about 12 percent."

**What sources show:**
- Wikipedia/NPS: Union 1,317 / Confederate 1,222 ✅
- ABT: Union 1,235 / Confederate 1,095 ✅
- Casualty rates: Union ~24–25%, Confederate ~12% ✅ (confirmed by essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com: "24.5% of 5,400"; ABT article: "quarter of their force"; "exceeded 12 percent")

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The range approach and the rates are both correct.

---

### ⚠️ 8. "1st Kansas Infantry — seventh-highest casualty rate of any Union regiment in the entire war"

**Draft says:** "The 1st Kansas Infantry alone suffered the seventh-highest casualty rate of any Union regiment in the entire war."

**Fact pack cites:** Wikipedia.

**What sources show:** Wikipedia's article on the 1st Regiment Kansas Volunteer Infantry does NOT make this claim. The regiment's Wikipedia page says it "suffered 106 soldiers killed in action or mortally wounded [at Wilson's Creek], one of the highest numbers of fatalities suffered by any Union infantry regiment in a single engagement" — referring to a *single battle*, not overall war casualties. No source reviewed (Wikipedia, ABT, Fox's Regimental Losses lists, civilwartalk.com rankings) confirms a "seventh-highest overall war casualty rate" for the 1st Kansas Infantry (the white regiment). This claim cannot be verified.

**Recommendation:** Soften or cut. Defensible alternatives:
- "The 1st Kansas Infantry suffered some of the heaviest single-battle losses of any Union regiment in the entire war" (supported by Wikipedia)
- Or simply cut the specific ranking claim and keep the general "among the heaviest" framing

**Status:** ⚠️ UNVERIFIED — the specific "seventh" ranking does not appear in any source checked. The pack cited Wikipedia but the Wikipedia article for this regiment does not contain this claim. SHOULD-FIX.

## Final Verdict: **PASS** (1 MUST-FIX before ship)

The draft is factually sound with one concrete error: "six-mile night march" misappropriates a pursuit distance. Fix that, soften the 1st Kansas ranking to "one of the highest casualty tolls in a single battle," and optionally sharpen "most" → "two-thirds" on the Price desertion figure. Everything else — dates, names, ranks, casualty ranges and rates, the handling of all three contested quotes, the command structure, the strategic context — checks out against authoritative sources.
