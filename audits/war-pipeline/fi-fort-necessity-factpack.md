# FACT PACK — Battle of Fort Necessity (July 3, 1754)

*French and Indian War. The ONLY permitted source. Stakes = imperial (Britain vs France) + Native-land (the Ohio Country). NO slavery angle.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- Date: July 3, 1754; ~9-10 hours of fighting; French asked for a truce in the evening (~8pm); Washington & Mackay signed the capitulation late that night/near midnight; garrison marched out the next morning, July 4, 1754.
- Location: Fort Necessity, Great Meadows, present-day Farmington, Fayette County, Pennsylvania (near Uniontown). Coords ~39.81,-79.59.
- Significance: followed Jumonville Glen (May 28, 1754) as direct French retaliation; the ONLY time in his career George Washington surrendered an army; an opening engagement of the F&I War.

## 2. BACKGROUND
- After Jumonville Glen (Tanaghrisson executed the wounded Jumonville; ~10-12 French killed, ~21 captured), Washington expected retaliation and fell back to Great Meadows.
- Built a small palisaded stockade he named "Fort Necessity" (construction ~May 31-June 3, 1754).
- Command/promotion: Washington had been Lt. Col. under Col. Joshua Fry; Fry died May 31, 1754 (fall from horse at Wills Creek); Washington succeeded to command. NUANCE: widely styled "Colonel" at the battle, but his formal full-colonel commission dated to Aug 14, 1755; some sources tag him Lt. Col. on July 3, 1754. Age 22 (firm).
- Reinforcements: the rest of the Virginia Regiment reached Great Meadows June 9, 1754 (command ~293 officers/men + nine swivel guns); plus Capt. James Mackay's Independent Company of British regulars from South Carolina (~100). Rank tension: Mackay held a royal (regular) commission and wouldn't serve under a provincial colonel — parallel commands.

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
- British/colonial: George Washington (Virginia Regiment cmdr, age 22) + Capt. James Mackay (British regular, SC). Total ~400 (~293 Virginia provincials + ~100 SC regulars).
- French/Canadian + Native: Capt. Louis Coulon de Villiers (older BROTHER of the slain Jumonville, leading a revenge expedition from Fort Duquesne) + ~100-300 Native allies. Force size CONTESTED: ~600 French/militia + ~100 Native (≈700) [WIKI/BRIT]; ~500 Canadian marines & militia + 100-300 Native [ABT/MV]; "~800 total" [BRIT text]. Working figure: ~600-700 French/Canadians plus Native warriors; ~600-800 all told.
- Washington's Native allies had melted away — he faced the battle with essentially NO Native support. Tanaghrisson had left, disillusioned, scornfully calling the fort "that little thing upon the meadow."

## 4. THE BATTLE
- Poor siting: a small circular palisaded stockade (~50 ft diameter, logs ~7 ft high) with an ammo shed and exterior entrenchments, set in an open, marshy meadow in a depression.
- It was commanded by wooded high ground within musket range; Washington misjudged the distance — the tree line was under ~100 yards away, letting the French and allies fire down from the woods/slopes while the defenders were exposed.
- The French appeared ~11am; Washington briefly formed up in the open; the French took cover and poured fire in; a daylong siege-style firefight.
- Weather: a drenching daylong rain flooded the trenches into "pools of slop and muddy water," soaked the powder, fouled/ruined many muskets; the French stayed dry behind the trees.
- By evening ~one-third of the command was killed or wounded, ammunition low, powder wet, and many men broke into the rum/alcohol supply and got drunk.
- Casualties (contested): British/colonial ~31 killed, ~70 wounded (of ~400); aggregate "~103 (≈one-third)." French/Native: 3 killed, ~17-19 wounded. (IGNORE an anomalous ABT box "1,100 British/400 French" — an outlier.)

## 5. THE SURRENDER & THE "ASSASSINATION" DOCUMENT
- In the evening Villiers (low on supplies, not eager to assault) offered terms under a white flag. Washington sent negotiators incl. his translator Jacob Van Braam.
- Van Braam was Dutch by birth (native language Dutch, not French/English); he read/translated the French Articles of Capitulation; Washington signed them in the rain, by candlelight, late that night.
- The document twice used the French word "l'assassinat" for Jumonville's death; Van Braam rendered it neutrally ("the death/killing of"); Washington unknowingly signed a document appearing to confess he had ASSASSINATED Jumonville. (18th-c. military French "assassin" could mean a soldier who attacks suddenly without warning; no Dutch cognate for Van Braam.) Washington later insisted it was a faulty translation and that he would not have signed had he understood. A French propaganda coup.
- Hostages: under the terms, two officers — Van Braam and Capt. Robert Stobo — were handed over as hostages guaranteeing the terms.

## 6. AFTERMATH
- Honors of war: the garrison marched out with colors flying, drums beating, keeping muskets and baggage; the articles granted them one swivel gun, but in practice the French kept all nine cannon (Villiers' journal: "we obliged them to leave us their Cannon, consisting of nine Pieces").
- March-out July 4, 1754, toward Wills Creek/Virginia; Native warriors and French looted the abandoned baggage as the column withdrew. The French burned Fort Necessity and returned to Fort Duquesne.
- Reception: despite the defeat Washington got a vote of thanks from the House of Burgesses; blame fell on poor supply/lack of colonial aid.
- Set up the larger British response — the Braddock Expedition of 1755 (regulars sent to retake the Forks; ended in disaster at the Monongahela).
- Date irony: surrendered the night of July 3, marched out July 4, 1754 — exactly 22 years before the Declaration of Independence.

## 7. STAKES / NATIVE POSITION
- Core contest: the Ohio Country, the Forks of the Ohio (present-day Pittsburgh); both empires wanted the land and the fur/Indian trade; France had just seized the Forks and built Fort Duquesne (1754). An imperial collision escalating into the worldwide Seven Years' War.
- Native nations caught between: Mingo (Ohio Iroquois), Lenape (Delaware), Shawnee — navigating between two empires to protect their own land and autonomy.
- Tanaghrisson (Half King, c.1700-Oct 4, 1754): backed the British, helped trigger Jumonville Glen, then became disillusioned with Washington (said Washington ignored his advice, treated Native people poorly; "that little thing upon the meadow"); withdrew before the battle; died of pneumonia Oct 4, 1754.

## 8. VERIFIED QUOTES
- Tanaghrisson: "that little thing upon the meadow" (his scorn for the fort). VERIFIED.
- French stated motive (surrender parley/French account): to "avenge the murder of one of our officers ... and also to prevent any establishment being made on the lands of my King." VERIFIED to ABT.
- Surrender terms: troops to march out "with Drums beating, and one Swivel Gun, being willing thereby to convince them, that we treat them as Friends"; Villiers' journal "we obliged them to leave us their Cannon, consisting of nine Pieces." VERIFIED (primary docs).
- Contrecoeur's recruitment appeal to Native nations (at Fort Duquesne, not the battlefield): "The English have murdered my children, my heart is sick." VERIFIED to ABT.
- DO NOT USE the "bullets whistle" quote here — it belongs to Jumonville Glen, not Fort Necessity.
- Washington's later "error of translation" claim — paraphrase, [UNVERIFIED] as a direct quote.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. French/Native force size ~600-800 (use range; outnumbered Washington's ~400 by well over half). 2. British casualties ~31 killed/70 wounded (or aggregate ~103/one-third); French 3 killed/17-19 wounded; ignore the "1,100" outlier. 3. Translation mechanism hedge: signed doc used "l'assassinat," Washington didn't understand it as "assassination," later disputed it — keep word-by-word mechanism hedged. 4. Washington's rank/title (functionally commander/"Colonel"; formal commission Aug 1755; age 22 firm). 5. Nine swivel guns; terms allowed one, French took all nine. 6. "Charming in the sound" belongs to Jumonville Glen — don't misattribute.
