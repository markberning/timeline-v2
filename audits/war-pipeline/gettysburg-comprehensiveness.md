## 3. MUST-ADD List

These are genuine essential gaps — the telling is incomplete or misleading without them. Listed in order of severity.

---

### MUST-ADD 1: Ewell's decision not to attack Cemetery Hill on Day 1 evening

**Why it matters:** This is one of the war's most discussed "if only" moments. After driving the Union back through the town, Lee sent Ewell discretionary orders to take Cemetery Hill "if practicable." Ewell did not attack. The Union spent the night digging in on heights they might have lost. Whether Ewell was right (the hill bristled with guns, his men were exhausted) or wrong (Jackson would have gone) is genuinely disputed — but the decision existed, was consequential, and has shaped how Gettysburg has been argued about ever since. The draft's Day 1 section ends with the Union on the heights and Lee resolving to attack — but it never mentions that a Confederate commander had a chance to take those heights before they were fortified, and chose not to. This is not a minor detail. It is why the July 2–3 attacks were so costly: the Union had a night to dig in that they might not have had.

**The misleading-by-omission test:** A reader who finishes the draft will wonder why the Confederates didn't just take the high ground while the Union was retreating in disorder. The answer is that they could have tried, one general had the discretion to try, and he didn't — and that absence of action determined the next two days. Without this, the Union's occupation of the fishhook looks like pure luck rather than the product of a contested Confederate decision.

**Fix required:** A paragraph in the Day 1 section (`mcpherson`), placed after Hancock holds the heights, noting that Ewell had the opportunity and the discretionary orders and did not attack. Acknowledge the genuine historical debate about whether he was right.

---

### MUST-ADD 2: How Lee learned the Union army was near — Harrison the spy

**Why it matters:** The draft correctly establishes that Stuart was absent and Lee was blind. It then cuts to the battle starting with Heth's probe. But it never explains HOW Lee discovered the Union army was close enough to fight — the event that caused him to concentrate his scattered forces. The answer is Henry Thomas Harrison, a paid spy working for Longstreet, who walked into Lee's camp on the night of June 28 and reported that the Army of the Potomac had crossed the Potomac and was near. This is the direct cause of the Confederate concentration that made the battle possible. Without it, the narrative has a gap: Lee is blind — then suddenly there's a battle. How?

**The misleading-by-omission test:** The setup in `setting` establishes Stuart's absence as the central intelligence failure of the campaign. It doesn't resolve it. A reader is left not knowing what broke the blindness. Harrison is the answer, and he also adds a dimension to the Longstreet-vs-Lee dynamic: it was Longstreet's own intelligence network that saved Lee from marching into a catastrophe.

**Fix required:** One sentence or short passage in `setting` (or the transition into `mcpherson`) noting that Lee's blindness was broken not by his cavalry but by a spy, and that this intelligence triggered the concentration at Gettysburg.

---

### MUST-ADD 3: The "Pickett's Charge" name is a misnomer — Pettigrew and Trimble's men

**Why it matters:** The draft names all three division commanders (Pickett, Pettigrew, Trimble) — which is correct and better than most tellings. But it never notes that the popular name "Pickett's Charge" is attached to an assault where Pickett commanded only 3 of the 11 brigades, and where Pettigrew's and Trimble's divisions suffered casualties broadly comparable to Pickett's. Pettigrew lost roughly 2,700 men; Trimble lost 885; Pickett lost 2,655. The name is a Lost Cause artifact: Pickett's Virginians were more celebrated in Southern memory; both Pettigrew (killed shortly after) and Trimble (captured and spent the rest of the war out of action) couldn't contest the naming. The draft's framing — "Pickett's Charge" as the section title, with a passing mention of the other two divisions — inadvertently perpetuates the distortion.

**The misleading-by-omission test:** A reader comes away thinking Pickett's men carried the attack; in reality it was three divisions from two corps. More importantly, the North Carolina, Tennessee, Mississippi, and Alabama men in Pettigrew's and Trimble's divisions who walked into the same killing ground are effectively erased from the story, their sacrifice swallowed by a Virginian's name.

**Fix required:** A sentence in the `pickett` section acknowledging that Pickett's name stuck to all three divisions, and that Pettigrew's and Trimble's men suffered roughly equal losses in the same ground.

---

### MUST-ADD 4: Long-term strategic consequence — Lee never invaded again

**Why it matters:** The `aftermath` section correctly calls Gettysburg and Vicksburg together "the hinge of the war." But it doesn't state the specific military consequence: the Army of Northern Virginia never invaded the North again. After July 1863 it was a purely defensive army, fighting in Virginia until Appomattox. This is the thing that makes Gettysburg THE turning point rather than merely A turning point. The South fought two more hard years, but they were years fighting the clock on Virginia soil, not years that could produce a Northern capitulation.

**The misleading-by-omission test:** The draft says the Confederacy "started running out of time" — but doesn't explain the mechanism. What changed strategically? The answer is: Lee lost the offensive option. Without stating that, the "turning point" label feels asserted rather than shown.

**Fix required:** One sentence in `aftermath` — after Vicksburg — noting that Lee's army never crossed into the North again. It never had to be long.

---

### MUST-ADD 5: The Confederate experience of the retreat from Pickett's Charge

**Why it matters:** The draft describes Pickett's Charge from the Union side with great vividness (waiting behind the wall, the grandeur and terror of the advancing lines, then the killing). The Confederate soldiers walking BACK across the same three-quarters of a mile — under fire, carrying wounded, in full view of what they had walked into — is mentioned in one clause ("came back, those who came back at all, in ones and twos, bleeding, some carrying wounded friends, all of them under fire"). That clause is good but thin. This is the draft's weakest dual-POV point: the Confederate subjective experience of the charge itself — the walk in, the canister, the moment at the wall — is narrated from the outside (Union perspective on the grandeur of the advance) rather than from inside the Confederate ranks.

**The misleading-by-omission test:** This is the draft's stated signature feature: dual POV. The charge section is significantly weighted toward the Union perspective. The Confederate soldiers who made the walk are present only as a spectacle; their terror, their exhaustion, and their experience of the killing ground are not shown.

**Fix required:** Two to three sentences (or a short subsection) inside `pickett` giving the Confederate soldier's experience of the approach and the killing: what it felt like to walk into artillery fire, close up the gaps, and keep going. Eyewitness accounts exist ("the very ground shook," "it was simply awful"). This doesn't need to be long — it needs to exist.

## 4. SHOULD-CONSIDER List (enriching, not blocking)

These are not essential omissions. A fair reader would not call the telling incomplete without them. They are listed in priority order.

**A. Armistead and Hancock's pre-war friendship**
The two men were close friends before the war. At the Angle, Armistead — who had just led men over a stone wall into Union guns — fell wounded at a Union cannon. A Union officer brought his personal effects to Hancock, who had himself been wounded nearby on the same day. The Killer Angels and the 1993 film made this famous, and modern historians have walked back the sentimentalism (the friendship was real but the deathbed reunion was not), but the underlying fact — that two old friends ended up on opposite sides of the most consequential 20 minutes of the war — is real and humanizing. The draft mentions Armistead; noting Hancock was his pre-war friend would add one of the war's true human ironies to the high-water mark.

**B. Meade's council of war on the evening of July 2**
After Day 2's attacks bent the line without breaking it, Meade convened his corps commanders at the Leister house. They voted unanimously to stay and fight. This is the decision that kept the Union army at Gettysburg for Day 3 — and it was a collective decision made by exhausted generals who had just survived the worst afternoon of the war. The draft says nothing about Meade's command process between Day 2 and Day 3. Adding a sentence would round out Meade's portrait: not just the defensive engineer holding the line, but the commander who consulted his generals and made the right call.

**C. East Cavalry Field — Stuart vs. Custer, July 3**
Stuart's arc: absent for the whole campaign, he shows up the evening of July 2. On July 3, simultaneously with Pickett's Charge, Stuart led three brigades around the Union rear, presumably to exploit any breakthrough. George Custer (age 23, newly promoted to brigadier general that week) blocked him. Stuart was repulsed. The Union rear was secure. This closes the Stuart story: he was absent when Lee needed him most, and when he finally came back, he was turned back by a 23-year-old. Not essential but a clean narrative arc.

**D. Jennie Wade**
The only civilian directly killed — struck by a stray bullet while baking bread on July 3. One sentence. The story of the town's civilian population is currently reduced to "families watching from their gardens." Jennie Wade gives it a name and a death.

**E. The months-long aftermath in Gettysburg**
The stench of the dead persisted until October. Residents complained through the fall. When Lincoln gave the Address on November 19, animal carcasses were still being burned. The contrast — the high eloquence of the Address and the still-present smell of death on the ground beneath the speakers — is one of the most striking facts about the day. The draft's `aftermath` section opens beautifully with rain on July 4 but doesn't carry the human cost of the town's experience beyond the photographs. This is optional but would deepen the Address section considerably.

**F. The Lost Cause and Longstreet as scapegoat**
After the war, Jubal Early led a campaign to blame Longstreet for Gettysburg — primarily because Longstreet had accepted Reconstruction and supported civil rights, making him a convenient target. This distorted Civil War memory for a century. The draft gives Longstreet full and sympathetic treatment as the man who was right — but it doesn't note that he was later blamed for the defeat, which is why many readers come to the battle thinking Longstreet was the villain. A sentence would inoculate against that mythology. NOT a blocker but historically significant.

**G. Pettigrew: the human detail of why his men were in the charge at all**
Pettigrew's division was fighting as replacements — their original commander Heth had been wounded on Day 1, Pender (Trimble's division) had been mortally wounded on Day 2. Both substitute commanders, Pettigrew and Trimble, were leading divisions they had commanded for less than 24 hours when they walked into Pickett's Charge. This adds a layer to the already-grim arithmetic of the charge.

## 5. Fact Pack Blind Spots

Things the fact pack itself does not address, which the draft therefore cannot include:

**5a. Harrison the spy — entirely absent from the fact pack**
The pack (§1) says Stuart was absent and Lee was blind. It never mentions Henry Thomas Harrison, Longstreet's paid spy who reported on June 28 that the Army of the Potomac had crossed the Potomac and was approaching. This is a factual gap in the pack. Without Harrison, the pack's narrative of Lee's blindness has no resolution: Lee is blind, then the battle starts. The pack does not give the author the tools to explain how the concentration at Gettysburg happened.

**5b. Ewell's decision on Day 1 evening — entirely absent from the fact pack**
The pack's §2 covers Day 1 through Hancock holding Cemetery Hill and Lee resolving to attack. It does not mention Ewell's discretionary orders or his decision not to assault Cemetery Hill. This is a significant omission from the fact pack. The "if practicable" order and Ewell's choice shaped the entire subsequent battle. The authors could not include it because the pack doesn't give it to them.

**5c. The Confederate kidnapping of free Black Pennsylvanians**
This is not in the fact pack at all. It is well-documented that Confederate units systematically seized free Black people in Maryland and Pennsylvania during the Gettysburg campaign — including organized roundups with participation by senior officers including Pickett, Longstreet, and Rodes. Hundreds, possibly more than a thousand, were sent south into slavery. This dimension of the invasion is entirely absent from the pack's §1 (Campaign background). It cannot appear in the draft because the pack doesn't include it. This is the pack's most serious substantive blind spot.

**5d. Stuart's arc closure — East Cavalry Field**
The pack's §1 establishes Stuart absent. The pack's §5 mentions Stuart escorting the wagon train of wounded during the retreat. But the pack has no entry for East Cavalry Field (July 3), where Stuart attempted to get behind Union lines during Pickett's Charge and was repulsed by Gregg and Custer. Stuart's cavalry arc — absent at the start, attempting to make good at the end — is not closed in the pack.

**5e. Meade's council of war July 2 evening**
Not in the fact pack. The decision to stay and fight was a collective command decision that preceded Day 3. It doesn't appear in §3 (Day 2) or §4 (Day 3). The authors cannot give Meade credit for it.

**5f. Long-term strategic consequence (ANV never invaded again)**
The pack (§0) says Gettysburg is "widely considered the war's turning point — paired with Vicksburg." It doesn't state the specific mechanism: that Lee never invaded the North again and the Army of Northern Virginia fought the remainder of the war on the defensive. This is the kind of contextual sentence the pack should have given the authors for the `aftermath` section.

**5g. "Pickett's Charge" as a misnomer / Pettigrew and Trimble's proportionate losses**
The pack names all three divisions (§4) and gives Pickett's casualty figure separately. But it doesn't note that the popular name attaches Pickett's Virginians to a charge two-thirds of whose men weren't his, or that the name is a post-war construction. The authors named the section "Pickett's Charge" (which is fine — it's the name everyone knows) but had no pack material to note the distortion.

**5h. The Armistead/Hancock friendship**
The pack covers Armistead at the Angle thoroughly. It does not mention that Armistead and Hancock were pre-war friends. The authors could not add this human dimension because the pack didn't give it to them.

---

## 6. One-Line Verdict

**HAS ESSENTIAL GAPS** — the draft is strong and its voice is excellent, but it is missing four blocking items: Ewell's Day 1 non-attack (which makes the fishhook's existence look accidental rather than contested), Harrison the spy (which leaves a hole in Lee's blindness arc), Pettigrew and Trimble's proportionate role in "Pickett's Charge" (which erases half the men who made the charge), and the permanent shift of the Army of Northern Virginia to the defensive (which is the specific mechanism that earns the "turning point" label). The Confederate kidnapping of free Black Pennsylvanians, fully absent from both draft and fact pack, is the pack's most significant substantive blind spot.
