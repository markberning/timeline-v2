# lee-attacks | Lee Takes the Offensive

In the last days of June 1862, the Confederate capital could hear the United States Army coming. Maj. Gen. George B. McClellan (North), commanding the Army of the Potomac, had spent the spring creeping up the Virginia Peninsula — the long neck of land between the James and York Rivers, southeast of Richmond — and by late May his men were close enough to see the church spires of Richmond, roughly 5 to 6 miles (8 to 10 km) off. He had something like 100,000 men. The Confederacy had a capital about to fall.

And underneath the maneuvering was the thing the whole war was actually about. The fight for Richmond was, at bottom, a fight over a slaveholding republic — a country of roughly 4 million enslaved people whose forced labor was the South's economy, and whose continued bondage was the reason the Confederate states had walked out of the Union in the first place. Take Richmond in the summer of 1862 and the rebellion might end before it became something larger. Save it, and slavery's republic got to keep fighting. That was the prize on the table outside the city.

The man who decided not to let it fall had only just taken the job. At the Battle of Seven Pines (also called Fair Oaks) on May 31 and June 1, the Confederate commander, Gen. Joseph E. Johnston, was badly wounded, and President Jefferson Davis handed the army to Gen. Robert E. Lee (South). Lee renamed it the Army of Northern Virginia and did the math: he could not win a siege against a bigger, better-supplied army parked outside his capital. Sitting still meant losing slowly. So he chose the opposite of sitting still. Within weeks of taking command, he attacked.

_ITALIC_: He had been a general for less than a month, and he was about to throw the largest army the South had ever fielded straight at the larger army in front of it.

## The Seven Days | Mechanicsville and the plan to crush Porter

On June 26, Lee opened what history would call the Seven Days Battles — a week of near-continuous fighting from June 25 to July 1 — with an assault at Beaver Dam Creek, near the village of Mechanicsville, just northeast of Richmond. It did not go well. The division of Maj. Gen. A.P. Hill (South) charged across the creek and was shot to pieces for no ground gained, somewhere around 1,000 to 1,500 men down. As a tactic, Mechanicsville was a failure.

As psychology, it worked perfectly — on the wrong man. McClellan, watching the Confederates suddenly turn aggressive, began that very day to plan a "change of base": army slang for picking up his whole supply line and shifting it somewhere safer, in his case from the York River side over to the James River to the south, where U.S. Navy gunboats could cover him. In plain terms, the man with the bigger army started planning his retreat the day the smaller army attacked him.

Lee's actual plan was sharper than Mechanicsville made it look. McClellan's army was split awkwardly by the Chickahominy River, a slow, swampy stream that wandered east of Richmond. Most of McClellan's force sat south of the river; one piece sat exposed on the north bank — the V Corps under Maj. Gen. Fitz John Porter (North). (A corps was an army's largest building block, on the order of 20,000 to 35,000 men, itself made of divisions of several thousand, those made of brigades of a few thousand.) Lee meant to leave only a thin screen of troops south of the river to bluff McClellan's main body, then throw the bulk of his army across the Chickahominy and destroy Porter's isolated corps before McClellan figured out that the force in front of his capital was mostly a bluff. Crush Porter, threaten McClellan's supply line, and the whole invasion might unravel.

The plan had one moving part that refused to move on schedule. To turn Porter's right flank — the exposed northern end of his line, the side you hit to roll a position up from its edge — Lee was counting on Maj. Gen. Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson (South), marching in from the Shenandoah Valley to the west. Jackson was the most feared marcher in either army. And he was late. He had left the day before reportedly six hours behind schedule, his men worn down from a brutal Valley campaign, slowed by burned bridges, mud, enemy skirmishers, and a supply train that kept failing. His tardiness was already bending Lee's plan out of shape before Gaines' Mill even began. It would do worse the next day.

### Meanwhile in Washington | McClellan sees an army that isn't there

McClellan's caution was not random. It was fed. His intelligence came largely from Allan Pinkerton's detective agency, and Pinkerton's estimates were wildly, catastrophically wrong: McClellan believed Lee commanded something like 180,000 to 200,000 men. Lee actually had about 92,000 — possibly less than half what McClellan thought he faced. So the general with the bigger army spent the entire campaign certain he was outnumbered, and acted accordingly: defending, hedging, planning his retreat, feeding his subordinate Porter only a trickle of help. The phantom army in McClellan's head shaped Gaines' Mill as much as any real soldier on the field.

# boatswains-swamp | The Line at Boatswain's Swamp

After the bruising at Beaver Dam Creek, Porter pulled his V Corps back to ground he could actually defend, and he chose it beautifully. His line ran behind Boatswain's Swamp (also called Boatswain's Creek), a boggy ravine that curved in a long semicircle, roughly 1.5 to 2 miles (2.5 to 3 km) end to end, around the plateau where a farmhouse called the Watt House stood. Any Confederate who wanted Porter had to cross open ground, slither down into the muck of the swamp, and then climb a steep hillside — about 50 feet (15 m) of rise — straight into massed gunfire.

And the gunfire was massed. Porter stacked his defense in three tiers: a first line down at the creek bottom with sharpshooters firing from behind felled trees, a second line halfway up the slope, and a third line of rifle pits along the plateau's crest. On top of the plateau he arrayed 96 cannon, with still more batteries firing in support from across the Chickahominy to the south. Morell's division held his left, on Turkey Hill; Sykes's division held the right; McCall's division waited in reserve along the crest. It was, in short, a wall — and Lee was about to run his army into it.

The bitter joke was that Lee did not know the wall was there. Boatswain's Swamp did not appear on Confederate maps at all. Lee's whole scheme assumed his troops would come around and hit Porter's flank; instead, because the swamp pushed Porter's line out farther than anyone on the Confederate side realized, his men kept marching straight into the front of a fortified position that, on paper, shouldn't have existed.

## June 27 | Hours of charging uphill into the guns

The attacks came in pieces, and the pieces broke one at a time.

Around noon, the division of Maj. Gen. D.H. Hill (South) reached the crossroads of Old Cold Harbor to the north and ran into Porter's line where it expected to find Porter's flank — the first sign that the map was lying. Around 2:30 p.m., A.P. Hill (South) sent his roughly 12,000-man division (grandly nicknamed the Light Division) straight at Boatswain's Swamp in the first major assault of the day. His brigades crossed the open ground, went down into the swamp, started up the hill, and were torn apart. In about two hours, A.P. Hill's division lost more than 2,000 men and gained essentially nothing.

Around 4 p.m., the division of Maj. Gen. Richard S. Ewell (South) fed into the same meat grinder — three brigades in ferocious, doomed charges. Ewell had his horse shot out from under him and kept directing the fight on foot. His attacks stalled too, with heavy loss. From the Union side, a soldier later described the volume of fire by saying the air "was too full of lead for standing room." Whoever exactly said it, the picture was accurate: men were charging uphill into a hailstorm and dying in rows for ground they couldn't hold.

_ITALIC_: This was the cost of Lee's missing map — assault after assault thrown in one at a time, each one bleeding out alone instead of all of them landing together.

Porter felt the pressure mounting. Around 5 p.m. he wired McClellan that he was "pressed hard, very hard." McClellan, directing the whole battle by telegraph from his headquarters south of the river — he never once appeared on the field in person — sent Porter two brigades from Sumner's II Corps. They had a long way to come and arrived roughly three hours later, only after the crisis had nearly arrived on its own.

And the one blow that might have ended it early never came. Jackson (South) was late again. His column had been led down the wrong road, costing an hour-long countermarch, and then dogged by burned bridges, roadblocks, and sharpshooters. When he finally reached Old Cold Harbor, he found, as one account put it, the Yankees in line facing him — not the open flank Lee had promised him. Then, through a tangle of miscommunication, he sat and waited for orders while the battle roared a short distance away. His Confederate artillery chief, Col. Edward Porter Alexander, said it flatly after the war: "Had Jackson attacked when he first arrived, or during A.P. Hill's attack, we would have had an easy victory." Instead, the South paid for Porter's wall in full, one division at a time, all afternoon.

### Meanwhile in the rest of the Seven Days | The bloodiest field yet

By the time the fighting at Boatswain's Swamp had run its course, it had become something grim and historic. By the morning of June 28, Gaines' Mill stood as the second-bloodiest battle in American history to that point, behind only Shiloh that April. Nine Union regimental commanders were killed or mortally wounded here. The 9th Massachusetts alone lost 206 men, the most of any Northern regiment on the field. And the campaign wasn't close to finished — this was only the third day of seven, with Savage's Station, Glendale, and Malvern Hill still ahead, the whole Army of the Potomac steadily backing toward the James.

# hoods-breakthrough | Hood Breaks the Line

For most of an afternoon, Lee's army had thrown itself at Porter's wall and bounced off, division by division. Late in the day, with less than an hour of daylight left, Lee finally did the thing he'd been failing to do all afternoon: he hit the whole line at once.

Around 7 p.m. he launched a general, coordinated assault across his entire front — roughly 32,000 men in about 16 brigades, going forward together. It was the largest single assault Lee would order in the whole war, and likely the largest Confederate assault of the Civil War, period. (For scale: the famous Pickett's Charge at Gettysburg a year later used about 12,500 men; Hood's later attack at Franklin, about 20,000.) After a day of piecemeal failure, the dam broke all at once.

## Dusk | The Texas Brigade goes through

The man who found the crack was Brig. Gen. John Bell Hood (South), leading the Texas Brigade as part of Whiting's division. Hood took his old regiment, the 4th Texas, plus companies of the 18th Georgia, and angled toward a spot across the swamp where the ground looked just slightly more forgiving. Then he did something that took iron discipline: he sent his men forward without firing a shot. No stopping to shoot, no losing momentum — just fixed bayonets, the Rebel Yell, and a flat-out rush down into the creek and up the deadly hillside that had swallowed assault after assault all day.

It cost them horribly on the way in. A 4th Texas private later described the fire pouring into them "with increasing fury, cutting down our ranks like wheat." But the unfired rush carried them into and over Porter's first line at the bottom of the ridge. And then the Union defense worked against itself: with their own retreating men streaming back in front of them, the Federal second and third lines couldn't shoot cleanly, and Hood's Texans drove through all three tiers and onto the plateau, capturing 14 cannon at the crest. By the time the battle ended, the Confederates had taken around 22 Union guns in all.

The price was savage. Hood's part of the line — Whiting's division — lost 1,018 men in the assault. The 4th Texas alone lost about 570, including every one of its field-grade officers. With Jackson (South) at last ordered into the fight, the final pressure came down across the line; reportedly he sent word to sweep the field with the bayonet. Surveying what Hood's men had done, Jackson is reported to have said simply that the men who carried this position were soldiers indeed.

_ITALIC_: A day of failed charges, and then one brigade walked up the same killing slope holding its fire, and the wall came down.

## The collapse and the consequence | Porter retreats, McClellan quits Richmond

Once the line broke, Porter's V Corps came apart in the dusk, men scattering back across open fields toward the Chickahominy. Two whole Union regiments were surrounded and surrendered. A desperate Union cavalry countercharge — about 200 troopers under Brig. Gen. Philip St. George Cooke (North), who happened to be the father-in-law of Confederate cavalryman J.E.B. Stuart — was thrown in to buy time and was driven back with roughly a quarter of its men cut down.

But darkness, which had failed Lee all day, now saved Porter. With night coming on, the Confederates couldn't press the pursuit that might have destroyed the V Corps outright. Through the night of June 27 to 28, with Sykes's Regulars covering the rear, Porter pulled his battered corps south across the narrow Chickahominy bridges, and Union engineers burned the bridges behind them.

The butcher's bill tells which kind of victory this was. The two sides together lost roughly 15,000 men. The Union total ran about 6,800 to 7,300 — but a large share of that, somewhere around 2,800, were captured, swept up when the line collapsed. The Confederate total was heavier, around 8,000, and far bloodier in killed and wounded (about 1,483 killed and 6,402 wounded, against just over 100 captured). Lee won by spending more men than his enemy — a pattern that would shadow his generalship for the rest of the war.

And it worked. McClellan, already half-decided to retreat and now more convinced than ever that he faced a vastly larger army, gave up the campaign. He ordered his whole Army of the Potomac to "change base" — to abandon the advance on Richmond and fall back southeast to Harrison's Landing on the James River, under the protection of the navy's guns. The church spires he'd come within a few miles of would stay Confederate. Lee had his first victory: costly, ugly, imperfectly run — and decisive. Gaines' Mill saved Richmond for 1862 and handed the war in the East to the man who would carry it, from here, all the way to Antietam and beyond.

### Meanwhile in McClellan's tent | The telegram too hot to send

Just after midnight, McClellan wired Washington his account of the day, ending with a line so insubordinate that the chief censor of the Military Telegraph Service quietly deleted it before it ever reached Secretary of War Edwin Stanton: "If I save this army now I tell you plainly that I owe no thanks to you or any other persons in Washington — you have done your best to sacrifice this army." Stanton and Lincoln never saw those words; the country wouldn't learn they'd been cut until a telegrapher revealed it in 1907. The general who outnumbered Lee two pieces to one had just lost his nerve, his campaign, and very nearly his manners — and was already blaming everyone but himself.
