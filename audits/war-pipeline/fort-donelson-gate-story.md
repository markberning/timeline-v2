## the-rivers | The Rivers into the South — GOOD

**What works.** The opening paragraph earns its keep: "You could float" is the voice at its sharpest, and "two of them lying in plain sight" frames the whole campaign as an embarrassing Confederate oversight. The Fort Henry subsection is clean and fast — the ironclad definition lands, the "boats win" payoff sets up the reversal properly. The weather beat ("it is not a figure of speech and it is not a small detail") follows the Antietam model of slowing down at a human cost to make the reader feel the consequence.

**What doesn't.**

1. **Hook is soft.** "The United States went looking for a way into the South, and found two of them" is adequate but not a pull. Compare Antietam's opener: "the Civil War was going badly for the United States, and Robert E. Lee decided to make it worse." That line generates forward motion from the first clause. The rivers opener states a situation; the Antietam opener creates a character doing something sharp. The rivers draft can hit this register. Fix: lead with the irony — two major rivers ran straight into the Confederate heartland, and the South had posted one smallish fort on each of them, and one of those forts was already half underwater. The strategic absurdity is the hook.

2. **The "western strategy / A twelve-mile march" subsection is the weakest writing in the entire draft.** It narrates logistics (march route, skirmish dates, arrival order) with almost nothing at stake. The prose knows the weather is coming and delays anyway: "The shape of the thing was set: army on the land side from the west, gunboats coming up the river from the north." That sentence adds zero human tension; it is a caption. The weather twist is genuinely strong but the approach to it is slow.

   Beat-level fix (no invented facts): Move the payoff image — "soldiers threw away their overcoats on the march to lighten the load" — to the first sentence of the subsection. Start with the coats, not with logistics. "Grant moved his force 12 miles overland on February 11–13, and his men were so certain of the warm spell that they threw away their overcoats on the march." Then land the storm. The moral snaps into place without the approach road.

3. **The slavery paragraph.** The framing content ("the war was finally about") is earned and right to include, but the phrase "a plan to crack that cordon open, sail up into the South, and start strangling the country built on slavery" is piling on three images at once. Pick one and let it do the work. The Antietam equivalent handles this with one turn: "a fight to hold the Union back together became, openly and on the record, a fight to end slavery." Single move, clean.

4. **Johnston "Meanwhile" sidebar** is a solid orientation tool but the line "pull the hinge out and the whole line had nothing to hold it together" slightly doubles the preceding "a mile wide and an inch deep" metaphor. One of them should go.

## the-gunboats | The Gunboats Repulsed — STRONG

This is the best-written section in the draft. It earns STRONG on three grounds.

**Hook and stakes.** "This is the reversal at the heart of the whole battle: the same weapon that had won the first fort almost by itself got beaten back from the second one, and beaten back hard." That is the Antietam-class setup — it tells you what to watch for and why it matters before a single gun fires. "Grant wasn't even there to see it. He was downriver in a meeting, which would become a pattern." Perfect. Two sentences, two things established, one of them ominous.

**The plunging-fire mechanics.** The explanation of the water batteries — two tiers, high bluff, plunging shot, strong current — is one of the draft's best paragraphs. It does what the civ narratives call for: explains the *mechanism*, not just the outcome. The reader understands exactly why the ironclads were helpless, which makes the hit counts land harder than a list of numbers alone would.

**The hit-count beat.** "The numbers are almost comic if you forget there were men inside" is the kind of aside that makes the Antietam voice work. The zero-casualty contrast lands. The pull-quote and italic coda ("answered with a shrug") is strong.

**Minor note only:** "Grant wasn't even there... which would become a pattern" sets up a pattern it delivers on once (the breakout). That payoff is not quite earned for a "which would become a pattern" claim. Either cut to "Grant wasn't even there — he was downriver in a meeting" (enough), or be ready to keep paying it off in the-breakout with the same wry note.

## the-breakout | The Escape That Wasn't — NEEDS WORK

This is the richest material in the entire battle — a perfect trap escaped, then voluntarily surrendered — and the draft never quite makes the reader feel the agony of it. It describes the escape instead of inhabiting it.

**The core problem: the play-by-play is the dominant mode.** Paragraphs run time-stamped without human consequence between them: "~5 a.m. Forrest cavalry stirred up a diversionary skirmish... around 7 a.m. Pillow threw his full assault... by early afternoon the Confederates had shoved the Union right back roughly 1 to 2 miles... The thing the whole plan was for had happened." That sequence is accurate and clear, but it reads like a briefing, not a story. Antietam's Cornfield covers similar move-by-move chaos and never feels like a briefing because it keeps dipping back to individual human moments (Hood's men pulled from their half-cooked breakfast; Hooker shot through the foot; Mansfield dying with white beard and long service). The-breakout has almost none of those.

**The Pillow withdrawal — a controversy presented as a briefing.** The Pillow-withdrawal paragraph is the hinge the whole battle turns on, and the draft presents it methodically but without the emotional weight it needs. The reader is told this is "the most argued-over decision of the battle" and then given Pillow's stated reasoning, Floyd's acceptance, Buckner's protest, and a historian consensus label. That is a table of facts, not a story. The Confederate soldier at the center of it — who has just bled for his escape and is now watching the road close again — is entirely absent.

Beat-level fix: The "Meanwhile in the fort" sidebar is the emotional note the breakout section is missing — and it's buried at the end as a sidebar. The image "some of them had been close enough to the open road to see it" is the strongest human line in this entire section. Move that beat — or a version of it — into the body of the breakout narrative when the withdrawal order lands. "Around 1:00 p.m., after hours of fighting, with the road to Nashville standing open, Pillow ordered the men who had just paid in blood for it to march back into the trenches they had left that morning." Then the soldier in the ranks, close enough to see daylight. That is the gut-punch the scene needs. The sidebar still works after.

**Grant's return — good instincts, under-executed.** The knapsacks deduction is one of the great command moments of the western war, and the draft knows it. But the paragraph buries it in a subordinate clause: "He grasped that the enemy was attempting escape, not victory, and that the side which moved first now would win the day." The deduction is told, not shown. The Antietam model for this kind of moment: "He had the map to the other man's army. Then he sat on it." Short, brutal, the reader feels it. The draft narrates Grant's reasoning. Let Grant act on it first and reason second.

Beat-level fix: Start with the action, not the cognition. "He rode back and he attacked." Then the knapsacks as the explanation: the captured men were carrying three days' rations packed for a march — this army was trying to leave, not press an assault, and whoever moved first now would win. Tighten and sharpen the command-voice quote from "several slightly different versions" into a single clean paraphrase.

**The Smith attack.** "All has failed on our right — you must take Fort Donelson" is the right pull-quote. But the counterattack sequence that follows is a list of division assignments again (McClernand rally, Wallace shift, Smith attack, 30th Tennessee, 5:30 p.m., outer entrenchments). It's accurate but the same briefing-mode problem. One concrete detail from Smith's charge would anchor the whole thing — the draft has none.

## unconditional-surrender | Unconditional Surrender — NEEDS WORK

The material here is extraordinary: Floyd and Pillow's midnight vanishing act, Forrest's cavalry riding out through icy water, and then the most famous exchange in the western theater — delivered between two men with the most unlikely personal history. The draft narrates all of it competently and humanizes almost none of it.

**The generals who fled — too clean, too fast.** Floyd and Pillow each get a paragraph with a stated motive and an exit. The comic/pathetic dimension of the scene is that two generals sequentially took command for the sole purpose of handing it off, and the draft observes this with a single summary sentence: "Two generals, one after the other, took charge of a surrendering army for just long enough to hand it to the next man and disappear." That summary is the right punch line. The problem is the setup is too businesslike to make the punch land. Where is the spectacle of these men — the senior commanders of an army — scrambling in the dark for boats while their soldiers slept in the trenches? The Antietam voice would have stayed with the absurdity one beat longer.

Beat-level fix: Let Pillow get a line. This is a man who, twelve hours earlier, had won the escape his army needed and then given it back. Now he is climbing into a small boat in the dark to avoid being captured for a surrender he helped engineer. The draft has the facts but no irony pressed into them. Something like: "Pillow, who twelve hours earlier had opened the road home and ordered it closed, chose a small boat and a dark crossing instead of a handshake with Grant."

**Forrest — the buried payoff.** "The escape routes were still open. Forrest's clean getaway is the proof of what the night cost the rest of the garrison." That sentence is the point. But it arrives in the middle of the paragraph after a summary of the route out, and it doesn't stop to let the reader feel it. Forrest's ride through Lick Creek is one of the defining images of the whole battle — icy, waist-deep water, 700 horsemen, no enemy, clean exit. The draft knows this is damning but tells the reader it's damning rather than letting the image do the work.

Beat-level fix: End the Forrest paragraph on the image, not the analysis. "They rode out through icy water up to their waists, found no enemy on the way, and were gone. The road home had been right there all night." Let the reader sit with that before the analytical sentence follows.

**The Grant–Buckner friendship — the best beat, slightly muffled.** The personal history is excellent and the draft handles it well up to a point. West Point, Mexico, Popocatépetl, the Astor House loan — these land. But the transition from that history to Grant's reply is too fast. "Grant did not let the friendship soften the terms by an inch. His reply is one of the most famous lines in American military history." That transition tells the reader the significance before delivering it, which dilutes the landing. The Antietam model lets the quote hit bare, then explains. Let the reader read "No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender" cold, before the label.

Beat-level fix: Cut "His reply is one of the most famous lines in American military history" entirely. Move straight from "Now the broke ex-soldier Buckner had bailed out was the general holding his army by the throat" to the blockquote. The famous-line label is unnecessary — the reader will know why it was famous.

**The "What it bought" subsection — good but back-loaded.** The scale and strategic consequence are well-stated. The cigar story is exactly the kind of informal voice detail that should be in every section. The single problem here is the section's coda: the italic "Two generals fled, one rode out through the ice, and the man left holding the bag was the old friend who had once paid Grant's hotel bill." That is a strong line, but it is placed AFTER the strategic-consequence section, which means the narrative spends paragraphs on army counts and theater maps and then tries to return to the personal. Consider moving that coda to the end of the Buckner-handshake scene (before "What it bought"), where it serves as the emotional period on the surrender itself. Let "What it bought" close on a forward-looking note about Grant, not backward on the personal irony.

## Cross-Cutting Notes

**Play-by-play tedium (the #1 failure mode):** the-breakout is the main offender. The pattern is: timestamp → action → timestamp → action, without the human dimension that makes tactical detail feel like story. The Antietam Cornfield section is the corrective model: same time-stamped chaos, but it anchors every fifteen-minute beat in a person (Hood's men and their cold breakfast; Hooker's wound; Mansfield's white beard). The fix is never an invented fact — it's choosing which documented human detail to stop for.

**Voice:** the-gunboats is at the Antietam register. the-rivers and unconditional-surrender are close. the-breakout is not — it is more like a military-history encyclopedia entry. The most reliable voice diagnostic is the italic coda on each section: "answered with a shrug" (gunboats — strong); "The Confederates had spent the morning winning their way out, and the afternoon getting walled back in" (breakout — good summary but too neutral emotionally); "Two generals fled, one rode out through the ice" (surrender — right idea, wrong placement as noted above).

**Pacing:** the-gunboats and the Buckner friendship subsection of unconditional-surrender have the right acceleration. The approach to the weather (the-rivers) and the execution of the Pillow withdrawal (the-breakout) both slow down in the wrong places — narrative logistics when human consequence is due.

**Endings:** the-gunboats ends well. the-rivers ends well (the italic transition is strong). the-breakout's ending ("more trapped than when the day began") is good but again in a sidebar, which soft-pedals it. unconditional-surrender needs the coda reordered as noted.

**One structural note:** the draft's organization into the-rivers / the-gunboats / the-breakout / unconditional-surrender is the right shape for this battle. The four beats represent the four reversals: the strategy opens a door, the gunboats lose the shortcut, the Confederates win their escape and give it back, and the generals flee while the old friends make history. That structure works and should be kept.

## Summary of Highest-Impact Fixes

1. **The-breakout: inject one soldier-in-the-ranks beat at the Pillow withdrawal.** "Some of them had been close enough to the open road to see it" is already in the draft (in the sidebar). Promote it to the body when the order is given. This is the single highest-impact fix in the draft.
2. **Unconditional-surrender: cut "His reply is one of the most famous lines in American military history" and let the blockquote land cold.** The label cheapens the quote.
3. **The-breakout: start with Grant's action on the knapsack intelligence, not his cognitive process.** Behavior before explanation, as Antietam consistently does.
4. **Unconditional-surrender: move the closing coda ("Two generals fled...") to after the Buckner handshake,** not after the strategic-consequence paragraphs.
5. **The-rivers: open the march subsection with the discarded coats,** not the logistics.
