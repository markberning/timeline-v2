### "Draft Week"
**MOSTLY CLEAR. Two NEEDS-GLOSS, one LOST.**

- "provost marshal's office" — second mention in the piece; the role was never defined. Here it's described as "the federal office running the draft" — that's functional but a provost marshal is a specific military law-enforcement title, not just any "federal office." The functional gloss is enough for comprehension; a sharper reader will wonder why a military police official is running a civilian draft. **NEEDS-GLOSS** (brief inline: "the military officer charged with enforcing the draft laws").
- "Engine Company 33, a unit nicknamed the 'Black Joke'" — the volunteer firemen angle is interesting but "Engine Company 33" is left unexplained as an institution. Most readers will infer these are firefighters, but "Engine Company" as a firehouse unit type is not glossed. Minor. **NEEDS-GLOSS.**

A 15-year-old is very unlikely to know it. The draft immediately explains what happened (beat Black New Yorkers, burned homes, lynched them), so the *what* is shown — but the word itself will stop a cold reader dead for a moment before the explanation arrives. **NEEDS-GLOSS** (inline: "a targeted campaign of organized mob violence against a specific group").

- "Army of the Potomac" — named without explanation. In this section it's enough to know they were Union soldiers just back from Gettysburg, which is clear from context. But "Army of the Potomac" as a named formation (the main Union field army in the East) is never defined. Likely covered in an earlier chapter; if not, **NEEDS-GLOSS.**
- "Provost Marshal General James B. Fry (Union)" and "Robert Nugent (Union)" — both tagged. **CLEAR.**
- "garrisoned" — "more than 4,000 soldiers garrisoned New York." Garrisoned means stationed to hold/defend. A 15-year-old may not know this word. **SHOULD-FIX.**

## Overall Assessment

The draft is substantially well-glossed for a first pass. The author clearly knew the house rule about inline definitions and applied it consistently to the major terms (conscription, commutation, impressment, tax-in-kind, greenbacks, inflation, Sanitary Commission). The side-tagging is consistently applied to military and political actors. Structural framing is excellent — the reader always knows where they are.

The principal weaknesses are:

1. **Bond sequencing** — "bonds" first appears unexplained in the Confederate currency-reform passage (Beat 5), before the full definition arrives in the Northern boom section. A reader working through linearly hits an undefined term and has to hold it.
2. **"Provost marshal"** — functional gloss appears ("federal office running the draft") but the title itself is never explained; it then recurs without further help.
3. **"Preliminary" Emancipation Proclamation** — the qualifier is puzzling without context about Lincoln's two-step announcement.
4. **"Pogrom"** — stops a cold reader even though the explanation follows immediately.
5. **"Overseer"** — the function is implied but the word is never flat-out defined.
