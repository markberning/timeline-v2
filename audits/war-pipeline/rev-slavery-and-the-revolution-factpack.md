# FACT PACK — "Slavery and the Revolution" (1775–1783)

*American Revolution vertical, war-years Off-the-Battlefield theme chapter (route slug `slavery-and-the-revolution`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against the cited sources (founders.archives.gov, Massachusetts Historical Society, Nova Scotia Archives, Encyclopedia Virginia, Mount Vernon digital encyclopedia, NPS, Museum of the American Revolution, Black Loyalist archive, cross-checked Wikipedia); confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. This topic is saturated with inflated AND deflated numbers — the pack's biggest job is honest ranges with the scholarly debate named. FRAMING (house rule): name slavery plainly, no euphemism, no both-sidesing slavery itself; but the framing gate also fails patriotic myth AND simple-villain caricature. The spine: a war fought in the name of liberty by a society holding roughly half a million people in slavery, in which Black Americans fought on BOTH sides for the same prize — their own freedom — and the war became the largest emancipation event in American history before the Civil War AND entrenched slavery more deeply in the South.*

**Orientation.** In 1775 slavery was legal in every one of the thirteen colonies — Massachusetts included — and roughly one person in five in British America was enslaved. The contradiction was not invented by later historians; contemporaries on every side saw it and said so, from Samuel Johnson's sneer in London to Abigail Adams's letter in Braintree to Patrick Henry's astonishing private confession. The war then forced the question. In November 1775 Lord Dunmore, the royal governor of Virginia, offered freedom to enslaved people of rebels who would fight for the King — military pragmatism, not abolitionism (Dunmore enslaved people himself) — and in 1779 the Philipsburg Proclamation widened the offer to anyone who could reach British lines. Tens of thousands ran; how many is a real scholarly fight (older claims of 80,000–100,000 vs. Cassandra Pybus's revision to roughly 20,000–25,000). Meanwhile some 5,000–8,000 Black men served the Patriot side — at Bunker Hill, in the 1st Rhode Island Regiment, as the spy James (Armistead) Lafayette at Yorktown — even as Washington banned, then reopened, Black enlistment, and South Carolina rejected the Laurens plan three times and paid recruiting bounties IN enslaved people. The same war produced the first state-scale abolition wave in modern history (Vermont 1777, Pennsylvania 1780, Massachusetts by court verdict 1781–83, Virginia's 1782 manumission law) and left slavery untouched and entrenched where nine-tenths of the enslaved lived. It ends at the docks of New York in 1783: the Book of Negroes, Washington demanding "property" back, Carleton refusing to break faith, ~3,000 free Black people sailing for Nova Scotia — and thousands of others carried off from Savannah and Charleston still enslaved. The sober landing: the 1790 census counts ~698,000 enslaved people, more than in 1775. Chapter cross-refs: Crispus Attucks already carried in ch.3 (one back-reference only); Jefferson's cut Declaration passage — ch.5 covers the cutting, this chapter carries the substance; the white Loyalist civil war is ch.6 (this chapter owns only the Black Loyalist thread); the Constitution's bargains are beyond scope (one forward-glance beat max).

## 1. THE LANDSCAPE IN 1775

- **Slavery was legal in EVERY one of the thirteen colonies in 1775**, Massachusetts and the rest of New England included. No colony had abolished it. (HIGH)
- **Scale:** approximately **500,000 enslaved people** out of a total population of roughly **2.5 million** — about **20%** of everyone living in the thirteen colonies. (HIGH on ~20%; the 500,000/2.5M figures are standard round estimates — phrase as "roughly half a million" / "about one in five," not as census-grade numbers. The first real count is 1790, below.)
- **Distribution — heavily Southern but truly everywhere:** (tags per line)
  - **Virginia** — the largest enslaved population by far; enslaved people were about **40% of Virginia's population** by the Revolution. (HIGH — and at the 1790 census Virginia held 292,627 enslaved people, ~42% of all enslaved people in the U.S.)
  - **South Carolina** — about **60% of the total population enslaved** — a Black-majority colony; in the rice-growing lowcountry parishes the enslaved share ran far higher still. (HIGH on the statewide majority; MED on any specific lowcountry percentage — say "far higher" rather than a number.)
  - **Maryland and North Carolina** — each held over 100,000 enslaved people by 1790. (HIGH)
  - **The North was not clean:** New York held the largest Northern enslaved population (21,324 at the 1790 census; slavery woven into New York City's economy); New Jersey 11,423. New England's shares were small (a few percent) but real — enslaved people worked in Boston, Newport, and on Connecticut farms, and Rhode Island merchants were the leading American carriers of the Atlantic slave trade. (HIGH on the census numbers; HIGH on Rhode Island's slave-trade role.)
- **KEY FRAMING.** Don't write "the South had slavery." Every colony had it; the difference was scale and economic centrality. The honest sentence shape: slavery was *legal everywhere, dominant in the South.* (HIGH)

## 2. THE CONTRADICTION CONTEMPORARIES SAW

- **Samuel Johnson, *Taxation No Tyranny* (London, 1775):** **"How is it that we hear the loudest yelps for liberty among the drivers of negroes?"** — verbatim, from his pamphlet answering the First Continental Congress. Johnson opposed both American independence AND slavery (he had a long record of anti-slavery statements). The line is a hostile witness's point, and it landed because it was true. (HIGH — quote verified)
- **Abigail Adams to John Adams, 22 September 1774:** **"I wish most sincerely there was not a slave in the province. It always appeared a most iniquitous scheme to me — fight ourselves for what we are daily robbing and plundering from those who have as good a right to freedom as we have."** (HIGH — verified at Massachusetts Historical Society / Founders Online; original spelling is "ourselfs," "allways appeard a most iniquitious Scheme" — modernize silently or quote exactly, don't mix.) Context: she wrote it amid rumors of a planned petition/conspiracy by enslaved Bostonians offering to fight for the British governor in exchange for liberty. (MED on the rumor framing — usable as context, keep brief.)
- **Patrick Henry to Robert Pleasants (a Quaker abolitionist), 18 January 1773** — thanking him for an anti-slavery book, while continuing to enslave people: slavery is "repugnant to humanity"; and then the stunning confession — **"I am drawn along by the general inconvenience of living without them. I will not, I cannot justify it."** And: **"I will so far pay my devoir to virtue as to own the excellence and rectitude of her precepts, and to lament my want of conformity to them."** (HIGH — verified, Wikisource/Teaching American History; original has "ye." for "the." This is the chapter's single most damning Patriot verbatim — the "give me liberty" man, two years before the speech, admitting he keeps people enslaved for convenience.)
- **Jefferson's cut Declaration passage (substance only — ch.5 covers the cutting).** Jefferson's draft accused the King of having **"waged cruel war against human nature itself, violating its most sacred rights of life and liberty in the persons of a distant people who never offended him, captivating & carrying them into slavery in another hemisphere"** — and then, in the same breath, of **"exciting those very people to rise in arms among us"** (i.e., Dunmore's offer of freedom counted as a royal crime). Congress cut the whole passage; Jefferson later blamed South Carolina, Georgia, and Northern slave-trading interests. (HIGH on the text and the cut; the double accusation — enslaving them AND freeing them both listed as crimes — is the tell to surface.) The final Declaration kept only the oblique line about exciting **"domestic insurrections amongst us."** (HIGH)
- **FRAMING.** These quotes prove the contradiction was *visible at the time* — this is not hindsight morality. Hold the two truths: many founders genuinely called slavery evil in private, AND almost none of the Southern ones acted against their own holdings. (HIGH)

## 3. DUNMORE'S PROCLAMATION (NOVEMBER 1775)

- **John Murray, 4th Earl of Dunmore** — last royal governor of Virginia, by late 1775 driven from Williamsburg and governing from a warship. **Signed the proclamation 7 November 1775 aboard the ship *William* off Norfolk; published/proclaimed 14–15 November.** (HIGH)
- **The exact offer — the limits matter:** **"I do hereby farther declare all indented servants, Negroes, or others (appertaining to rebels) free, that are able and willing to bear arms, they joining his Majesty's troops as soon as may be…"** (HIGH — verbatim). Unpack the three limits: (1) only people enslaved by **rebels** — Loyalists' slaves stayed slaves; (2) only those **able and willing to bear arms** — effectively able-bodied men, not their families; (3) the stated purpose was **"the more speedily reducing this colony to a proper sense of their duty"** — crushing the rebellion, not ending slavery. (HIGH)
- **Dunmore enslaved people himself** — Encyclopedia Virginia: he "personally enslaved dozens of Africans in Virginia and the Caribbean" (he bought the Porto Bello plantation outside Williamsburg in 1774). Military pragmatism, not abolitionism. (HIGH on the fact he was an enslaver; MED on any specific count — use "dozens," no number.)
- **The response:** estimates of those who reached Dunmore **range from ~800 to ~2,000** — men, and also women and children he hadn't invited. (HIGH on the range; give it as a range.)
- **The Ethiopian Regiment** — the all-Black unit Dunmore formed from the men, with white officers. A *Virginia Gazette* report (2 December 1775) said the soldiers wore the motto **"Liberty to Slaves"** — per the contemporary report, on their uniforms/sashes; if accurate, a pointed echo-and-rebuke of Patrick Henry's "Liberty or Death." (HIGH that the regiment existed and fought; **MED on the "Liberty to Slaves" inscription — it rests on the one Gazette report; phrase as "a Virginia newspaper reported," don't assert as certain.**)
- **Battles:** Kemp's Landing (a small Black-Loyalist share in an early success, Nov 1775); then **Great Bridge, 9 December 1775** — Dunmore's defeat, after which he abandoned Norfolk for his ships. (HIGH)
- **The smallpox catastrophe.** Disease, above all **smallpox** (plus typhus/"camp fever"), tore through Dunmore's floating town. In May 1776 his forces moved to **Gwynn's Island** in the Chesapeake; hundreds of the Black recruits sickened and died there. When Patriot forces landed on the island in July 1776 they found the dying and the unburied dead. When Dunmore finally quit the Chesapeake in **August 1776**, only about **300** Black survivors sailed with him. (HIGH on the spine and the ~300; the grim landing-scene details are from Patriot officers' accounts — usable, attribute as such.)
- **The bitter irony — the proclamation made revolutionaries.** Threatening planters' human property pushed hesitant white Southerners toward independence. Historian Gary Nash: the proclamation "became a major factor in convincing white colonists that reconciliation with the mother country was impossible." Edward Rutledge (SC) said it tended "more effectually to work an eternal separation between Great Britain and the Colonies… than any other expedient." Richard Henry Lee: "Lord Dunmore's unparalleled conduct in Virginia has, a few Scotch excepted, united every Man in that large Colony." (HIGH — all three verified; pick one or two.)

## 4. THE PHILIPSBURG PROCLAMATION & SELF-EMANCIPATION AT SCALE

- **Sir Henry Clinton's Philipsburg Proclamation, 30 June 1779** (issued from headquarters at Philipsburg, NY) — the war-wide widening of Dunmore's offer: **"I do promise to every NEGROE who shall desert the Rebel Standard, full security to follow within these Lines, any Occupation which he shall think proper."** (HIGH — verbatim) Key differences from Dunmore: applied **across the colonies**, required **no military service** — any enslaved person of a rebel who reached British lines was protected, women and children included. It also still protected slavery where convenient: it threatened that Black men **captured in arms for the rebels** would be *sold*, and it never touched Loyalists' slaves. (HIGH — both halves are in the text; keep both.)
- **The scale of self-emancipation — THE NUMBERS FIGHT (DISPUTED, the chapter's most important range):**
  - **Jefferson's famous claim:** Virginia lost **"about 30,000" enslaved people in 1781 alone** during Cornwallis's campaign, most dying of smallpox and camp fever (he put the deaths at ~27,000). This number echoed through two centuries of scholarship. (HIGH that Jefferson claimed it; the claim itself is what's disputed.)
  - **The older high estimates:** historians Sylvia Frey and Gary Nash put total wartime flight to the British at **80,000–100,000**. (HIGH that these estimates exist and were standard.)
  - **The revision:** **Cassandra Pybus** ("Jefferson's Faulty Math," *William and Mary Quarterly*, 2005; *Epic Journeys of Freedom*, 2006) reworked the evidence and concluded Jefferson's 30,000 was a baseless extrapolation; her war-wide estimate is roughly **20,000–25,000 (at most ~30,000) who actually got to British lines** — and of those, **perhaps half or more died of disease**, smallpox above all. Maya Jasanoff's work aligns with the ~20,000 order of magnitude. (HIGH that this is Pybus's argument and the current scholarly correction; MED on any single number inside it.)
  - **AUTHOR RULE:** never print one total. The honest sentence: *tens of thousands ran — older histories said as many as 100,000, the careful modern count is closer to 20,000–25,000 — and disease killed a horrifying share before they ever saw freedom.* Flight = HIGH-certainty mass phenomenon; the count = genuinely unsettled. (DISPUTED by design)
- **Smallpox is the dark constant.** From Gwynn's Island to Yorktown, the camps where escapees gathered were death traps; the British also at times expelled the infected. The largest emancipation movement of the century ran straight through an epidemic. (HIGH on the pattern)
- **A human thread worth carrying: Harry Washington** — enslaved at Mount Vernon, escaped to Dunmore's fleet in 1776, served in the British lines, evacuated via the Book of Negroes to Nova Scotia in 1783 (listed there, age given as 43), and sailed to Sierra Leone in 1792. George Washington's own runaway closes every arc of this chapter. (HIGH — Mount Vernon, Smithsonian, Book of Negroes entry) Related: in **April 1781, 17 enslaved people fled Mount Vernon itself** to the British warship HMS *Savage* in the Potomac. (HIGH — Mount Vernon's own documentation)

## 5. BLACK PATRIOTS

- **Crispus Attucks** — already carried in ch.3; ONE back-reference only (the first to die on King Street was a man the law of every colony allowed to be owned). Do not re-narrate. (per chapter plan)
- **Bunker Hill (June 1775) — what's documented vs. legend:**
  - **Salem Poor** — free Black soldier (had bought his own freedom); **documented**: on 5 December 1775, fourteen Massachusetts officers petitioned the General Court that Poor "behaved like an Experienced officer, as Well as an Excellent Soldier," and that "in the Person of this Sd. Negro Centers a Brave & gallant Soldier." No other soldier at Bunker Hill got such a testimonial. **But the petition never says what he did** ("to set forth the particulars of his conduct would be tedious"). The later story that he shot Lt. Col. Abercrombie is **19th-century tradition, not evidence**. (HIGH on the petition and its wording; DISPUTED/legend on any specific deed.)
  - **Peter Salem** — formerly enslaved in Framingham, Mass. (freed in connection with his enlistment — MED on the exact arrangement); fought at Concord and Bunker Hill. The famous claim that he **fired the shot that killed Major Pitcairn** is **legend**: it surfaces decades later (popularized by abolitionist William Cooper Nell, 1855), has no contemporary corroboration, and British accounts suggest Pitcairn was hit by multiple shots. Trumbull's 1786 painting includes a Black soldier often *said* to be Salem — the identification itself is uncertain. (HIGH that he served; DISPUTED on Pitcairn; flag the painting caveat if the image is used.)
- **Washington's reversals on Black enlistment — the sequence (HIGH):**
  1. Black men (free and enslaved) had already fought at Lexington, Concord, and Bunker Hill before Washington took command.
  2. **July 1775** — shortly after taking command, Washington's headquarters barred recruiting "any deserter from the Ministerial army, stroller, negro, or vagabond" (10 July 1775 recruiting instructions). (HIGH)
  3. **12 November 1775** — general orders barred ALL Black men, free or enslaved, from enlisting. (HIGH)
  4. **Five days later Dunmore's proclamation was published.** (HIGH — the juxtaposition is the story)
  5. **30 December 1775** — Washington reversed himself for free Black veterans: "As the General is informed, that Numbers of Free Negroes are desirous of inlisting, he gives leave to the recruiting Officers, to entertain them, and promises to lay the matter before the Congress, who he doubts not will approve of it." Congress concurred (16 January 1776) for free Black men who had already served. (HIGH — order text verified)
  6. As manpower bled away, practice loosened far past the rule: by the later war years the Continental Army was the most racially integrated American army before Vietnam (a widely used line — MED, attribute as "historians have called it" if used). Black soldiers served overwhelmingly in integrated units, not segregated ones. (HIGH)
- **The 1st Rhode Island Regiment ("the Black Regiment" — handle the nickname with care):**
  - **14 February 1778** — Rhode Island's assembly, desperate to fill its Continental quota, voted that "every able-bodied negro, mulatto, or Indian man slave" could enlist; each "shall, upon his passing muster before Colonel Christopher Greene, be immediately discharged from the service of his master or mistress, and be absolutely free." **Owners were compensated** at market value by the state. (HIGH — act text verified)
  - **~88 enslaved men enlisted in the first four months**; with free Black and Native recruits the regiment counted roughly **140 men of color out of ~225**. The assembly, alarmed at the cost, **closed slave enlistment on 10 June 1778**. (HIGH)
  - **Battle of Rhode Island, 29 August 1778** — in the retreat from the failed Newport operation, the regiment helped repel repeated assaults (including by Hessian troops) on the American right. A real, creditable combat record — don't inflate it into the battle's decisive force. (HIGH on the role; MED on "three times drove back the Hessians" specifics — phrase as repeated assaults repelled.)
  - **Caveats for the "Black Regiment" label:** it was never all-Black (white officers throughout, and later white replacements made it fully integrated); it was a one-state, four-month experiment, not a Continental policy. (HIGH)
- **Total Black service on the Patriot side — give a range:** the long-standard estimate is **~5,000** (Benjamin Quarles's "respectable figure"); the DAR's *Forgotten Patriots* project documents **~6,600 named soldiers and sailors of color**; recent scholarship argues for **8,000–10,000+**. Author phrasing: *somewhere between five and ten thousand, the records too thin for better.* (MED-by-nature — the range IS the verified fact.)
- **James (Armistead) Lafayette** — enslaved in New Kent County, Virginia; with his owner William Armistead's consent, served the Marquis de Lafayette in 1781 **as a spy inside Cornwallis's camp** — a double agent feeding Lafayette intelligence and Cornwallis disinformation in the run-up to Yorktown. (HIGH) **The freedom law skipped him:** Virginia's 1783 act freeing enslaved *soldier*-substitutes didn't cover a spy. Lafayette wrote him a testimonial (1784); the legislature finally **freed him by special act, with the assent of 9 January 1787**, compensating his owner £250. He took the name James Lafayette. (HIGH — note the grind: the man who helped win Yorktown needed six years and a celebrity reference to get free.)
- **CARE:** "freedom for service" was never universal on the Patriot side — it was state-by-state, often grudging, sometimes dishonored (see §6 substitutes). (HIGH)

## 6. SLAVERY INSIDE THE PATRIOT CAUSE

- **Enslaved substitutes.** Masters in several states sent enslaved men to serve **in their places** in the draft; freedom was sometimes promised, sometimes granted, and sometimes denied after service. Virginia found the abuse common enough that its **1783 act** freed enslaved men whose masters had passed them off as free substitutes — the legislature calling out owners who, "contrary to principles of justice and to their own solemn promise," tried to re-enslave veterans. (HIGH on the practice and the 1783 Virginia act; MED on the act's exact quoted wording — paraphrase unless re-verified.)
- **The Laurens plan (1779–1782) — the road not taken:**
  - **John Laurens** — South Carolinian, Washington's aide-de-camp, son of Henry Laurens (president of Congress and one of the biggest slave traders in America — the family irony is the point). Proposed raising **battalions of enslaved men** who would be **freed for service**. (HIGH)
  - **Alexander Hamilton backed it** in his letter to John Jay, **14 March 1779** (quotes in §QUOTES below). (HIGH)
  - **Congress, 29 March 1779,** recommended South Carolina and Georgia (**"if they shall think the same expedient"**) raise **3,000 able-bodied Black soldiers**; owners compensated up to **$1,000 per man**; the soldiers unpaid but **emancipated at war's end with $50**. Conditional, hedged — and dead on arrival. (HIGH)
  - **South Carolina rejected it furiously — three times** (1779, 1780, 1782 in the legislature), even with the British occupying Charleston in between. Contemporary SC reaction: "We are much disgusted here at the Congress recommending us to arm our Slaves… it was received with great resentment, as a very dangerous and impolitic Step." (HIGH on the triple rejection; MED on attributing the quote — commonly given as Christopher Gadsden to Samuel Adams, July 1779; attribute as "one South Carolina patriot leader" if unsure.)
  - **Washington's verdict, to John Laurens, 10 July 1782:** "That spirit of freedom which at the commencement of this contest would have gladly sacrificed every thing to the attainment of its object has long since subsided, and every selfish passion has taken its place." (HIGH — verified, Founders Online; Washington — himself an enslaver who never backed the plan with his weight — diagnosing the failure is its own layer; don't present him as the plan's champion.)
  - Laurens died in a minor skirmish at the Combahee River, **August 1782**. (HIGH)
- **Enslaved people as ARMY LABOR.** On both sides, enslaved people dug fortifications, drove wagons, cooked, and served officers; Southern states impressed enslaved laborers for war work. Unfree labor underwrote the war effort itself. (HIGH — keep as one solid beat.)
- **Enslaved people as BOUNTY PAYMENT — "Sumter's Law."** From **April 1781**, South Carolina general **Thomas Sumter** recruited state troops by promising payment in enslaved people **confiscated from Loyalists** — a scale of one enslaved person per private for ten months' service, more for officers. In **February 1782 the South Carolina legislature formalized it** (a "grown negro" between roughly ten and forty years old per enlistee, bonuses for recruiters). **Georgia went further**, using enslaved people to pay officials and settle military debts. **Francis Marion refused to recruit on these terms.** (HIGH on the spine — Sumter's policy, the 1782 legislative adoption, Georgia's extension, Marion's refusal; MED on the exact per-rank schedule. This is the chapter's bluntest fact: the army of liberty paying its soldiers in human beings.)

## 7. THE FIRST ABOLITION WAVE — AND THE ENTRENCHMENT

- **Vermont, 8 July 1777** — its founding constitution (Vermont was an unrecognized breakaway republic, not one of the thirteen) declared **no adult could be held as a slave**: no male "after he arrives to the age of twenty-one Years, nor female… after she arrives to the age of eighteen years," unless bound by their own consent. **First written constitution to ban adult slavery — with loopholes**: children could still be held to those ages, and enforcement was weak for years. (HIGH on text and date; HIGH on the loopholes — state both.)
- **Pennsylvania, 1 March 1780 — An Act for the Gradual Abolition of Slavery** — the first abolition *statute* passed by an elected legislature. **The mechanism is the story: it freed nobody then alive.** Everyone enslaved in Pennsylvania on that day stayed enslaved for life (if registered by their owners); only **children born after the act** were born "free" — and even they owed **indentured service to their mother's enslaver until age 28**. Pennsylvania still had enslaved residents into the 1840s. (HIGH — all verified. The gradualism is not a footnote; it's the honest shape of Northern abolition.)
- **Massachusetts — abolition by jury verdict, 1781–1783:**
  - **Elizabeth Freeman ("Mum Bett")** — enslaved by Colonel John Ashley of Sheffield; sued under the brand-new 1780 Massachusetts constitution ("all men are born free and equal"), with attorney Theodore Sedgwick, joined by an enslaved man named **Brom** (*Brom and Bett v. Ashley*, county court, **August 1781**). **The jury freed them** and awarded 30 shillings damages. She took the name Elizabeth Freeman. (HIGH)
  - **Quock Walker** — enslaved man in Worcester County; won his own freedom suit in **1781**; in the linked criminal case (*Commonwealth v. Jennison*, **April 1783**) Chief Justice **William Cushing** charged the jury that slavery was inconsistent with the new constitution — his notes: "there can be no such thing as perpetual servitude of a rational creature…" Jennison was convicted of assault. (HIGH)
  - **The nuance (CARE):** Massachusetts never passed an abolition statute and never amended its constitution to ban slavery — slavery **withered** under the legal precedent and collapsing social sanction. By the **1790 census Massachusetts reported zero enslaved people** — the only original state at zero. Phrase as "ended by court verdict and collapse, not by statute." (HIGH)
- **Virginia's 1782 manumission act** — for the first time since 1723, owners could free enslaved people **privately, by deed or will**, without petitioning the legislature per case. Virginia's free Black population jumped from roughly **1,800–3,000 (1782) to ~12,800 (1790) and ~30,000 (1810)**; total freed under the law ~20,000. **Scale check:** against ~293,000 enslaved Virginians in 1790, manumission freed a few percent. **One forward beat only:** in **1806** Virginia gutted the door it had opened, requiring newly freed people to leave the state within a year or face re-enslavement. (HIGH on the act, the growth figures, and the 1806 rollback; the 1782 baseline number is soft — use "a couple of thousand.")
- **The other side of the ledger — entrenchment.** South of Pennsylvania the war ended with slavery **legally untouched everywhere** and, in South Carolina and Georgia, positively reinforced (see Sumter's Law; the furious defense of "property" in the peace). Planters rebuilt their labor forces; the international slave trade reopened in the lower South after the war; and the legal architecture of slavery emerged from a revolution for liberty **stronger** where nine in ten enslaved Americans lived. The cotton boom was still unknowable in 1783 — don't backload it — but the entrenchment was real before cotton. (HIGH on every element; the no-cotton-anachronism rule is mandatory.)
- Forward-glance allowance (ONE beat max): the new nation's constitutional bargains over slavery belong to a later chapter. (per chapter plan)
- Optional context beat: Connecticut and Rhode Island passed gradual-abolition acts in **1784**, extending the wave just past the war's end. (HIGH — one clause, no more.)

## 8. THE EVACUATION (1783)

- **The panic at the peace.** When news of the provisional peace reached New York — its **Article 7** barred the British from "carrying away any Negroes or other property" — terror swept the Black refugees behind British lines. **Boston King** (formerly enslaved in South Carolina, escaped to the British, later a Methodist preacher) wrote in his 1798 memoir: a "report prevailed at New-York, that all the slaves, in number 2000, were to be delivered up to their masters… This dreadful rumour filled us all with inexpressible anguish and terror, especially when we saw our old masters coming from Virginia, North-Carolina, and other parts, and seizing upon their slaves in the streets of New-York, or even dragging them out of their beds." (HIGH — verbatim verified; his "in number 2000" is his own guess, not a count.)
- **Washington vs. Carleton — Orangetown (Tappan), New York, 6 May 1783.** Washington met Sir **Guy Carleton** (British commander-in-chief) and pressed for the return of escaped enslaved people as American **property** under the treaty — including, pointedly, his own runaways from Mount Vernon. **Carleton refused**: Britain's proclamations had promised freedom *before* the treaty, and (per the contemporary memoir of William Smith, who was present) "no interpretation could be put upon the Articles inconsistent with prior Engagements binding the National Honor, which must be kept with all Colours" — adding that the only remedy was compensation to the owners. Carleton had already begun embarking free Black people; he kept a registry precisely so that compensation claims could be settled later. (HIGH on the meeting, positions, and outcome; the Smith quote is a participant's memoir — attribute it as such. CARE: Carleton's stand was honorable AND framed entirely inside property law — he proposed paying the owners, not disputing that people could be owned.)
- **The Book of Negroes** — the ledger British officials (under Brigadier General **Samuel Birch**, name behind Birchtown) kept of Black refugees embarking at New York, **23 April – 30 November 1783**: names, ages, descriptions, former owners, and whether each held a freedom certificate ("Birch certificates" / General Musgrave's). It records **about 3,000 people** (Nova Scotia Archives: 3,000 registered to Nova Scotia; commonly given as ~3,000 total entries). Two original copies survive (British and American); **digitized at the Nova Scotia Archives**. It is the largest single document of its kind in 18th-century North America — a census of self-emancipation. (HIGH — verify "~3,000" phrasing as approximately, not exactly.)
  - Boston King again, on the certificates: "the English had compassion upon us in the day of distress, and issued out a Proclamation… each of us received a certificate from the commanding officer at New-York, which dispelled all our fears, and filled us with joy and gratitude." (HIGH — verbatim verified)
- **Destinations and what came next:**
  - Most of the New York evacuees went to **Nova Scotia** — Birchtown (near Shelburne) became the largest settlement of free Black people in North America. The promises (land, provisions) were largely broken: little or poor land, wage exploitation, legal inequality, and in 1784 anti-Black riots at Shelburne. (HIGH on the broken promises; MED on the "largest settlement" superlative — phrase as "one of/then the largest.")
  - **David George** (escaped enslavement in Georgia, freed behind British lines at Savannah, Baptist preacher) on arriving to preach at Shelburne: "I began to sing the first night in the woods at a camp, for there were no houses then built… The Black people came far and near." (HIGH — verbatim from his 1793 account)
  - **Sierra Leone, 1792:** disillusioned, **1,196 Black Loyalists in 15 ships** left Halifax on 15 January 1792, organized by Thomas Peters and John Clarkson, and founded **Freetown**. Boston King, David George — and **Harry Washington**, once enslaved at Mount Vernon — were among them. (HIGH)
- **The other evacuations — the ones the freedom story leaves out (mandatory, both truths):** New York was the *free* registry. From **Savannah (July 1782)** and **Charleston (December 1782)** the British carried off far larger numbers of Black people who were **still enslaved** — the human property of departing Loyalists — roughly **3,500 from Savannah** and **5,000+ from Charleston**, most shipped to **Jamaica, East Florida, and other plantation colonies** to remain in or be sold into slavery. Some Black people the British themselves held as sequestered "rebel property" were kept or sold. The same empire that kept faith at New York re-enslaved or kept enslaved thousands elsewhere. (HIGH on the pattern and destinations; MED on the per-port totals — use "roughly"/"thousands." This is the anti-sanitizing beat: British emancipation was real AND selective AND self-interested.)

## 9. THE SOBER LANDING — WHAT THE REVOLUTION DID AND DIDN'T DO

- **Did:** produced the **first wave of abolition at state scale in the history of Atlantic slavery** — five Northern jurisdictions ended or began ending slavery between 1777 and 1784, the first polities anywhere to legislate slavery toward extinction; freed (by flight, service, court, or manumission) tens of thousands of people; created the first large free Black communities and institutions (churches, schools, petitions) of the new nation. (HIGH — the "first abolition wave" claim is standard scholarship; phrase as first at state/polity scale, not "first antislavery sentiment ever.")
- **Didn't:** touch slavery where it mattered most. **The first U.S. census, 1790: 697,697 enslaved people** (with ~59,500 free people of color) in a population of 3.93 million — *more* enslaved people than the ~500,000 of 1775. Virginia alone: 292,627. The republic born of the Revolution was the largest slaveholding nation in the hemisphere after Brazil and the Caribbean sugar empires — a slaveholders' republic with a free-soil fringe. (HIGH — census figures exact; round to "almost 700,000" in prose. CARE: the commonly seen "694,280" is an older census-tabulation variant; use ~698,000/"almost 700,000," not a false-precision single figure.)
- **Closing shape (per the chapter spine):** both Black Americas the war made — Freetown and the cotton frontier — descend from the same eight years. The Revolution was the largest emancipation event in American history before the Civil War, and the moment slavery's American foundations were re-poured. Hold both; end on the census number, not on uplift. (framing, locked)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **THE NUMBERS — the chapter's biggest integrity risk.** Three contested counts, each gets a RANGE, never a single figure: (a) **fled to the British**: older scholarship 80,000–100,000 (Frey/Nash) vs. Pybus's revision ~20,000–25,000 reaching the lines, with perhaps half dying of disease — name the debate; (b) **Jefferson's "30,000 in Virginia in 1781"** is *his claim*, now considered a wild overestimate ("Jefferson's faulty math") — quote it only AS a claim; (c) **served the Patriot side**: ~5,000 (Quarles) to ~6,600 documented (DAR) to 8,000–10,000+ (recent) — "between five and ten thousand."
2. **British motives were military, not humanitarian.** Dunmore enslaved people himself; both proclamations freed only REBELS' slaves and threatened or preserved slavery where useful (Philipsburg promised to SELL Black men captured in rebel arms); the empire kept slavery in the Caribbean for another half-century and re-enslaved or kept enslaved thousands evacuated from Savannah/Charleston. AND the freedom delivered to ~3,000 at New York was real and defended at real cost (Carleton). Both truths, no sanitizing either way.
3. **Patriot motives equally instrumental.** Rhode Island enlisted enslaved men to fill a quota and compensated their owners; Congress's 1779 plan was conditional and unfunded; "freedom for service" was state-by-state, sometimes dishonored (substitute scandals); SC/GA paid bounties IN human beings. Black freedom was currency to both empires. The people with consistent motives were the enslaved themselves — freedom, whichever army sold it cheapest. That's the chapter's organizing insight (and standard scholarship — Quarles's thesis).
4. **"Liberty to Slaves" sashes** — rests on one Virginia Gazette report (2 Dec 1775); write "a Virginia newspaper reported," not flat assertion.
5. **Salem Poor / Peter Salem documentation thinness.** Poor's petition is gold-standard contemporary evidence but names no deed; Salem's Pitcairn shot is 19th-century legend (Nell, 1855). State what the documents say; flag the legends as legends. Trumbull-painting identification is itself uncertain.
6. **Quock Walker / Mum Bett nuance** — no Massachusetts statute ever abolished slavery; it ended through jury verdicts under the 1780 constitution and social collapse; "abolished by the courts" needs the "withered, not legislated" gloss. 1790 census: zero enslaved in MA (and don't cite Vermont's census line — its small "slaves" entry is a known clerical-error mess; just say Vermont's ban was constitutionally first but leaky).
7. **Gradual abolition freed nobody immediately** — Pennsylvania 1780 left every living enslaved person enslaved and indentured the freeborn children to 28. Don't let "first abolition law" read as liberation day.
8. **Washington is two characters here** — the commander who reopened Black enlistment and came to respect Black soldiers, AND the enslaver demanding his runaways back at Orangetown in 1783. Both documented; show both, editorialize neither.
9. **No anachronistic cotton.** The cotton kingdom postdates the chapter (gin 1793); the entrenchment argument must stand on 1775–1783 facts (legal continuity, Sumter's Law, the planter mobilization Dunmore provoked, the 1790 census).
10. **Crispus Attucks** — one back-reference to ch.3 only; do not re-narrate or hagiographize.
11. **Quote hygiene** — Henry's "ye.", Abigail's "ourselfs": pick exact-or-modernized and be consistent; Boston King's "in number 2000" is his guess, not a count; the SC "much disgusted" quote's attribution (commonly Gadsden to Samuel Adams, 1779) is MED — soften attribution if not re-verified.
12. **Article 7 / Carleton framing** — Carleton's refusal was about honoring prior promises, argued inside property law (he proposed compensating owners and kept the Book of Negroes partly as a claims ledger). Honor AND property logic together; not an abolitionist stand.
