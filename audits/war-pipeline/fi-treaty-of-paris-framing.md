## FIRST CHECK (ship-blocking)

### (a) Are Native nations first-class actors with their own aims, not props? — PASS

The draft does not reduce Native nations to a backdrop or a tool of either empire. It does the opposite, repeatedly and structurally:

- The opening paragraph establishes the spine immediately: "made a promise to the Native nations who lived on most of the ground in question. The nations whose homelands were being signed back and forth were not in the room for any of it." (lines 5)
- The closing section "A Promise Drawn on a Map" names their agency explicitly: "These were not bystanders to the great-power war. They had spent it pursuing their own aims, playing France and Britain against each other to protect their land and their independence, fighting for themselves rather than for either crown." (line 117) — this is the exact first-class-actor framing the rule demands, and it is in the section's own prose.
- It names actual nations by their own names (Odawa/Ottawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Wyandot, Shawnee, Lenape/Delaware, Mingo, Miami — line 117), not a generic "Indians" blur.
- Pontiac's War is framed as Native-initiated leverage, not a British event: "many of those same nations rose against the British, under the leadership of the Odawa war leader Pontiac, whose name in his own language was Obwandiyag" and "an answer to a war the nations themselves had forced" (line 119). The Proclamation is presented as a Crown *reaction* to Native power, not a gift.

### (b) Is the human cost / dispossession named in the section's own prose? — PASS

Dispossession is named plainly and is the emotional climax, not a buried euphemism:

- "So the Proclamation Line was a real promise, and that is exactly what makes it a tragedy rather than a footnote... By the late 1760s it was being taken from them" (line 123).
- "the West was reserved for Native nations in October 1763 and was being taken from them within a decade" framing recurs at lines 123 and 125: "the Native nations a promise... The other was already being broken as the ink dried."
- The contest is explicitly named as being over Native land: "The war the British colonists named for 'the Indians' was, for those nations, a fight over who would get to keep living where they had always lived." (line 117)

### "French and Indian War" naming acknowledged — PASS (exceeds requirement)

The draft explicitly flags that the name centers the British-colonial POV, twice: "To the British colonists it was the French and Indian War, named for the enemies they faced" (line 13) and "The war the British colonists named for 'the Indians'" (line 117). It also makes clear the "Indians" fought for themselves. This is handled better than the rule requires.

**Acadian expulsion:** Out of scope for this theme (the fact pack owns the *1763 settlement*, not the 1755–64 deportation), so its absence is not a framing failure here. Not flagged.

## FINDINGS

### F1 — Native exclusion from the treaty — FAIR
The "no Native nation a party" point is stated as fact, accurately, and without false balance: "they did not consult a single Native leader. They did not weigh Native interests... with no Native nation present and none consenting" (line 115). Correctly tied to the lead-plates / royal-grant pattern as "the same silence." Strong, fair framing.

### F2 — The Proclamation as recognition vs. undercutting — FAIR
The draft resists the sanitized "Britain protected Native land" story. It presents the recognition as real ("the first time British colonial law had formally acknowledged that Native nations held title") AND immediately undercut, correctly attributing the contested reading to Calloway: "scholars disagree on whether the proclamation recognized or undermined tribal sovereignty" (line 91). **Web-verified:** the Calloway quote and the two-sided scholarly debate it summarizes are accurately represented (Royal Proclamation of 1763, Wikipedia; The Scratch of a Pen review). No romanticization of the Crown's motives — line 83 explicitly says "The Crown did not draw the line out of tenderness."

### F3 — Washington as the colonial gentry's true attitude — FAIR
Using Washington's private letter to expose that the gentry treated the line as a sham to be evaded is sharp, accurate framing that refuses to launder colonial land-hunger. **Web-verified:** the quoted phrases ("temporary expedien[t] to quiet the Minds of the Indians & must fall of course in a few years"; "keep this whole matter a profound Secret"; "under the pretence of hunting other Game") are verbatim-faithful to the 17 Sep 1767 Washington–Crawford letter (Encyclopedia Virginia / Founders Online). The draft's ellipses are honest; it correctly heeded the fact pack's caution and wrote "land bounties for his service" rather than asserting a hard acreage.

### F4 — Empire not romanticized — FAIR
Britain's victory is framed with cost attached, not as glory: "That was a triumph, and it was also a problem" (line 61). The "few acres of snow" debate is handled as a real argument with the conclusion correctly attributed ("some of the sharpest minds in London genuinely thought," line 53) rather than asserted — matching the fact pack's CONTESTED caution. No false balance, no both-sides-ing of the dispossession.

### F5 — Violence neither sanitized nor gratuitous — FAIR
Pontiac's War is referenced as cause/context (seizing "roughly ten British forts") without re-narrating sieges or atrocity, respecting the dedup boundary; the smallpox-blankets episode is correctly left to `fi-pontiacs-war`. No gratuitous detail, no sanitizing.

### F6 — Presentism — FAIR
The draft applies no anachronistic moral lecture. Where it reaches into the present (Canada's Constitution Act, 1982 naming the Proclamation as a foundation of Aboriginal rights, line 91) it is factually grounded and attributed, not editorializing. The tragedy framing is drawn from the period facts themselves, not imposed.
