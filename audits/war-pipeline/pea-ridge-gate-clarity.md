## Unexplained Leaps / Scene-Set Gaps

1. **Why does Missouri matter (the Mississippi River)?** The opening states "whoever controlled Missouri controlled the upper Mississippi River" but never tells the reader WHY the Mississippi was the war's great highway (troop movement, supplies, splitting the Confederacy). The stakes are stated but not explained.

2. **What is the Confederacy?** The draft assumes the reader knows the Confederacy is the breakaway Southern government. It never introduces it cleanly. The very first sentence names "the Confederacy" without a gloss. A newcomer in 2026 may genuinely not know.

3. **The rank hierarchy is never established.** "Brig. Gen." and "Maj. Gen." appear cold. When Price (Maj. Gen.) and McCulloch (Brig. Gen.) feud "over who was in charge," a newcomer can't tell which outranks the other (Maj. Gen. outranks Brig. Gen. = Price should be in charge), which is the whole point of the feud.

4. **Unit-size hierarchy never introduced.** The draft uses regiment, division, wing, company, squadron, and battery fluidly. None is defined. A newcomer doesn't know a division is larger than a regiment, which is larger than a company. This creates confusion about scale ("2,000 infantry" vs. "a small squadron").

5. **The Confederacy burning Fayetteville.** "The Confederates burned Fayetteville behind them" — why? This is a retreating-army scorched-earth tactic but is never explained. A newcomer will wonder why an army would burn its own towns.

6. **"He had left detachments behind to guard his supply line, which reduced his force to about 10,250 men."** A newcomer doesn't know what a supply line is — the chain of wagons and roads that brings food/ammunition to an army in the field. Not glossed.

7. **"Come down on Curtis from the north, from his rear, cutting his supply line."** The strategic logic of cutting a supply line (starving the army of food and bullets) is implied but not stated. A newcomer may understand "behind him" but not why that's catastrophic.

8. **Jefferson C. Davis / Jefferson Davis name collision** (see side-tag section above).

9. **"The Trans-Mississippi slid to the margins of the war."** A newcomer doesn't know what "the Trans-Mississippi" is as a war theater. First use (section 3, "What it meant") needs a gloss: "the vast country west of the Mississippi River."

10. **Shiloh drop.** "The bloodbath at Shiloh" lands with zero context. Shiloh needs one phrase — it's one of the most important battles of the war and a newcomer will be lost.
