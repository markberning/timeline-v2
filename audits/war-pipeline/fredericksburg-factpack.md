## 1. Dates / location / theatre

- **Dates:** December 11–15, 1862. The main assaults fell on **December 13, 1862**; crossing began Dec 11; withdrawal completed the night of Dec 15. [WP, ABT]
- **Location:** Fredericksburg, Virginia, and Spotsylvania/Stafford Counties, on the **Rappahannock River**, roughly midway between Washington, D.C. and Richmond. [WP, ABT]
- **Theatre:** Eastern Theater. [WP]
- **Result:** Decisive **Confederate victory**. [WP, ABT]
- **Coordinates:** ~38°17′42″N 77°28′12″W. [WP]

## 2. Forces & commanders (each tagged SIDE)

### North — Army of the Potomac
- **Maj. Gen. Ambrose E. Burnside (North)** — overall commander; newly appointed (replaced McClellan early Nov 1862). [WP]
- Organized into three **"Grand Divisions"** (two corps each):
  - **Right Grand Division — Maj. Gen. Edwin V. Sumner (North)** — drove the Marye's Heights assaults. [WP]
  - **Center Grand Division — Maj. Gen. Joseph Hooker (North)** — crossed Dec 13, fed in the later Marye's assaults. [WP]
  - **Left Grand Division — Maj. Gen. William B. Franklin (North)** — attacked Jackson's right, south of town. [WP]
- Other key Union officers (all **North**): **Maj. Gen. John F. Reynolds** (I Corps, Franklin's wing); **Maj. Gen. George G. Meade** (division that broke Jackson's line); **Brig. Gen. John Gibbon** (supporting division, wounded); **Maj. Gen. Darius N. Couch** (II Corps, Marye's); **Brig. Gen. Thomas F. Meagher** (Irish Brigade); **Brig. Gen. Andrew A. Humphreys** (late Marye's assault); **Col. Norman J. Hall** (river-crossing landing party). [WP]

### South — Army of Northern Virginia
- **Gen. Robert E. Lee (South)** — overall commander; observed from Telegraph Hill / "Lee's Hill." [WP]
- **Lt. Gen. James Longstreet (South)** — First Corps; held **Marye's Heights** and the stone wall (the Confederate left/center). [WP]
- **Lt. Gen. Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson (South)** — Second Corps; held the Confederate **right** at Prospect Hill / Hamilton's Crossing. [WP]
- **Maj. Gen. J.E.B. Stuart (South)** — cavalry division, anchoring the far Confederate right. [WP]
- Other key Confederate officers (all **South**): **Brig. Gen. William Barksdale** (Mississippi brigade, defended the riverfront/town); **Maj. John Pelham** ("the gallant Pelham," horse artillery enfilading Meade); **Maj. Gens. A.P. Hill, Jubal A. Early, William B. Taliaferro** (Jackson's front); **Brig. Gen. Maxcy Gregg** (killed plugging the gap); **Brig. Gen. Thomas R.R. Cobb** (mortally wounded at the stone wall); **Brig. Gen. Joseph B. Kershaw** (took over the stone-wall front); **Lt. Col. E. Porter Alexander** (artillery). [WP]

## 4. Geography (for map accuracy — relative positions)

Lay it out **east → west** (the direction the Union attacked) and **north → south**.

- **Rappahannock River** — runs roughly NW→SE through the scene. The Union army massed on the **east/north (Stafford) bank** (notably **Stafford Heights**, where Union artillery sat); the town and the Confederate army were on the **west/south bank.** The river is the central obstacle of the whole battle. [WP, ABT]
- **Pontoon bridges** — Union engineers built **six** pontoon bridges: **two opposite the town** (upper crossing), **one just below the town**, and **three downstream near the mouth of Deep Run** (lower crossing, opposite Franklin's front). The town crossings drew the fire; the downstream ones crossed with little opposition. [WP]
- **Town of Fredericksburg** — on the **west bank**, directly below Marye's Heights. Site of the contested river crossing, the urban fighting, and the looting/"sack." [WP]
- **The canal ditch (millrace)** — ran roughly **N–S about 200 yards west of the town**, between the town and the open plain; crossed by **three narrow bridges.** Attacking troops had to funnel over it, then re-form, **under fire**, before crossing ~**600 yards of open, muddy, rising ground** to the wall. A killing bottleneck. [WP]
- **Marye's Heights** — a ridge **~600 yards west of the town**, rising **~40–50 ft** above the plain. Made up (north→south) of Taylor's Hill, Stansbury Hill, **Marye's Hill**, and **Willis Hill.** Lined with massed Confederate artillery on the crest. This is the Confederate **left/center** (Longstreet's front). [WP]
- **The Stone Wall / Sunken Road (Telegraph Road)** — a sunken farm lane in a slight cut at the **foot** of Marye's Heights, fronted by a **~4-ft stone wall** (with log breastworks/abatis). Confederate infantry stood **multiple ranks deep** behind it, completely sheltered, firing into the open slope. The single most lethal position of the battle. [WP, ABT]
- **Prospect Hill** — high ground **south of the town**, near **Hamilton's Crossing**, held by **Jackson's Second Corps** (the Confederate **right**). This is where Franklin/Meade attacked. [WP]
- **The gap in Jackson's line** — a **~600-yard gap** in A.P. Hill's front, a swampy, wooded, triangular patch of boggy ground between the brigades of **Lane** and **Archer**, left undefended because it looked impassable. Meade's men found and pierced it. (Area later called the **Slaughter Pen Farm**.) [WP, ABT]
- **Telegraph Hill / "Lee's Hill"** — Lee's command post, a high knoll **behind the Confederate center**, with a view over both fronts. Site of the famous Lee quote. [WP]
- **Hamilton's Crossing** — a railroad point at the **southern (right) end** of the Confederate line, anchoring Jackson's flank. [WP]
- **Deep Run** and **Hazel Run** — small streams crossing the plain; **Deep Run** roughly separates the two battlefronts (Marye's to its north, Jackson's front to its south); **Hazel Run** is near the south edge of the Marye's sector. [WP]

**Two distinct battlefronts (critical for map clarity):**
1. **NORTH front — Marye's Heights / the Stone Wall** (Longstreet, South vs. Sumner & Hooker, North): the famous slaughter.
2. **SOUTH front — Prospect Hill / Hamilton's Crossing** (Jackson, South vs. Franklin & Meade, North): the one near-breakthrough.

## 5. Sequence (with turning points)

**Phase 0 — The delayed pontoons (the original sin).** Burnside's plan was speed: cross the Rappahannock before Lee could concentrate, and march on Richmond. He requested pontoon bridges in early November, but **bureaucratic/logistical delays in Washington** meant they arrived **weeks late** (first bridge ~Nov 25). The delay **gave Lee time to fortify the heights** and concentrate his whole army. **Turning point #1:** the battle was half-lost before a shot, by a supply failure. [WP, ABT]

**Phase 1 — The river crossing under fire + the sack of the town (Dec 11–12).** Engineers tried to lay the upper (town) bridges before dawn Dec 11 but were shot off the boats by **Barksdale's Mississippians (South)** firing from cellars and houses. Union artillery on Stafford Heights fired **thousands of shells** (WP body: "more than 5,000") into the town — ineffective against sheltered sharpshooters. **Col. Norman J. Hall (North)** then ran an **infantry assault crossing**: ~**135 men** of the 7th Michigan and 19th Massachusetts rowed across in pontoon boats (~3 p.m.) and cleared the riverbank in **the first major urban / house-to-house combat in American history.** The army crossed Dec 11–12; the downstream bridges (Franklin's front) went in with little opposition. Once in the town, **Union troops looted and vandalized** it severely (Lee likened them to "Vandals"); **civilian deaths were very few (≈4).** The South later raised ~**$170,000** to rebuild the town. [WP, ABT — `[VERIFY]` the "5,000 shells," the 135-man figure, and the $170,000.]

**Phase 2 — Franklin vs. Jackson at Prospect Hill (Dec 13, morning–early afternoon).** Dense fog lifted ~10:30 a.m. Franklin committed only a fraction of his huge wing: **Meade's division (~4,500, North)**, supported by Gibbon. As Meade advanced, **Maj. John Pelham (South)** held up the whole attack with **two guns** firing in enfilade from the flank (Lee: *"It is glorious to see such courage in one so young."*). Meade's men then found the **undefended swampy gap** in A.P. Hill's line and **pierced Jackson's front** — the battle's **one real breakthrough (turning point #2).** They overran Lane's and Archer's flanks; **Brig. Gen. Maxcy Gregg (South)** was mortally wounded rallying. But Meade had **no reinforcement** (Franklin held back most of his wing); Confederate reserves under **Early and Taliaferro (South)** counterattacked, drove Meade and Gibbon back across the railroad, and **sealed the gap.** Meade reportedly: *"My God, General Reynolds, did they think my division could whip Lee's whole army?"* `[VERIFY wording]` Franklin's wing lost ~**5,000**; Jackson's front ~**3,400**. [WP]

**Phase 3 — The Marye's Heights slaughter (Dec 13, ~noon–dark). THE battle.** Sumner ordered the assault ~11 a.m. The terrain forced each attack to **funnel over the canal bridges, re-form, and cross ~600 yards of open slope** into the fire of infantry stacked **multiple ranks deep** behind the **stone wall**, with artillery raking from the crest. Artillerist **E. Porter Alexander (South)** had told Longstreet: *"A chicken could not live on that field when we open on it."* `[VERIFY exact wording]` Burnside fed in **seven divisions, generally one brigade at a time — fourteen separate charges, every one a failure.** Notable: **Kimball's brigade** cut down ~125 yards short; the **Irish Brigade (Meagher, North)** lost **545 of ~1,200** men; **Humphreys's** green regiments charged at dusk with **unloaded rifles and fixed bayonets** and were shredded ~50 yards out. **No Union soldier reached the wall.** Confederate **Thomas R.R. Cobb (South)** was mortally wounded; **Kershaw (South)** took over the line. **Turning point #3:** by dark, the attacks were not a tactic but a massacre — the assault model itself had failed. [WP, ABT]

**Phase 4 — The night, the wounded, and withdrawal (Dec 13–15).** Thousands of wounded Union men lay on the frozen field overnight, unable to move under Confederate fire (the setting for the Kirkland story, §7). Dec 13 night, Burnside vowed to **personally lead his old IX Corps** in a renewed assault; his generals talked him out of it the next morning. **Dec 14:** armies held position; a truce let the Union recover wounded/dead. **Dec 15 (night):** the Army of the Potomac **withdrew back across the Rappahannock**, taking up its pontoon bridges; the campaign was over. [WP]

## 6. The cause — slavery (SHIP-BLOCKING)

> **STANDING SHIP-BLOCKING RULE — NAME THE CAUSE.** The section's own prose MUST,
> somewhere it sets the stakes, name **slavery** as what the war (and each side) was
> ultimately fought over. Omission auto-fails the framing gate. These are the factual
> hooks; the author writes one or two clear sentences from them.

- **The Emancipation Proclamation took effect ~3 weeks after this battle — Jan 1, 1863.** Lincoln had issued the **preliminary** proclamation **Sept 22, 1862** (days after Antietam), declaring that on **January 1, 1863**, all persons held as slaves in the rebelling states "shall be then, thenceforward, and forever free." So Fredericksburg was fought **in the final weeks** before the war's purpose was formally redefined to include destroying slavery. [Emancipation Proclamation, National Archives / WP; ECW]
- **The throughline is literal and local, not abstract.** Even before Jan 1, enslaved people were **fleeing to Union lines** at Fredericksburg; the Union army already sheltered them as **"contraband."** ECW's Fredericksburg case study documents enslaved people in and around Fredericksburg/Stafford crossing to freedom as the armies maneuvered, with **"small and large flocks of contrabands"** passing Union camps after emancipation took effect. The most-cited local witness is **John Washington**, an enslaved man in Fredericksburg who self-emancipated to Union lines in 1862 and left a memoir. `[VERIFY John Washington dating/details before asserting specifics]` [ECW; Discover Stafford / NPS "Trail to Freedom"]
- **The stakes the author can name plainly:** Burnside's men were dying in front of the stone wall **for** a cause that, three weeks later, would openly become the abolition of slavery; Lee's army was defending the slaveholding Confederacy. The contrast — a futile, bloody defeat for the Union, on the eve of the Proclamation — is honest and dramatic, not a tacked-on moral.
- **Do NOT romanticize the Confederate stand** (Lost Cause failure mode): the "magnificent defensive position" and "gallant" Pelham are real, but the section must keep clear who was defending what.

## 7. Human cost (named people, documented detail)

- **Richard Rowland Kirkland — "the Angel of Marye's Heights" (South).** Confederate **sergeant, Co. G, 2nd South Carolina** (Kershaw's Brigade), stationed at the stone wall. On **Dec 14**, with the field in front of the wall covered in wounded Union men crying for water, Kirkland got **Brig. Gen. Joseph B. Kershaw's** permission and climbed over the wall — **without a flag of truce (Kershaw refused one)** — to carry **water (and reportedly blankets/clothing)** to wounded **Union** soldiers in the open, repeatedly, while both sides held their fire. [WP-Kirkland; SC Enc.; ECW]
  - **`[VERIFY — story is contested.]`** The episode was **first recorded in 1880** by Kershaw, ~17 years after Kirkland was killed at Chickamauga (1863); there are **no contemporary accounts**, and historians (e.g., CWMemory) note it "may have been embellished." **Author must present it as a documented-but-contested legend**, not a verified fact — attribute to Kershaw's 1880 account. Memorialized by a **Felix de Weldon statue** at the Fredericksburg & Spotsylvania NMP. [WP-Kirkland; CWMemory; NPS]
- **The frozen field.** Thousands of wounded Union men lay overnight on the open ground before the wall, unable to move under fire; the dead in front of the wall (**~918 counted by a burial party**) were in many cases **stripped of shoes and uniforms** by Confederates in the night — the documented, un-romantic texture of the slaughter. [WP]
- **The Irish Brigade (North).** ~**545 of ~1,200** men of Meagher's Irish Brigade fell charging the wall — a single brigade's near-annihilation, one of the most-cited human details of the day. [WP — `[VERIFY]` exact 545/1,200.]
- **Aurora borealis, night of Dec 14.** An unusually strong **aurora** appeared over the battlefield — rare for that latitude — and was noted in soldiers' diaries as the dead and wounded lay below. A vivid, documented image for the close. [WP — `[VERIFY]` and keep it as soldier-diary detail, not over-claimed.]

## 9. Significance / what it changed

- **One of the most one-sided Union defeats of the war** — a numerically superior army wrecked itself against a fortified position. The frontal assaults on Marye's Heights became a byword for **futile, doomed attacks.** [WP, ABT]
- **Demonstrated the lethality of the defensive** (entrenched infantry + massed artillery vs. open frontal assault) — a lesson the war kept re-teaching at greater cost. [WP, ABT]
- **Burnside's command was finished** — the disaster, plus the bungled **"Mud March"** weeks later (Jan 1863), led to his replacement by **Hooker (North)** in late Jan 1863. [WP — `[VERIFY]` Mud March dating if used.]
- **Hammered Northern morale at a low point** — just **weeks before the Emancipation Proclamation took effect (Jan 1, 1863)**, deepening Northern war-weariness and political pressure on Lincoln. The battle's despair is inseparable from that moment in the war's moral turn. [WP; ECW]
- **Confederate high-water of confidence in the East** — fed the over-confidence that carried Lee through Chancellorsville (May 1863) toward Gettysburg. [WP]
