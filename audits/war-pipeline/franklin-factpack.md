## 1. Identity / Date / Location / Theatre

- **Name:** Battle of Franklin (1864) — the **Second** Battle of Franklin (a minor first
  engagement occurred at Franklin in 1863; the 1864 fight is the famous one). Nicknamed
  **"the Pickett's Charge of the West."** **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Date:** **November 30, 1864** (a single afternoon-into-night action). **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Location:** **Franklin, Williamson County, Tennessee** (Middle Tennessee, ~18 mi
  south of Nashville on the Columbia Pike). Coordinates ~35°55′N 86°52′W. **[WIKI]**
- **Theatre:** **Western Theater.** The climactic battle of the **Franklin–Nashville
  Campaign** (a.k.a. Hood's Tennessee Campaign / the Middle Tennessee Campaign). **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Result:** **Union (tactical) victory** — the Confederate assault failed and Schofield
  **(North)** then carried out his planned, orderly withdrawal to Nashville that night. **[WIKI] [ABT]**

### Why Franklin happened — Hood's last-gasp invasion (strategic spine)
- After the **fall of Atlanta (September 1864)** to Sherman **(North)**, Confederate Gen.
  **John Bell Hood (South)** declined to chase Sherman and instead **drove north into
  Tennessee**, gambling that an invasion of Tennessee and a threat to the Ohio could pull
  Sherman back, rally the cause, and recapture lost ground. **[WIKI] [ABT]** *(Sherman
  detail is context — Sherman was NOT at Franklin; he had turned the other way, toward the
  March to the Sea. Author may reference lightly.)*
- Hood's immediate objective at Franklin: **destroy Schofield's smaller Union force
  before it could unite with Maj. Gen. George H. Thomas's ~25,000 Federals concentrating
  at Nashville.** **[ABT]** Once Schofield reached Nashville's fortifications and joined
  Thomas, Hood's already-outnumbered army had no realistic path to victory — so Hood
  needed to catch and smash Schofield in the open. He failed to do so at Spring Hill (§5),
  which set up the desperate frontal assault at Franklin.

## 4. Geography (for map accuracy)

Relative positions (north = up):

- **The town of Franklin** sits on the **south bank of the Harpeth River**, which loops
  around the town's **north and east.** The **Harpeth is BEHIND (north of) the Union line** —
  Schofield's army had its back to the river, with only the bridges/crossings as its escape
  route. This is why Schofield fortified: he needed to hold while his **wagon trains crossed
  the Harpeth** to the north before he could withdraw to Nashville. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **The Union semicircle of works** ran in an **arc SOUTH and southwest of the town**,
  curving from the river on the west around to the river on the east — a **bridgehead
  perimeter** covering the southern approaches and the river crossings to its rear. The works
  were **strong, prepared entrenchments**: a ditch, an earthen parapet, and in the center a
  **second "retrenchment" line ~200 ft behind** the main line. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **The Columbia Pike** runs **south-to-north** straight into the town center and **pierces
  the Union line** at its center. A **gap was deliberately left in the works where the Pike
  passed through** (to let wagons/troops pass); that gap, backed by the retrenchment line,
  became the focal breach. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **The Carter House** stands **just inside (north of) the main line, on the west side of the
  Columbia Pike** — at the literal center of the Union position; it was **Cox's (North)
  headquarters.** Its **outbuildings (the Carter office, smokehouse, etc.) are still pocked
  with hundreds of bullet holes** — among the most battle-scarred structures of the war. **[WIKI] [ABT] [BOFT]**
- **The Carter cotton gin** stood **east of the Columbia Pike**, just in front of / at the
  Union line — forming a **small salient.** The fighting around the gin and the Pike was the
  bloodiest, most concentrated, hand-to-hand killing of the battle; **Cleburne fell ~50 yds
  in front of the cotton gin.** **[WIKI] [BOFT] [WHN]**
- **The Confederate approach:** Hood's army formed on the **open plain ~2 miles SOUTH of the
  works** and advanced **NORTH across roughly two miles of open, gently rolling farmland — no
  cover, in full view of the Union works.** **[WIKI] [ABT]** *(This open-field approach from
  the south is the map's defining feature; attack arrows come from the S, striking the
  southern arc of works, concentrating at the Columbia Pike / cotton gin center.)*
- **Wagner's forward brigades (North)** were posted **out in front (south) of the main line**
  in an exposed advanced position; when they were overrun, retreating Federals partly **masked
  the main line's fire**, letting the Confederates close the last half-mile. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Carnton plantation** (the McGavock house, the field hospital — §6) lies **southeast of the
  battlefield**, behind the Confederate right. **[BOFT]**
- **Map orientation summary:** town on the **south bank of the Harpeth (river wraps the N/E,
  behind the Union line)**; Union arc of works **bowed across the S/SW**; **Columbia Pike
  pierces the line S→N at the center**, Carter House just inside on the **W of the Pike**,
  cotton gin salient just outside on the **E of the Pike**; Confederate attack arrows sweep
  **N across ~2 miles of open ground from the S**; Carnton field hospital to the **SE.**

## 5. Sequence & turning points

### The prelude — the Spring Hill affair (Nov 29) [present the debate, don't assert motive]
- On **Nov 29**, Hood **(South)** out-marched Schofield **(North)**: he sent Cheatham's and
  Stewart's corps on a flanking march, crossing the Duck River east of Columbia, and by
  nightfall **Confederate troops had reached the Columbia Pike at Spring Hill — the very road
  Schofield's army had to use to retreat north.** Hood appeared to have the Union army
  **trapped.** **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Then, through a cascade of command failures (disputed orders, confusion in the dark, and
  Hood's premature confidence that Schofield was already trapped), the Confederates failed to
  block the road or attack.** Through the night of Nov 29–30, **Schofield's entire army and
  its wagon train marched up the Columbia Pike — in some accounts within yards / "within
  feet" of sleeping Confederate troops and campfires — and slipped past to Franklin
  unscathed.** **[WIKI]** *(The "within feet / past the campfires" vividness is widely
  repeated; [VERIFY] the most defensible phrasing — "within hailing distance" / "yards" is
  safest. The hard fact is the army escaped up the Pike overnight without being stopped.)*
- By dawn Nov 30 Schofield had reached **Franklin (~12 mi north)** and **entrenched.** **[WIKI]**
- **The historiographic debate (DO NOT assert motive as fact — report what sources say):**
  Hood awoke furious. ABT describes him as **"wrathy as a rattlesnake … accusing the army of
  cowardice"** at Spring Hill. **[ABT]** Cheatham's reported pre-battle warning — *"I do not
  like the looks of this fight; the enemy has an excellent position and is well fortified"* —
  shows his own generals thought the assault reckless. **[WIKI]** **Many historians argue the
  Franklin assault was driven by Hood's rage and humiliation over Spring Hill — a punitive,
  prove-yourselves charge.** Others note Hood's own stated military rationale (catch Schofield
  before Nashville; the short distance left no time to flank — see his quote, §7). **The
  author must present this as a debated interpretation, NOT as established fact: report that
  scholars commonly link the assault to Hood's Spring Hill fury, while Hood himself gave a
  strategic justification.** **[VERIFY]** any single-cause motive claim. **[WIKI] [ABT]**

### The assault (afternoon → dusk → night, Nov 30)
- **Hood ordered a massive frontal assault** despite the strong works and the open ground,
  and **despite objections** (Forrest **(South)** urged a flanking move; Cheatham **(South)**
  warned against it). **There was essentially no artillery preparation** — only **one to two
  batteries** in support, versus the **two-hour bombardment** that preceded Pickett's Charge. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **~4:00 p.m.** — roughly **20,000 Confederates** stepped off and advanced **~2 miles across
  open ground**, in full view, against the works. **Sunset that day was ~4:34 p.m.** — the
  bulk of the fighting was fought **into dusk and on into the night.** **[WIKI]**
- **The break-in at the center:** **Wagner's (North) two exposed forward brigades** were
  overwhelmed and fled back toward the works; the Union line **held its fire to avoid hitting
  its own men**, so **"the last half-mile of the Confederate advance was largely
  uncontested,"** and the Confederates (Cleburne's, Brown's, and French's troops) **broke
  through the gap at the Columbia Pike** and into the Carter House yard. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Opdycke seals the breach (the turning point):** **Col. Emerson Opdycke's (North)** reserve
  brigade — which he had **refused to post in the exposed forward line**, holding it ~200 yds
  behind the Carter House instead — **counterattacked into the breach** and, with rallied
  troops of Wagner's and the Carter-House defenders, **drove the Confederates back out of the
  works.** This sealed the only Confederate penetration. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **The slaughter at the works:** along the line — especially at the **cotton gin and the
  Columbia Pike** — the fighting was **hand-to-hand, at point-blank range, in the dark**, for
  roughly **five hours.** The Confederate brigades were shot to pieces against the parapet and
  in the ditch. This is where **six generals fell** (§6). **[WIKI] [ABT] [BOFT]**

### The withdrawal — the "victory" abandoned
- The Union held; but **holding Franklin was never Schofield's goal.** Once his **trains were
  across the Harpeth**, Schofield **(North)** carried out his planned move: **after dark, his
  army quietly withdrew across the river and marched north to Nashville**, abandoning the
  battlefield to Hood. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- Hood **(South)** thus held the field at dawn — a hollow "result." His army was wrecked, and
  it **limped north to Nashville** in Schofield's wake, where it would be **destroyed two
  weeks later (Battle of Nashville, Dec 15–16, 1864).** **[WIKI] [ABT]** *(Nashville is the
  sequel — set it up; full Nashville facts are out of scope of this pack.)*

## 6. Human cost — named people / vivid documented detail

### The six fallen generals (the "Pickett's Charge of the West" toll)
- **Five Confederate generals killed outright + one mortally wounded** — framed as **"six
  generals killed or mortally wounded"** **[WIKI] [ABT]:**
  1. **Maj. Gen. Patrick R. Cleburne (South)** — killed; the most famous loss (detail below).
  2. **Brig. Gen. John Adams (South)** — killed (reportedly trying to ride his horse over the
     parapet; *[VERIFY] the "horse on the works" detail before printing as fact*).
  3. **Brig. Gen. States Rights Gist (South)** — killed.
  4. **Brig. Gen. Hiram B. Granbury (South)** — killed.
  5. **Brig. Gen. Otho F. Strahl (South)** — killed.
  6. **Brig. Gen. John C. Carter (South)** — **mortally wounded** at Franklin, **died Dec 10,
     1864** (the "one mortal" of the six). **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **~14 Confederate generals total were casualties**: the six above, plus wounded generals
  incl. **Maj. Gen. John C. Brown (South)**, **Brig. Gen. Francis M. Cockrell (South)**,
  **Brig. Gen. Zachariah C. Deas (South)**, **Brig. Gen. Arthur M. Manigault (South)**,
  **Brig. Gen. Thomas M. Scott (South)**, **Brig. Gen. Jacob H. Sharp (South)**, plus
  **Brig. Gen. George W. Gordon (South) captured.** **[WIKI] [ABT]** *([VERIFY] exact total
  count — "fourteen" is the standard framing.)*
- **Comparison (the killing field for generals):** Franklin's **six generals killed/mortal**
  exceeded the general-officer death toll of **Gettysburg, Antietam, Chickamauga, and

- Irish-born, the Army of Tennessee's finest division commander ("the Stonewall of the West").
  **[ABT-CLEB]**
- Before the charge, his brigadier **Daniel C. Govan (South)** said something like, *"Well,

- Cleburne **had his horse shot from under him, went forward on foot, and was killed ~50 yds

  *([VERIFY] the precise "~50 yards / below the heart" detail before printing as exact.)*
- **The Carnton porch image (the war's bleakest still life):** on the morning of **Dec 1,
  1864**, the bodies of **four Confederate generals — Cleburne, Granbury, Adams, and Strahl —

- **Burial:** Cleburne was buried first at Rose Hill (Columbia), then reinterred at **St.
  John's Episcopal Church, Ashwood, Tennessee**, alongside Granbury and Strahl; in **1870**

### Tod Carter (South) — killed yards from his own home
- **Capt. Theodrick "Tod" Carter (South)**, a Confederate officer and **son of the Carter
  family whose house anchored the Union center.** Captured at Chattanooga (Nov 1863), he had
  **escaped** Federal captivity and made his way back; Franklin was his **first time home in
  ~three years.** **[TOD] [ABT]**
- Though his quartermaster duties didn't require him to charge, he rode forward with the
  attack, reportedly shouting **"Follow me, boys, I'm almost home!"** — and was **struck by a
  Union bullet roughly 500 ft / a few hundred yards from his own front yard.** **[ABT] [TOD]**
  **[VERIFY]** the "Follow me boys, I'm almost home!" wording before printing as a verbatim
  quote (widely repeated, attribution is traditional).
- His family **found him on the field after the battle, carried him into the Carter House,
  and he died there on Dec 2, 1864 — in the home he had been trying to reach.** **[TOD] [ABT]**

### The Carter House and Carnton — the human horror
- **The Carter family sheltered in the cellar** of the Carter House through the night while
  the battle's bloodiest fighting raged in their **own yard and around their outbuildings.**
  The buildings still carry **hundreds of bullet holes.** **[WIKI] [ABT] [BOFT]** *([VERIFY]
  "in the cellar" as the family's exact refuge — it is the standard account; confirm wording.)*
- **Carnton (the McGavock plantation)** became **the largest Confederate field hospital** in
  the area. **John and Carrie McGavock** opened their home to the wounded; a contemporary
  account: **"the wounded, in hundreds, were brought to [the house] during the battle, and all
  the night after,"** and when the house filled, **"the yard was appropriated until the
  wounded and dead filled that."** **[BOFT]** As many as **~300 wounded** were treated inside
  Carnton; the **wooden floors remain blood-stained** to this day. **[BOFT]** *([VERIFY] the
  "~300" / "150 died the first night" figures — sources vary slightly.)*
- **McGavock Confederate Cemetery:** in **early 1866**, John and Carrie McGavock set aside
  **~2 acres** beside their family plot and reburied **nearly 1,500 (≈1,481) Confederate
  soldiers** killed at Franklin — the **largest privately owned military cemetery in the
  United States.** **[BOFT] [WIKI]**
- **Note:** Carrie McGavock's lifelong tending of the cemetery is the basis of Robert Hicks's
  novel *The Widow of the South* — *cultural-afterlife context only; [VERIFY] before any use.*

## 8. THE CAUSE — SLAVERY (ship-blocking) + the local hook

> **NAME THE CAUSE (locked, ship-blocking).** The section MUST, in its own prose, name
> **slavery** as the thing the war — and each side — was ultimately fought over. Omission is
> the default Lost Cause failure; "Hood's invasion" / "a brave doomed charge" framing alone
> fails the gate. Franklin gives us **two** concrete, documented hooks tying this slaughter
> directly to slavery — use at least one, ideally both.

### The documented throughline
- Hood's **(South)** army marched into Tennessee to revive a Confederacy fighting to **preserve
  and extend slavery.** The men who died in the ditch at the cotton gin charged for a
  government whose reason for existing was the ownership of human beings — including the **44
  enslaved people held at Carnton**, the very plantation that became their death-house field
  hospital. **[BOFT]** The war's cause was not abstract here; it was the property line of the
  ground they bled on.

### STRONG HOOK #1 — Cleburne's own proposal to arm and FREE the slaves **[ABT-CLEB] [BOFT]**

## 9. Significance

- **A tactical Union victory that was immediately abandoned** — Schofield **(North)** held the
  line, then withdrew to Nashville as planned, exactly as he had intended. Holding Franklin
  was never the goal; getting the army and trains safely to Nashville was. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **It wrecked the Army of Tennessee as a fighting force** — Hood **(South)** lost ~6,000+
  men, **six generals killed/mortally wounded, ~14 generals as casualties, and ~55 regimental
  commanders** in five hours. The **veteran officer corps was irreplaceable**; the army
  **"had shattered itself beyond the possibility of ever doing so again."** **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **It set up Nashville** — the gutted Confederate army limped north and was **destroyed two
  weeks later at the Battle of Nashville (Dec 15–16, 1864)**, after which the Army of Tennessee
  **"would never fight again as a cohesive force."** Franklin + Nashville together **ended
  Confederate military power in the West.** **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **The strategic verdict on Hood's invasion:** the whole Tennessee gamble — Spring Hill,
  Franklin, Nashville — **failed catastrophically**, costing the Confederacy one of its two
  major field armies for no gain. Franklin is remembered as the **futile, self-inflicted
  bloodbath** at the heart of that failure — the **"Pickett's Charge of the West,"** but worse:
  larger, with no artillery prep, across twice the open ground, into stronger works, at dusk. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Preservation note:** much of the Franklin battlefield was lost to 20th-century development
  (a strip mall, a Pizza Hut lot on the cotton-gin ground); the Carter House, Carnton, and
  reclaimed acreage are now preserved interpretive sites. *(Context; [VERIFY] specifics if used.)* **[ABT]**

## 10. Blind spots / open items for the fact-checker

- **HOOD'S MOTIVE IS THE BIG ONE (historiographic minefield).** The popular reading — that
  Hood ordered the suicidal frontal assault out of **rage/humiliation over Spring Hill**,
  to punish an army he'd accused of cowardice — is an **interpretation, widely argued but NOT
  settled fact.** Hood gave a **strategic justification** (catch Schofield before Nashville;
  no distance left to flank — quote #3). **The author must report the debate, attribute the
  rage-reading to historians, and NOT assert a single motive as established.** This is the
  section's main accuracy risk.
- **Confederate casualty total is contested** — Schofield's ~6,252 vs. Hood's ~4,500. Use a
  **range (~6,000–7,000 total, ~1,750 killed the cited figure)**, not a false-precision number.
- **General-officer casualty TOTAL** — sources say "fourteen"; the precise count (13/14/15)
  and the exact wounded list need confirming. The **six killed/mortal** list IS solid:
  Cleburne, Adams, Gist, Granbury, Strahl (killed) + John C. Carter (mortally wounded, d. Dec 10).
- **Quotes #1, #2, #4, #5, #6** (Cleburne "die like men," Cheatham's warning, "wrathy as a
  rattlesnake," Tod Carter's "almost home," Schofield's post-battle line) — confirm verbatim
  wording and that they're the named person's own words before printing inside quotes.
- **Cleburne's death specifics** (~50 yds from the gin / ball below the heart), **John Adams's
  "horse on the parapet" death**, and **the Carter family "in the cellar"** are all the
