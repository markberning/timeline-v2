# FACT PACK — "War Finance & the Road to Taxation" (1763–1765)

## 1. THE DEBT — the war was ruinously expensive

**Gloss a "national debt" for the newcomer:** money the government had borrowed (largely by selling bonds to lenders) and had to pay back over time WITH INTEREST. The yearly interest payments are a fixed bill that comes due whether or not there's a war on.

- **Britain's national debt roughly doubled across the war.** From about **£75 million in 1756** (war's start) to roughly **£130–133 million by 1763** (war's end). Commonly cited precise figure: **£132.6 million**.
  - Source: [Financial costs of the Seven Years' War — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_costs_of_the_Seven_Years%27_War); [Lumen Learning, "Confronting the National Debt"](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-ushistory1/chapter/confronting-the-national-debt-the-aftermath-of-the-french-and-indian-war/)
  - NOTE on numbers: ~£75M → ~£130–133M. The "roughly doubled" framing and "about £130 million" are both safe. The £132.6M figure is the most precise commonly cited. The American Battlefield Trust says only "nearly doubled" without a number.
- **Interest payments alone consumed a huge share of the budget.** Sources state interest on the debt consumed **over half the national budget** in the aftermath of the war. [Lumen Learning](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-ushistory1/chapter/confronting-the-national-debt-the-aftermath-of-the-french-and-indian-war/)
  - [CONTESTED / soften]: "over half the national budget" is a common textbook figure but the exact share varies by source and by how you count. Safe to say interest "ate a large share of the budget" or "consumed much of the budget"; cite "over half" as the high commonly-given figure if you want a number.

## 2. THE STANDING ARMY — ~10,000 troops kept in North America

- **Britain decided to keep a peacetime standing army of about 10,000 regulars in North America** after 1763, to garrison the vast new territory won from France.
  - The decision was made by the **Bute ministry in early 1763** — BEFORE Pontiac's War broke out and before Grenville took office. (Important: not a reaction to Pontiac; the garrison was planned, then Pontiac's War seemed to confirm the "need" for it.)
  - Source: [American History Central — George Grenville](https://www.americanhistorycentral.com/entries/george-grenville-prime-minister/)
- **Annual cost** [CONTESTED — range]: sources give different figures. Common ones:
  - **~£200,000/year** (the figure tied to Grenville's planning; the predecessor Bute's recommendation).
  - **~£225,000/year** (another commonly cited figure for 10,000 regulars).
  - Up to **£360,000–£400,000/year** in some sources (likely including West Indies garrisons / fuller establishment).
  - Sources: [American History Central — George Grenville](https://www.americanhistorycentral.com/entries/george-grenville-prime-minister/); secondary works summarized in search. SAFE TO WRITE: "roughly £200,000 a year" or "a few hundred thousand pounds a year"; flag the range rather than asserting one exact number.
- **Colonial suspicion of a peacetime standing army.** A standing army in peacetime was, to many colonists, an alarming and unprecedented presence — long associated in the British political tradition with tyranny and the suppression of liberty. (This is the author's connective point; the fact base for it is the colonial constitutional anxiety documented across the period. State it as colonial perception, which is well attested, not as a single sourced quote unless one is added.)

## 3. BRITAIN'S OWN HEAVY TAXATION + THE 1763 CIDER TAX RIOTS

- **The British public at home was already heavily taxed** — among the most heavily taxed populations in Europe — which is part of why raising more revenue at home was politically impossible and Grenville looked to America.
- **The Cider Bill / Cyder Act of 1763** (Lord Bute's ministry) imposed a tax on cider production — **4 shillings per hogshead of cider made**.
  - It gave **sweeping powers to excise collectors**, including the power of **warrantless searches of private homes** (cider was made domestically), which is what made it explosive — it was seen as government intrusion into the home as much as a money grab.
  - It **set off riots and widespread outrage** across classes, especially in the cider-producing west of England, and badly damaged the Bute ministry. **Bute was hanged in effigy.**
  - **Grenville (Bute's successor) pushed the cider tax through and defeated a repeal motion in February 1764.** The cider tax was eventually **repealed in 1766** (same year as the Stamp Act repeal).
  - Source: [Cider Bill of 1763 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cider_Bill_of_1763)
- **The connective point (well attested):** because new taxes at home triggered riots, Grenville turned to the colonies for revenue. Historians note Grenville proceeded with the Stamp Act partly because earlier American measures had NOT provoked the kind of fury the cider tax had at home — he misjudged how the colonies would react. [Cider Bill of 1763 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cider_Bill_of_1763)

## 4. GEORGE GRENVILLE — the reasoning

- **George Grenville** became First Lord of the Treasury / Prime Minister in **April 1763** and led the ministry that designed the colonial revenue program.
  - Source: [Britannica — George Grenville](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Grenville); [American History Central](https://www.americanhistorycentral.com/entries/george-grenville-prime-minister/)
- **His reasoning:** the American colonists were the war's **chief beneficiaries** (France had been driven from the continent), the army was being kept for **their** defense, and they should therefore **help pay for it.** He did NOT expect the colonies to pay down the national debt or its interest — only to **contribute a portion of the cost of their own defense.**
  - **Estimated defense cost ~£200,000/year; his revenue program was projected to raise only ~£78,000–79,000/year** — i.e., a partial contribution, not the whole army. (Sugar Act portion estimated at ~£78,000 by the Treasury.)
  - Source: [American History Central — George Grenville](https://www.americanhistorycentral.com/entries/george-grenville-prime-minister/)
- **The fatal gap:** the colonies **elected no members to Parliament.** Parliament was taxing people who had no vote in it — the constitutional nerve the colonists pressed (see §7).

## 5. THE SUGAR ACT (1764) + THE CURRENCY ACT (1764)

### Sugar Act 1764 (a.k.a. American Revenue Act / American Duties Act)
- **Passed 5 April 1764.** [Sugar Act — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Act)
- **It LOWERED the molasses duty — from 6 pence to 3 pence per gallon** on foreign (non-British) molasses. The earlier **Molasses Act of 1733** had set the 6-pence duty but was widely evaded by smuggling and barely enforced.
- **The crucial point: the lower rate was set to be ENFORCED, to actually RAISE REVENUE** — not merely to regulate trade. The act's preamble explicitly stated revenue intent:
  - VERBATIM (preamble): **"it is just and necessary that a revenue should be raised … for defraying the expenses of defending, protecting, and securing"** the colonies. [Sugar Act — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Act)
  - This is the conceptual hinge: a duty colonists could smuggle around at 6d became a real, collected tax at 3d. The point was revenue, not trade regulation — the first time Parliament taxed the colonies *for money* rather than to steer commerce.
- **Enforcement mechanisms:** required detailed ship cargo manifests and paperwork; expanded customs officers' powers; and — most resented — **violations tried in vice-admiralty courts** (Crown-appointed judge, NO local jury, presumption of guilt) instead of colonial courts (local juries who routinely acquitted smugglers).
  - Source: [Sugar Act — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Act); [NPS — Sugar & Stamp Acts](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/sugar-and-stamp-acts.htm)
- **Treasury revenue estimate: ~£78,000/year.** (Grenville proposed it 9 March 1764; enacted April.)

### Currency Act 1764
- **Royal assent 19 April 1764; in force 1 September 1764.** [Currency Act — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_Act)
- **What it did:** it did NOT ban colonial paper money outright, but it **forbade the colonies from making future paper-currency issues legal tender** for public or private debts.
- **Scope:** extended to **ALL the British North American colonies** the restriction the **1751 Currency Act** had placed only on New England (Rhode Island, Connecticut, Massachusetts, New Hampshire).
- **Why it hurt:** **gold and silver coin (specie / "hard money") was chronically scarce in the colonies.** Forbidding paper money as legal tender squeezed an economy that had little hard coin to trade with — a "tight money" pinch arriving at the same moment Britain wanted to be paid. [Currency Act — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_Act)

## 6. THE STAMP ACT (1765) — the first direct internal tax

- **Passed by Parliament 22 March 1765; took effect 1 November 1765.** [Stamp Act 1765 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamp_Act_1765)
- **It was the FIRST DIRECT INTERNAL TAX Parliament ever laid on the colonies.**
  - VERBATIM (Wikipedia summarizing): the first **"internal tax (a tax based entirely on activities within the colonies) levied directly on the colonies by Parliament."**
  - **Gloss "direct / internal tax" for the newcomer:** earlier duties (like the molasses duty) were *external* — taxes on goods crossing a border, collected at the port. The stamp tax was *internal* — a tax on everyday transactions happening entirely inside a colony, with nothing crossing any border. That distinction was the whole fight (see Franklin §8).
- **What needed a stamp:** a purchased, embossed government stamp was required on **newspapers, pamphlets, legal documents, magazines, playing cards, dice, and many other kinds of paper** used in the colonies. It touched lawyers, printers, merchants, tavern-keepers, and ordinary people at once.
- **Payable in hard currency:** the stamp duties **had to be paid in specie (gold/silver coin), not colonial paper money** — and hard coin was exactly what the colonies lacked (tie back to the Currency Act, §5). [Stamp Act 1765 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamp_Act_1765)

## 7. "NO TAXATION WITHOUT REPRESENTATION"

**Gloss the principle for the newcomer:** the colonists held that a free people can only be taxed by a body they have voted for. They elected NO members to the British Parliament, so (they argued) Parliament had no right to tax them — only their OWN elected colonial assemblies could. To be taxed by a legislature you can't vote in is, in their view, to have your property taken without consent.

- **On the slogan itself [IMPORTANT — be careful]:** the famous phrase **"no taxation without representation"** is NOT cleanly traceable to one 1765 speaker. The exact phrase **first appeared in print in February 1768** in the *London Magazine* (Lord Camden's speech). The line **"taxation without representation is tyranny"** is *attributed* to James Otis but was a later (early-1800s) paraphrase by a biographer, NOT a verified verbatim Otis quote. [No taxation without representation — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_taxation_without_representation)
  - SAFE TO WRITE: that the *principle/cry* of no taxation without representation became the colonists' rallying idea — but do NOT put the exact slogan in quotation marks as a verbatim 1765 utterance by a named person. Treat it as the era's distilled cry, not a sourced quotation.
- **James Otis Jr.**, pamphlet *The Rights of the British Colonies Asserted and Proved* (**1764**) — the real, verifiable articulation:
  - VERBATIM: **"The very act of taxing, exercised over those who are not represented, appears to me to be depriving them of one of their most essential rights"** (Otis, 1764). [No taxation without representation — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_taxation_without_representation) (Otis serves as the safe, sourced colonial voice on the principle.)
- **The British counter-argument — "virtual representation":** Britain claimed the colonists *were* represented, "virtually," the same way most Britons (who also couldn't vote) were said to be represented by Parliament as a whole.
  - VERBATIM (Samuel Johnson, *Taxation No Tyranny*): **"They are represented by the same virtual representation as the greater part of England."** Colonists dismissed virtual representation as sophistry. [No taxation without representation — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_taxation_without_representation)
  - NOTE: Johnson's pamphlet is 1775 — fine as a clear statement of the British position, but don't date it to 1765.

## 8. THE COLONIAL RESPONSE

### Stamp Act Congress (1765)
- **Met in New York City, October 1765**, with **27 delegates from 9 colonies.** [Stamp Act 1765 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamp_Act_1765)
- It issued a **Declaration of Rights and Grievances.**
  - VERBATIM (Declaration of Rights and Grievances, 1765): taxes could be imposed only **"with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives."**
  - VERBATIM: **"The only Representatives of the People of these Colonies, are Persons chosen therein by themselves, and that no Taxes ever have been, or can be Constitutionally imposed on them, but by their respective Legislature[s]."** [No taxation without representation — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_taxation_without_representation)
- **James Otis was a delegate** and sat on the committee that drafted the address to the House of Commons. [Britannica — James Otis](https://www.britannica.com/biography/James-Otis)

### The Sons of Liberty / direct action
- **Andrew Oliver**, the appointed **Massachusetts stamp distributor, was hanged in effigy on 14 August 1765** (Boston), his property attacked; he was **forced to resign.**
- **By 16 November 1765, twelve stamp distributors had resigned** under pressure.
- The **Sons of Liberty** organized coordinated intimidation and protest across the colonies, and **non-importation (boycott) agreements** pressured British merchants. [Stamp Act 1765 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamp_Act_1765)
- Net effect: by the day it took effect (1 Nov 1765), the Stamp Act was largely **unenforceable** — there was almost no one left willing to distribute the stamps.

### Franklin's testimony — repeal
- **Benjamin Franklin examined before the House of Commons, 13 February 1766** (the "Examination"); he answered **174 questions over ~4 hours**, arguing for repeal.
  - VERBATIM (Franklin, 1766): **"I had never heard any objection to the right of laying duties to regulate commerce; but a right to lay internal taxes was never supposed to be in parliament, as we are not represented there."** [Founders Online — Examination before the Committee of the Whole of the House of Commons](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-13-02-0035); [MHS — The Examination of Doctor Benjamin Franklin](https://www.masshist.org/database/viewer.php?item_id=251&pid=2) — this is the cleanest verbatim line tying the internal/external-tax distinction to no-representation.
  - **[CORRECTED in reconcile, web-verified against Founders Online 01-13-02-0035]:** the prior wording of this quote was MISQUOTED — it opened with an invented clause, "Before 1763, there was no objection to the right of [Parliament] laying duties to regulate commerce…". That phrasing does NOT appear in Franklin's Examination. The true verbatim opening is **"I had never heard any objection to the right of laying duties to regulate commerce…"** (the "Before 1763" framing and the bracketed "[Parliament]" insertion were not in the source). Use the corrected text above; do not reintroduce the "Before 1763" opening.
  - VERBATIM (Franklin, 1766, on the boycott's effect): asked what was now the pride of Americans — **"To wear their old cloaths over again, till they can make new ones."** [MHS — The Examination](https://www.masshist.org/database/viewer.php?item_id=251&pid=2)

### Repeal + Declaratory Act (1766)
- **The Stamp Act was repealed — royal assent 18 March 1766** (via the Duties in American Colonies Act / repeal act). [Stamp Act 1765 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamp_Act_1765)
- **On the SAME DAY (18 March 1766), Parliament passed the Declaratory Act** to save face and assert its authority.
  - Full title: **"An Act for the better securing the Dependency of His Majesty's Dominions in America upon the Crown and Parliament of Great Britain."** Citation: American Colonies Act 1766 (6 Geo. 3. c. 12); commenced 1 May 1766.
  - VERBATIM key phrase: Parliament had power to make laws binding the colonies **"in all cases whatsoever."** [Declaratory Act — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaratory_Act)
  - NOTE: Wikipedia's Declaratory Act page did not reproduce "in all cases whatsoever" in the fetched text, but the phrase is the act's well-attested statutory language and appears verbatim across standard sources (incl. the Stamp Act page summary). Safe to quote; it is the act's actual wording.
- **The meaning:** Britain gave up the tax but NOT the *principle*. It asserted total legislative supremacy — setting up the next clash (Townshend Acts, 1767) rather than settling anything.

## 9. THE THROUGHLINE — one victory, two fuses

This is the section's spine. All facts below are established above; this just states the connective logic the author should drive.

- **The war debt drove BOTH fuses at once:**
  - **Fuse 1 — the Native nations / the West.** To hold the new land, Britain kept the standing army AND issued the **Royal Proclamation of 1763 (7 October 1763)**, drawing a **Proclamation Line along the crest of the Appalachians** and reserving the west as Indian territory (forbidding colonial settlement and private land purchase beyond it). This was tied to **Pontiac's War** (see §10) — itself partly provoked by the new British posture (Amherst ending the customary diplomatic gifts, settler encroachment). Holding the conquest had a price, and that price was the army the debt couldn't easily afford. [Royal Proclamation of 1763 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Proclamation_of_1763)
  - **Fuse 2 — the colonists.** The same debt + same army cost produced the **Sugar / Currency / Stamp Acts** that enraged the colonies.
  - The author's frame from the brief: *the same victory that doomed the Native nations and burdened Britain lit the fuse of the American Revolution.* Both fuses run back to one number: the cost of winning.
- **The idea of colonial union came back in anger.** The **Albany Plan of Union (1754)** — Benjamin Franklin's rejected proposal at the Albany Congress (10 July 1754) for an intercolonial government, paired with his **"Join, or Die"** snake cartoon (Pennsylvania Gazette, 1754) — had FAILED to unite the colonies for defense. A decade later that same idea of intercolonial cooperation returned, this time AGAINST Britain, at the **Stamp Act Congress (1765)** and later the **First Continental Congress (convened 5 September 1774).** The union Britain couldn't get the colonies to form for the war, the colonists formed against the bill for it. [Albany Plan — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albany_Plan); [State Dept — Albany Plan](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1750-1775/albany-plan)
- **One-line synthesis (facts, not prose to copy):** the quarrel over who would pay for the LAST war set the colonies on the road to the NEXT one — against Britain itself.

## 10. THE NATIVE NATIONS — first-class actors (Pontiac's War)

The Proclamation Line and the standing army are not abstractions: they sit on top of an active Native war. Treat these nations as actors with their own aims, not backdrop.

- **Pontiac's War: 27 April 1763 – 25 July 1766.** [Pontiac's War — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontiac%27s_War)
- **Nations involved (use correct names):**
  - Great Lakes: **Odawa (Ottawa), Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Wyandot (Huron)**
  - Ohio Country: **Lenape (Delaware), Shawnee, Seneca, Mingo**
  - Illinois Country: **Miami, Kickapoo, Wea, Mascouten, Piankashaw**
  - NOTE: Wikipedia stresses these were loosely coordinated communities, NOT one centralized confederacy under single command. Don't overstate unified command.
- **Leaders / inspiration:**
  - **Pontiac** — Odawa (Ottawa) war leader, the most prominent figure; **laid siege to Fort Detroit** (from May 1763) with several hundred warriors — a siege that held for roughly six months. (Modern historians debate how much he actually coordinated the wider war.)
  - **Neolin, "the Delaware Prophet"** — Lenape spiritual leader whose teaching gave the resistance its ideological charge: the **Master of Life was displeased that Indians had taken up the white men's bad habits** (especially dependence on European goods and alcohol) and that they should return to older ways and push back the British. Pontiac drew on Neolin's preaching.
  - Sources: [Pontiac's War — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontiac%27s_War); [Royal Proclamation of 1763 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Proclamation_of_1763)
- **Their OWN aims / leverage:** to halt British settler encroachment on their lands, and to force the British to resume the **diplomatic gift-giving** (powder, goods) that the French had practiced and that **General Jeffery Amherst had cut off (from Feb. 1761)** as a cost-saving and contemptuous policy. Native nations regarded gifts as a necessary part of diplomacy, not charity; ending them was read as hostility. This was a war to defend sovereignty and the terms of trade, not a mere "uprising."
- **Scale / human cost:**
  - **Eight British forts fell between 16 May and 19 June 1763**; Forts Pitt and Detroit held.
  - **~400 British soldiers killed**; an estimated **2,000 settlers killed or captured** on the frontier. [Pontiac's War — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontiac%27s_War)
  - [CONTESTED]: casualty figures are estimates and vary; present as "roughly" / "an estimated."
- **Outcome:** historians now read it as a **military stalemate, not a Native defeat** — and one direct result was the **Royal Proclamation of 1763** boundary protecting Native land west of the Appalachians. So the army the war debt paid for, the Native war it was fighting, and the Proclamation Line that enraged land-hungry colonists are ONE knot — all downstream of the conquest.
  - DISPOSSESSION caveat the author should keep honest: the Proclamation "protected" Native land only on paper and only temporarily; colonists ignored the line, and it was steadily eroded. Don't present 1763 as a durable safeguard.

## SUPPLEMENT — the Quartering Act of 1765 (added in reconcile)

*Web-verified June 2026 against Wikipedia (Quartering Acts), American Battlefield Trust, History.com, and the UK Statutes Project (5 Geo. III c. 33). This beat belongs with the Stamp Act (§6) and the "one debt, two fuses" thesis (§9): it is the standing army's upkeep (§2) landed directly on colonial purses.*

- **The Quartering Act of 1765 (5 Geo. III c. 33)** was an amendment to the Mutiny Act. **Royal assent: 15 May 1765** — passed in the **same parliamentary session as the Stamp Act** (Stamp Act assent 22 March 1765, §6), both under the Grenville ministry.
  - **[DATE — web-verified, brief corrected]:** the supplement brief gave "24 May 1765," but the well-attested date across authoritative secondary sources (Wikipedia, History.com) is **15 May 1765**. (A minority of sources give 24 March 1765.) **Author should write "15 May 1765," or just "in the spring of 1765, the same session as the Stamp Act," and NOT assert 24 May.** Source: [Quartering Acts — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartering_Acts); [Parliament passes the Quartering Act — History.com](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/may-15/parliament-passes-the-quartering-act).
- **What it required:** it put the burden on the **colonial ASSEMBLIES / colonial authorities** to **provide and PAY for the housing and provisioning of British regular troops** stationed in their colony — barracks first, and the **food, supplies, and necessaries** (the act enumerated items such as bedding, firewood, candles, cooking utensils, and a drink ration) to go with them. The colonists themselves, not the Crown, footed the bill. Source: [Quartering Acts — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartering_Acts); [The Quartering Act — American Battlefield Trust](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/quartering-act).
- **[CONTESTED / clarification — keep the 1765 beat tight]:** the 1765 Act did **NOT** authorize billeting troops in **occupied private homes.** It directed quartering in **barracks**, and where those were full, in **public houses / inns / alehouses / livery stables / victualling houses**, and beyond that in **uninhabited houses, outhouses, barns, and other buildings** — never in homes where people lived. The "redcoats forced into your parlor" image is a later popular myth; **do not overstate private-home quartering for 1765.** Source: [The Quartering Act — American Battlefield Trust](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/quartering-act); [Quartering Acts — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartering_Acts).
- **The most explosive enforcement came in 1767 — OUTSIDE this section's window.** When ~1,500 troops arrived and the **New York Provincial Assembly refused to fully comply** with the Act, Parliament retaliated with the **New York Restraining Act of 1767**, suspending the New York assembly until it complied. That fight is a **1767 Townshend-era story**, not a 1763–1765 one — the author should gesture to it as a downstream consequence at most, and keep the in-window beat to the Act's passage and its meaning. Source: [Quartering Acts — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartering_Acts).
- **Why it matters to "one debt, two fuses" (§9):** the Quartering Act took the **upkeep of the ~10,000-man peacetime standing army (§2)** and dropped it **directly onto the colonists' own purses**, by act of a Parliament they did not elect. It therefore **fused the two grievances the section is built on**: the old British fear of a **standing army as the instrument of tyranny** AND the new cry of **taxation without consent (§7)**. To the colonists a forced levy to feed and house the King's soldiers — voted by Parliament, payable by their assemblies — was **a tax in disguise**, indistinguishable in principle from the Stamp Act. It is the same fuse (army cost born of the war debt) lit from a second angle.
- **It became a grievance in the Declaration of Independence (1776).** The Quartering Acts are echoed in the Declaration's bill of indictment against the King, including the clause **"For quartering large bodies of armed troops among us"** (and the charge that he kept "Standing Armies" among them in peacetime without consent of the legislatures). Source: [Quartering Acts — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartering_Acts).
