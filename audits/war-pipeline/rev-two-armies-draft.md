# Two Armies
> eyebrow: The year Britain ran two wars at once, hundreds of miles apart, and only one of them mattered

In the summer of 1777 Britain had two armies loose in America, and they were marching in opposite directions. One came down out of Canada, hacking south through the woods toward Albany on the Hudson River, sure it was about to cut New England off from the rest of the rebellion and end the war. The other loaded itself onto a fleet at New York and sailed away, south by sea, to take Philadelphia, the rebel capital, sure that capturing the largest city in America would end the war. Neither commander thought he needed the other. Nobody in London had forced the two plans to mesh. And across the ocean, in Paris, the foreign minister of France was watching both campaigns for one thing only: proof that this rebellion could survive long enough to be worth a war. The year would end with Britain holding a captured capital that decided nothing and missing an entire field army that had ceased to exist. The story of 1777 is the story of two clocks ticking out of time with each other, and of the men under each who could not hear the other one running.

## The plan that wasn't one
> eyebrow: Two field commanders, two war plans, and a minister who approved both

Start with the man who would lose the army. Lieutenant General John Burgoyne was a cavalry showman, a member of Parliament, and a playwright with one comedy already staged in London, and his friends called him "Gentleman Johnny." Over the winter of 1776 and 1777 he had been in London lobbying past his own superior in Canada, and in February he handed the government a paper titled "Thoughts for Conducting the War from the Side of Canada." The plan had a clean logic to it. A main army would push from Canada up Lake Champlain to Albany; a smaller force would come down the Mohawk Valley to the west to meet it; and the whole point was to seize the line of the Hudson and split New England, the granary and the manpower of the rebellion, off from the rest of the colonies, the head cut from the body. On paper, after the lessons of 1776, it made sense.

The other British general had his own plan, and he changed it more than once. General Sir William Howe, holding New York, had drawn up a scheme the previous November that included a strong prong up the Hudson to junction with whatever came down from Canada. By his letters of early April 1777 he had dropped it. He would take Philadelphia instead, the rebel capital and the largest city in America, and he would take it by sea. He told London plainly that he could no longer support a junction with the northern army. His theory was that seizing the capital would finally drag George Washington into the open, pitched, decisive battle that the war kept refusing to produce. It was a real theory, wrongly weighted, not idiocy.

The man in the middle was Lord George Germain, the cabinet secretary running the American war from London across an ocean that took a letter six to ten weeks to cross one way. He approved Burgoyne's plan in March 1777, and Burgoyne had it in hand in Quebec by early May. He approved Howe's Philadelphia plan too, and Howe got the word in early June while he was already preparing to embark. On the eighteenth of May Germain wrote Howe a letter approving the Philadelphia expedition while trusting that it would be done in time for Howe to cooperate with the army coming from Canada. That was a hope, not an order. It reached Howe in mid-August, at sea, long past the point where he could have turned around to act on it. Nobody in London ever issued a positive command making one of the two plans serve the other.

There is a famous story that explains the disaster as an accident of paperwork, and it is mostly a myth. The tale goes that an order positively directing Howe up the Hudson was drafted, but that Germain, impatient to leave for his country estate in Kent, would not wait for a clean copy, so the order was filed in a pigeonhole and never sent, dooming Burgoyne for want of a single dispatch. It is a good story. It traces to a much-later secondhand reminiscence, and historians working from the full published correspondence reject it as the explanation. Howe knew Burgoyne's plan; Germain had sent him a copy of Burgoyne's instructions. Howe had told London in April that he would not support a junction. Germain approved the Philadelphia move anyway. The failure was not a missing piece of paper. It was a system that let two field commanders file separate war plans and a minister who ratified both without ever forcing them to fit together. A lost dispatch is a better story. It is not the true one.

[The idea of independence](/war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/idea-of-independence)

Sir Henry Clinton, left holding New York with a reduced garrison of about seven thousand men, mostly loyalist and German units, told Howe to his face that the move was wrong. He was overruled. So in the summer of 1777 the two British armies in the thirteen colonies set off in opposite directions, Burgoyne south toward Albany expecting cooperation, Howe south by sea toward Chesapeake Bay and away from him, with the Atlantic too slow for anyone to fix it once it had started.

## Burgoyne comes south
> eyebrow: A fortress that fell to a hill, a thousand axmen, and a killing that recruited an army

Burgoyne left Canada in June with something like seven thousand eight hundred regulars, roughly half of them British and half German troops from Brunswick and Hanau under Major General Friedrich Riedesel, plus a long artillery train, several hundred Canadians and loyalists, and ultimately around five hundred Native allies whose numbers rose and fell through the campaign. By the first of July he stood before Fort Ticonderoga, the great stone-and-earth work guarding the narrows between Lake Champlain and Lake George that the colonists had been calling the Gibraltar of the North. It was held by Major General Arthur St. Clair with an understrength garrison of perhaps two thousand five hundred to three thousand men.

The fort fell without a battle, and it fell to a hill. Southwest of Ticonderoga rose Sugar Loaf, a steep height the Americans had left unfortified on the assumption that nobody could drag guns up it. Burgoyne's engineers cut a road and dragged cannon to the summit. As the army's own story had it, the artillery commander put the principle simply: where a goat can go a man can go, and where a man can go he can drag a gun. When the garrison woke to a battery looking straight down into their works, the position was finished. On the night of the fifth and sixth of July, St. Clair evacuated, slipping his men out southeast and floating his supplies down to Skenesborough, and the British walked into Ticonderoga on the sixth without firing a shot. The rearguard was caught and mauled the next day at Hubbardton, the only real fight of the episode, but the covering force got the army away. The American public, raised on the legend of the Gibraltar of the North, was stunned; St. Clair was court-martialed for giving up the fort and later acquitted, having saved his army by losing the fortress. In London, by Horace Walpole's gossipy account, George III burst in on the Queen crying that he had beaten the Americans, all of them. It is the same ground, exactly, where a British army had bled to death in front of these walls in the last war, at the battle the French called Carillon in 1758; in 1777 the place changed hands twice over without a battle.

Then the woods slowed everything down. From Skenesborough, Burgoyne chose the overland route to the Hudson through the drowned lowlands of Wood Creek rather than backtracking to the Lake George portage, and Major General Philip Schuyler, commanding the American Northern Department, turned the wilderness into a weapon. Schuyler put about a thousand axmen to work felling great trees across the track, destroyed some forty bridges, rolled boulders into the creek, burned the crops, and drove off the cattle. Burgoyne's army, hauling its guns and an enormous baggage train, made roughly twenty miles in about three weeks, close to a mile a day, and reached the Hudson at Fort Edward at the end of July. Schuyler got no thanks for it. Congress, full of New England delegates who detested him and wanting a scapegoat for Ticonderoga, replaced him in August with Major General Horatio Gates. The fairness of it is plain in hindsight: Schuyler's delay bought the weeks that made September possible.

[The civil war among themselves](/war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/civil-war-among-themselves)

It was on that slow march, near Fort Edward on the twenty-seventh of July, that a killing became a weapon. Here is what is solid. Jane McCrea, a young woman from a patriot family but engaged to a loyalist officer serving in Burgoyne's own army, was taken by Native warriors attached to the British advance, and she was killed and scalped; her scalp was carried into the British camp, where her fiancé recognized her hair. What is not solid is how she died. The man blamed was said by one account to have killed her in a quarrel over the reward for escorting her; he himself claimed she had been hit by the musket fire of a pursuing American party. A nineteenth-century exhumation was reported to show bullet wounds and no tomahawk wound, and that report is itself contested. The honest line is the narrow one: she was killed in the custody of Britain's Native allies, and the details beyond that were argued over from the first week and still are.

What was not in doubt was the use the story was put to. Burgoyne demanded that the killer be executed, then pardoned him, afraid that hanging the man would cost him his entire Native contingent, which read in the American press as proof that the British general could not, or would not, police his own allies. The killing of a loyalist's fiancée, a woman engaged to a man in Burgoyne's own ranks, became the perfect recruiting poster: an invading army that could not protect even the women of its own friends. The story ran through the New England newspapers and the recruiting speeches all that August, and the militia turned out in numbers in the weeks that followed. McCrea was not the only thing swelling the American ranks, the shock of Ticonderoga and the news from around Bennington fed the same surge, but the recruiting literature carried her everywhere, and Gates put her into a public letter to Burgoyne in September as a set piece of British savagery, propaganda built to travel. It traveled all the way to London and into the speeches of the opposition in Parliament.

John Vanderlyn painted it in 1804, twenty-seven years after the fact and tied to an American epic poem, and it stages the contested tomahawk version as if it were settled truth. The army the McCrea story was helping to raise was the same army Burgoyne now needed supplies to fight, and he was running short of nearly everything. Short of horses, draft animals, and food, in mid-August he sent Lieutenant Colonel Friedrich Baum with seven or eight hundred men, dismounted German dragoons, loyalists, Canadians, and Natives, toward Bennington in the Hampshire Grants, the disputed country that is now Vermont, on intelligence that a rebel supply depot there was lightly held. It was not. Brigadier General John Stark was there, a veteran of Bunker Hill and Trenton who had quit the Continental Army over a passed-over promotion and then raised some fifteen hundred New Hampshire militia in under a week on the condition that he answer to New Hampshire alone. On the sixteenth of August Stark, with his militia and Seth Warner's Green Mountain men arriving late in the day, enveloped Baum's hilltop and destroyed his force; Baum was mortally wounded, and a German relief column came up too late, was wrecked at dusk, and barely escaped. The cost to Burgoyne was about nine hundred men he could not replace, commonly given as around two hundred killed and seven hundred captured, against roughly thirty American dead. Stark's famous line about his wife sleeping a widow that night is a tradition recorded in variant forms decades later, not anything we can prove he said.

This was the hinge of the northern campaign, eighteen days before the next one turned in Pennsylvania. Burgoyne had lost a tenth of his army for nothing, got no supplies, and watched his Native allies, already soured by his scolding over McCrea, begin to drift away in numbers. His own letter to Germain on the twentieth of August is the campaign's epitaph written in advance: the Hampshire Grants, he wrote, "a country unpeopled and almost unknown in the last war, now abounds in the most active and most rebellious race of the continent, and hangs like a gathering storm upon my left." In the same letter he admitted that he had had no word from Howe confirming that anyone would cooperate with him at all.

## The western prong
> eyebrow: A third army, a confederacy split in two, and a siege beaten by a rumor

Burgoyne's plan had a third arm, and it failed in the same weeks. Brigadier General Barry St. Leger came east by way of Lake Ontario and Oswego with somewhere between seventeen hundred and two thousand men, a few hundred regulars and German riflemen, loyalist regiments, and eight hundred to a thousand Haudenosaunee warriors, largely Mohawk and Seneca, with the Mohawk leader Joseph Brant among them. His job was to take the Mohawk Valley and meet Burgoyne at Albany. In his way stood Fort Stanwix, rebuilt and garrisoned by about seven hundred and fifty Continentals; St. Leger laid siege to it at the start of August.

On the sixth of August a column of some eight hundred Tryon County militia, mostly German-speaking Palatine settlers under Brigadier General Nicholas Herkimer, with Oneida allies, marched to relieve the fort and was ambushed in a ravine at Oriskany, about six miles short of the walls. The fight was point-blank and hand-to-hand, neighbor against neighbor and Oneida against Mohawk and Seneca, the Haudenosaunee confederacy's own civil war beginning inside the American one. The native-nations chapter owns that story. The militia took appalling losses, commonly given as around four hundred of the eight hundred, among the bloodiest small actions of the entire war, and the column never reached the fort. But while the ambushers were away from the siege lines, the garrison sortied and sacked the nearly empty Native and loyalist camps, a blow to St. Leger's allies that soured them on the whole enterprise.

[Native nations](/war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/native-nations)

The siege then collapsed over a rumor. Schuyler, before he was removed, had sent Major General Benedict Arnold west with about nine hundred Continentals. Too short of men to fight his way through, Arnold weaponized a prisoner instead, releasing a loyalist-leaning local man named Hon Yost Schuyler, respected among the Mohawks, to run ahead and spread word that Arnold was coming on in overwhelming force. The core of this is documented: St. Leger's own report of the twenty-first of August records intelligence that Arnold was advancing "by rapid and forced marches, with 3,000 men," roughly triple the truth, and an Oneida messenger working the same rumor among the warriors mattered at least as much as Hon Yost did. The storybook garnish, the prisoner answering "how many?" by pointing at the leaves, is nineteenth-century decoration. On the twenty-second of August St. Leger's Native allies quit, and the siege fell apart in a near-rout back to Oswego, tents standing and guns abandoned. Burgoyne's western prong was gone, and Arnold had not fired a shot.

## Howe sails away
> eyebrow: An armada heading the wrong way, a flanking march, and bayonets in the dark

Now hold the two clocks side by side, because this is the heart of the year. In the same weeks that Ticonderoga fell and Burgoyne began hacking toward the Hudson, William Howe was loading his army onto ships to sail in the opposite direction. Between the fifth and the twenty-third of July he embarked something like fifteen to seventeen thousand troops on a fleet usually given as more than two hundred ships, his brother Admiral Richard Howe commanding the water, and on the twenty-third of July the whole armada sailed from Sandy Hook. Its destination was a secret even Washington could not crack for weeks; he marched his army back and forth across New Jersey and Pennsylvania chasing guesses while the British masts dropped below the horizon.

The fleet made it worse by sea. Off the mouth of the Delaware at the end of July, Howe took naval advice that the river approach to Philadelphia was too well defended, and stood back out into the Atlantic to go around by Chesapeake Bay, adding three sweltering weeks at sea that killed horses and wrecked the cavalry. The army finally waded ashore at Head of Elk in Maryland on the twenty-fifth of August, about fifty-five miles from Philadelphia and, the whole point of it, hundreds of miles by sea away from Burgoyne. The braid is in the dates. Howe's troops were stepping onto the Maryland mud in the same ten days that Stark was wrecking Burgoyne's flank at Bennington and St. Leger's siege was collapsing on the Mohawk. Two British armies, two campaigns, the same fortnight, and four hundred miles of water between them. When Germain's letter of the eighteenth of May finally caught up with Howe, hoping Philadelphia would still leave time to help the northern army, Howe was at sea and the season was spent. He had already written Burgoyne, months before, that the northern army should expect little assistance from his direction.

Washington decided to fight for the capital, and he made his stand behind Brandywine Creek at Chadds Ford on the Philadelphia road on the eleventh of September, with around eleven thousand men. Howe, with roughly fifteen thousand, replayed the move that had beaten the Americans on Long Island the year before. One division demonstrated noisily at the ford to fix Washington's attention while Lord Cornwallis, with Howe and some eight to nine thousand men, swung seventeen miles north, crossed the creek at fords Washington's intelligence had dismissed, and came down on the American right and rear in the afternoon. This time it was not the Long Island panic. The American right wheeled to face the new threat and fought hard around Birmingham Meetinghouse; Nathanael Greene's division covered a real retreat in good order. A nineteen-year-old French volunteer named the Marquis de Lafayette, two months into his American service and serving without a command, was shot in the leg trying to rally troops, a small wound that would matter a great deal to the alliance story. By dark Washington's army was beaten but intact, falling back toward Chester. The losses ran to around six hundred British killed and wounded against something like a thousand to thirteen hundred Americans, the American returns too incomplete to give a hard number. A planned rematch a few days later dissolved when a downpour soaked tens of thousands of cartridges, and Congress had already slipped out of Philadelphia in the small hours of the nineteenth of September, warned that the river fords were open.

A night attack followed that the Americans would remember by a name. Brigadier General Anthony Wayne, detached with about fifteen hundred men to harry Howe's rear near his own boyhood home, believed his camp near Paoli Tavern was a secret. Local loyalist intelligence had given it away. On the night of the twentieth and twenty-first of September, Major General Charles Grey attacked at midnight with the flints pulled from his men's muskets so that no accidental shot could warn the camp, bayonets only, and the camp was overrun by firelight in minutes. About fifty-three Americans were killed, many of them with multiple bayonet wounds, which fed sworn depositions of atrocity and gave the night its name, the "Paoli Massacre." The brutality needed little embellishment, but the name overstates the case: quarter was given, and roughly seventy prisoners were taken. Modern assessments read it as a legal and brutally efficient night attack rather than a slaughter of the helpless. The Americans called it a massacre, and the name stuck, and "Remember Paoli" became a revenge cry.

[The civil war among themselves](/war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/civil-war-among-themselves)

On the twenty-sixth of September, Cornwallis marched about three thousand men into Philadelphia unopposed, cheered by the loyalist remnant of a half-emptied city. The largest city in America, the seat of Congress, the symbol Howe had spent the entire year and the whole strategy to reach, was British. And it decided nothing. Congress had not surrendered; it had simply moved. Having left on the nineteenth, it sat one day in Lancaster, crossed the Susquehanna River, and reconvened at York, Pennsylvania, on the thirtieth of September, where it would sit out the winter running the war from a small courthouse town. In Europe, taking the enemy's capital ended wars. This capital was a meeting, and the meeting got up and moved. The river was not even open to supply the prize: the American forts below the city kept Howe's fleet out until late November, months of hard fighting after Philadelphia had "fallen."

A week later, stung and reinforced, Washington turned and attacked. Howe had posted about nine thousand men at Germantown, five miles above Philadelphia, and on the fourth of October Washington came at them at dawn with some eleven thousand in four converging columns on a complicated night-march plan. In a dense fog thickened by powder smoke the plan came apart. Columns lost their schedule and lost each other; in the murk, by Washington's own later account, American units fired into one another. A small British force barricaded itself in a stone mansion, the Chew House, square in the American line of advance, and instead of leaving it masked and pushing on, the command stopped to batter the house and spent men and the morning's momentum on it; a large share of the American casualties fell around its walls. The army lost around a hundred and fifty killed, five hundred wounded, and four hundred captured or missing against British losses of perhaps seventy killed and four hundred and fifty wounded, and it was a defeat. But the army marched off intact and, oddly, proud of itself, and Washington reported its spirits unbroken. It is widely said that this beaten army's nerve to attack a victorious enemy three weeks later impressed France as much as anything that autumn. The strong version, a single sentence put in Vergennes's mouth, cannot be pinned to any contemporary document, so put it softly: the news crossing to Paris that fall carried Philadelphia's loss together with Germantown's audacity, and French observers read even this defeat as evidence that the American army would not die.

It also occasioned them to mistake One another for the Enemy, which, I believe, more than any thing else contributed to the misfortune which ensued. — George Washington, on the fog at Germantown, 1777

## Saratoga
> eyebrow: A bridge of boats, a feud between two difficult men, and an army that came apart

Back in the north, the other clock was running down. On the thirteenth to fifteenth of September, Burgoyne crossed his army to the west bank of the Hudson on a bridge of boats, cutting his own line back to Canada to gamble everything on reaching Albany. Below him Gates had dug in on Bemis Heights, bluffs commanding the river road, with the works laid out by the Polish engineer Thaddeus Kościuszko, and Gates's army was now something like seven to nine thousand strong and swelling every week with the militia that McCrea and Bennington had helped raise. Burgoyne had perhaps six or seven thousand men left and about a month of rations.

On the nineteenth of September, eight days after Brandywine, two armies four hundred miles apart fought on the same week's calendar. Burgoyne probed for the American left with three columns, and the fight opened at a clearing called Freeman's Farm, where Daniel Morgan's riflemen, the corps Washington had sent north, fired the first shots. Through the afternoon Arnold's wing fought the British center to a standstill in volley and countercharge across the farmyard until Riedesel's Germans, marching up from the river road, struck the American flank at dusk and saved the field. Where exactly Arnold himself was that afternoon is genuinely debated, on the field by some accounts, at headquarters by others; either way his wing fought the battle. The British held the ground and lost nearly six hundred men killed, wounded, or captured against more than three hundred Americans, and that arithmetic was the whole story: Burgoyne could not replace a single man, and Gates's camp grew by more than the day's losses inside a week. Burgoyne dug in and waited for help from New York.

The help came too late and too small. Sir Henry Clinton sortied up the Hudson from New York in early October, took two forts in the Highlands and burned Kingston, but he was more than a hundred and thirty miles short of Burgoyne and the season was closing. His couriers were caught; one was taken carrying his message inside a hollow silver bullet, which was retrieved and which hanged him. The relief that Burgoyne's whole campaign had assumed simply did not exist.

[The army from nothing](/war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/army-from-nothing)

Inside the American camp the victory of the nineteenth had started a war of its own. Gates reported the battle to Congress without once naming Arnold, stripped Morgan's corps out of Arnold's command, and in the shouting match that followed relieved Arnold of command altogether. Arnold, vain and brave and nursing a year of real grievances over rank, refused a pass to leave and hung around the camp without a job, which is how matters stood when the army that the best combat officer on the continent had been forbidden to lead had to fight again. It was a quarrel between two difficult men, told straight, and one of them was the finest fighting general in the army that fall.

On the seventh of October, rations failing and the season gone, Burgoyne pushed a reconnaissance in force of fifteen hundred or more men toward the American left, and Gates broke it in under an hour. Morgan, Poor, and Learned shattered the probe; Brigadier General Simon Fraser, rallying the British line, was shot dead by one of Morgan's riflemen. And then Arnold, without orders and without a command, rode into the fight. Accounts have Gates sending an aide to recall him who never caught up. Arnold helped sweep the field, led the failed rushes on one British redoubt, then swung against the redoubt on the far British right and was inside it when it fell, and in the last volleys his horse went down on top of him and shattered his left leg, the same leg he had been wounded in at Quebec two years before. The fall of that redoubt left Burgoyne's whole camp turnable, and he pulled back into the dark that night.

Burgoyne fell back to Saratoga in the rain, was surrounded by an American force now grown past seventeen thousand with militia still coming in, and opened talks. He extracted terms striking for a beaten army: not a surrender but a "Convention," under which his troops would march to Boston, sail home to Europe, and not serve again in North America. It was a fig leaf with a hole in it, since troops shipped home would free others to come over in their place. On the seventeenth of October, somewhere around six thousand British and German soldiers grounded their arms in the meadow by the Hudson. By the story as it was later told, the American bands did not jeer, and Gates received Burgoyne with courtesy. With Ticonderoga and Bennington and the campaign's battles added to the six thousand who laid down their arms here, Britain's entire northern army of around ten thousand had simply ceased to exist.

The Convention had a strange afterlife. Congress, smelling the fig leaf and seizing on Burgoyne's refusal to hand over a list of his troops, repudiated the deal, and the army never sailed: legally shabby on the American side and strategically obvious in the same breath, and both things are true. The "Convention Army" sat a year around Boston and was then marched some seven hundred miles south to Charlottesville in Virginia, leaking deserters and escapees the whole way, many of them melting quietly into the American population; by war's end only a fraction of it remained in custody. A treaty-shaped object that turned into a long captivity.

## York, Paris, Valley Forge
> eyebrow: A homeless Congress, a fast brigantine, and an army marching into the snow

So the year ran down on two notes at once. In a borrowed courthouse at York, the Congress that had been chased out of its capital did the year's most underrated work: on the fifteenth of November it adopted the Articles of Confederation and sent them to the states, the rebel government writing its own constitution while it was homeless. And on the Hudson, the news of Saratoga went onto a fast ship for France.

Massachusetts put the Saratoga dispatches aboard a brigantine within hours and sent her off at the end of October. The courier, Jonathan Loring Austin, landed at Nantes at the end of November and reached Benjamin Franklin at Passy, outside Paris, on the fourth of December. The scene survives because Austin himself told it. Franklin, braced for the worst, called out before the rider had even dismounted to ask whether Philadelphia had been taken. Yes sir, came the answer. And then, as Austin told the story afterward: "But, sir, I have greater news than that. General Burgoyne and his whole army are prisoners of war." Within two days the French foreign minister's office had asked the American commissioners to reopen the conversation about an alliance. France committed to recognition in mid-December, and the treaties were signed early the next February. The french-alliance chapter owns the treaty story; the spine carries only the hinge. Saratoga did not win the war by itself, and France did not enter on the strength of one battle. The decision rode on a year of covert aid and naval rearmament and ministerial argument, and the same dispatch pouch that carried Saratoga's proof carried Philadelphia's loss and Germantown's nerve. What the battle did was give the foreign minister of France the proof he needed to do the thing he already wanted to do. The army lost in the woods above Albany had purchased a navy.

[The French alliance](/war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/french-alliance)

Franklin had a line for the other capital, too, the one Howe thought he had won. Told that Howe had taken Philadelphia, Franklin is said to have answered that Philadelphia had taken Howe. It is an anecdote, reported early and often, and it has the shape of the year in it.

On the nineteenth of December, Washington marched his army, eleven or twelve thousand men with perhaps a third of them lacking serviceable shoes, into winter quarters at Valley Forge, eighteen miles from a British army wintering warm in the captured capital. What that winter made of that army, the hunger and the drill and the force that marched out in the spring a different thing, belongs to the army-from-nothing story. The spine carries only the freeze-frame at the end of the year. There was a whispering campaign that fall, too, a loose network of grumblers who admired Gates and thought less of Washington, which the survivors would later dress up with the name of a "cabal"; it was never an organized plot to remove him, no motion to do so was ever made, and it collapsed early the next year.

[The army from nothing](/war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/army-from-nothing)

So the year's ledger, told flat. Britain took Philadelphia and got nothing for it: Congress governed on from York, the army stood intact in its huts at Valley Forge, and the captured city decided nothing at all. Britain lost an entire field army, surrendered with its general and its guns and its band playing, the first time in the war that European opinion had a thing it could measure. Two campaigns, two prizes, and only one of them was ever real, because Howe got the capital while Burgoyne needed the army Howe took to get it. Hold the four pictures together at the year's end: a homeless Congress legislating in a country courthouse at York; the snow starting to fall on a barefoot army at Valley Forge; Burgoyne's veterans tramping under guard toward Boston and a captivity that would outlast the war; and at Passy, by a fire, a printer from Philadelphia reading that his city was lost and that the war, quite possibly, was won.

- Two British armies moving in opposite directions; Burgoyne south from Canada toward Albany to split New England, Howe south by sea to take Philadelphia; neither thought he needed the other; London never forced the plans to mesh; France watching for proof of survival; year ends with a captured capital that decided nothing and a lost field army. (Orientation, §1, §7)
- The "two clocks ticking out of time" framing = the chapter's method, stated once. (Orientation directive)

### Section: "The plan that wasn't one" (§1)
- Burgoyne = Lt. Gen., cavalry showman, MP, playwright, "Gentleman Johnny"; in London winter 1776–77 lobbying past his superior in Canada; "Thoughts for Conducting the War from the Side of Canada" Feb 1777; main army up Lake Champlain to Albany + diversion down the Mohawk; seize the Hudson, split New England (granary/manpower), head from body; made sense on paper. (§1)
- [GLOSS-PACK] Ticonderoga / Hudson corridor logic glossed inline as "head cut from the body"; the strangle-New-England framing is Burgoyne's own. (§1)
- Howe = Gen. Sir William Howe, New York; Nov 1776 plan included a Hudson prong; by early April 1777 dropped it; would take Philadelphia (rebel capital, largest city) by sea; told London he could no longer support a junction; theory = capital draws Washington into a decisive battle; "a real theory wrongly weighted, not idiocy." (§1, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #10)
- Germain = cabinet secretary running the war from London at a 6–10-week one-way letter lag; approved Burgoyne's plan March 1777 (in hand in Quebec early May); approved Howe's plan (word early June, already embarking); 18 May letter approved Philadelphia while *trusting* in cooperation, a hope not an order, reached Howe mid-August at sea past the point of return; no positive order ever made one plan serve the other. (§1)
- [GLOSS-CK] the six-to-ten-week ocean letter-lag stated as the structural cause. (§1)
- PIGEONHOLE STORY AS MYTH, named as one: drafted order, Germain's Kent trip, filed and never sent — traces to a much-later secondhand reminiscence, rejected by scholarship working from the full correspondence; Howe knew Burgoyne's plan (Germain sent a copy), told London in April he would not support a junction, Germain approved Philadelphia anyway; failure = structural not clerical. (§1, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #1)
- Clinton left holding New York with ~7,000 (mostly loyalist/German), warned Howe to his face, overruled; the standing geometry (opposite directions, Atlantic too slow). (§1)

- "Both as reasoning actors": Howe's theory real-but-mis-weighted, Burgoyne's plan sensible on paper, Germain at a letter-lag. (HANDLE-WITH-CARE #10)

### Section: "Burgoyne comes south" (§2)
- Burgoyne left Canada June with ~7,800 regulars (~half British, ~half Brunswick/Hanau Germans under Maj. Gen. Riedesel) + artillery train + several hundred Canadians/loyalists + ~500 Native allies (numbers fluctuated). (§2, ranges per #9)
- Before Ticonderoga ("Gibraltar of the North") by 1 July; held by Maj. Gen. Arthur St. Clair, ~2,500–3,000. (§2)
- Sugar Loaf (Mount Defiance) left unfortified; engineers cut a road and dragged cannon up; "where a goat can go… drag a gun" badged "as the army's own story had it" (Phillips line traditional). (§2, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #8)
- Night 5–6 July evacuation southeast, supplies to Skenesborough; British walked in 6 July without a shot; Hubbardton (7 July) the only real fight, rearguard mauled but army got away; public stunned; St. Clair court-martialed and acquitted. (§2)
- George III "beaten all the Americans" badged as Walpole's gossipy account. (§2, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #8)
- CARILLON ECHO: same ground as 1758; British bled to death before these walls; 1777 changed hands twice without a battle. ONE beat. (§2)
- [PILL] /war-french-indian/battles/carillon (the F&I page exists).
- Wilderness slog: Skenesborough overland through Wood Creek lowlands; Schuyler (Northern Dept) ~1,000 axmen, ~40 bridges destroyed, boulders in the creek, crops burned, cattle driven off; ~20 miles in ~3 weeks (~a mile a day); reached Fort Edward end of July; "about" hedges per #9. (§2)
- Schuyler replaced by Gates (Aug); New England delegates detested him + Ticonderoga scapegoat; fairness point (delay bought September) stated plainly. (§2)
- [PILL] civil-war-among-themselves (loyalists/patriots along the invasion route; McCrea/David Jones).
- JANE McCREA, 27 July near Fort Edward — SOLID: patriot-family woman engaged to a loyalist officer in Burgoyne's army; taken by Native warriors of the British advance; killed and scalped; scalp brought into camp, fiancé recognized her hair. (§2, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #2)
- McCREA CONTESTED (never narrated as fact): killed-in-quarrel-over-reward vs American pursuing-party musket fire; 19th-c. exhumation report (bullet wounds, no tomahawk) itself contested; honest line = killed in the custody of Britain's Native allies, details argued from the first week. (§2, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #2)
- McCREA AS PROPAGANDA: Burgoyne demanded the killer's execution then pardoned him (fear of losing the contingent) — read as failure to police his allies; loyalist's-fiancée recruiting poster; ran through NE press + recruiting speeches all August; militia surge multi-causal (Ticonderoga shock + Bennington fed it too — no single-cause claim); Gates's September public letter to Burgoyne made her a set piece; traveled to London + the parliamentary opposition. (§2, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #2)

- BENNINGTON, 16 Aug: short of horses/animals/food; Baum sent with ~700–800 (dismounted German dragoons, loyalists, Canadians, Natives) toward Bennington (Hampshire Grants = present Vermont) on intelligence depot lightly held; Stark there (Bunker Hill + Trenton vet, quit over passed-over promotion, raised ~1,500 NH militia in under a week answering to NH alone); Stark + Warner's Green Mountain men enveloped Baum's hilltop, destroyed the force, Baum mortally wounded; Breymann's relief came too late, wrecked at dusk, barely escaped. (§2, ranges per #9)
- Cost: ~900 men Burgoyne couldn't replace (~200 killed/~700 captured) vs ~30 American dead. (§2)
- "Molly Stark sleeps a widow" badged as 19th-c. tradition in variant forms, not provable. (§2, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #8)
- HINGE: eighteen days before Pennsylvania turned; lost a tenth of the army for nothing, no supplies, Native allies (already soured by the McCrea scolding) drifting away in numbers (actors, not weather — "drift away," reasons given). (§2, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #11)
- QUOTE: Burgoyne to Germain, 20 Aug 1777, "a country unpeopled and almost unknown in the last war, now abounds in the most active and most rebellious race of the continent, and hangs like a gathering storm upon my left." (VERIFIED QUOTES #2) — placed as prose-embedded quote, not a pull quote.

### Section: "The western prong" (§3)
- St. Leger ~1,700–2,000 (few hundred regulars + German riflemen, loyalist regiments, ~800–1,000 Haudenosaunee largely Mohawk/Seneca, Joseph Brant) via Lake Ontario + Oswego to take the Mohawk Valley and meet Burgoyne at Albany; Fort Stanwix garrisoned by ~750 Continentals; siege opened start of August. (§3, ranges per #9)
- ORISKANY, 6 Aug: ~800 Tryon County militia (German-Palatine settlers) under Brig. Gen. Herkimer + Oneida allies, ambushed in a ravine ~6 miles short; point-blank/hand-to-hand, neighbor vs neighbor, Oneida vs Mohawk/Seneca = Haudenosaunee confederacy's own civil war beginning inside the American one (ONE clause; theme owns it); ~400 of ~800 lost ("commonly given as"); column never reached the fort; garrison sortied and sacked the empty Native/loyalist camps, souring St. Leger's allies. (§3, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #11, casualty MED)

### Section: "Howe sails away" (§4)
- SIMULTANEITY SIGNATURE: same weeks Ticonderoga fell + Burgoyne hacked toward the Hudson, Howe loaded to sail the opposite way; 5–23 July embarked ~15,000–17,000 on a fleet given as >200 ships (Adm. Richard Howe commanding); sailed Sandy Hook 23 July; destination a secret Washington couldn't crack for weeks (marched NJ/PA chasing guesses). (§4, ranges per #9)
- Off the Delaware Capes end of July, took naval advice the river was too defended, stood back out for the Chesapeake (+3 sweltering weeks, killed horses/wrecked cavalry); landed Head of Elk, Maryland, 25 Aug, ~55 miles from Philadelphia and hundreds of miles by sea away from Burgoyne. (§4)
- THE BRAID BEAT (explicit): Howe's troops ashore in Maryland in the same ten days Bennington wrecked the flank + St. Leger's siege collapsed; "two armies, two campaigns, the same fortnight, four hundred miles of water." (§4 directive)
- Germain's 18 May letter caught up at sea, season spent; Howe had already written Burgoyne (months before) to expect little assistance. (§4)
- BRANDYWINE, 11 Sep: Washington ~11,000 behind Brandywine Creek at Chadds Ford; Howe ~15,000 replayed Long Island; one division demonstrated at the ford while Cornwallis + Howe + ~8,000–9,000 swung 17 miles north, crossed dismissed fords, hit the American right/rear in the afternoon; NOT the Long Island panic — right wheeled, fought hard at Birmingham Meetinghouse, Greene covered a retreat in order; Lafayette (19, French volunteer, 2 months in, no command) shot in the leg rallying troops (alliance braid, one clause); beaten but intact, fell back toward Chester. (§4, ranges per #9)
- Casualties: British ~580–600 killed/wounded vs American ~1,000–1,300 (returns incomplete, no hard number). (§4, #9)
- Rematch dissolved in a downpour soaking cartridges; Congress slipped out of Philadelphia small hours 19 Sep (fords open). (§4)
- PAOLI, night 20–21 Sep: Wayne detached ~1,500 to harry Howe's rear near his boyhood home, thought the camp secret (local loyalist intelligence gave it away); Grey attacked at midnight with flints pulled (bayonets only, no warning shot); camp overrun by firelight in minutes; ~53 killed (many multiple bayonet wounds), fed atrocity depositions, name "Paoli Massacre." (§4)
- "MASSACRE" with the quotation marks ON: name overstates — quarter given, ~70 prisoners taken; modern reading = legal/brutally-efficient night attack, not slaughter of the helpless; "the Americans called it a massacre, and the name stuck"; "Remember Paoli" revenge cry. (§4, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #3)
- [PILL] civil-war-among-themselves (Paoli's loyalist intelligence, loyalist Philadelphia).
- PHILADELPHIA, 26 Sep: Cornwallis marched ~3,000 in unopposed, cheered by the loyalist remnant; largest city/seat of Congress/the year's whole symbol, British — AND DECIDED NOTHING. (§4)
- Congress commuted not capitulated: left 19th, one day in Lancaster, crossed the Susquehanna, reconvened York 30 Sep, sat out the winter from a small courthouse town; "in Europe taking the capital ended wars; this capital was a meeting, the meeting moved." (§4, the chapter's cleanest irony; York pop. "small courthouse town")
- River not even open: American forts kept Howe's fleet out until late November (one clause). (§4)
- GERMANTOWN, 4 Oct: Howe ~9,000 five miles above Philadelphia; Washington reinforced/stung attacked at dawn ~11,000 in four converging columns on a complicated night-march plan; dense fog + powder smoke, plan came apart, columns lost schedule + each other, American units fired into one another (hedged "by Washington's own later account"). (§4, friendly-fire MED, kept brief/hedged)
- Chew House: small British force barricaded a stone mansion in the line of advance; command stopped to batter it instead of masking it, spent men + momentum; "a large share" of American casualties fell around it. (§4, "large share" per #9)
- Casualties: American ~152 killed/~521 wounded/~400 captured-missing vs British ~70 killed/~450 wounded; a defeat but the army marched off intact + proud, "spirits" unbroken. (§4, verified return)
- "FRENCH IMPRESSION" handled at evidence level: widely said Germantown impressed France; strong single-quote version unpinnable to any contemporary document; stated softly — autumn's news carried Philadelphia's loss + Germantown's audacity, French read even this defeat as evidence the army would not die; NEVER quote Vergennes. (§4, HANDLE-WITH-CARE re #6/MED)
- QUOTE (pull): Washington on the fog at Germantown, 1777 (verbatim, founders.archives.gov 03-11-02-0419). (VERIFIED QUOTES #4)

### Section: "Saratoga" (§5)
- Burgoyne crossed to the Hudson's west bank 13–15 Sep on a bridge of boats, cutting his line to Canada to gamble on Albany; Gates dug in on Bemis Heights (river-road bluffs), works by Kościuszko (Polish engineer); Gates ~7,000–9,000 swelling weekly with militia (McCrea/Bennington surge); Burgoyne ~6,000–7,000 + ~a month of rations. (§5, ranges per #9)
- SIMULTANEITY: Freeman's Farm 19 Sep, eight days after Brandywine, "two armies four hundred miles apart on the same week's calendar." (§5 braid directive)
- FREEMAN'S FARM, 19 Sep: Burgoyne probed the American left with three columns; Morgan's riflemen (Washington sent them north — one clause) opened it; Arnold's wing fought the British center to a standstill in volley/countercharge; Riedesel's Germans from the river road struck the flank at dusk and saved the field. (§5)
- ARNOLD WHEREABOUTS genuinely debated (field vs headquarters) — NOT staged; "his wing fought the battle either way." (§5, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #5)
- Arithmetic: British held the ground, lost ~600 vs >300 American; Burgoyne couldn't replace a man, Gates's camp grew by more than the day's losses within a week; Burgoyne dug in to wait for New York. (§5)
- CLINTON's gesture (one beat): sortied up the Hudson early Oct, took two Highlands forts + burned Kingston, >130 miles short, season closing; courier caught with the message in a hollow silver bullet (retrieved, hanged him); the assumed relief never existed. (§5)
- [PILL] army-from-nothing (the army that held / grew).
- GATES–ARNOLD FEUD told straight, no 1780 foreshadowing: Gates reported the 19th without naming Arnold, stripped Morgan's corps, relieved Arnold of command; Arnold (vain, brave, year of grievances over rank) refused a pass, hung around without a job; "two difficult men, one the finest fighting general in the army that fall." (§5, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #5)
- BEMIS HEIGHTS, 7 Oct: Burgoyne pushed a reconnaissance-in-force ~1,500+ toward the American left; Gates broke it in under an hour; Morgan/Poor/Learned shattered the probe; Brig. Gen. Simon Fraser shot dead by "one of Morgan's riflemen" (Timothy Murphy attribution DISPUTED, NOT named). (§5, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #8)
- ARNOLD's unauthorized charge (solid): without orders/command rode in; aide sent to recall him never caught up; helped sweep the field, led failed rushes on one redoubt, swung to the far-right redoubt + was in it when it fell; horse shot down on him, left leg shattered (same leg as Quebec); the redoubt's fall made Burgoyne's camp turnable, he pulled back that night. NO Boot Monument pathos, no "would later." (§5, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #5)
- SURRENDER, 17 Oct: fell back to Saratoga in rain, surrounded by an American force past 17,000 (militia still arriving), opened talks; extracted a "Convention" not a surrender (march to Boston, sail home, not serve again in NA) = a fig leaf with a hole (home troops free others); ~6,000 grounded arms in the meadow; bands did not jeer + Gates received Burgoyne with courtesy, both badged "as the story was later told." (§5, ranges per #9, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #8)
- SCALE CHECK: Ticonderoga + Bennington + the battles + the ~6,000 surrendered = Britain's ~10,000 northern army ceased to exist (loosely phrased). (§5)
- CONVENTION ARMY afterlife told dry: Congress smelled the fig leaf + seized on Burgoyne's refusal of a troop list, repudiated the deal, army never sailed; "legally shabby AND strategically obvious," both said; a year around Boston then ~700 miles to Charlottesville VA, leaking deserters/escapees melting into the population, only a fraction left in custody by war's end. (§5, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #7, loose numbers only)
- [FIGURE] Trumbull "Surrender of General Burgoyne" (1821) with MANDATORY caveat: painted 44 years later as national myth-making, a calm group portrait of a rain-soaked field surrender, Gates's magnanimity staged center. /war-img/rev-twoarmies-saratoga.jpg (candidate 1)

### Section: "York, Paris, Valley Forge" (§6)
- CONGRESS IN EXILE keeps legislating: at York adopted the Articles of Confederation 15 Nov, sent to the states — "wrote its constitution while homeless" (one beat; idea-of-independence owns the arc). (§6)
- NEWS TO PARIS: Massachusetts put the dispatches on a fast brigantine within hours (sailed ~end of Oct); courier Jonathan Loring Austin landed Nantes ~end of Nov, reached Franklin at Passy 4 Dec; Franklin braced for the worst, called out before the rider dismounted (is Philadelphia taken?), "Yes sir," then Austin "But, sir, I have greater news than that. General Burgoyne and his whole army are prisoners of war!" — badged "as Austin told it." (§6, QUOTE #7, dialogue MED/as-told)
- HINGE (one beat, OUT): within two days Vergennes's office asked the commissioners to reopen the alliance talk; France committed to recognition mid-December; treaties signed early February — french-alliance owns the treaty arc. (§6)
- NO SARATOGA-AS-DESTINY: didn't win the war alone, France didn't ally on one battle; decision rode a year of covert aid + naval rearmament + ministerial argument; same pouch carried Philadelphia's loss + Germantown's nerve; "Saratoga gave Vergennes the proof he needed to do what he already wanted"; "the army lost in the woods purchased a navy." (§6, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #6)
- [PILL] french-alliance (the treaty hinge).
- FRANKLIN'S QUIP for the other capital: "Philadelphia has taken Howe" badged "is said to have answered" (early/famous anecdote). (§6, QUOTE #8)
- VALLEY FORGE (one beat): 19 Dec, ~11,000–12,000, perhaps a third without serviceable shoes, into winter quarters 18 miles from a British army wintering warm in the capital; institution story (hunger/drill/the army that marched out) belongs to army-from-nothing; spine carries only the freeze-frame. Steuben NOT narrated (he arrives Feb 1778). (§6, HANDLE-WITH-CARE #12)
- CONWAY "CABAL" at documented level, no more: a loose network of grumblers admiring Gates and thinking less of Washington, dressed up later as a "cabal" by survivors; never an organized plot, no motion to remove him ever made, collapsed early next year. (§6, HANDLE-WITH-CARE re Conway; "loose network not conspiracy")
- [PILL] army-from-nothing (Valley Forge).

### Section: close (§7, folded into §6's final passage)
- THE LEDGER FLAT: Britain took Philadelphia and got nothing — Congress governed from York, the army stood intact at Valley Forge, the city decided nothing; Britain lost an entire field army, surrendered with general/guns/band, first time European opinion could measure. (§7)
- STRATEGIC CLOSE: two campaigns, two prizes, only one real; Howe got the capital, Burgoyne needed the army Howe took to get it. (§7) Note: did NOT say the city would be evacuated within a year (stayed in 1777 per #12).
- SIMULTANEITY FREEZE-FRAME (the method, one last time): York's borrowed courthouse; the snow starting at Valley Forge; Burgoyne's veterans tramping toward Boston under guard; at Passy a printer from Philadelphia reading that his city was lost and the war quite possibly won. (§7 directive)
