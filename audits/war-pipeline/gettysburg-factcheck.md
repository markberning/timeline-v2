## Section: `setting`

### Claim-by-claim

- **"Two weeks earlier, at Chancellorsville…"** (Chancellorsville relative to the June 3 march-out)
  ⚠️ UNHEDGED-TIMING. Chancellorsville ended May 6, 1863. Lee's army began moving June 3. That is roughly four weeks, not two. The prose says "Two weeks earlier" — wrong by half.

- **"Stonewall Jackson (South) had marched up and down [the Valley] for two years until his death at Chancellorsville a month before"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Jackson was mortally wounded at Chancellorsville May 2, 1863, died May 10, 1863. Relative to the July 1 battle, "a month before" is slightly off (it was ~7 weeks), but relative to the June 3 march-out the phrasing is approximate. The core fact — Jackson died at Chancellorsville — is correct and uncontroversial. (History.com, Wikipedia: Stonewall Jackson)

- **Chancellorsville — Lee beat "a Union army roughly twice the size of his own"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Hooker commanded ~134,000; Lee ~60,000. The "roughly twice" characterization is standard. (American Battlefield Trust)

- **Strategic motives for invasion: Virginia exhausted; feed army off Pennsylvania; draw force from Vicksburg; frighten Northern voters; push Europe toward recognition**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. All listed as genuine motives in both primary and secondary scholarship. (Battlefields.org; Wikipedia: Gettysburg campaign)

- **"Vicksburg, the river fortress a thousand miles down the Mississippi that Ulysses S. Grant (North) was slowly strangling"**
  ⚠️ UNSUPPORTED-OR-UNHEDGED. The draft says "a thousand miles down the Mississippi." Vicksburg, MS to Gettysburg, PA is approximately 1,026–1,047 miles (air/road), so the figure appears to conflate a Gettysburg-to-Vicksburg straight-line distance with "down the Mississippi." The river route from, say, the Ohio River confluence to Vicksburg is far less than 1,000 miles. The fact pack itself carries this "thousand miles" figure from v0 without verification. The prose should read "hundreds of miles" or "far to the southwest." The "800 miles to the southwest" used in `aftermath` is closer to accurate (Vicksburg is ~800–850 miles SW of Gettysburg as the crow flies). SHOULD-FIX: change to "hundreds of miles down the Mississippi" or drop the mileage.

- **"They left for the North on June 3rd"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Lee's army began moving June 3, 1863. (American Battlefield Trust; Wikipedia)

- **Route: up the Shenandoah Valley; ~70,000+ men; Lee's orders restraining looting**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Gettysburg campaign; American Battlefield Trust)

- **Hooker in command of Army of the Potomac; feuding with Washington; resigned; replaced by Meade on June 28, three days before the battle**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Hooker replaced by Meade June 28, 1863. (Wikipedia: George Meade; Battlefields.org)

- **Meade "a sour, reliable Pennsylvanian" — woken in the dark, half-suspected he had come to arrest him**
  ✅ CONFIRMED (color details). Chief of staff James Hardie woke Meade "at 3 o'clock in the morning" at his tent; Meade thought he was about to be arrested. The "woken in the dark / suspected arrest" detail is documented history. The "Pennsylvanian" characterization: Meade was born in Cádiz, Spain, but raised in Philadelphia and strongly identified as a Pennsylvanian. The draft's "reliable Pennsylvanian" is an acceptable characterization in context. The "sour" characterization (he was nicknamed "Old Snapping Turtle") is documented. (Encyclopedia Virginia; Battlefields.org; multiple biography sources)

- **Meade "had not asked for the job, had not been told he was even being considered"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Multiple sources confirm Meade did not seek the command. (Wikipedia: George Meade)

- **Stuart's cavalry off on a "long, glory-seeking ride clear around the Union army"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Stuart's ride around the Union army is well-documented. (Wikipedia: J.E.B. Stuart)

- **Gettysburg: "roughly twenty-four hundred people in 1860, a Lutheran seminary, a college, nothing of any military value … ten of them came together there"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. 1860 census: 2,390 residents. Ten roads converged at Gettysburg. Lutheran Theological Seminary and Pennsylvania College (now Gettysburg College) both located there. (Census.gov; Gettysburg Almanac; Wikipedia)

- **"first shots cracked out west of town a little after seven in the morning"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. First contact approximately 7:30 a.m. July 1 at/near McPherson's Ridge. (NPS; Battlefields.org)

- **Shoes story = LEGEND, recast as reconnaissance in force**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The shoes story is not documented. Heth's move was a reconnaissance in force. (Battlefields.org; NPS; American Battlefield Trust — explicitly called a legend by most authoritative sources)

- **"roughly fifty thousand men killed, wounded, or captured"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Combined casualties ~46,000–51,000 (killed + wounded + captured/missing). Hedged correctly; phrasing "killed, wounded, or captured" correct. (§0 fact pack; Wikipedia)

- **MEANWHILE London — Confederate envoys pressing Britain for recognition since 1861**
  ✅ CONFIRMED as acceptable framing. No unverified names retained; the recognition-seeking effort is documented history.

## Section: `mcpherson`

### Claim-by-claim

- **John Buford with ~2,748 troopers, dismounted, breech-loading carbines; arrived June 30; chose the ground deliberately**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Buford arrived June 30. ~2,748 troopers, breech-loading carbines, deliberate ground selection — all well-documented. (NPS: John F. Reynolds; Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, first day)

- **Henry Heth's division ~7,600 infantry, reconnaissance in force on the Chambersburg Pike**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Heth's division ~7,600; Chambersburg Pike advance as reconnaissance in force. (Wikipedia: Henry Heth; American Battlefield Trust)

- **Heth told there might be militia, didn't believe veteran cavalry was near**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Standard account. (Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, first day)

- **First shots ~7:30 a.m.**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (NPS; Battlefields.org)

- **John F. Reynolds, senior Union I Corps commander, killed instantly ~10:15–10:30 a.m., "shot behind the ear"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Reynolds was killed instantly, shot in the head (specifically described in most sources as behind the ear, at the base of the skull). The §FIXES correction from "through the neck" to "behind the ear" is applied and correct. (NPS: John F. Reynolds; Wikipedia: John F. Reynolds)

- **Reynolds "highest-ranking man to die at Gettysburg"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Reynolds is described as "the highest ranking officer on either side killed at Gettysburg" by multiple sources including the Gettysburg Sentinels and Shelby Foote. (NPS; Gettysburg Sentinels; Cupola Gettysburg College)

- **Reynolds "some in Washington had wanted to give Meade's job to"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Lincoln met with Reynolds on June 2, 1863, and is believed to have offered him command of the Army of the Potomac; Reynolds reportedly declined unless given independence from political interference. Lincoln then gave the command to Meade on June 28. This is documented and not merely a rumor. (Wikipedia: John F. Reynolds; Battlefields.org)

- **Iron Brigade — Indiana, Michigan, and Wisconsin men; "tall black hats"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The Iron Brigade comprised Wisconsin, Indiana, and Michigan regiments. They wore the tall black 1858 Hardee hat and were known as "The Black Hats." (Wikipedia: Iron Brigade; Wisconsin Veterans Museum)

- **Iron Brigade: "earned their grim nickname in earlier fighting for refusing to break"**
  ⚠️ PARTIALLY CONFIRMED / SLIGHTLY MISLEADING. The "Iron Brigade" name originated at the Battle of South Mountain (prelude to Antietam, September 1862), reportedly when McClellan saw them fight. The draft says they "earned their grim nickname in earlier fighting for refusing to break" — this is a reasonable characterization of how they earned the name, though the specific battle (South Mountain) is not named. The phrasing is not wrong but slightly vague. SHOULD-FIX: can keep as is, or add "at South Mountain" for precision.

- **Reynolds "dead before he reached the ground"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. He was killed instantly. (NPS; Wikipedia)

- **Afternoon: Union I and XI Corps driven back through town; Heth, Pender, Rodes, Early from the north — "three divisions converging from two directions"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Multiple Confederate divisions from west and north. Rodes and Early came from the north; Heth and Pender from the west. The "three divisions / two directions" framing is blessed explicitly in the fact pack and accurate. (Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, first day)

- **Cemetery Hill: high ground south of town, crowned by the town's graveyard**
  ✅ CONFIRMED.

- **Winfield Scott Hancock arrived after 4 p.m., sent ahead by Meade with written authority to judge and command; chose to hold**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, first day; Battlefields.org)

- **Hancock quote: "I think this the strongest position by nature upon which to fight a battle that I ever saw."**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Verbatim from §QUOTE BANK; this is the documented quote. (American Battlefield Trust)

- **Lee arrived on Seminary Ridge that evening, resolved to attack the next day; Longstreet's corps not yet up**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, first day; Battlefields.org)

## Section: `hooks`

### Claim-by-claim

- **Fishhook geography: Culp's Hill (barb NE) → Cemetery Hill → Cemetery Ridge ~2 miles south → Little Round Top / Round Tops**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Geographically accurate. (Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, second day)

- **Interior lines = Meade could shift troops fast across the inside of the curve**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Standard tactical concept, accurately described. (Battlefields.org)

- **"Meade … the careful Pennsylvania engineer handed command four days earlier"**
  ⚠️ PARTIALLY ACCURATE / MINOR ISSUE. Meade was indeed a trained engineer (West Point, Corps of Topographical Engineers). However, he was born in Cádiz, Spain — he is not literally a Pennsylvanian by birth, though he grew up in Philadelphia and is strongly associated with Pennsylvania. The "four days earlier" from the July 2 perspective (June 28 → July 2) is arithmetically correct. The "Pennsylvania engineer" description is acceptable given his upbringing and identification with Pennsylvania; the "engineer" characterization is accurate. LOW RISK.

- **Lee's plan: Longstreet attacks Union left obliquely; Ewell attacks right at Culp's Hill; center feints**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, second day; Battlefields.org)

- **"Longstreet opposed it… urged maneuvering around Meade instead" / "had opposed this invasion from the start" / "wanted to get between Meade and Washington"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Longstreet urged Lee to slide around the Union left and interpose between the Army of the Potomac and Washington. This is documented in Longstreet's own writings and multiple secondary sources. He had opposed the invasion and the Day 2 attack. (Battlefields.org: Longstreet at Gettysburg; Wikipedia: James Longstreet)

- **Longstreet's attack delayed until ~4 p.m.; waited on Law's brigade; concealed approach march that doubled back when the route threatened to expose them to a Union signal station**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The signal station detail is documented: a Union signal detachment on Little Round Top spotted Longstreet's column, causing a countermarch/detour. Law's brigade is specifically cited for the delay. (Civil War Signals website; Battlefields.org; Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, second day)

- **Sickles, III Corps, advanced ~¾ mile forward without authorization; Peach Orchard / Wheatfield salient, both flanks hanging in air**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, second day; Battlefields.org; NPS)

- **Sickles "a New York politician in a soldier's coat"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Daniel Sickles served as a Democratic U.S. Representative from New York (1857–1861) before the war. The "New York politician" description is accurate. (Wikipedia: Daniel Sickles; House of Representatives history)

- **Sickles offered to withdraw; Meade told him too late**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The exchange is documented; Meade's quote that follows reflects that he acknowledged the exposed position was now a fait accompli. (Wikipedia; NPS)

- **Meade to Sickles quote: "this is neutral ground, our guns command it, as well as the enemy's."**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Verbatim from §QUOTE BANK.

- **"argued for the next sixty years" — Sickles's self-justifying claim**
  ✅ CONFIRMED as acceptable. Sickles lived until 1914 (died age ~94), about 51 years after Gettysburg — "sixty years" is slightly long. The fact pack says "the rest of his life"; the draft says "the next sixty years." SHOULD-FIX: change to "for the rest of his life" (which matches the fact pack and is precisely accurate).

- **Hood's division attacked ~4:30 p.m.; Hood wounded early by air-bursting artillery shell (left arm), carried off**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: John Bell Hood; Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, second day)

- **Hood asked to swing wider around the Union flank and was refused — asked twice**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Hood protested at least three, possibly four times, to Longstreet, who cited Lee's orders. The draft says "asked twice" — sources say at least three times. SHOULD-FIX: change "asked twice" to "asked repeatedly" or "asked several times."

- **Devil's Den: jumble of boulders; Ward's Union brigade ~2,200, six regiments including 124th New York; Col. Ellis and Maj. Cromwell both killed**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Ward's brigade ~2,200 men, six regiments including 124th New York. Col. Augustus van Horne Ellis and Maj. James Cromwell both killed. (Wikipedia: 124th New York; killedatgettysburg.org)

- **Full names "Augustus Van Horne Ellis" and "James Cromwell"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Augustus van Horne Ellis is the documented name. James Cromwell is confirmed as the major killed. (Wikipedia: Augustus van Horne Ellis; killedatgettysburg.org)

- **Casualties at Devil's Den: 821 Union of 2,423 engaged; ~1,814 Confederate of 5,525**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. From fact pack §3; these figures appear in Wikipedia and NPS sources.

- **Ward "held the boulders for the better part of an hour"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The brigade held before being driven off. Timing consistent with sources.

- **"two Confederate brigades" drove Ward off**
  ⚠️ UNSUPPORTED. The fact pack does not specify the number of Confederate brigades at Devil's Den; the draft says "weight of two Confederate brigades." The attacking Confederate force around Devil's Den included brigades from Hood's division (primarily Robertson's and Law's brigades). Two brigades is accurate per sources, but as noted in the ledger, the pack doesn't specify. LOW RISK — can keep "two Confederate brigades" as it's factually supportable, but a softer hedge ("the Confederate assault") would be safer.

- **Little Round Top ~4:30–5 p.m.; Warren found the hill nearly undefended, saw Confederate line coming, sent urgently for troops**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Little Round Top; Battlefields.org)

- **Strong Vincent's brigade arrived first; Vincent mortally wounded**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Strong Vincent; Wikipedia: Little Round Top)

- **Patrick "Paddy" O'Rorke, 140th New York, killed**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. O'Rorke led the 140th NY up the slope and was killed in the first volley. (Wikipedia: Patrick O'Rorke)

- **Charles Hazlett "manhandled his guns onto that impossible rocky crest," killed there**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Hazlett's battery was literally manhandled up the rocky slope by hand. Hazlett was killed on Little Round Top while kneeling near a mortally wounded general (Weed). The "manhandled his guns" phrasing is accurate. (Wikipedia: Charles E. Hazlett)

- **Chamberlain, 20th Maine, Maine college professor, taught rhetoric and modern languages**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Chamberlain was Professor of Rhetoric at Bowdoin College and was appointed professor of modern languages in 1861. Both subjects confirmed. (Wikipedia: Joshua Chamberlain; Bowdoin College records)

- **Chamberlain: far left of the entire Union army, out of ammunition, downhill bayonet charge, Medal of Honor**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Joshua Chamberlain; Wikipedia: Little Round Top; Chamberlain's official report)

- **"about twenty minutes" for the Chamberlain charge vs. Warren's larger decisive act**
  ✅ CONFIRMED as sound framing. The fact pack explicitly endorses this nuance. The Warren role is well-documented. (Wikipedia: Little Round Top)

- **William C. Oates, 15th Alabama — middle initial C.**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. William Calvin Oates. The middle initial "C." is correct. (Wikipedia: William C. Oates; American Battlefield Trust biography)

- **15th Alabama had marched "twenty-five miles that day in the heat"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED (within range of disputed sources). Sources give 18–28 miles, with 25 miles specifically cited in Col. Oates's own account. The "twenty-five miles" figure is from Oates himself and is one of the more commonly cited numbers. This is acceptable with the existing hedge ("had already marched"). LOW RISK.

- **Wheatfield ~19 acres; ~19–20 brigades; commonly "six times"; ~20,000 engaged; ~30% casualties; Cross and Zook mortally wounded**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg, second day; fact pack §3). The "six times" is correctly hedged as the popular figure.

- **Peach Orchard ~6 p.m.; Barksdale (Mississippi brigade) killed leading the charge on horseback; "white hair streaming"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Barksdale was a Mississippi congressman (served four terms, 1853–1861). He rode on horseback, hat off, his white hair "flying" — multiple contemporary sources describe this. He was killed leading the charge. (Wikipedia: William Barksdale; Battlefields.org)

- **Sickles wounded by a cannonball in the right leg; leg amputated that evening; "carried off sitting up smoking a cigar"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED (appropriately hedged as "by one well-worn account"). The cannonball wounding of his right leg and amputation are documented; the cigar story is traditional. (Wikipedia: Daniel Sickles)

- **Culp's Hill: Johnson's ONE division (not three), minus a brigade; Greene's lone XII Corps brigade; Greene 62 years old, West Point class of 1823, distinguished civil engineer recalled to service; breastworks**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Johnson's division attacked Greene's lone brigade. Greene: born 1801 (age 62 in 1863), West Point class of 1823, prominent civil engineer. He insisted on breastworks, and they proved decisive. (Wikipedia: George S. Greene; Rhode Island Heritage Hall of Fame; Union Generals biography)

- **Greene "helped lay out New York City's water system" (Croton Aqueduct)**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Greene's largest project was the Croton Aqueduct in New York; he also designed the Croton reservoir in Central Park and the enlarged High Bridge over the Harlem River. The "New York City's water system" description is accurate. (Wikipedia: George S. Greene; Rhode Island Heritage Hall of Fame)

- **"The rest of the corps that should have been there had been marched south to help in the crisis around the Wheatfield"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. When Meade ordered XII Corps to support the left, five brigades were sent south; only Greene's brigade remained on Culp's Hill. (Wikipedia: Culp's Hill; Battlefields.org: Culp's Hill July 2)

- **Day 2 casualties: ~10,000 Union / ~6,800 Confederate (Trudeau)**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. From fact pack §3 citing Trudeau; prose rounds correctly.

- **Lee resolved to attack the center on Day 3**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Battle of Gettysburg; Battlefields.org)

## Section: `pickett`

### Claim-by-claim

- **Longstreet "so sick at heart he could barely give the order … by the traditional account he only nodded"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED (appropriately hedged). The "only nodded" detail is traditional — Longstreet himself wrote that he could not give the verbal order and only nodded when E.P. Alexander asked if he should attack. Prose correctly attributes this "by the traditional account." (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; Longstreet's memoirs)

- **Force ~12,500 men; three divisions — Pickett, Pettigrew, Trimble — under Longstreet**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; Battlefields.org)

- **Pickett's division the freshest; "not yet bloodied at Gettysburg"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Pickett's division arrived at Gettysburg late on July 2 and was not committed on Day 1 or Day 2. It was the last completely fresh division in Lee's army on July 3. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; American Battlefield Trust)

- **Pickett "a flamboyant figure with perfumed ringlets in his hair"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Multiple sources describe Pickett's perfumed ringlets and flamboyant appearance. James Longstreet described his "long ringlets flowed loosely over his shoulders, trimmed and highly perfumed." (Wikipedia: George Pickett; HistoryNet)

- **Target: copse of trees / the Angle (stone wall), ~¾ mile of open ground (~1,300 yards)**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. ¾ mile ≈ 1,320 yards; "approximately 1,300 yards" is accurate. The copse and Angle are the traditional target. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; Battlefields.org)

- **Emmitsburg Road fence partway across funneled and slowed the attackers**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The Emmitsburg Road and its fences are documented as an obstacle that bunched the attackers under fire. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; Battlefields.org)

- **Cannonade ~1 p.m., ~1–2 hours; ~150–170 Confederate guns vs. ~80 Union; "largest artillery bombardment ever conducted on the North American continent"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Timing, gun counts, and the superlative are all documented. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; American Battlefield Trust)

- **"reportedly heard in Pittsburgh, more than a hundred and fifty miles away"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED (appropriately hedged with "reportedly"). Pittsburgh is ~152 miles straight-line from Gettysburg. Multiple sources report the cannonade was heard ~140–150 miles away in Pittsburgh, though acoustical experts note it is "extremely improbable" but theoretically possible. The draft correctly says "reportedly." (LancasterOnline; Civil War Blog: How Far Was Gettysburg Heard; TravelMath distance)

- **Smoke obscured Confederate aim; most shells overshot the Union infantry and hit the rear — supply trains, horses, staff officers**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; NPS)

- **Union guns slackened to save ammunition and feign being silenced; artillery chief sent word to Longstreet**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The artillery chief (E.P. Alexander, not named in draft) sent word to Longstreet that now was the moment. The draft leaves him unnamed, which is acceptable. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; E.P. Alexander's memoirs)

- **Charge ~3 p.m.; divisions advanced in long lines ~a mile wide**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The charge stepped off near 3 p.m. (between 2 and 3 p.m. per some sources; most converge on "near 3 p.m."). Lines approximately a mile wide. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; Battlefields.org)

- **Union artillery used canister at close range + rifle fire; staged ranges (solid shot far, canister close, rifles last)**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The staged use of shot, then shell, then canister, then rifle fire is documented. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; Battlefields.org)

- **A few hundred reached the wall at the Angle**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge)

- **Lewis Armistead led men over the wall, hat on his sword, mortally wounded with "his hand on a Union gun," died two days later in a Union hospital**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Armistead put his hat on the tip of his sword. He was struck with his hand on a Union cannon. He died July 5, 1863, two days after being wounded, at a Union field hospital. (Wikipedia: Lewis Armistead; Battlefields.org)

- **"High-water mark of the Confederacy" = the spot at the Angle**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia; NPS; American Battlefield Trust)

- **Confederate losses in the charge over 50%; ~6,555 total; Pickett's division alone ~2,655**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; fact pack §4 citing documented figures)

- **DISPUTED QUOTE — Lee: "This has all been my fault." / "It is all my fault."**
  ✅ CONFIRMED AS PROPERLY HEDGED. The quote is traditional and disputed. Historians Hess, Sears, and Wert question whether the Lee-Pickett meeting happened as told. Multiple eyewitnesses document Lee saying similar things to returning survivors, though the exact words to Pickett specifically are disputed. Draft correctly hedges with "Lee is remembered to have told them" and "by the honest reckoning of modern historians, one of the most uncertain." KEEP. (CivilWarChat.wordpress.com; CivilWarTalk forum)

- **DISPUTED QUOTE — Pickett: "General Lee, I have no division."**
  ✅ CONFIRMED AS PROPERLY HEDGED. Traditional and disputed. Draft correctly hedges with "is said to have replied" and frames it as "the story the survivors kept." KEEP. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge; multiple sources)

- **Historians Earl Hess, Stephen Sears, and Jeffry Wert dispute the meeting**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. These names appear in the fact pack and are documented Civil War historians who have written skeptically about the Lee-Pickett encounter. (Wikipedia: Pickett's Charge)

## Section: `aftermath`

### Claim-by-claim

- **July 4: rain; dead still in the fields**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Retreat from Gettysburg; multiple sources)

- **"~7,000 of them [dead] between the two armies"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED (with existing "by some counts" hedge). Combined killed at Gettysburg is typically cited at ~7,000–7,863. The prose hedges correctly with "by some counts." KEEP.

- **"close to fifty thousand casualties — killed, wounded, or captured — split roughly evenly; Union ~23,000, Confederate ~23,000–28,000"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Union: ~23,055; Confederate: ~23,000–28,000 (Busey & Martin: 23,231). Combined ~46,000–51,000. The "roughly evenly" and the given ranges are accurate. Phrasing "killed, wounded, or captured" is correct. (Fact pack §0; Wikipedia)

- **"the costliest battle ever fought in the Western Hemisphere"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. This claim is widely made and supported by authoritative sources including the American Battlefield Trust and Wikipedia. While there is some debate about "biggest" vs. "bloodiest," the casualty-based "costliest" framing is defensible and widely accepted. KEEP.

- **Lee's ~15-mile wagon train of wounded retreated south, escorted by Stuart's cavalry**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The wagon train was 15–20 miles long per the retreat sources. The fact pack gives "~15 miles"; the Wikipedia article on the Retreat from Gettysburg describes a train "15–20 miles" long. Escorted by Stuart's cavalry: partially confirmed — the train was commanded by Gen. John Imboden, not Stuart. Stuart's cavalry covered the army's rear during the retreat, but Imboden specifically commanded the wounded train. 
  ⚠️ MINOR INACCURACY: The draft says the wagon train was "escorted by J.E.B. Stuart's (South) cavalry." In fact, the wounded wagon train was specifically escorted by Gen. John D. Imboden's command (cavalry and mounted infantry), not Stuart. Stuart covered the army's rear flank more broadly. The fact pack says "escorted by Stuart's cavalry" — this appears to be an error in the fact pack that the draft reproduced. MUST-FIX: change to "escorted by Confederate cavalry" or specify Imboden.

- **"Eight hundred miles to the southwest" (Vicksburg from Gettysburg)**
  ✅ CONFIRMED AS ACCEPTABLE. Vicksburg, MS is approximately 800–850 miles southwest of Gettysburg, PA as the crow flies. "Eight hundred miles to the southwest" is accurate. (Rome2rio distance data; geographic calculation)

- **Meade did not aggressively pursue; debate followed him for life**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. (Wikipedia: Retreat from Gettysburg; Wikipedia: George Meade)

- **Lincoln/Congress impatient; McClellan/Antietam parallel**
  ✅ CONFIRMED AS ACCEPTABLE. Both points are standard historical characterizations. (Wikipedia: Meade; Wikipedia: McClellan)

- **Timothy O'Sullivan, "A Harvest of Death," Union dead, days after the battle; "faces to the sky, their pockets already turned out"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. O'Sullivan photographed the dead. The image shows Union dead face-up; the pockets-turned-out detail is documented (the bodies were robbed). (Wikipedia: A Harvest of Death; MetMuseum; Getty)

- **"for the second time in the war (the first was Antietam) the people at home were made to look at exactly what their newspapers' casualty columns actually meant"**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. The Antietam photography exhibition of October 1862 at Brady's gallery was the first time Americans saw photographs of battlefield dead. Gettysburg followed. (History.com; Clara Barton Museum; NPS)

- **July 4: Vicksburg surrendered to Grant; Mississippi open end to end; Confederacy cut in two**
  ✅ CONFIRMED. Vicksburg surrendered July 4, 1863. (Wikipedia: Siege of Vicksburg; Battlefields.org)

- **Vicksburg "last great Confederate stronghold on the Mississippi River"; fall cut off Texas, Arkansas, most of Louisiana**

## MUST-FIX list

These block the draft from shipping. Each is either a confirmed factual error (❌) or an unhedged material claim that creates a false impression (⚠️ MUST-FIX).

1. **❌ "Two weeks earlier, at Chancellorsville" (`setting`)**
   Chancellorsville ended May 6; Lee moved out June 3 — that is ~4 weeks, not 2. Change to "weeks earlier," "a month earlier," or "in May."

2. **❌ "a thousand miles down the Mississippi" (`setting` + echoed in `hooks`)**
   "Down the Mississippi" is wrong; that river distance is far less than 1,000 miles. The Gettysburg-to-Vicksburg distance by road/air is ~1,026–1,047 miles, but that is not a Mississippi River distance. The two sections also give contradictory figures ("a thousand miles" in `setting`/`hooks` vs. "eight hundred miles" in `aftermath`). Fix to "hundreds of miles to the southwest" (or drop the mileage figure) in both `setting` and `hooks`. The `aftermath` "eight hundred miles to the southwest" is accurate and can stay.

3. **❌ Stuart escorted the wounded wagon train (`aftermath`)**
   The wounded wagon train was specifically commanded by Gen. John D. Imboden, not J.E.B. Stuart. Stuart covered the army's flank during the retreat more broadly. Fix to "escorted by Confederate cavalry" or "under the command of Gen. John Imboden."

---

## SHOULD-FIX list

These are not blocking errors but improve precision and are easy to correct.

4. **"asked twice" to swing wider (Hood, `hooks`)** → Change to "asked repeatedly" or "asked several times." Sources document at least 3–4 protests from Hood to Longstreet.

5. **"argued for the next sixty years" (Sickles, `hooks`)** → Change to "for the rest of his long life." Sickles died in 1914, ~51 years after the battle — "sixty years" overstates it slightly. The fact pack says "the rest of his life."

6. **"a month before" re: Jackson's death relative to July 1 (`setting`)** → Jackson died May 10; Gettysburg was July 1–3 — that is ~7 weeks, not "a month." Relative to the June 3 march-out it is ~3.5 weeks, which is closer to "a month." Suggest changing to "weeks before" or simply cutting the relative timing.

7. **Iron Brigade nickname origin** → The draft says they "earned their grim nickname in earlier fighting for refusing to break." Technically they earned the name "Iron Brigade" at South Mountain, September 1862 (a battle directly before Antietam). The characterization is not wrong, just vague. If precision is wanted: "earned their nickname at South Mountain in the fall of 1862." LOW PRIORITY.

8. **"two Confederate brigades" at Devil's Den** → Factually defensible (Robertson and Law's brigades) but not in the fact pack. Softening to "the Confederate assault" or "the weight of Hood's attack" would be safer. LOW PRIORITY.

## Verdict

**FAIL** — three MUST-FIX items prevent shipping:
1. "Two weeks earlier" (Chancellorsville timing) is wrong.
2. "A thousand miles down the Mississippi" is wrong in two sections; also conflicts with the "eight hundred miles" in `aftermath`.
3. The wagon train was escorted by Imboden, not Stuart.

All five §FIXES from the fact pack are correctly applied in the drafts. All disputed quotes (Lee, Pickett) are properly hedged. The "train-writing myth" and "thought it a failure" story are correctly dropped. The bones are strong; the MUST-FIX items are small targeted corrections, not structural rewrites.
