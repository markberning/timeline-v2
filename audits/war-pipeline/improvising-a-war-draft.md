## two-republics · The shape of the fight · Two republics, unequal
### A war over slavery, fought by two countries built for opposite tasks

Start with the thing the maps and the troop counts can hide, because it is the whole reason there were armies in the field at all. In the spring of 1861 eleven Southern states broke away from the United States and raised armies for one purpose: to preserve the enslavement of roughly **four million** human beings. The Confederacy was founded to protect slavery, and its own leaders said so, in their own documents, at the time. The two enormous armies that would spend the next four years killing each other were not raised over an abstraction about “states’ rights” in general. They were raised over whether people could be owned. Everything that follows in this chapter — the plans, the railroads, the manpower math, the two presidents learning their jobs on the fly — sits on top of that fact. (The South’s founding documents make the case in their own words; that primary-source story lives in [Two Governments](/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/two-governments).)

Now the math, because the math is where the strategy starts. On paper this looked like a wildly lopsided fight. The Union had about **22 million** people; the Confederacy had fewer than **9 million** — and of those, nearly **4 million were enslaved**, roughly a third of the South’s entire population, and the very people the war was being fought to keep in chains. The South would not, for most of the war, put guns in their hands. So the pool of free men the Confederacy could actually mobilize was a *fraction* of the North’s. Split the national population and you get something like **71 percent** on the Union side, **29 percent** on the Confederate side.

The industrial gap was even starker. By 1860 the North made nearly all the country’s manufactured goods: somewhere around **97 percent** of its firearms, **96 percent** of its railroad locomotives, **94 percent** of its cloth, **93 percent** of its pig iron (the raw iron you start with to make everything else), and over 90 percent of its boots and shoes. The industrial revolution had, more or less, stopped at the Mason–Dixon line — the old surveyor’s boundary that had come to mark the divide between free North and slave South. The Confederacy was a farming country about to fight an industrial war.

Then there were the railroads, and railroads in this war were not a convenience — they were the nervous system that let a government move and feed armies across a continent. The North had roughly **21,000 to 22,000 miles** of track; the South had about **9,000** (again, near enough to that 71/29 split). Worse for the South, it had maybe a third of the country’s freight cars, a fifth of the locomotives, a tenth of the telegraph stations — and its track was a patchwork of different **gauges** (the distance between the rails), so cars from one line often couldn’t run onto the next. A Northern army could be supplied down one continuous iron road. A Southern army frequently had to unload, cart its supplies across a gap, and load them onto a different train. And the U.S. Navy, though small, was a real *national* navy with ships, yards, and trained officers; the Confederacy began the war with essentially no navy at all and had to build one from scratch.

So: fewer people, almost no factories, a crippled rail network, no fleet. By the numbers the South should have lost in a season. It did not. And the reason it didn’t is the strategic heart of this chapter.

The Confederacy had one colossal advantage that none of those numbers capture: **it only had to survive.** The North had to *win*. The South could “win” simply by not losing — by hanging on long enough and bleeding the North badly enough that the Northern public gave up and let the eleven states go. A draw was a Confederate victory. To make a draw happen, the Union had to do the genuinely hard thing: invade, conquer, AND garrison a territory comparable in size to all of Western Europe. The South had to do the easier thing: endure, and outlast the enemy’s patience.

Fighting on home ground sharpened that advantage two ways. The South could use **interior lines** — the shorter routes that run *inside* your own territory, which let you shift troops quickly from one threatened point to another, like a defender moving across the middle of a circle. The Union, attacking from outside, had to operate on **exterior lines** — the long way around the rim. Shifting a Northern army from one front to another often meant a vast detour the South could short-cut. And the South was defending its own soil, with shorter supply lines and the home-field knowledge that comes with it.

> **MEANWHILE — the bet on cotton.** The South had one more reason for confidence, and it sat outside the battlefield entirely. Southern leaders believed cotton was so essential to the mills of Britain and France that those powers would have to break the Union blockade to get it — that “King Cotton” would drag Europe into the war on the Confederate side. It was a bad bet (Britain happened to be sitting on a cotton surplus in 1861), but in 1861 it shaped Southern nerve. The full diplomatic story is in [Britain, France & Cotton](/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/cotton-diplomacy).

That is the asymmetry this whole chapter turns on. The numbers favored the North. The *nature of the task* — conquer a continent versus simply refuse to die — partly evened it out. The Union’s problem was never beating the Confederate army on a given afternoon. It was beating, occupying, and holding an entire country the size of Western Europe, and persuading its own people to pay for it, year after year, in blood. Almost nobody, on either side, understood that yet.

## raising-armies · From almost nothing · Building armies in a hurry
### How two countries conjured a million soldiers out of citizens

Here is the fact that should reframe everything you think you know about 1861: the United States Army that was supposed to put down a continental rebellion was almost laughably small. On January 1, 1861, the **Regular Army** — the full-time, professional, peacetime force — stood at **16,403** officers and men on paper, with only about **14,658** actually present for duty. Call it **about 16,000** soldiers, total, for the whole country. And most of them weren’t even gathered anywhere useful; they were scattered in tiny posts out on the western frontier, guarding wagon routes and watching for raids. The Confederacy had it even leaner in one sense: it had **no regular army at all.** Every Confederate regiment had to be raised from absolute scratch.

So both sides had to invent armies, fast, almost from zero. The basic building block was the **regiment** — a unit of about **1,000 men** at full strength (usually fewer once disease and desertion went to work). The way they actually got built in 1861 tells you everything about how amateur this all was. A prominent local man — a lawyer, a merchant, a politician with a name people knew — would stand up in his town and raise a regiment from his own neighbors. The men would frequently **elect their own officers**, voting for the company captain the way you’d vote for a club president; the state governor then formally appointed the regimental officers. Units often supplied their own uniforms and sometimes their own weapons. The result was glorious chaos: regiments in every color and cut, armed with whatever was at hand, led by men whose qualification was popularity rather than training.

Three days after the bombardment of Fort Sumter — the federal fort in Charleston harbor whose surrender on April 14, 1861, opened the war ([the full story of Fort Sumter](/war-civil-war/naval/fort-sumter)) — Lincoln (North) made his first move to fill that gap. On **April 15, 1861**, he issued a proclamation calling for **75,000 militia**. (**Militia** are citizen-soldiers called up for a short term of service, as opposed to the full-time regulars.) The men were to serve **90 days** — three months — and Lincoln summoned Congress into special session for the Fourth of July. Their first stated job: to “repossess” the federal property the seceded states had seized.

That **90-day** number is worth pausing on, because it is the chapter’s irony engine. Why ninety days? Partly because an old law, the Militia Act of 1795, capped this kind of call-up at three months. But also because almost *everyone* assumed the war would be over by then. Ninety days felt generous. The paperwork itself was built on the illusion of a short war — which is exactly why, when the first Union army marched toward its first big battle that July, its soldiers’ enlistments were literally running out underneath it.

And these were green troops on both sides — citizen-volunteers who had been drilling for a few weeks, led by amateur officers, neither trained nor scaled for the war that was actually coming. The two armies that would collide in July 1861 were, in the plain words of the time, “very green recruits.” The hard skill of running an army of hundreds of thousands — feeding it, moving it, keeping it from dying of dysentery before it ever saw the enemy — had to be *learned*, the expensive way, in the field. That learning curve is the spine of this entire pillar.

## the-anaconda · The slow war · Scott and the “Anaconda”
### The one man thinking in years, and why everyone laughed at him

In all the noise of 1861, one professional soldier was thinking about the war as a single, continent-sized problem — and the country mocked him for it.

He was **General-in-Chief Winfield Scott (North)**, the senior officer of the U.S. Army. (The **General-in-Chief** is the top *uniformed* commander — not to be confused with the **commander-in-chief**, who is the civilian president; readers mix these up constantly, and the difference will matter by the end of this chapter.) Scott was a genuine legend, a hero of the War of 1812 and the Mexican–American War. He was also, by 1861, in his mid-seventies, heavy, and so infirm he could barely mount a horse. But his mind was clear, and it was working on a scale nobody around him wanted to hear.

In early May 1861 — first in a letter to Maj. Gen. George B. McClellan (North) on **May 3**, then in a fuller version dated **May 21** — Scott sketched out a strategy. It had two parts. **One:** a strict naval **blockade** of every Confederate port, sealing the South off from the world by sea. **Two:** a powerful army column — Scott proposed perhaps **80,000 men** — driving straight down the **Mississippi River** to split the Confederacy in two and cut its western states off from its eastern ones. The whole idea was to *envelop and strangle* the South economically, to squeeze it until it gave up — in Scott’s own framing, to “bring them to terms with less bloodshed than by any other plan.” It was a strategy of patient pressure, not of one glorious knockout battle.

The country hated it. Critics jeered that it was too slow, too passive, too cowardly — a plan that would take *years*. Opponents likened it to the coils of an **anaconda**, the great snake that suffocates its prey by slowly squeezing, and the sneering nickname stuck: the “Anaconda Plan.” The press piled on; the *Chicago Tribune* ran it down as a “Torpid Anaconda.” What the public wanted instead could be chanted in three words — **“On to Richmond!”** — a fast, glorious march on the new Confederate capital that would end the whole thing in an afternoon. The mood of 1861 was *action*, and a wheezing old general talking about blockades and river campaigns measured in years was exactly the wrong music.

(One thing to keep straight, because it gets overstated: the “Anaconda Plan” was never an official, adopted master-plan with that name. It was Scott’s *proposal*, mocked into a nickname. The Union never formally signed off on “the Anaconda Plan” as such.)

Scott didn’t last the year. Aged and ailing, and elbowed aside by younger men hungry for the offensive, he **retired on November 1, 1861**, and the dashing McClellan replaced him as General-in-Chief. He left town a figure of fun.

He was also, it turned out, more right than nearly everyone who laughed at him. His two pillars — choke the coast with a blockade, seize the Mississippi to cut the Confederacy in half — would in fact become the backbone of eventual Union victory. The river was finally severed when the fortress city of [Vicksburg](/war-civil-war/western/vicksburg) fell in July 1863, splitting the Confederacy exactly as Scott had drawn it; the blockade tightened, slowly, for years. He was right strategically, even if his “plan” was never literally adopted as one. Hold onto that, because in a few sections — at a place called Bull Run — the joke is going to stop being funny.

## the-blockade · The patient weapon · The blockade proclaimed
### A strategy of strangulation, declared in week one

If “On to Richmond!” was the war the public wanted, the **blockade** was the war Scott was actually describing — and Lincoln (North) reached for it almost immediately, days into the fighting.

On **April 19, 1861**, Lincoln issued a proclamation declaring a **blockade** — a naval closing of the ports — of the seceded states: South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Florida, Mississippi, Louisiana, and Texas. On **April 27** he extended it to add Virginia and North Carolina. On paper, the entire Confederate coast was now to be sealed off from the world.

On paper. Because the task was staggering and the means weren’t there. The Confederate coastline ran roughly **3,500 miles**, pocked with dozens of ports, river mouths, and inlets — and the U.S. Navy in 1861 was nowhere near big enough to actually plug it. For a long time the blockade was a *legal declaration* far more than a physical reality, a line drawn on a chart that the Navy spent years growing into.

It also came with a diplomatic snag worth a sentence, because it shows how every strategic choice in this war had a political shadow. By proclaiming a “blockade” — rather than simply announcing he was closing his own country’s ports — Lincoln implicitly treated the Confederacy as a **belligerent** under international law, something close to a real warring nation rather than a band of rebels. That mattered for the question of whether Europe would recognize the South, and it’s a thread that runs through the cotton-diplomacy story. (Touch it lightly here; the full version lives in [Britain, France & Cotton](/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/cotton-diplomacy).)

The reason the blockade belongs in a strategy chapter is that it was the patient, unglamorous half of the whole war — the exact opposite of “On to Richmond.” It promised no quick triumph, no flag planted on Richmond by summer. It promised, instead, a slow tightening of the noose over *years*: cutting off the South’s ability to sell its cotton and buy the guns, machinery, and medicine it couldn’t make for itself. It was strangulation, not a knockout. In April 1861, almost nobody wanted that war. It was the war they got. (The naval combat and the strange new ironclad warships that fought over the blockade are their own story — see [Ironclads & the Blockade](/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/ironclads).)

## border-states · The prize in the middle · The border states
### The slave states that didn’t leave — and why they decided everything

There were slave states that did *not* secede, and in 1861 they were arguably the most important ground in the war — not for sentiment, but for cold strategy.

**Border states** were the slaveholding states that stayed in the Union: **Kentucky, Maryland, Missouri**, and tiny Delaware. They sat in the gap between North and South, and what they held was decisive — railroads, river crossings, manpower, and the physical approaches to Washington itself and to the Ohio and Mississippi rivers. Losing them could have flipped the whole strategic problem from merely hard to nearly impossible. This wasn’t a matter of which side they *felt* loyal to. It was geography and logistics, and Lincoln understood it as clearly as he understood anything.

He understood it so clearly that he put it in writing, in a private letter to Senator Orville H. Browning on **September 22, 1861**, in words that have become the single clearest statement of his 1861 strategy:

> “I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game. Kentucky gone, we can not hold Missouri, nor, as I think, Maryland. These all against us, and the job on our hands is too large for us.” — Abraham Lincoln (North)

Lose Kentucky, he was saying, and you lose Missouri and Maryland after it; lose all three, and the war is simply too big to win. So watch how carefully he played the most delicate of them.

**Kentucky** tried to sit the war out. On **May 20, 1861**, Governor Beriah Magoffin declared the state **neutral** — refusing to send troops to *either* side, North or South. It was a strategic tightrope, and Lincoln, instead of stomping on it, deliberately handled Kentucky with enormous care. He declined to force the issue, declined to march in, declined to give either side an excuse to call him the aggressor. He waited.

His patience paid off when the Confederacy lost its nerve and moved first. On **September 4, 1861**, the Confederate general Maj. Gen. Leonidas Polk (South) marched troops into **Columbus, Kentucky**, violating the neutrality. It was a blunder. It let the Union respond as the *defender* of Kentucky rather than its invader: Brig. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant (North) seized Paducah two days later, on September 6, and the Kentucky legislature — furious at the Confederate move — ordered only the *Confederates* out. The neutrality broke, and it broke toward the Union. Lincoln had won Kentucky largely by refusing to grab for it.

The other two get a quicker word. **Maryland** wrapped around Washington, D.C., on three sides; lose it and the national capital would have been an island in hostile territory. Lincoln moved hard to hold it, including suspending **habeas corpus** (the legal right that stops the government from jailing you without showing cause before a court) along the vital rail line so he could arrest secessionists without the usual process. **Missouri** controlled where the Missouri and Mississippi rivers met and held the big federal arsenal at St. Louis — and Missouri, unlike the others, was settled by force. Which is where the war’s second front opens.

## the-west-opens · A second front · Missouri, Wilson’s Creek, and Frémont
### The war proves it will be fought across a continent

While the country fixated on the hundred miles between Washington and Richmond, the war quietly became *continental* — and it did so out in Missouri, in the summer of 1861.

The biggest battle west of the Mississippi that year was **Wilson’s Creek**, fought on **August 10, 1861**, about ten miles southwest of Springfield, Missouri. The strategic shorthand is enough here, because the blow-by-blow lives in its own dossier. A Union force of roughly **5,400** men under the aggressive Brig. Gen. Nathaniel Lyon (North) attacked a combined Confederate and Missouri State Guard army of **more than 10,000** under Brig. Gen. Benjamin McCulloch (South) and Maj. Gen. Sterling Price (South). Lyon was badly outnumbered, and in the fighting he was killed — **the first Union general to die in the Civil War.** Tactically, the Confederates won the field. ([The full story of Wilson’s Creek](/war-civil-war/trans-mississippi/wilsons-creek).)

But here’s what it *meant*, which is the only thing this chapter cares about. Even though the South won the battle, Lyon’s reckless, headlong 1861 campaign had already done the larger strategic job: it kept Missouri from falling into the Confederacy. People started calling Wilson’s Creek “the Bull Run of the West,” and the nickname carried the real lesson. This war was not going to be decided on one front along the Richmond–Washington line. It had just sprouted a second front, a thousand miles away, in its very first summer. The map of the war was the whole country.

Missouri also produced the year’s sharpest collision between strategy and the war’s central issue — slavery — and it came from a Union general getting *ahead* of his commander-in-chief. On **August 30, 1861**, Maj. Gen. John C. Frémont (North), commanding in Missouri, declared **martial law** and issued his own proclamation: he would confiscate the property of armed rebels and **free the people they held as slaves.** This was **sixteen months before** Lincoln’s own Emancipation Proclamation, and it was politically explosive — because Lincoln feared that freeing slaves *now*, by military order, would terrify the border states (Kentucky above all) into bolting to the Confederacy. He asked Frémont to soften it. Frémont refused unless ordered. So Lincoln **publicly overruled him on September 11, 1861**, rescinding the emancipation order.

It is worth being exact about what that fight was, because it is the war’s real subject showing through the strategy. Lincoln did not object to the *idea* of freeing enslaved people. He objected to the *timing* — and the timing was governed entirely by the need to hold Kentucky. In 1861, military necessity outranked emancipation in his calculus: the right end, at the wrong moment. The cause the war was being fought over was already sitting right on the surface of the strategy, and the strategy was making it wait. (When the moment was finally right, the full story is told in [Emancipation](/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/emancipation).)

## bull-run-illusion · The day the dream died · First Bull Run
### When the short war ended, and the long one began

And then came the day the whole illusion of 1861 died on a single battlefield.

The battle was **First Bull Run** — the Confederates called it First Manassas — fought on **July 21, 1861**, near Manassas, Virginia. It was the first major battle of the war, and the public approached it as a spectacle rather than a slaughter. Washington civilians, U.S. senators among them, packed picnic baskets and rode out with opera glasses to *watch* the Union army win a quick, glorious victory and start the march to Richmond. They expected an afternoon’s entertainment with a happy ending.

What they got was a rout. The green Union troops — many of them the very **90-day men** from Lincoln’s April call, their enlistments quite literally expiring as they marched — broke under Confederate pressure and fled back toward Washington in a panicked, tangled mess, picnickers and soldiers streaming up the roads together. The casualties were shocking for 1861 (though they would look almost trivial by the war’s later standards): roughly **2,900 Union** and **2,000 Confederate**, with something like **850 men killed** all told. These are approximate — the exact counts vary by source — but the magnitude was the point. ([The full story of Bull Run](/war-civil-war/eastern/bull-run).)

This is the chapter’s hinge, so let it land. Bull Run changed the country’s entire understanding of what the war would be. It dispelled, in one afternoon, the illusion of a short conflict. The “picnic battle” turned into the day the **90-day dream** died — for the public, for the press, and for Lincoln. The men who had marched out singing about a three-month war marched back having learned, at a cost of hundreds of lives, that there wasn’t going to be one.

And here is where the slow-war joke stops being a joke. Back in §3, Winfield Scott had been mocked for proposing a patient, years-long strategy of strangulation while everyone else chanted “On to Richmond!” Bull Run was “On to Richmond!” — the quick, decisive march the public demanded — and it ended in a humiliating wreck. The lesson the rout taught was *exactly* the lesson Scott had been laughed out of the room for teaching: this was going to be a long, grinding, continental war, and you could not win it with one glorious charge. After Bull Run the North turned to raising **long-term armies** instead of three-month militias, and settled in for the patient war the old general had described and everyone had ridiculed. He’d been right. It took a defeat to prove it.

## two-commanders · Two presidents at war · Lincoln and Davis
### The professional soldier and the prairie lawyer — and who learned faster

The two men who had to direct all of this — the manpower, the plans, the border-state chess, the long war nobody wanted — had never run a war in their lives. And the paradox of 1861 is that the side with the *better-credentialed* war president was the South.

On paper, **Jefferson Davis (South)** was almost perfectly built for the job. He was a **West Point graduate** (Class of 1828), a U.S. Army officer in his youth, and a genuine combat hero of the Mexican–American War: as colonel of the **1st Mississippi Rifles** he was wounded in the heel at the Battle of Buena Vista in 1847, where his regiment helped stop a Mexican assault cold. He had been **U.S. Secretary of War** from 1853 to 1857, literally running the United States Army, championing its modernization. He’d been a U.S. senator. Elected provisional Confederate president in February 1861, he was a soldier-statesman with deep military knowledge. The telling detail: Davis had wanted a *military command*, not the presidency. He’d have rather been a general. And as president he tended to involve himself deeply — often too deeply — in military matters, keeping tight personal control of strategy and, before long, feuding with his own generals.

**Abraham Lincoln (North)** had almost none of that. His entire military résumé was a brief, combat-free stint in the Illinois militia during the Black Hawk War of 1832 — service so uneventful that he joked his only battles had been with mosquitoes. He was a self-taught prairie lawyer who had never commanded anything. But he had a particular kind of mind: fast, analytical, and humble enough to learn out loud. He checked books on military strategy out of the Library of Congress and read them. He studied his generals’ wins and losses, learned from his own mistakes, and applied a relentless, hard-edged common sense to problems the West Pointers overcomplicated. Historians broadly agree on the verdict, and it is the paradox’s payoff: despite the enormous experience gap, **Lincoln became the more effective commander-in-chief of the two.**

Their command setups in 1861 reflected the two men. Lincoln (North) sat above the aging General-in-Chief Winfield Scott (North) — soon replaced by McClellan (North) — and he would cycle through general after general for years before he finally found Ulysses S. Grant. Davis (South), the soldier-president, kept the strategy close to his own chest and worked through commanders like P.G.T. Beauregard (South) and Joseph E. Johnston (South), relationships that were prickly almost from the start. Davis’s early strength was a coherent doctrine — the **“offensive-defensive,”** meaning defend the homeland along those short interior lines but strike north when a real opening appeared. His early weakness was the micromanaging and the feuding. Lincoln’s early strength was political-strategic: the border-state restraint and the overruling of Frémont, both of which subordinated military romance to cold necessity. Both men shared the era’s great error — both badly under-planned for *length*. The 90-day call and the universal short-war assumption were the mistake they made together.

But strip away the symmetry of “two untested presidents,” because the symmetry lies about the most important thing. These two men were raising armies for opposite causes, and the causes were not morally equivalent. Davis was raising an army to defend a republic founded to keep four million people enslaved — that was the Confederacy’s reason for existing, stated plainly in its own founding. Lincoln was raising an army to preserve the Union — a war that, as Frémont’s overruled order already hinted, would in time become a war to end slavery itself. Two presidents, two armies, one of them fighting to keep human beings as property and the other on its way, step by reluctant step, to setting them free. That difference is not a footnote to the strategy. It is the thing the strategy was *for*. (Lincoln’s own road to that war — the election that put him in the chair — is told in [Lincoln’s Rise & the Election of 1860](/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/lincolns-rise).)

So 1861 closed with the illusion gone. Two presidents who had each expected a short, contained war now understood — one of them faster than the other — that they were locked into a long one: a continental war of conquest on one side and a war of survival on the other, fought over the ownership of human beings, with the armies still being invented even as they marched. They had improvised their way into it. Now they would have to learn how to fight it.

(Flag #4: 80,000 = Scott’s proposed figure, not a hard plan — phrased “proposed perhaps 80,000.”)
- Derision: mocked as slow/passive; “anaconda” snake nickname (originally derisive); Chicago Tribune “Torpid Anaconda”; public cry “On to Richmond!” — factpack §3 [Anaconda Plan, Wikipedia; National Civil War Museum; americanheritage.com].
- “Anaconda Plan” never officially adopted as a named master-plan — factpack flag #5 (honored explicitly in prose).
- Scott retired Nov 1, 1861, replaced by McClellan — factpack §3.
- Payoff: pillars (blockade + Mississippi) became backbone of victory; river split CSA at Vicksburg July 1863 (LINK DOWN /war-civil-war/western/vicksburg) — factpack §3. (Did NOT overstate as adopted plan.)

**§ the-blockade**
- April 19, 1861 Proclamation: blockade of SC, GA, AL, FL, MS, LA, TX; extended April 27 to add VA and NC — factpack §4 [Proclamation 81, American Presidency Project; history.state.gov].
- Coastline ~3,500 mi, dozens of ports/inlets; Navy too small — legal declaration before physical reality — factpack §4 [history.state.gov].
- Diplomacy wrinkle: declaring a “blockade” implicitly granted belligerent status (touched lightly; LINK ACROSS Britain/Cotton) — factpack §4 [EBSCO; history.state.gov].
- Strategic role: patient/unglamorous half of Scott’s plan, years to bite; counter to “On to Richmond” — factpack §4. (LINK ACROSS Ironclads & the Blockade for naval combat/tech.)

**§ border-states**
- Border states = slave states that stayed in the Union: KY, MD, MO, DE; held railroads/rivers/manpower/approaches to Washington + Ohio/Mississippi line — factpack §5 [Border states, Wikipedia; nps.gov].
- Lincoln–Browning quote, Sept 22, 1861, VERBATIM incl. “can not” (two words): “I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game. Kentucky gone, we can not hold Missouri, nor, as I think, Maryland. These all against us, and the job on our hands is too large for us.” — factpack §5 / flag #6 [House Divided/Dickinson; Collected Works Vol. 4].
- Kentucky neutrality declared May 20, 1861 (Gov. Magoffin); Lincoln handled with care; CSA broke it first — Polk (South) occupied Columbus KY Sept 4, 1861; Grant (North) seized Paducah Sept 6; legislature ordered only Confederates out — factpack §5 [Kentucky in the ACW, Wikipedia].
- Maryland surrounded Washington on 3 sides; Lincoln moved hard incl. suspending habeas corpus (glossed) along the rail line — factpack §5 [Border states, Wikipedia].
- Missouri: Missouri/Mississippi confluence + St. Louis arsenal; contested by force (→ §6) — factpack §5.

**§ the-west-opens**
- Wilson’s Creek, Aug 10, 1861, ~10 mi SW of Springfield MO; Union ~5,400 (Lyon, North) vs CSA + Missouri State Guard >10,000 (McCulloch, South; Price, South) — factpack §6 [Battle of Wilson’s Creek, Wikipedia; Britannica]. (Flag #8: troop numbers approximate; Lyon badly outnumbered — solid.)
- Nathaniel Lyon (North) killed = FIRST Union general to die in the Civil War — factpack §6 (firmly established) [Nathaniel Lyon, Wikipedia; npg.si.edu].
- Tactically a CSA victory; strategically Lyon’s campaign kept Missouri out of the Confederacy; “Bull Run of the West”; proved a second/western front — factpack §6 (LINK DOWN /war-civil-war/trans-mississippi/wilsons-creek).
- Frémont (North) Aug 30, 1861: martial law + proclamation confiscating rebels’ property and freeing their slaves; 16 months before Emancipation Proclamation — factpack §6 [Frémont Emancipation, Wikipedia].
- Lincoln feared it would push border states (esp. KY) to CSA; asked Frémont to modify; Frémont refused unless ordered; Lincoln publicly ordered it rescinded/modified Sept 11, 1861 — factpack §6 / flag #7.
- NAME-THE-CAUSE beat: in 1861 military necessity (hold border states) outranked emancipation; Lincoln objected to timing, not the idea — factpack §6 (LINK ACROSS Emancipation).

**§ bull-run-illusion**
- First Bull Run / First Manassas, July 21, 1861, near Manassas VA; first major battle — factpack §7 [Battlefields.org; Britannica].
- “Picnic battle”: Washington civilians incl. U.S. senators rode out with picnic baskets/opera glasses to watch — factpack §7 [senate.gov; History.com].
- Union rout; green troops fled toward Washington — factpack §7 [Battlefields.org; Britannica].
- Casualties (APPROXIMATE, flagged): Union ~2,896; Confederate ~1,982; ~850 killed total — printed as “roughly 2,900 / 2,000 / ~850,” cited as approximate — factpack §7 / flag #1.
- Army largely the 90-day men, enlistments running out — factpack §7 [History.com].
- Meaning: dispelled illusion of a short conflict; day the 90-day dream died; North turned to long-term armies — factpack §7 (LINK DOWN /war-civil-war/eastern/bull-run). §3+§7 payoff arc landed.

**§ two-commanders**
- Jefferson Davis (South): West Point Class of 1828; U.S. Army officer; col. 1st Mississippi Rifles, wounded in the heel at Buena Vista 1847; U.S. Secretary of War 1853–1857; U.S. senator; provisional CSA president Feb 1861; wanted a military command not the presidency; tended to micromanage / feud with generals — factpack §8 [Jefferson Davis, Wikipedia]. (Flag #11: “wanted a military command” = attested characterization, kept.)
- Abraham Lincoln (North): only military service = Black Hawk War 1832 (combat-free); joked his battles were with mosquitoes; self-taught prairie lawyer; fast analytical learner who read strategy + studied generals; historians judge him the MORE effective commander-in-chief despite the gap — factpack §8 [Essential Civil War Curriculum].
- Command structures: Lincoln over Scott (→ McClellan Nov 1861), cycled through generals before Grant; Davis kept tight control, worked through Beauregard (South) + Johnston (South), friction-filled — factpack §8.
- Davis doctrine = “offensive-defensive” (defend on interior lines, strike north on opportunity); weakness = micromanage/feud. Lincoln strength = border-state restraint + overruling Frémont. Both under-planned for length (the shared 1861 error) — factpack §8.
- “commander-in-chief” (civilian president) vs “General-in-Chief” (top uniformed officer) glossed — factpack §8 clarity note.
- NAME-THE-CAUSE (anti-both-sides): Davis raising an army for a republic founded to keep 4 million enslaved; Lincoln raising one to preserve the Union, becoming a war to end slavery; causes NOT morally equivalent — factpack §8 framing note. (LINK ACROSS Lincoln’s Rise.)
