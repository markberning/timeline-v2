**Orientation.** Yorktown ended a campaign, not the war. For two more years two armies kept facing each other across the Hudson while a third American army went ragged outside Charleston; the frontier produced the war's ugliest year (Gnadenhutten and Blue Licks, both AFTER Yorktown); the world war kept burning and actually turned Britain's way (the Saintes, Gibraltar); a New Jersey militia captain was hanged by Loyalists and a twenty-year-old British captain drew the slip marked "unfortunate" in a Lancaster tavern, until a queen of France helped talk the Americans out of hanging him back; and an unpaid army waiting at Newburgh came closer to turning on the civil power than at any moment of the war — and stood down. Then it all unwound in a long series of leavings: Savannah, Charleston, the Loyalist fleets, the Wednesday inspections at a Pearl Street tavern, Evacuation Day, the same tavern's Long Room nine days later, and a statehouse in Annapolis where the commander handed the war back. The chapter's method is the braid carried to the end: a war that is over and not over at once, told as the people inside it felt it — nobody in 1782 knew the fighting was finished. No elegy, no triumphalism, no Washington deification: the close is a man giving a piece of paper back and riding home.

## 1. THE WAR THAT WOULDN'T ADMIT IT WAS OVER (November 1781 – spring 1782)

- **Washington refused the victory lap — verified verbatim, eight days after the surrender.** To Virginia's governor **Thomas Nelson, Jr.**, 27 October 1781: *"I will candidly confess to your Excellency, that my only apprehension (which I wish may be groundless) is, lest the late important success, instead of exciting our exertions, as it ought to do, should produce such a relaxation in the prosecution of the War, as will prolong the calamities of it."* (HIGH — founders.archives.gov full text re-verified this pass. This is the chapter's keynote: the commander treating Yorktown as a danger.)
- **What the map still said.** Britain still garrisoned **New York City, Charleston, and Savannah**, still held Canada and East Florida, still had the world's largest navy; tens of thousands of British and German troops in North America were untouched by Yorktown. (HIGH on the spine; keep troop totals qualitative — matches the treaty pack.) The Continental Army marched back north from Virginia and took up its old watch on the Hudson; from spring 1782 Washington headquartered at **Newburgh, New York** (the Hasbrouck farmhouse), with the army cantoned nearby at New Windsor — within a long day's ride of the British lines around New York City, where both armies sat facing each other for two more years, patrolling, raiding, and waiting. (HIGH on the standoff and Newburgh HQ; the cantonment's strength fluctuated — keep qualitative, "some ten thousand men" at most as a soft figure, see uncertainties.)
- **The waiting had a texture — use it, don't decorate it.** No great battles in the north after 1781: instead forage raids, whaleboat fights on the Sound, sniping along the Westchester "neutral ground," and in the camps drill, court-martials, and rumor. Every packet ship from Europe brought a new version of the peace; none brought the peace. (HIGH as characterization; specific small actions only if individually verified at author time.)
- **The enemy changed faces and orders.** **Sir Guy Carleton** arrived at New York in **early May 1782** (commonly given as 5 May) replacing Henry Clinton as British commander-in-chief — with instructions, as London turned toward peace, to suspend offensive war and prepare to evacuate. The treaty chapter owns WHY London turned (the Commons votes, North's fall — pill `treaty-of-paris`); this chapter carries what it looked like from the American lines: the raids slackening, then the letters from Carleton proposing to treat the war as ending. Washington's standing posture, per the Nelson keynote: don't believe it until it's signed. (HIGH on Carleton/May 1782/the wind-down orders; MED on the exact day — "early May.")
- **And the navy's war was NOT slackening** — see §3. Keep the simultaneity honest: the same spring the land war went quiet, the sea war produced two of the era's biggest battles. (Framing)

## 2. THE FRONTIER'S WORST YEAR (March – November 1782)

*(The native-nations theme owns these stories in full; this chapter carries each as ONE beat in the timeline — proof that for the Ohio and Kentucky country, "after Yorktown" meant nothing.)*

- **Gnadenhutten, 7–8 March 1782 — one sentence + pill.** Pennsylvania militia under Col. David Williamson murdered **96** unarmed, pacifist Moravian Christian Delaware — men, women, and children — at their mission town on the Muskingum in Ohio; no one was ever punished. Keep to the native-nations chapter's verified register: the 96 total (HIGH); do NOT use the 28/29/39 breakdown (MED, reserved); do not retell the vote/the hymns/the two boys (the theme owns them). Frame here: five months after Cornwallis surrendered, the ugliest single act of the war. (HIGH; matches `rev-native-nations-factpack.md` exactly.)
- **Blue Licks, 19 August 1782 — Kentucky's disaster, one beat.** A force of British rangers and allied Wyandot/Shawnee fighters, withdrawing from a failed raid on Bryan's Station, baited pursuing **Kentucky militia (~182 men)** across the Licking River at a salt-lick ford. **Daniel Boone** — present as a militia officer — warned the ground was an ambush; **Hugh McGary** spurred his horse into the ford calling on all who weren't cowards to follow (the wording survives in several later forms — "Them that ain't cowards, follow me" is the traditional one; badge it as tradition). The trap closed in minutes: roughly **70–77 Kentuckians killed** of ~182 engaged — about 40 percent — including colonels John Todd and Stephen Trigg, ten months after Yorktown. (HIGH on date/ambush/the ~70–77 of ~182; the McGary line DISPUTED-as-verbatim — tradition only. Treaty pack uses "about 70" — consistent.)
  - **The humanizing beat — Israel Boone, verified with care.** Among the dead was **Israel Boone, Daniel Boone's 23-year-old son**, shot down in the rout near his father; Boone had to leave his son's body on the field and returned later to bury him. By family accounts Israel, ill that summer, had refused to stay home — and refused a horse out of the rout — but those details are family-transmitted; the verified core is: Boone warned against the attack, fought in it, and lost Israel in it. (HIGH on Israel's death at Blue Licks with his father present; MED on age and the refusal details — badge any of them "by the family's accounts." Boone's traditional "We are all slaughtered men" at the ford — DISPUTED-as-verbatim, badge or omit.)
  - **"Last battle of the Revolution" hygiene:** Blue Licks is often so called; it wasn't quite — George Rogers Clark's retaliatory burning of the Shawnee towns came in **November 1782**. Use "among the last." (HIGH; matches treaty pack.)
- **The point of the section, stated plainly, not editorialized:** the peace being drafted in Paris had nothing in it for this war — the frontier's combatants weren't at the table and mostly weren't told. One forward clause; `native-nations` owns the arc. (HIGH)

## 3. THE GLOBAL LAST ACTS — STALEMATE EVERYWHERE AT ONCE (April – October 1782)

- **The Saintes, 12 April 1782 — one beat + the leverage effect.** In the Caribbean near Guadeloupe, Adm. **Sir George Rodney** (36 ships of the line) shattered the French fleet under the **comte de Grasse** — the same admiral whose fleet had sealed Yorktown six months earlier. De Grasse's flagship ***Ville de Paris*** struck her colors with **de Grasse captured aboard**; about five French ships were taken or destroyed; the planned Franco-Spanish invasion of Jamaica died with the battle. (HIGH; matches treaty pack.) **The leverage beat (one clause, MED-HIGH as framing):** the news reached Europe just as the peace talks were forming, and it stiffened Britain's hand against France — Britain would negotiate America's independence while *winning* its world war, which is part of why it could be generous to the Americans and hard on everyone else. The full negotiation logic belongs to `treaty-of-paris` — pill, don't retell. (Note the resonance for readers of the spine: the man who won Yorktown's sea-lock spent the next two years a prisoner-guest in England — one clause, no more.)
- **Gibraltar, 13 September 1782 — the scene-scale beat (vivid and underknown — this chapter's one set piece abroad).** Spain and France, three years into besieging the Rock (since 1779 — the siege the `french-alliance` chapter chained the American war to), staged their Grand Assault: **ten purpose-built floating batteries** — engineered to be fireproof and unsinkable, with soaked timber, sand-filled hulls, and water-circulation systems — carrying roughly **5,260 men** and well over a hundred heavy guns, anchored off the fortress walls, while a combined army waited ashore and **spectators in the tens of thousands** (contemporary accounts claim as many as 80,000, including French princes) crowded the Spanish hills to watch the Rock fall. The British garrison under Gen. **George Augustus Eliott** answered with **red-hot shot** — cannonballs heated in furnaces until they glowed, fired to lodge and smolder in the "unburnable" hulls. By night the batteries were burning; by morning **all ten were destroyed**, and British gunboats under Roger Curtis rowed into the inferno to pull some 350 enemy survivors out of the water. Allied losses for the assault ran ~**1,400–1,500**; the siege was effectively decided, though it formally dragged on until the peace. (HIGH on date/10 batteries/5,260/red-hot shot/all destroyed/~1,473 allied casualties/Curtis rescue; MED on the 80,000 spectators — "tens of thousands, by some accounts eighty thousand." Matches treaty pack's beat; this chapter gets the SCENE, the treaty chapter gets the consequence — Gibraltar stayed British and gutted Spain's war aim. Pill `treaty-of-paris` for the consequence.)
- **The shape of 1782 at sea and abroad: stalemate everywhere at once.** Britain winning in the Caribbean and at Gibraltar, holding in India; France and Spain unbroken but out of winning moves; America unable to touch the British garrisons. Every party now had better reasons to sign than to fight. State it in one breath and pill to `treaty-of-paris`; do NOT narrate the Commons/North's fall here (the theme owns London). (HIGH as framing)

## 4. GREENE'S SKIRMISH WAR — THE SOUTH WINDS DOWN (1782)

- **The army outside Charleston.** **Nathanael Greene**'s southern army spent 1782 penning the British garrison into Charleston — unpaid, half-supplied, sickly with malaria, its men at times near-mutinous (one plot to betray camp secrets ended in a soldier's execution in April 1782 — use only if re-verified; otherwise keep the condition qualitative: ragged, unpaid, waiting). The fighting that remained was foraging skirmishes — rice boats, picket lines, cattle raids — a war of patrols in the lowcountry heat. (HIGH on the blockade-by-land posture and the army's misery as a characterization; keep specific mutiny/execution details out unless re-verified.)
- **John Laurens, 27 August 1782 — the pointless death, one beat + pill.** Lt. Col. **John Laurens** — Washington's former aide, the South Carolinian who had spent the war pressing his state to raise regiments of enslaved men with freedom as the bounty (the `slavery-and-the-revolution` chapter owns that fight; pill it there) — rose from a sickbed to join an operation against a British **foraging party** on the **Combahee River** and led a charge against a prepared position; he fell in the first volley, age 27. Nearly a year after Yorktown, weeks before the British left the state for good. Washington's verdict, written later: *"he had not a fault, that I ever could discover, unless intrepidity bordering upon rashness could come under that denomination; and to this he was excited by the purest motives."* Greene's: that he fell in *"a paltry little skirmish."* (HIGH on date/place/foraging-party scale/age; MED-HIGH on both quotes as verbatim — widely reproduced from the correspondence; re-verify exact wording at quote time. The beat's register: not martyrdom — waste. His father Henry was negotiating the peace in Europe that same season; one clause, no more — the treaty chapter owns Henry Laurens.)
- **Savannah, 11 July 1782 — the first leaving.** The British garrison under Brig. Gen. Alured Clarke marched out and sailed for New York and St. Augustine; Brig. Gen. **Anthony Wayne**'s Continentals took possession for Georgia's restored government. With the garrison went Loyalist civilians and **thousands of enslaved people carried off still enslaved** — ONE beat + pill `slavery-and-the-revolution` (the theme and treaty pack carry the ~3,500 figure; don't re-argue it here). (HIGH on date/handover; numbers live in the sibling chapters.)
- **Charleston, 14 December 1782 — the choreographed leaving.** By arrangement between Gen. **Alexander Leslie** and the Americans, the evacuation was a procession, not a battle: the British rear guard marched down to the boats at the morning gun and Wayne's advance followed them into the city **about two hundred yards behind**, by agreement, no shots fired — Greene and Governor Mathews entering in form later the same day. The garrison, some 4,200 Loyalist civilians, and **more than 5,000 enslaved people** sailed away (one beat + pill; numbers match the slavery chapter and treaty pack). The South's war ended as a measured walk behind a departing enemy. (HIGH on date and the followed-them-in choreography — Charleston Time Machine/contemporary accounts; the 200-yard spacing detail MED — "at a deliberate distance, by agreement," if not re-verified.)

## 5. THE HUDDY–ASGILL AFFAIR — THE WAR ALMOST EATS ONE MORE MAN (April – November 1782)

*(The braided set piece of the in-between war: retaliation logic meeting peace logic. Verified against Mount Vernon's encyclopedia, the JAR 2023 reassessment, and the standard accounts — with the modern-scholarship caveat built in.)*

- **The hanging that started it.** **Capt. Joshua Huddy**, New Jersey militia, was taken from British custody in New York by men of the **Board of Associated Loyalists** (the Loyalist irregular command — `civil-war-among-themselves` owns that institution; pill) under **Capt. Richard Lippincott**, carried to the Jersey shore, and **hanged on 12 April 1782** — retaliation for the death of the Loyalist **Philip White** in Patriot hands two weeks earlier. Pinned to Huddy's chest was a placard ending **"UP GOES HUDDY FOR PHILIP WHITE."** (HIGH on all of it, including the placard — Mount Vernon. Note the date: the same day de Grasse's fleet was being shattered at the Saintes — a usable braid beat. CARE: claims about HOW Philip White died — escape attempt vs. mutilation — were propaganda in both directions; don't adjudicate, don't inventory.)
- **Washington's retaliation order.** Within days Washington, with his officers' unanimous concurrence, demanded the British hand over Lippincott; refused, he ordered Gen. Moses Hazen at the Lancaster prisoner camp to select by lot a British captain to hang in Huddy's place — and when no *unconditional* prisoner of the right rank could be found, ordered (18 May) the lot drawn from **Yorktown prisoners, men protected by Article 14 of the Yorktown capitulation** — a step over a line the war had so far held. (HIGH — the order chain is documented; state the treaty-violation problem plainly, it was understood at the time.)
- **The slip of paper, 27 May 1782.** At the **Black Bear Tavern in Lancaster, Pennsylvania**, thirteen British captains drew lots; by the standard accounts a drummer boy drew the slips from a hat; **Capt. Charles Asgill** of the 1st Foot Guards — nineteen or twenty years old (sources split; he turned 20 that spring), four months in America before Yorktown, heir to a London banking fortune — drew the paper marked **"unfortunate."** (HIGH on date/place/13 captains/Asgill/the "unfortunate" slip; MED on the drummer boy as the drawer and on the exact age — give "barely twenty" or "nineteen or twenty.")
- **Five months under sentence.** Asgill was held in New Jersey awaiting a gallows while the case tangled: Carleton — newly arrived and trying to end the war quietly — court-martialed Lippincott, who was **acquitted** (he had, the court found, acted under the Associated Loyalists' orders), condemned the Huddy hanging, and warned that executing a capitulated officer would *"destroy all future confidence in Treatys"* (MED on that phrase verbatim — attribute loosely or re-verify). Washington, boxed in — retaliation announced, the precedent poisonous either way — privately looked for an exit while the sentence stood. (HIGH on the arc; the JAR/Ammundsen caveat below governs the register on Asgill's treatment.)
- **A mother, a queen, and a way out.** **Lady Asgill**, the captain's mother (of French Huguenot descent), appealed to the French court; **Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette** directed foreign minister **Vergennes** to intercede, and his letter to Washington (**29 July 1782**) asked, in the monarchs' name, for the young man's life — France, the indispensable ally, requesting mercy as a favor. Washington forwarded it to Congress; on **7 November 1782** Congress resolved that Asgill be released — "as a compliment to the King of France" in the standard telling — and Washington sent Asgill his pass on 13 November; he sailed for England within the month. Nobody was ever punished for Huddy. (HIGH on the chain Lady Asgill → Vergennes → 29 July letter → Congress 7 Nov → pass 13 Nov; the Vergennes sentence *"If it is in your power Sir to consider & to have regard to it you will do what is agreeable to their Majesties"* MED as verbatim — re-verify at quote time. Mount Vernon dates the congressional decision 13 November — the resolution is commonly dated 7 November; phrase "early November" if not re-pinned.)
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — the affair's afterlife (do not ship the old pro-Washington gloss).** The traditional American telling has Asgill held in gentlemanly comfort and Washington straining every nerve for him. **Modern scholarship (Anne Ammundsen, 2019–2023, via JAR and the Lancaster historical journal) surfaced Asgill's own long-suppressed 1786 account claiming close confinement and harsh treatment**, and credits Carleton's delays and the French court — not Washington's tenderness — for his survival; Asgill himself thanked "the French Court particularly Her Majesty." Tell the story with both layers: the documented arc (HIGH) and the contested texture of his captivity (DISPUTED — present as "Asgill later said," not as fact in either direction). No Washington deification; his retaliation order against a protected prisoner is part of the record. (HIGH that the dispute exists; the underlying treatment facts DISPUTED.)

## 6. THE ARMY'S DANGEROUS WAITING (1782 – June 1783)

*(The domestic heart of the chapter. `army-from-nothing` owns the institution arc — Newburgh in full, the furloughs, the Cincinnati; this chapter carries the lived beats and pills out.)*

- **The Badge of Military Merit, 7 August 1782 — the purple heart's ancestor, one beat.** From Newburgh, with no money and an army he could not pay, Washington created an honor instead: his general orders established a badge for **enlisted men and NCOs** — *"the figure of a heart in purple cloth, or silk, edged with narrow lace or binding"* — for "any singularly meritorious action," its wearer entitled to pass guards and sentinels like an officer. In European armies decoration was for officers; the orders said the point out loud — the road to glory in a patriot army being open to all (the famous "road to glory" sentence is in the orders; re-verify exact wording at quote time — MED verbatim, HIGH substance). Only **three men are known to have received it**, all Connecticut sergeants: **Elijah Churchill and William Brown (presented 3 May 1783)** and **Daniel Bissell (June 1783)**. Forgotten for a century and a half, it was revived on Washington's 200th birthday — **22 February 1932 — as the Purple Heart**. (HIGH on date/wording-of-badge/three recipients/1932 revival — Mount Vernon's text of the orders + National Purple Heart Hall of Honor. One forward clause on the Purple Heart only; no modern-military digression.)
- **Newburgh, March 1783 — ONE beat + pill (`army-from-nothing` owns it).** An anonymous address (by Maj. John Armstrong Jr.) called the unpaid officers to an irregular meeting; Washington forbade it, called his own for **15 March 1783**, spoke against the appeal — and reached for his spectacles to read a letter, saying he had **grown gray in their service and now found himself growing blind**. Capt. **Samuel Shaw**, in the room: *"There was something so natural, so unaffected, in this appeal, as rendered it superior to the most studied oratory; it forced its way to the heart, and you might see sensibility moisten every eye."* The officers stood down; the army did not turn on the government it had made. THIS CHAPTER'S CAMERA: one scene-beat in the waiting timeline, then pill — the theme owns the conspiracy's machinery, the nationalists, and the meaning. (HIGH on dates/arc/Shaw's sentences — Shaw's contemporary account via Gilder Lehrman/TAH; the polished direct quote "Gentlemen, you will permit me to put on my spectacles…" survives via eyewitnesses with wobbling wording — the shipped army chapter already badges it "as one of them recorded it afterward"; match that handling exactly.)
- **The ceasefire news, April 1783 — the date is the beat.** Word of the general preliminaries reached America in March; Congress proclaimed the cessation of hostilities on 11 April (treaty pack carries that date soft); and at Newburgh Washington's general orders of **18 April 1783** ordered the **cessation of hostilities publicly proclaimed to the army at noon the next day — 19 April 1783, eight years to the day after Lexington green**. Use the anniversary plainly (the proclamation at the New Building, read at the head of every regiment that evening); whether Washington chose the date for the echo is not established — don't claim intent, let the calendar do it. (HIGH on the orders of 18 April and noon-on-the-19th proclamation — founders/LOC; DON'T assert the anniversary was deliberate.)
- **The army dissolves on credit — one beat + pill.** Congress furloughed the war men home from **13 June 1783** with three months' pay in Morris notes and settlement certificates most sold at a discount (the army theme owns this; numbers and the Martin "worn-out horses" quote live there — do NOT reuse that quote). THIS chapter takes the parting itself, in a soldier's voice — **Joseph Plumb Martin**, on the day his regiment broke up: *"there was as much sorrow as joy transfused on the occasion. We lived together as a family of brothers for several years (setting aside some little family squabbles, like most other families,) had shared with each other the hardships, dangers and sufferings incident to a soldier's life…"* (HIGH on the furlough frame matching the shipped army chapter; MED-HIGH on the Martin passage's exact wording — from the 1830 narrative, widely transcribed; re-verify against the text at quote time and badge as his 1830 memoir.)
- **Washington's last word to the country in uniform — the Circular to the States, 8 June 1783.** Sent to all thirteen governors as he prepared to give up command: the war is won, and the winning is the easy part. Verbatim (founders.archives.gov, re-fetched this pass): *"This is the time of their political probation"* is the standard gloss — quote instead the verified core: *"There are four things, which I humbly conceive, are essential to the well being, I may even venture to say, to the existence of the United States as an Independent Power: 1st. An indissoluble Union of the States under one Federal Head. 2dly. A Sacred regard to Public Justice. 3dly. The adoption of a proper Peace Establishment, and 4thly. The prevalence of that pacific and friendly Disposition, among the People of the United States…"* — and: *"This is the moment when the eyes of the whole World are turned upon them"* … *"according to the System of Policy the States shall adopt at this moment, they will stand or fall."* **The "four great pillars" phrase is usable as a gloss, not a quote** — Washington calls them "four things… essential"; he later calls them "the Pillars on which the glorious Fabrick of our Independency and National Character must be supported" (re-verify that pillar sentence verbatim before using; MED). (HIGH on the "four things" list, "eyes of the whole World," and "stand or fall" — verified against the founders text this pass, with light spelling variance between the Founders transcription and popular printings — quote from founders. Note: "the time of their political probation" phrase also appears in the Circular — re-verify verbatim if used.)

## 7. THE LEAVING (April – November 25, 1783)

- **The Loyalist fleets — one beat + pill.** Through 1783 New York harbor emptied northward: fleet after fleet carrying Loyalist refugees — around **thirty thousand to Nova Scotia alone** by year's end (matches the shipped civil-war chapter; ~60,000 total who left the new republic is the treaty/civil-war figure — do not re-total here). The first big fleet sailed in the spring; the town of Shelburne and the colony of New Brunswick grew from the camps. ONE beat: the harbor seen from the American lines, full of other people's endings. Pill `civil-war-among-themselves`. (HIGH; numbers anchored to the sibling chapters.)
- **The Wednesday inspections at Fraunces Tavern — one beat + pill, and a deliberate echo.** Under Carleton's stand that people freed by British proclamation would not be returned (the slavery chapter owns the Orangetown confrontation and Boston King's story), a **joint British-American board met every Wednesday at Fraunces Tavern** on Pearl Street through 1783 to examine Black refugees' claims to freedom and register the names — some **3,000** — in what became the **Book of Negroes**, before the transports sailed for Nova Scotia. (HIGH — Fraunces Tavern Museum; the ~3,000 matches the slavery chapter and treaty pack.) **The braid the spine alone can carry:** the room where Washington would say goodbye to his officers in December spent that whole year hosting hearings on who owned whom — state it as fact, zero editorial. Pill `slavery-and-the-revolution`.
- **Evacuation Day — 25 November 1783, scene scale (this chapter's set piece; the treaty chapter has it as one beat — keep the angles distinct).**
  - A cold, bright morning, the timetable agreed in advance: the last British troops fell back through the city to the boats; a cannon shot about **1 p.m.** (accounts vary between noon and early afternoon — "around midday") marked the handover; the city had been British for **seven years**, since the `the-north` chapter's autumn of 1776. (HIGH on the date and choreography; MED on the exact hour — range it.)
  - **The American entry:** Gen. **Henry Knox** led the advance down from the Bowery; then **Washington and Gov. George Clinton** rode in at the head of a column of about **800 soldiers** and a parade of citizens, down to Cape's Tavern on Broadway. The city they entered was scarred — burned-over districts (the 1776 fire), wrecked churches, wharves rotten — fill physical detail only from verified sources at author time. (HIGH on Knox/Washington/Clinton/the route; ~800 MED — "a few hundred" safe.)
  - **The eyewitness contrast — badge the chain.** A woman who watched as a girl, in a recollection recorded decades later: *"The troops just leaving us were as if equipped for show… with their scarlet uniforms and burnished arms, made a brilliant display. The troops that marched in, on the contrary, were ill-clad and weather-beaten, and made a forlorn appearance; but then they were our troops… and my heart and my eyes were full."* (MED as verbatim — a later-recorded reminiscence, commonly traced to Griswold's *The Republican Court* (1855); attribute "one New Yorker remembered, years later," never as contemporary. The sentiment is the chapter's thesis said by a witness — use it WITH the badge.)
  - **The flagpole — LEGEND BADGE MANDATORY (shared rule with the treaty pack).** The story New York told for a century: the departing garrison nailed the Union Jack up, cut the halyards, and greased the pole at Fort George; a young veteran sailor, **John Van Arsdale**, nailed on cleats, climbed, and ran up the Stars and Stripes before the fleet cleared the harbor. Its fullest telling was written for the 1883 centennial **by Van Arsdale's own grandson**, and details shift across versions. Tell it AS the story ("the story New Yorkers told…"), never as documented fact. ## 8. THE GOODBYES (November 2 – December 24, 1783)

- **The Farewell Orders, 2 November 1783 (Rocky Hill, near Princeton) — the army's own ending, verified verbatim.** To "the Armies of the United States," before the discharges: the war's survival had been *"little short of a standing Miracle"* — in full: *"the unparalleled perseverence of the Armies of the United States, through almost every possible suffering and discouragement, for the space of eight long years, was little short of a standing Miracle"* — and of himself: *"The Curtain of seperation will soon be drawn—and the Military Scene to him will be closed for ever."* (HIGH — verified verbatim this pass, Encyclopedia Virginia/founders text; the 18th-c. spellings "perseverence"/"seperation" are the transcription's. THE spine quote for the soldier's-farewell angle — the treaty chapter does not use it.)
- **Fraunces Tavern, 4 December 1783 — nine days after Evacuation Day; Tallmadge badged (shared with the treaty pack — keep wording consistent).** In the Long Room, Washington took leave of his officers. **The only firsthand account is Col. Benjamin Tallmadge's memoir, written in 1830, forty-seven years later — badge it.** Tallmadge's telling: Washington, filling a glass: *"With a heart full of love and gratitude I now take leave of you. I most devoutly wish that your latter days may be as prosperous and happy as your former ones have been glorious and honorable."* Knox stepped forward first; Washington, *"suffused in tears,"* embraced him in silence, then every officer in the room, one by one; *"Not a word was uttered to break the solemn silence."* Then he walked to the Whitehall wharf between ranks of his men, boarded the barge, and waved his hat. (HIGH that this is Tallmadge's 1830 text and quotes are verbatim FROM IT — present as his recollection; matches the treaty pack exactly. SPINE camera: the men in the room — most had been with him since 1775–76; the Book-of-Negroes echo of the same building stays in §7, don't belabor it twice.)
- **The journey south — the country says goodbye town by town, one beat.** From New York to Annapolis the road became a procession: addresses and replies, escorts of light horse, bells and bonfires at New Brunswick, Trenton, **Philadelphia (arrived 8 December)**, Wilmington, Baltimore. In Philadelphia he did one piece of unfinished business: **settled his wartime expense accounts with the comptroller (13 December 1783)** — he had taken no salary for eight and a half years, only expenses, and he itemized them to the end. He reached **Annapolis on 19 December**; Congress gave him a public dinner and ball on **22 December** (thirteen toasts, thirteen guns). (HIGH on the route/dates/accounts — LOC financial papers, USNI/Maryland accounts; the no-salary-expenses-only arrangement HIGH — it dates from his 1775 acceptance. Keep the honors to one beat — no pageant inventory.)
- **Annapolis, 23 December 1783, around noon — THE SOLDIER'S-FAREWELL ANGLE ONLY.** In the Old Senate Chamber of the Maryland State House, before Congress, Washington — hand trembling, steadying the paper with both hands at the passage about his officers (McHenry's same-day letter; the treaty pack carries the full McHenry quote — this chapter may reference the trembling hands but should NOT reuse the McHenry quotation) — read his nine sentences and handed over the commission Congress had voted him in June 1775 (`outbreak`, §6). The verified close, verbatim (shared with the treaty pack — fine per the brief): *"Having now finished the work assigned me, I retire from the great theatre of Action—and bidding an Affectionate farewell to this August body under whose orders I have so long acted, I here offer my Commission, and take my leave of all the employments of public life."* (HIGH — founders/Maryland State Archives.) **THIS chapter's meaning-beat is the ARMY'S story ending — the commander handing the war back to the civil power that had created him commander, eight and a half years and three days after he accepted; the army that began as militia on a green now formally out of existence, its general a private citizen on the road home.** The republican-precedent meaning, Mifflin's irony, and the George III "greatest man" story belong to `treaty-of-paris` — DO NOT TAKE THEM. He left the chamber, mounted, and **reached Mount Vernon on Christmas Eve** — home for Christmas for the first time since the war began. (HIGH)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **Don't duplicate the treaty theme — the standing integration check.** That chapter (same batch) owns: London's turn and North's fall; the commissioners and the negotiation; the articles; Vergennes's protest and Franklin's letter; the who-paid ledger (Loyalist totals, Native silence, Article 7's afterlife, the dead); the West unfinished-painting story; the greased-flagpole-as-memory-culture framing; the McHenry "spectators all wept" quote; Mifflin's Valley-Forge irony; the George III "greatest man" story; the republican-precedent meaning of Annapolis. THIS chapter never states a treaty term, never names Oswald/Jay/the articles, and takes Annapolis only as the army's story ending. At integrate time, read the treaty FINAL and verify zero beat-level overlap beyond the deliberately shared facts (dates, the resignation close quote, the Tallmadge handling).
2. **The Asgill affair's two layers.** The arc (Huddy 12 Apr → lot 27 May → Vergennes 29 Jul → Congress early Nov → home by Christmas) is HIGH. The TEXTURE is contested: the traditional account of comfortable captivity and Washington-the-rescuer is challenged by Asgill's own 1786 account (close confinement, ill-treatment) surfaced by Anne Ammundsen (2019–2023). Present the captivity conditions only as "Asgill later said"; present Washington as boxed in by his own retaliation order (which targeted a treaty-protected prisoner — say so plainly), not as the quiet hero. Philip White's death and Huddy's guilt are propaganda-fogged in both directions — don't adjudicate either.
3. **Tradition badges are mandatory on:** the greased flagpole / Van Arsdale climb (centennial family telling, 1883); McGary's "Them that ain't cowards, follow me" and Boone's "We are all slaughtered men" (frontier tradition, wording varies); the polished Newburgh spectacles sentence (eyewitness-derived, wording wobbles — match the shipped army chapter's badge verbatim-handling); the Evacuation Day "ill-clad and weather-beaten" reminiscence (recorded ~1850s); Tallmadge's Fraunces Tavern quotes (1830 memoir, 47 years on). None of these may appear as clean contemporary quotation.
4. **Blue Licks numbers and the Boone beat.** Killed: roughly **70–77 of ~182** (about 40 percent) — give the range, never a single hard number; the treaty pack's "about 70" is consistent. Israel Boone's death beside his father is HIGH; his age (23), his sickness, his refusal to leave — family-transmitted, badge "by the family's accounts." Boone warned against the crossing: HIGH. "Last battle of the Revolution": say "among the last" (Clark's November 1782 raid follows).
5. **Gnadenhutten discipline.** ONE sentence, the verified register only: Pennsylvania militia under Williamson; 96 unarmed Moravian Delaware men, women, and children; no one punished. NO breakdown numbers (28/29/39 is MED and reserved to the native-nations chapter), no scene detail — the theme owns the story. The spine's only framing: it happened five months AFTER Yorktown.
6. **No elegy, no triumphalism, no Washington deification.** The chapter's emotional register comes from verified concrete beats (the slip of paper, the spectacles, the ledger at the tavern, the trembling hands), never from editorial swelling. Washington in this chapter: warns against complacency (verified), orders a protected prisoner hanged by lot (verified), nearly loses his army to a coup mood (verified), gives the honors system to enlisted men (verified), cries at a goodbye (memoir-badged), hands the commission back (verified). The full man, not the marble one.
7. **The ceasefire anniversary (19 April 1783) is a calendar fact, not a claim about intent.** The orders of 18 April set the proclamation for noon the next day; nothing verified says Washington chose the date for the Lexington echo. Let the date sit; don't write "he chose."
8. **Numbers as ranges, sourced to the sibling chapters where they own them:** Loyalists to Nova Scotia ~30,000 (civil-war chapter), total exodus ~60,000 (treaty/civil-war), Book of Negroes ~3,000 (slavery), Charleston carried-off enslaved 5,000+ (slavery/treaty), Blue Licks ~70–77 of ~182, Gibraltar batteries 10 / ~5,260 aboard / allied losses ~1,400–1,500 / spectators "tens of thousands (by some accounts 80,000)" (the 80,000 MED), Saintes ~5 ships taken/destroyed, Evacuation Day column ~800 (MED — "a few hundred" safe), Asgill's age "nineteen or twenty."
9. **Both sides as reasoning actors, to the end.** Carleton winds the war down honorably (his Lippincott court-martial and his Book-of-Negroes stand are both on the record); Lippincott's acquittal followed the orders-defense, the Associated Loyalists' command responsibility is the honest framing; the Kentucky militia walked into Blue Licks over their best scout's warning — hot blood, not stupidity-cartoon; the floating-battery engineers genuinely believed they'd solved fire. No villains-of-convenience.
10. **Stay in the spine's lane on the global war.** The Saintes and Gibraltar get one beat and one scene respectively, as FELT events of 1782 (the Yorktown admiral captured; the Rock not falling under the eyes of 80,000) — the strategic consequences for the peace table belong to `treaty-of-paris`, the naval doctrine to `war-at-sea` (no pill route to it from this chapter's map — keep the beat self-contained).
11. **The close (§9) must not announce its own meaning.** "From a green at dawn to a commission handed back" is the architecture, not a sentence to print. No "and so the Revolution ended," no "a new nation was born," no narrating the symmetry — assemble the verified images and stop.
