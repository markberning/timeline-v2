## FACT PACK BLIND SPOTS

1. **Francis Porcher / "Resources of the Southern Fields and Forests" (1863)**: The fact pack mentions native-plant substitutes generically (dogwood, willow, other barks) but does not flag the Porcher reference — the Confederate Surgeon General actually commissioned a botanist to produce a full pharmacopeia of Southern medicinal plants. This is a notable omission from the pack. However, because it is a specialized depth item (not an essential beat), it belongs in SHOULD-CONSIDER rather than MUST-ADD for the draft.

2. **Prosthetics / artificial limb industry**: The fact pack is entirely silent on the post-amputation story. An estimated 30,000–60,000 amputees needed artificial limbs; Congress established a federal program to supply them to Union veterans ($75 per leg, $50 per arm); between 1861–1873 the Patent Office issued 133 new prosthetic limb patents (vs. 34 in the prior 16 years); the war birthed the U.S. prosthetics industry. Confederate veterans were ineligible for federal subsidies, though some Southern states funded their own programs. J.E. Hanger (Confederate amputee who became a prosthetics manufacturer) is a notable story. This is a factual domain the pack simply does not include. The draft inherits that silence. Whether prosthetics belongs here vs. a "Veterans" section is a scoping call, but the pack should flag it.

3. **"Soldier's heart" / psychological trauma**: The pack does not mention the period diagnosis of "nostalgia" (the 19th-century category for homesickness/despair severe enough to kill), "soldier's heart" / "irritable heart" (rapid pulse, anxiety, trouble breathing — proto-PTSD framed as a physical cardiovascular complaint), or the war's role as an early chapter in military mental health history. This is a real gap in the pack's scope definition — it chose not to include it, but a comprehensiveness critic should note its absence.

4. **Hospital trains and ships**: The pack mentions the three-tier evacuation system but does not note that rail and river/sea transport became its own innovation category — improvised boxcars evolved into purpose-built hospital railcars and steamships. A minor gap; not essential for this narrative.

5. **8% mortality rate statistic for late-war pavilion hospitals**: The fact pack notes these hospitals had "surprisingly low death rates" but does not give the 8% figure that multiple sources cite. Not critical, but the draft's "surprisingly low mortality" could be made more concrete.

## MUST-ADD

*Essential omissions — items a comprehensive section on Civil War medicine must address.*

**None that the draft is responsible for.** Every item on the core checklist is COVERED and often covered well. The two gaps below trace to the fact pack's scope, not authorial omission.

However, two items should be flagged for the author/editor to make a deliberate scoping decision:

**MUST-ADD (scope decision needed — new facts require web verification):**

1. **Prosthetics / artificial limb program.** After the amputation section, the draft goes silent on what happened next to the 30,000–60,000+ amputees. Congress created a federal supply program for Union veterans (standardized pricing, approved manufacturers, ~$75 per leg); the Patent Office logged 133 new prosthetic patents 1861–1873 (vs. 34 in prior 16 years); the war essentially founded the U.S. prosthetics industry. Confederate veterans were cut out of federal benefits, though some states created their own programs. This is the direct consequence of the amputation story the draft tells so well, and its absence is the most important gap. *New facts — web-verify before drafting.* (Sources: National Museum of Civil War Medicine "After the Amputation"; ASME Civil War prosthetics; NLM "Life and Limb" exhibition.)

   **NOTE on scope:** If a "Veterans" section exists in the war vertical, prosthetics may belong there. But if not, it belongs here, immediately following the amputation beat.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER

*Good-to-have depth items; not essential to the core story.*

1. **"Soldier's heart" / nostalgia (proto-PTSD)**. The period diagnoses of "nostalgia" (despair/homesickness severe enough to cause death) and "irritable heart" / "soldier's heart" (cardiovascular framing of what was likely trauma-linked anxiety) were real medical categories during the war. The draft does not address psychological casualties at all. This is not a fatal gap — it is a legitimate scoping choice — but it would deepen the "how they actually died and suffered" frame. A brief paragraph after the disease killers section would serve it. *Requires web-verification of period case statistics before drafting.*

2. **Francis Porcher and "Resources of the Southern Fields and Forests" (1863)**. The Confederate section mentions native-plant substitutes but does not name the Porcher compendium — a botanist-surgeon commissioned by Moore to produce a full Southern medicinal plant pharmacopeia, drawing on Native American and enslaved African healing traditions. Naming the book and its origins adds texture to the "improvising under the blockade" beat. The modern angle (these Civil War plant remedies are being tested against drug-resistant bacteria) is a nice kicker if space allows. *Fact-pack gap — verify against primary sources.*

3. **8% late-war hospital mortality figure**. The draft's "surprisingly low death rates" claim for the pavilion hospitals could be made more concrete. The figure of ~8% mortality cited in contemporary army reporting for well-run late-war general hospitals is a striking contrast to what soldiers might have expected. A single sentence would suffice.

4. **Hospital trains and river steamers**. The three-tier Letterman system relied on rail and river transport as the connective tissue between tiers 2 and 3 — improvised boxcars evolved into purpose-built hospital railcars; steamships served the coastal and river theaters. A one-sentence nod acknowledges the logistical innovation without requiring a full beat.

5. **The Sanitary Commission's data legacy more specifically**. The draft mentions the Commission "helped seed the methods of modern public health and medical research" but could be slightly more concrete: Olmsted's hospital directories (tracking units, ranks, demographic data) were early casualty-data systems that foreshadow modern medical record-keeping and vital statistics. A small sharpening, not a new beat.
