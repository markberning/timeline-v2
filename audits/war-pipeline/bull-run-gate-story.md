# Storytelling Critic Gate — First Bull Run
**Gate: CRAFT ONLY (accuracy is a separate gate)**
Date: 2026-05-30

---

## Overall Grade: GOOD (with one NEEDS WORK section)

The draft has genuine strengths — a strong opening section, several wry voice moments, and good structural instincts (the "Meanwhile" sidebars earn their place). The fact-ledger discipline is visible and the draft doesn't tip into dry recitation as often as first-battle sections usually do. But the middle two sections sag. The flank march plays as a competent military briefing rather than a story, and the stone-wall section is the draft's biggest missed opportunity: it has the most dramatic raw material in the entire battle and delivers it at the pace of a Wikipedia summary. The rout section recovers nicely. No section is a REWRITE, but "the-flank-march" and "stone-wall" need focused revision to match the voice the opening establishes.

---

## Section Grades

### 1. on-to-richmond | STRONG

**Hook & stakes:** Excellent. The 90-day enlistment framing is the right key to unlock this section — it immediately gives the reader a clock and a deadline, which is the correct emotional engine. Opening with "a remarkable number of Americans believed the war... would be over by autumn, and that they would win it in an afternoon" is a strong statement of the section's thesis.

**Human dimension:** The blackberry-pickers detail is exactly right — it tells you who these soldiers are without editorializing. The picnic-basket senators work the same way. Both feel observed rather than told.

**Play-by-play tedium:** Not a problem here. The section is thematic, not chronological, so there is no brigade-by-brigade trap to fall into.

**Voice:** The best lines in the draft are here. "Nobody enlists for ninety days if they think they're starting a four-year war" is a perfect sentence — punchy, wry, and true. The *New York Tribune* drumbeat framing ("printed day after day like a drumbeat") is propulsive. The "wall" metaphor for the pressure to attack lands.

**Pacing:** Moves well. The "Meanwhile in Washington" sidebar is mostly redundant with the main text (it recaps what the section has already established about carnival certainty), which is the one pacing problem — the sidebar ends up restating rather than adding.

**Ending:** The section ends on a strong beat: "The men riding out with their opera glasses were about to be the first Northern civilians to learn, in person, what they had been cheering for." Clean and earned.

**One fix:** The "Meanwhile in Washington" sidebar covers ground the main section has already covered (picnickers, certainty of victory, high-water mark of innocence). The sidebar is stronger when it adds something the main text doesn't — consider pivoting it to what Washington *sounds like* that morning: the rumor mill, the toasts in hotel bars, the specific flavor of certainty that only people who have never seen a battlefield can feel. That gives the sidebar a different emotional register from the main text instead of echoing it.

### 2. the-flank-march | NEEDS WORK

**Hook & stakes:** The opening is the section's biggest craft problem. "McDowell's plan was genuinely good. That's the cruel part of First Bull Run: it was not a stupid plan badly conceived." This is the draft telling the reader what to feel before showing them why. The voice bar (Antietam's "Then he sat on it" for McClellan) demonstrates a better approach: show the smart plan briefly, get into the action, and let the reader feel the cruel irony themselves. The explicit statement of theme before dramatization is a trust problem — it signals the writer is worried the reader won't get there on their own.

**Human dimension:** The wig-wag signal is the section's best human beat (a sharp-eyed officer, glinting bayonets, a message that changed the day). But the person who sent it — Captain Edward Porter Alexander — is named and then immediately depersonalized back into a mechanism. The draft gives him the signal and moves on. Alexander's wig-wag moment is actually famous precisely because of the human acuity it required: he saw sun glinting off bayonets miles away and worked out what it meant. That is a story beat, not a piece of logistics.

**Play-by-play tedium:** The "Morning, July 21 / A punch and a hook" section is the draft's most list-like passage. It introduces Tyler, Hunter, Heintzelman, the Stone Bridge, the feint, the ford, Sudley Springs, and the flanking concept in quick succession. Each item is explained competently but none of them accumulates emotional weight. The reader gets the tactical picture but not the feeling of being in it. Compare to Antietam's Cornfield section: "The corn was tall and ripe, taller than a man, and it hid soldiers until they were close enough to kill each other in the face." One image, kinetic, visceral. The Bull Run flank march has no equivalent image — just the plan's geometry.

**Voice:** Wry voice mostly disappears in this section. There are technically correct sentences ("a wide arc to the north and west to hit the Confederate flank, the exposed end of an army's line where it can be rolled up sideways like a rug") but they feel like textbook writing. The voice that made the opening section work is absent.

**Pacing:** The section has a pacing problem unique to battle narratives: the "Meanwhile in the Shenandoah Valley" sidebar (Johnston's railroad move) is placed AFTER the Matthews Hill fight ends. But Johnston's men are already stepping off trains while McDowell's column is still marching to Sudley Springs — the railroad-as-loaded-gun stakes should land BEFORE "the hour it looked won," not after, so the reader watches the Confederate reinforcements loading onto trains while watching the Union army come close to victory. The emotional effect of "Johnston's men are already riding the rails RIGHT NOW as you read this flanking success" is exactly the dread this section needs.

**Ending:** "McDowell's plan had a fatal flaw, and it ran on a railroad timetable" is a good closing line for the sidebar. But the main section's ending ("It was the high point of the day for the United States, and it was about to become the turning point against it") is flat — it states the turn rather than letting the reader feel it. The Antietam draft uses italics for the gut-punch turning-point moments: "Then he sat on it." / "And then, out of the south, came the dust of marching men." Find the equivalent one-sentence gut-punch here.

**Concrete beat to add:** The flanking column's march is described as delayed but never felt. What did two hours on bad roads in July heat actually look like? Men falling out, stopping at streams, officers cursing, the column accordioning — stretching and bunching — because nobody had drilled them for long marches. The march's failure isn't just a clock: it's a visible collapse of discipline. One paragraph of this, with a single specific image, would make the reader understand why the delay happened instead of just knowing that it did.

### 3. stone-wall | NEEDS WORK (closest to REWRITE of any section)

This section has the richest material in the draft — Jackson's stand, the stone-wall quote, Judith Henry's death, the batteries changing hands, the rebel yell — and it is the section most in need of revision. The problem is not facts; it is pace and emotional architecture. The section moves from beat to beat at the same speed for everything, so the stone-wall moment (the most famous moment of the battle) does not feel more significant than the gun battery confusion.

**Hook & stakes:** "Henry House Hill is where First Bull Run stopped being a Union victory and started being a Confederate one." This is accurate but underpowered as an opening sentence. The reader has already been told (in the previous section's ending) that the morning looked like a Union victory. The section needs an opening that creates dread and anticipation, not a thesis statement.

**Human dimension:** The Bee/Jackson moment is handled well — the ambiguity about Bee's meaning is one of the draft's best passages, and the detail that Bee was mortally wounded minutes later and never explained himself is genuinely affecting. This is the section's strongest passage and should serve as the model for the rest.

Judith Henry's death is the detail that should stop the reader cold — the first civilian killed in the war, dying in her own bedroom because artillery thought sharpshooters were firing from her house, while a battle that she had no part in consumed the hill that bore her name. The draft gives this about 100 words, in the same declarative register as everything around it, and moves on. It deserves more weight. Judith Henry is not a footnote — she is what this section is "about" below the military narrative, the same way the Dunker Church is what the Antietam Cornfield section is about. Compare how the Dunker Church passage handles its irony: "There is no way to make that irony gentle." The draft never gives Judith Henry that sentence.

**Play-by-play tedium:** The "trains turn the tide" subsection is the section's weakest passage. It introduces Ricketts, Griffin, the 33rd Virginia, Kirby Smith, Elzey, Early, and Chinn Ridge in about 200 words, in a sequence of actions rather than a story. The reader processes the information but doesn't feel it. This is classic play-by-play accumulation — the names and unit identifiers become noise. The actual story is: the Union army was winning, and exhaustion + fresh Confederate troops delivered by train broke it. The mechanism is the interesting part; the specific brigade names are not.

**Voice:** The section's most voiceless passage: "Union artillery batteries — the battery being a cluster of cannon, here about 11 guns under captains James Ricketts (North) and Charles Griffin (North) — were pushed forward onto the hill, and the fighting got close and confused. At one point a Confederate regiment in blue uniforms, the 33rd Virginia, advanced on the Union guns; Griffin took them for friendly troops and held his fire, and the Confederates overran and captured both batteries." This is structurally correct but reads like a report. The 33rd Virginia's blue-uniform moment is actually one of the most striking tactical details in the battle — it is the war's early chaos rendered concrete, men killing each other while dressed alike. The draft treats it as a logistics note.

**Pacing:** The rebel yell passage is sandwiched between two pieces of dry tactical summary (Elzey's charge and the aftermath), which kills its impact. The yell is a sensory event; it needs to land like one. The Antietam reference for how to do this: "And then, out of the south, came the dust of marching men." — one short sentence, on its own line, purely kinetic. The yell passage in this draft is paragraph 3 of an operational summary.

**Ending:** The "Meanwhile in the Henry house" sidebar is weak because it doesn't add information — it recaps what the main section already said (spectators, certainty curdling, the gap between the picnic and the war). The "Meanwhile" format earns its place when it adds a perspective the main text can't have — Lincoln in Washington, Johnston loading troops onto trains. A sidebar from Judith Henry's perspective, or from a spectator who has just realized what is happening on the hill, would add what the main text can't.

**Concrete beats to add:**
1. The rebel yell needs one sentence of pure sensation before the analysis: what it actually sounded like to be standing in front of it — not "somewhere between a scream and a howl" (which is accurate but abstract) but what it did to the body of the man hearing it. The coordinator should source this from firsthand accounts.
2. Judith Henry needs a closer: the contrast between her name on a hill that the whole country would learn and the fact that she was trying to die quietly in her own bed. That is the section's real gut-punch and the draft buries it.
3. The 33rd Virginia / blue uniforms moment needs one sentence that makes the reader feel the chaos, not just understand it.

### 4. the-rout | GOOD

**Hook & stakes:** "A battle that has gone one way for six hours can change in twenty minutes" is a strong opener — it immediately frames the rout's dramatic speed and creates anticipation.

**Human dimension:** Alfred Ely is the section's best beat, and the draft uses him well. The specific detail of how he identified himself ("the Honorable Alfred Ely, Representative in Congress from New York") and the irony of his reaching Richmond as a prisoner rather than a conqueror is exactly the kind of human-scale reversal the whole battle's theme calls for. This is the section's peak.

The collision of the fleeing army and the picnickers on the same narrow road is also well executed — it fulfills the promise made in the opening section (the spectators will learn in person what they came to watch) without over-explaining the irony.

**Play-by-play tedium:** Not a problem here. The section moves at the pace of a rout, which is right.

**Voice:** Strong. "The Southern press gleefully named it The Great Skedaddle" is a good wry beat. The Confederate army being "nearly as wrecked by victory as the losing army was by defeat" is a clean, smart formulation.

**Pacing:** The George Templeton Strong quote lands. The pivot to Lincoln's three-year volunteer authorization is slightly rushed — the single most important consequence of Bull Run (the formal acknowledgment that this would be a long war) is given about two sentences. But this is a minor pacing issue, not a structural problem.

**Ending:** Two endings compete here, and neither fully lands. The final sentence of the Strong quote section ("the four-year war that was actually coming finally started to feel real") is soft — it tells rather than shows the morning-after reckoning. The closing italic sentence ("There stood Jackson like a stone wall — and Stonewall he would be until the day he died") is a better option for an ending, but it caps the whole section on Jackson rather than on the war's transformation. Consider whether the section should end on Lincoln's three-year authorization — the legal act that formally admitted the 90-day illusion was dead — as the final beat, because that is the direct answer to the "90-day war" thread the opening section established.

**One fix:** The "Meanwhile in the North | The morning the long war began" sidebar is the most redundant sidebar in the draft. The main text has already told this story (Strong's diary, the authorization, McClellan's appointment). The sidebar should either go deeper into a specific perspective (Strong's full diary entry is reportedly devastating — if there is a longer quote available, use it) or be cut. As written it restates.

## Cross-Section Findings

**The "Meanwhile" sidebar problem, pattern-level:** Four of the five sidebars restate material the main text has already covered. The sidebars earn their place in the Antietam draft when they go somewhere the main narrator cannot — Washington while the battle happens, the diplomatic consequences the field can't see. In this draft, three sidebars (on-to-richmond Washington, stone-wall Henry house, the-rout North) close beats the main text has already closed. The pattern fix: every sidebar should add a perspective or fact the main section does not contain.

**The quote format:** The draft uses the blockquote format for two voice-of-record moments (Strong's diary, the "FORWARD TO RICHMOND" framing) and for the Bee/Jackson line. The Antietam draft uses `q: true` for blockquotes and `i: true` for italicized gut-punch turning-point sentences. The draft should consistently reserve blockquotes for documented historical voices and italics for the narrator's one-line gut-punches. The italic sentence in the flank-march section ("It was the high point of the day for the United States, and it was about to become the turning point against it") is an attempt at this technique but is too long and too on-the-nose. The Antietam models ("Then he sat on it." / "The North kept sending men into the same field, one corps after another, and the field kept handing the men back broken." / "And then, out of the south, came the dust of marching men.") succeed because they are brief, kinetic, and trust the reader.

**The highest-impact fix across the whole draft:** The stone-wall section needs to treat Judith Henry's death the way the Antietam draft treats the Dunker Church — as the section's moral center, the image that makes the whole day feel like something more than military operations. She is the civilian cost, the first one, given a name by the hill she had lived on long before the armies arrived. The section has all the facts; it needs one paragraph that slows down, stays with her, and says the thing that cannot be made gentle.

---

## Summary

**Overall grade: GOOD.** The draft has a strong opening section, a serviceable rout section, and good structural bones. The weakest section is stone-wall (too flat at its most dramatic moments, Judith Henry underplayed, too much brigade-logistics in the trains passage). The highest-impact single fix is in stone-wall: give Judith Henry a passage that matches the Dunker Church treatment in the Antietam draft — slow down, name the irony, and let it land.
