## Section 1 · fight-for-missouri

**Grade: NEEDS WORK**

### Hook & Stakes

The opening paragraph is good: Lyon's introduction as "short, red-bearded, perpetually furious" lands, and "start a war before anyone gave him permission to" is exactly the wry entry the voice calls for. The stakes (Missouri as "keystone," the river logistics) are stated clearly in the first sub-section. That's the high point.

The problem is that the hook pays the energy forward into sub-section headers that feel like a Wikipedia table of contents: "The border state / A keystone Lincoln could not lose," "Camp Jackson / The capture and the blood in the streets," "The Planters' House / Lyon chooses war," "The chase / Run them into the corner." Each sub-section re-states the thing that is about to happen in the header, then tells it in the body. The reader is told the reveal twice, at half-speed. Antietam never does this: "The three cigars" doesn't tell you what the cigars mean before you read about them; "And then, out of the south, came the dust of marching men" withholds just enough to propel. fight-for-missouri front-loads resolution into every header, which deadens the propulsive pull through the section.

**Fix as BEAT:** The headers should tease or orient, not summarize. "Camp Jackson" is fine as a header — it does not give away the riot. "The capture and the blood in the streets" in the sub-header flattens the drama before it starts. Recommend stripping all double-header spoilers back to single evocative labels. This is a structural revision the coordinator can make to the existing text without inventing new facts.

### Human Dimension — Lyon

Lyon is described but not felt. We get his physical description and his professional profile ("start a war before anyone gave him permission to") — that is good — but the Camp Jackson and Planter's House scenes run on abstracted decisions rather than on the weight of the man making them. The Camp Jackson disguise anecdote ("is said to have scouted the camp personally while disguised as an old blind woman") is the single richest human detail in the section. It is handled correctly — flagged as colorful-but-sourced — and then dropped and forgotten. It gets one sentence and the story moves on. The Antietam voice bar would milk that detail for two sentences more: the image of this furious little Regular Army man in a carriage playing the role long enough to count cannon. Even the uncertainty ("an espionage detail almost too good to be true and one the sources hand down anyway") is good copy that earns more than one sentence.

**Fix as BEAT:** Give the disguise detail another clause of breath. Let the reader sit one extra moment in the image of Lyon, spectacles on, carriage jolting, counting captured Confederate guns — then let that build to the decision on May 10. The detail is already there; it needs permission to expand.

The Planter's House confrontation is the dramatic center of this section — Lyon walking out of a four-hour peace meeting and declaring war on his own authority — but it is handled almost entirely as a quote-and-attribution problem rather than a scene. The draft is correct to flag both quote versions and to state that the substance (the ultimatum) is solid. But the emotional architecture around the moment is thin: we are told that "Lyon would not have it" and "Lyon stood up, told Jackson and Price they were now enemies, and sent them out the door." That is accurate and bloodless. There is no felt sense of the audacity: a brigadier general, newly promoted, essentially deciding that his country is at war without waiting for his country to decide. The Antietam voice handles McClellan's mirror-image failure — "He sat on it" — with a single italicized kicker that punches far above its word count. The Planter's House scene needs a kicker that crystallizes Lyon's version of the same moment.

**Fix as BEAT:** Close the Planter's House sub-section with a kicker sentence that captures the audacity of what Lyon just did — not a new fact, just the right final beat on what is already here. Something in the register of the "it was the decision of a man at the end of his rope who had decided to swing rather than fall" line that closes the fight-for-missouri section (itself the best sentence in the draft). That register should arrive earlier, not just at the very end.

### Play-by-Play Tedium

The "The chase" sub-section is the closest this section comes to list-mode. Lyon seized Jefferson City, chased Jackson at Boonville, Sigel went to Rolla and got bloodied at Carthage. Three events, paragraph form, but running at the same tempo without a differentiating detail on any of them. Boonville is the one Civil War engagement where a Union general literally ran a Confederate governor off a battlefield — that specific image (Jackson fleeing the capital city he claimed to govern) is the right detail to slow down on. Instead the draft gives equal weight to Boonville, Carthage, and the arithmetic of 7,000 men at Springfield.

**Fix as BEAT:** Collapse the Carthage mention to a clause ("Sigel ran into a larger force and got bloodied in a fighting retreat at Carthage on July 5 — a foreshadowing of what his impetuousness would cost him in six weeks" — the coordinator can verify if this characterization is fair to the fact pack) and give the Boonville image one concrete sentence: Jackson's government, chased off the capital of the state it claimed to govern. That differentiates the chase's end without adding length.

### Voice

Mostly strong. "It was the decision of a man at the end of his rope who had decided to swing rather than fall" is the best sentence in the draft and belongs in the final product. The "Meanwhile" sidebar on the two rival governments is well-written — crisp, clear stakes. The pull-quote handling for "This means war" is correctly cautious and readable.

Two voice slips:
1. "A border state was a slave state that stayed in the Union — and Missouri was the biggest prize of them all." The sub-section header already said "A keystone Lincoln could not lose." Opening the first full paragraph by re-defining a term the reader just got in the header ("Start with why Missouri mattered, because nothing else in this story makes sense without it") is over-managing the reader. Trust them.
2. "Here is what Lyon decided to do" in the bloody-hill section (same formula appearing there). The phrase is a tic. It signals the writer orienting rather than the story moving.

**Fix as BEAT:** Cut "Start with why Missouri mattered, because nothing else in this story makes sense without it" — that work is already done by the opening paragraph and the section framing. Trust the structure.

### Pacing

The section is the longest of the three and bears the most scene-setting work, which is appropriate. Pacing is generally good except for the "End of the tether" sub-section, which is excellent — the math of Lyon's situation (shrinking supplies, expiring enlistments, Frémont refusing him reinforcements, a larger army sitting on his doorstep) lands with real weight. The final sentence of that sub-section ("It was the decision of a man at the end of his rope...") is where the section reaches its storytelling peak. The problem is that this peak comes at the very end of a long wind-up, and nothing earlier in the section rises close to this level of craft.

### Ending

The "Meanwhile" sidebar ("A state with two governments forming") is correctly placed and gives the reader the political stakes that underpin everything that follows. Its final sentence ("The battle about to be fought along Wilson's Creek was, underneath the gunfire, a fight over which of these two governments got to keep the state") is strong. No notes on the ending.

## Section 2 · bloody-hill

**Grade: GOOD**

### Hook & Stakes

The opening paragraph is the best-constructed in the draft. "Lyon's plan was the kind of plan you only make when you've run out of safe ones" — that is the Antietam register, right on pitch. "The textbook answer... Lyon did the opposite" gives the tactical logic quickly. The structural symmetry at the end — "audacious, reckless, and for a few hours on the morning of August 10, 1861, it very nearly worked" — earns its comma-triplet. This is how the section should read throughout.

### Human Dimension

The gray-uniform catastrophe sub-section is the best piece of storytelling in the full draft. The setup (both sides dressed in gray, the 3rd Louisiana walking to within 40 yards), the hold-fire, the annihilating volley, Sigel hiding in a cornfield with one orderly — these land as a sequence with real physical texture. "One mistaken glance at a gray coat had erased half of Lyon's army from the battle" is a proper kicker, exactly the right weight. The Antietam voice ("He let the day pass") proves one sentence can do enormous structural work when placed right, and this section earns several of those moments.

The Woodruff quote ("obliged to give direction to their pieces by the flash") is correctly used — vivid, specific, period-appropriate.

The command-friction sub-section ("McCulloch held a Confederate commission; Price held only a Missouri State Guard commission") is clear and informative, but it runs slightly long for what it needs to establish. The key beat — the two halves of the Confederate army never quite becoming one — is said twice: once in the body ("the result was that the larger force kept hammering the hill in pieces") and once in the italic kicker ("Lyon had split his army on purpose and was outnumbered two to one; his enemy had twice his men and split itself by accident"). The kicker is sharp and should stay. The body paragraph can tighten.

**Fix as BEAT:** The command-friction paragraph can drop its last two sentences ("The result was that the larger force kept hammering the hill in pieces rather than landing one coordinated, decisive blow" + the parenthetical about the italic kicker position) since the italic kicker says it better. Cut the body summary; keep the kicker.

### Play-by-Play Tedium

The "The hill that earned its name / Wave after wave" sub-section is where this section comes nearest to play-by-play mode. "Three great surging assaults up the slope... Missourians climbing into the muzzles of Lyon's line, formations three and four ranks deep firing while prone, kneeling, and standing all at once, the fighting closing to shotgun range and at one point to within twenty feet of the Union cannon." This is good tactical texture — specific and grounded — but it runs through the three assaults as an undifferentiated block. We never hear anything individual from the Union side in this sub-section at all: Lyon is absent until the next section, no Union officer is named or felt here, and the Confederates are represented only by Price (organizing) and Woodruff (quote). The sub-section gives the terrain and the physics of the fight without a human witness from the side that is holding.

This is a harder structural note: the Antietam voice solves this problem routinely by dropping into one person's experience to anchor a larger action (Hood's furious, hungry division; Father Corby giving absolution to the Irish Brigade). The bloody-hill section's middle has no equivalent anchor on the Union side. The coordinator would need to identify a specific Union-side figure or moment from the fact pack to provide one — but the section would be stronger for it.

**Fix as BEAT:** Identify one Union voice from the three assaults — an officer's account, a soldier's letter, any attributed observation from the hill itself — that can do what the Woodruff quote does for the Confederate side. One sentence would suffice to give the reader a human anchor on the Union side of the uphill grind. (Source this from the fact pack; do not invent.)

### Voice

Mostly strong and consistent with the Antietam bar. "The plan was actually Sigel's idea, not Lyon's, and other officers objected to dividing a force this small in front of an enemy this large. Lyon took it anyway, partly because Sigel was a popular German-born officer whose standing made him politically awkward to overrule" — this is good: wry, specific, lets the reader feel the institutional calculus without editorializing. The "Here is what Lyon decided to do" opener of the plan sub-section is the same tic noted above; it can go.

One voice slip: "A quirk of the terrain — the steep creek valley seems to have swallowed the sound" uses a dash where the house style calls for a parenthetical or comma construction (WRITING-RULES: use commas and parentheses, not em-dashes). Minor; easy fix.

### Pacing

Correct throughout. The section moves from plan to surprise to catastrophe to grind in the right order, each sub-section doing one job. The "Meanwhile" sidebar on the two-headed Confederate command is positioned correctly and is well-written.

### Ending

The italic kicker ("Lyon had split his army on purpose and was outnumbered two to one; his enemy had twice his men and split itself by accident") is sharp and earns its place. No notes on the ending.

## Section 3 · death-of-lyon

**Grade: NEEDS WORK**

### Hook & Stakes

The opening paragraph works hard and mostly succeeds. "Shot in the leg and grazed across the head, and his horse had been killed under him. One observer remembered him as masked in blood from his earlier wounds." The cumulative detail is right. "He did the only thing he knew how to do" lands correctly as a character summary.

The stakes, however, go missing in the second half of this section. After Lyon dies, the draft shifts from battle narrative to logistics, statistics, and history — and it never re-establishes what is emotionally at stake for the reader. "Sturgis pulls the army out" is an accurate account of what happened next, but the section's emotional center has just died on a hillside and the prose does not grieve it for even one sentence before moving to orderly withdrawal procedures. Antietam handles the death of a battle's meaning by returning always to the human cost ("The dead in those frames were somebody's son, and now everyone could see it") before moving to consequence. death-of-lyon does not have an equivalent pivot.

**Fix as BEAT:** Between the Lyon death paragraph and the Sturgis withdrawal paragraph, insert a single transitional beat — not a new fact, just the right tonal marker — that acknowledges Lyon's death before the story resumes its forward movement. Something in the register of: the battle continued without him, as battles do, and Sturgis faced a very different problem than Lyon had. The coordinator can write this in one sentence without new sourcing.

### Human Dimension — The Death Scene Itself

The death of Lyon is the first Union general killed in combat in the Civil War. The draft handles the act of death correctly — "a bullet struck him in the chest, through the heart, and he died almost at once" is clean and unsentimentalized — but the scene around the moment is thin. Two very specific details are available: (1) Lyon was wearing "an old captain's coat rather than a general's uniform" when he died — a detail that is in the draft but is introduced two paragraphs earlier and not present at the death beat itself; (2) his body was laid out on the Ray family's bed while the same house was being used as a Confederate field hospital. The second detail is in the draft and is good, but it is given one sentence and no weight. The image of a Union general's body on a farm family's bed while Confederate wounded filled the house around him is one of the three or four most arresting details in the entire draft — it deserves two sentences, not one.

**Fix as BEAT:** Expand the Ray farmhouse image by one sentence. What was happening in the rooms around where Lyon was laid out? The fact pack has the Ray house detail; let the coordinator add one clause of texture about the Confederate wounded in the same building. The irony is already implicit — make it explicit, gently.

### Play-by-Play Tedium

"Sturgis pulls the army out" is the section most at risk of tipping into logistics-list mode, and it mostly avoids this — the Pearce quote ("We watched the retreating enemy through our field-glasses, and were glad to see him go") is the right note of relief-exhaustion from the Confederate side, and the detail of Granger's cavalry covering the withdrawal gives the retreat some human shape. No critical failure here, but the sub-section is the flattest passage in the full draft — it does the job and nothing more.

### The Casualty Statistics Sub-section

"The cost / A small battle's terrible rate" is the best-executed part of this section. "The story is the rate" is the right move. Grounding the 1st Kansas at the seventh-highest regimental casualty rate in the entire war is exactly the kind of specific comparison that makes the abstract number land. "This was a small battle in a far corner of the country, and it bled like a great one" is a proper closing kicker. No notes.

### "The victory they couldn't spend" and "Missouri's fate"

These are the two weakest sub-sections in the draft. Both are accurate and clear, but they read as analytical summary rather than felt story. "Here is the cruel irony at the center of Wilson's Creek" announces the irony before it arrives, which is the same front-loading problem noted in fight-for-missouri. "The Victory They Couldn't Spend" and "Missouri's Fate" do necessary work explaining the aftermath, but they lack a human anchor — no one's voice, no specific scene, no felt moment. They are the draft's equivalent of Antietam's "The reserves that never moved" sub-section in structure, but without the emotional punch of "The hole closed. The chance passed."

The guerrilla-war paragraph is important and the draft handles it competently. "Wilson's Creek was the opening shot of three and a half years of guerrilla war that would make Missouri the third most-fought-over state in the conflict" lands. But the transition into this paragraph is abrupt — we go directly from the McCulloch/Price coalition coming apart to "a state with two governments and a population genuinely divided down the middle became the cockpit." A single transitional clause — what did that collapse feel like to the people of Missouri who had been watching armies maneuver across their farms? — would connect the strategic narrative to the human consequence.

**Fix as BEAT:** In "The victory they couldn't spend": drop "Here is the cruel irony at the center of Wilson's Creek" — just state the irony, don't announce it. In "Missouri's fate": add one transitional clause before the guerrilla-war paragraph that bridges from the broken coalition to the people who were left in the state it abandoned. (No new fact required — just the right connecting tissue.)

### The Closing Italic Kicker

"Lyon lost the battle, lost his life, and somehow still won the thing he'd died for — Missouri stayed in the Union." This is a strong kicker and the right note to end on. It earns its place. No notes.
