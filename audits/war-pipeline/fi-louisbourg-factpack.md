## A. WHAT LOUISBOURG WAS / THE STAKES

1. Louisbourg was a fortified French town and naval base on the southeast coast of Île Royale (Cape Breton Island), in present-day Nova Scotia, Canada. [S1][S4]
2. It guarded the approach to the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the mouth of the St. Lawrence River, the sea road to Quebec and the rest of New France (French Canada). Taking it opened the river road for an invasion of Canada. [S1][S4][S7]
3. It was nicknamed the "Gibraltar of the North" (also "Gibraltar of North America"). [S7] In 1745 New Englanders called it the "American Dunkirk" for its use as a privateer base. [S2]
4. Pitt's 1758 strategy: British Secretary of State William Pitt planned three simultaneous offensives against New France for 1758: (a) capture Louisbourg (to open the St. Lawrence), (b) take Fort Carillon/Ticonderoga (the Lake Champlain corridor), (c) take Fort Duquesne (the Ohio forks). Louisbourg and Fort Duquesne succeeded; the assault on Fort Carillon was repulsed (Battle of Carillon, the French victory of 1758). [S7]

## B. THE 1745/1748 BACKSTORY

5. In 1745, during King George's War, a force of New England colonial troops under William Pepperrell (of Kittery, in present-day Maine), with Royal Navy support under Commodore Peter Warren, besieged and captured Louisbourg. [S2][S8]
6. The 1745 force was roughly 4,200 soldiers and sailors in about 90 ships, sailing from Boston in early 1745; it was primarily a New England effort (Massachusetts, Connecticut, New Hampshire, Rhode Island). [S2]
7. The 1745 fortress surrendered in June 1745. **DISPUTED date/length:** sources give the surrender as 17 June 1745 after a 49-day siege [S8/web], OR 28 June 1745 after a 47-day siege (12 May – 28 June) [S2]. Use "in June 1745, after roughly seven weeks." NOTE: a French garrison surrender in 1745 is firmly established; only the exact day/length varies.
8. By the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748), which ended King George's War, Britain returned Louisbourg to France. [S1][S2][S4]
9. The return to France left New Englanders with a sense of betrayal toward British colonial policy; they had captured it and seen it handed back. [S2]

## C. FORCES, 1758

**British:**
10. Overall ground command: Major General Jeffery Amherst (commander-in-chief of the expedition). [S1][S4]
11. Naval command: Admiral Edward Boscawen (Royal Navy). [S1][S4]
12. Brigadier generals included James Wolfe (who led the landing), Charles Lawrence, and Edward Whitmore. Chief engineer: John Henry Bastide. [S1]
13. British ground force: ~11,000 regulars plus a small body of American rangers (sources say ~200, or "four companies"); land force often given as ~13,000–14,000 total. [S1][S4] Drucour's biographer says Amherst attacked "with 27,000 troops" (this larger figure likely counts sailors/fleet personnel). [S3] **DISPUTED total:** use "around 14,000 soldiers landed, carried by a fleet of about 40 warships and 150 transports" [S1][S4] and avoid the 26,000–27,000 figure as the landing-army number.
14. British fleet: ~40 warships (ships of the line) and ~150 transports; ~157 ships total. [S1][S4]

**French:**
15. Governor and commander of Louisbourg / Île Royale: the Chevalier de Drucour (Augustin de Boschenry de Drucour), governor from 1754. [S1][S3]
16. French naval squadron commander in the harbor: Louis-Joseph Beaussier de l'Isle. [S1]
17. French garrison: ~3,500 regular troops plus ~3,500 marines/sailors from the warships; total defending force ~6,000–7,000. [S1][S3]
18. French harbor squadron: about 5 ships of the line and several frigates/smaller vessels present (Drucour's biographer: "11 vessels" with ~3,800 crew). Wikipedia lists the major warships individually (see F below). [S1][S3]

## D. THE LANDING — FRESHWATER COVE (Anse de la Cormorandière), June 8, 1758

19. The amphibious landing took place June 8, 1758, at Freshwater Cove (French: Anse de la Cormorandière), a few miles southwest of the fortress. [S1][S4]
20. Wolfe commanded the assault division: grenadier companies, light infantry (marksmen), rangers, and Highlanders. [S4]
21. The French had entrenched along the shore and opened fire with grapeshot and musketry; the surf was heavy and the rocky coast was lashed by breakers. The initial assault proved too costly and Wolfe gave a hand signal to "sheer off" (recall / call it off). [S4]
22. The turning point: three boats of light infantry, under Lieutenants Hopkins and Brown and Ensign Grant, either mistook or willfully ignored the recall signal and made for a craggy point of rocks that was sheltered from the French cannon by a small projecting point of land. They landed in the surf, secured a foothold, and were reinforced. [S4][S1: "a boatload of Howe's light infantry in Wolfe's division found a rocky inlet protected from French fire and secured a beachhead."]
23. Wolfe then redirected the rest of his division to the sheltered spot; Lawrence's brigade came onto the French flank; the French were forced from their shore positions and retreated to the fortress. [S1][S4]
   - NOTE on names: [S1] credits "Howe's light infantry"; [S4] names Lieutenants Hopkins and Brown and Ensign Grant. Treat the three-boats-found-shelter event as solid; name it generally ("a few boats of light infantry") and optionally name the officers per [S4]. (This is Brig. Gen. George Howe's light infantry of the expedition, not Lord Howe of Carillon — keep attribution loose.)

## E. THE SIEGE

24. After the landing the British conducted a formal 18th-century siege: digging trenches and advancing artillery batteries toward the walls. [S1] (American Battlefield Trust summary)
25. June 12: Wolfe seized Lighthouse Point (across the harbor mouth) with ~1,220 men, to mount batteries against the harbor and the Island Battery. [S1]
26. June 19: British batteries in position; ~70 cannon opened fire. [S1]
27. The French ships were ordered by Drucour to remain in the harbor (over their captains' reluctance), believing their presence would hinder the British. [S3]
28. **Madame Drucour and the cannon — VERIFIED.** Drucour's wife, Marie-Anne Aubert de Courserac (Mme Drucour), fired guns daily to encourage the troops. Dictionary of Canadian Biography, verbatim: "Each day throughout the siege Mme Drucour had fired three guns to encourage the French troops." [S3] (Note: THREE guns daily, not "a cannon daily" — use the precise version.)

## F. THE FRENCH FLEET DESTROYED

29. July 21: a British mortar shell from Lighthouse Point set the 64-gun *Célèbre* ablaze; fire spread to *Entreprenant* (74 guns) and *Capricieux* (64 guns). *Entreprenant*, the largest ship in the Louisbourg fleet, was lost. [S1]
30. July 23: British heated shot ("hot shot") ignited the King's Bastion, the fortress's main barracks/headquarters building. [S1]
31. July 25 (night): a Royal Navy "cutting-out" boat party rowed into the harbor and captured the *Bienfaisant* (64) and burned the *Prudent* (74). [S1]
32. Other French warships were scuttled (sunk deliberately) before or during the siege (*Apollon*, and several frigates/smaller vessels). [S1]
33. James Cook (the future Pacific explorer), then a Royal Navy sailing master, took part and recorded the July 25 action in his log. [S1] (Optional color.)

## G. THE SURRENDER — July 26, 1758

34. Drucour surrendered the fortress. **DISPUTED date:** terms were agreed and the place capitulated July 26, 1758 [S1][S3]; some sources date the formal surrender July 27 [S4 + ABT]. Use "July 26, 1758" as primary (per Wikipedia and DCB), note July 27 appears in some accounts.
35. The French expected the honours of war (the customary right of a beaten garrison to march out with arms, flags, and dignity), as the British garrison had been granted at Minorca. Amherst refused: the surrender was unconditional. He cited French/allied conduct at the surrenders of Fort Oswego (1756) and Fort William Henry (1757), where surrendered/paroled British prisoners had been attacked. [S1]
36. Drucour ultimately accepted unconditional terms, partly persuaded by the financial commissary Prévost's plea on behalf of the civilian population. [S3]
37. The Cambis regiment, in protest, broke its muskets and burned its regimental colors rather than surrender them. [S1]

## H. CASUALTIES & PRISONERS, 1758

38. British losses: about 172 killed and 355 wounded [S1] (BritishBattles gives "approximately 500 killed and wounded" [S4] — consistent as a round total). Use "around 170 killed and 350 wounded."
39. French losses: about 102 killed and 303 wounded. [S1]
40. Prisoners: roughly 5,600 soldiers surrendered; total captured (including sailors/others) given as ~6,600. [S1][S4] Use "more than 5,600 soldiers" / "some 5,600 prisoners." ~200 cannon also taken. [S4]

## I. AFTERMATH — DEPORTATION & THE ROAD TO QUEBEC

41. The fall of Louisbourg deprived New France of naval protection in the Atlantic and opened the St. Lawrence to British attack. [S1][S6]
42. It triggered the second wave of the Expulsion of the Acadians. Campaigns followed against Acadian populations on Île Saint-Jean (present-day Prince Edward Island), the Île Royale Acadians, and elsewhere (St. John River, Petitcodiac, Cape Sable, Gulf of St. Lawrence). [S6]
43. **DISPUTED/specific:** ~3,100 Acadians were deported in this wave, of whom an estimated ~1,649 died by drowning or disease (ships lost at sea, epidemics). [S6] Treat the death figure as an estimate.
44. Wolfe's Gulf raids: after the siege, Wolfe and Sir Charles Hardy led ~1,500 troops in nine vessels into the Gulf of St. Lawrence, reaching Gaspé Bay September 5, 1758, then striking Miramichi, Grande-Rivière, Pabos, and Mont-Louis. Hardy destroyed about 200 fishing vessels and took about 200 prisoners. The aim was to strip resources that supplied Quebec ahead of the 1759 campaign. [S5]
45. Louisbourg became the staging base for Wolfe's 1759 Siege of Quebec, which broke French rule in North America. [S1][S7]
46. The British later demolished the fortress with explosives (1760) so it could never be returned to France again; by 1763 (Treaty of Paris) Cape Breton was ceded to Britain. [S1]

## DISPUTED / RANGE SUMMARY (use ranges, no false precision)
- 1745 surrender date/length: June 1745, ~7 weeks (17 June/49 days OR 28 June/47 days). [B7]
- British 1758 land force: ~14,000 landed (NOT 26,000–27,000, which counts fleet). [C13]
- 1758 surrender date: July 26 (primary) or July 27 (some accounts). [G34]
- British casualties: ~170 killed + ~350 wounded (~500 total). [H38]
- Prisoners: ~5,600 soldiers / ~6,600 total. [H40]
- Acadian deportation deaths: ~1,649 estimated of ~3,100 deported. [I43]
- Landing officer names: [S4] names Hopkins/Brown/Grant; [S1] says "Howe's light infantry" — keep attribution loose.
