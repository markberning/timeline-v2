# FACT PACK — Battles of Lexington and Concord, April 19, 1775 (`rev-lexington`, size L)

*American Revolution battle page. EXTENDS (never contradicts) the verified spine baseline in
`rev-outbreak-factpack.md` §1–3 — the spine owns the news-war/Quero story and the strategic arc;
this page owns the day at tactical depth. HANDLE-WITH-CARE set: the first shot is genuinely
unresolved (both sides' depositions are propaganda instruments — present them AS instruments);
atrocity claims ran BOTH directions and both have a real core (the hatchet killing happened; the
British plundering and house-clearing killings happened; the "scalping" elaboration was propaganda);
no minuteman romance (it was an organized, officered town-militia system); Percy professional, not
caricatured. Web-verified June 2026 against NPS Minute Man NHP, archives.gov, JAR/allthingsliberty,
teachingamericanhistory.org, Concord Museum, Paul Revere House, battlefields.org.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **April 19, 1775** — a single ~15-hour running engagement across **Lexington, Concord, Lincoln,
  Menotomy (now Arlington), and Cambridge/Charlestown**, Massachusetts, opening the war. Three named
  phases (Lexington green ~5:00 a.m.; Concord's North Bridge ~9:30 a.m.; the 16-mile fighting retreat
  noon–7:30 p.m.) but ONE story: a secret raid that found almost nothing, then had to march home
  through a countryside that had been organizing for this exact day for months. (HIGH)
- **The real battle is the road.** The green and the bridge together account for a few dozen
  casualties; the retreat — Meriam's Corner, the Bloody Angle, Parker's Revenge, Fiske Hill,
  Menotomy — produced nearly all of the day's ~368 killed and wounded. Menotomy, not Lexington or
  Concord, was the day's worst killing. (HIGH)
- **Winner: American.** The stores were mostly gone before the regulars arrived; the column was saved
  from destruction only by Percy's relief brigade; by nightfall Boston was under siege. British
  officers said it themselves (Percy quote, §7). (HIGH)
- Battle id `rev-lexington`. State MA. Spine chapter: `rev-outbreak` ("The war begins").

## 2. BACKGROUND — the orders and the leaked secret
- **Gage's orders (verbatim, verified at teachingamericanhistory.org):** night of April 18 Gage handed
  Lt. Col. Francis Smith written orders: having intelligence "that a quantity of Ammunition, Provisions,
  Artillery, Tents and small Arms, have been collected at Concord, for the Avowed Purpose of raising and
  supporting a Rebellion against His Majesty, you will March with a Corps of Grenadiers and Light
  Infantry... with the utmost expedition and Secrecy to Concord, where you will seize and distroy all
  Artillery, Ammunition, Provisions, Tents, Small Arms, and all Military Stores whatever." The orders
  ALSO ordered restraint: "But you will take care that the Soldiers do not plunder the Inhabitants, or
  hurt private property." Gage even specified method — trunnions knocked off each gun, powder and flour
  shaken into the river, tents burnt. He attached a map of Concord with the supply caches marked (his
  spies were good). It was a police raid on paper: stores, not people, and no first shot. (HIGH)
- **The secret leaked before the boats were in the water.** Dr. Joseph Warren had a source close to the
  top of the British command and consulted that source on April 18 before dispatching Revere and Dawes.
  Gage later said he had told the full plan to only one person besides his second-in-command. **The
  identity of Warren's informant has never been established.** The famous candidate — Gage's
  American-born wife **Margaret Kemble Gage** — was nominated by David Hackett Fischer and others, and
  Gage did send her to England that summer; but the evidence is entirely circumstantial and several
  specialists call the spy story a myth. Present as: the leak is a fact, the leaker is unknown, Margaret
  Gage is an unproven suspicion. (Leak HIGH; Margaret Gage DISPUTED — never assert.)
- **Concord had weeks of warning regardless.** The Provincial Congress's stores had been dispersed for
  days (Revere had ridden to Concord April 8); on the 19th the regulars were raiding a half-emptied
  warehouse. (HIGH)

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS

### Numbers (dossier stats — ranges)
- **British:** ~**700** regulars in Smith's column (21 companies of grenadiers and light infantry drawn
  from the garrison's regiments + marines) + **~1,000–1,400** in Percy's relief brigade (1st Brigade +
  marines, **two 6-pounder field guns**) — total engaged **~1,700–1,900** (NPS: "1,700+"; some sources
  credit Percy with 1,400). (HIGH on 700; Percy's strength MED — use the range.)
- **American:** Parker's ~**77** on the green at dawn → ~**400** at the North Bridge → roughly
  **3,500–4,000** engaged in relays by day's end, from dozens of towns — companies arriving, firing,
  falling back, replaced by fresher ones from the next town. Never one army under one command. (HIGH
  as ranges)
- **Duration:** regulars under arms ~21+ hours (mustered ~10 p.m. April 18, behind Charlestown's lines
  ~7:30 p.m. April 19); shooting from ~5:00 a.m. to nightfall; the retreat fight ~7 hours over ~16
  miles (26 km) — Smith's own men marched ~35–40 miles total. (HIGH on the day-shape; mileage MED.)

### Commander card material (60–90 words each available)
- **Capt. John Parker (American, Lexington)** — b. July 13, 1729, Lexington; farmer, mechanic, town
  militia captain; French & Indian War veteran (served at Louisbourg 1758 and Quebec 1759 —
  battlefields.org; MED on the exact engagements). On April 19 he was 45 and **dying of tuberculosis
  ("consumption")** — some accounts say he could barely speak above a whisper. Commanded the ~77 on the
  green at dawn; that afternoon he rallied his survivors — men who had buried neighbors that morning —
  and ambushed Smith's column at the Lincoln–Lexington line ("Parker's Revenge"). Too ill for Bunker
  Hill; **died September 17, 1775**, age 46, buried in Lexington's Old Burying Ground. **No authentic
  likeness of him exists** (HIGH — the 1900 Kitson Minuteman statue on the green is an idealized young
  man, not a portrait). (HIGH except where tagged)
- **Lt. Col. Francis Smith (British, expedition commander)** — 1723–1791; a heavy, methodical 30-year
  professional of the 10th Foot (lieutenant 1741; lieutenant colonel 1762; brought the 10th to America
  1767). Chosen by Gage for seniority and steadiness, criticized afterward for slowness — though his
  early decision to send back for reinforcements arguably saved the column. **Shot in the thigh** in the
  fighting near Fiske Hill / the Parker's Revenge stretch of the road (sources split between the two
  adjacent sites — MED on the exact spot). Despite the criticism he kept rising: brigade command at Long
  Island (1776) and Quaker Hill, Rhode Island (1778); eventually lieutenant general (1787). Died 1791.
  Portrait: Francis Cotes, 1764 (National Army Museum). (HIGH except as tagged)
- **Maj. John Pitcairn (British, advance commander)** — b. 1722, Dysart, Fife, Scotland; Royal Marines
  officer (lieutenant 1746; major 1771), in Boston from late 1774 commanding ~600 marines. Unusually,
  Bostonians respected him as one of the fairer officers of the occupation. Led the advance companies
  onto Lexington green; his horse threw him at Fiske Hill and bolted into the American lines carrying
  his silver-mounted Scottish pistols — captured, auctioned, declined by Washington, and carried through
  the war by Israel Putnam (now displayed at Lexington's Hancock-Clarke House). (Pistols chain MED-HIGH —
  Coburn's chain of custody; display HIGH.) **The one forward line: killed two months later leading
  marines up Breed's Hill at Bunker Hill, June 17, 1775** — he fell into the arms of his son Thomas, a
  marine lieutenant, and died in Boston that day; buried at Old North Church. **No authentic portrait of
  him is known to exist** (HIGH — Trumbull used Pitcairn's son David as the model in his Bunker Hill
  painting). (HIGH except as tagged)
- **Brig. Gen. Hugh, Earl Percy (British, relief commander)** — 1742–1817; heir to the Duke of
  Northumberland, MP, professional soldier who had personally **opposed the government's coercive
  American policy** yet marched when ordered. Led the relief brigade with two 6-pounders out of Boston
  on the morning of the 19th (delayed hours by a botched order delivery — MED on the exact mishap
  detail), met Smith's broken column east of Lexington ~2:00–3:00 p.m., used the cannon to break up
  militia concentrations, ran a disciplined rotating rearguard, and made the day's smartest call:
  swerving to Charlestown instead of Cambridge, dodging a planned ambush at the Great Bridge. His April
  20 letter (§7) is the day's most honest British verdict. Later 2nd Duke of Northumberland (1786).
  (HIGH)
- **Brig. Gen. William Heath (American, the afternoon's coordinator — optional 5th card)** — Roxbury
  farmer, one of the Massachusetts militia's general officers, and the **first American general officer
  on a battlefield of the Revolution**. Reached Lexington mid-afternoon with Dr. Joseph Warren (who had
  a hairpin shot away beside him — MED); directed arriving companies into a moving ring of skirmishers
  around Percy's column rather than massed targets for the cannon. **EVIDENCE LEVEL: his "directing"
  role rests heavily on his own 1798 memoirs, amplified by Fischer; treat as real-but-self-reported**
  (MED). Later a Continental major general. (Tagged inline)
- **Col. James Barrett (American, Concord — context figure, not necessarily a card)** — 64-year-old
  colonel of Middlesex militia; the stores were hidden on HIS farm (cannon plowed into furrows —
  regulars searched the farm and found almost nothing); commanded the muster above the North Bridge and
  gave the order to advance with orders not to fire first. (HIGH)

## 4. THE BATTLE — the full day

### 4a. The night: crossing and the alarm (10 p.m.–5 a.m.)
- ~10:00 p.m. April 18: the companies mustered quietly on the Back Bay beach of Boston Common; ~11:00
  p.m. rowed across the Charles to Lechmere Point/Phipps Farm (East Cambridge); the men waded ashore
  through marsh, stood soaked for hours waiting on provisions, and only began marching ~**2:00 a.m.**
  Secrecy was already dead: Warren's riders were out before the boats. (HIGH — NPS timeline)
- **The alarm network mechanics (the spine owns the Revere myth-hygiene; summary):** Warren sent
  **Dawes** by Boston Neck and **Revere** by the Charlestown crossing; the two Old North lanterns were a
  pre-arranged BACKUP signal, not the message. From Lexington the word propagated through a pre-built
  system — fresh riders, church bells, signal guns, drums — town to town. **Prescott**, not Revere,
  reached Concord (Revere was captured in Lincoln and released on foot). That system, not one horseman,
  put thousands of armed men on the road by morning; some companies (e.g., Danvers) marched 16 miles in
  ~4 hours and got to Menotomy ahead of the British. (HIGH; Danvers pace MED)
- Hearing the bells, Smith did two smart things early: sent Pitcairn's six light companies ahead at
  speed, and sent a rider back to Gage requesting reinforcements — the request that produced Percy's
  brigade. (HIGH)

### 4b. Lexington green, dawn (~5:00 a.m.)
- Parker's ~77 stood in parade-ground lines on the green as Pitcairn's advance came up the Bedford Road —
  positioned NOT blocking the Concord road; a presence, not an ambush. Parker's own sworn deposition
  (April 25): assembled "to consult what to do," resolved not to "meddle or make with said Regular
  Troops... unless they should insult or molest us," and on their approach he "immediately ordered our
  Militia to disperse and not to fire." (HIGH — archives.gov facsimile)
- Mounted officers (commonly said to include Pitcairn) rode forward shouting at them to disperse — words
  reported variously ("Disperse, you rebels!" / "Lay down your arms, you damned rebels"); the militia
  were filing off, NOT grounding arms, when a shot came from somewhere. (Scene HIGH; words MED — "by
  colonial accounts")
- **THE FIRST SHOT — NEVER RESOLVE IT.** Nobody has ever established who fired: a regular, a militiaman,
  an onlooker (some regulars swore it came from behind a hedge or a tavern corner), an accident. Both
  sides then swore the other began it — and both sets of sworn statements were produced AS WEAPONS: the
  Provincial Congress collected roughly a hundred depositions within days expressly to prove the regulars
  fired first (and rushed them to London — spine owns that story); Gage's counter-narrative claimed the
  rebels fired first. A captured, wounded British officer, Lt. Edward Gould, deposed that he could not
  say which side fired first — the most honest contemporary line on it. (DISPUTED by design — the
  depositions are instruments, present them as instruments.)
- The regulars fired volleys and charged with bayonets; officers lost control of the men for some minutes
  (Pitcairn and other officers had to beat the firing down). **8 Americans killed, 10 wounded; one
  regular lightly wounded.** Among the dead: Jonathan Harrington, who dragged himself to his own
  doorstep and died at his wife's feet (HIGH — Lexington tradition with strong early attestation; keep
  one line). Minutes, start to finish. (HIGH)

### 4c. Concord and the search (~7:00 a.m.–noon)
- The column reached Concord ~7:00 a.m. The militia — too few yet to fight — withdrew across the North
  Bridge to Punkatasset Hill and watched the town fill with regulars while neighboring companies marched
  in to join them. (HIGH)
- **The search — and the pathetic trophies (verified, Concord Museum + NPS):** the grenadiers spent the
  morning searching marked locations and found the leftovers of a stockpile that had been melting away
  for two weeks: **three 24-pounder cannon** (siege guns too heavy to move — trunnions knocked off),
  **gun carriages and entrenching tools** (burned), **~500 pounds of musket balls** (dumped in the
  millpond — the townspeople later fished most of them back out), **barrels of flour and salt fish**
  (staved/dumped — about half the flour was later recovered usable), and the town's liberty pole (cut
  down). A detachment sent 2 miles to Col. Barrett's farm — Gage's map said the heavy weapons were
  there — found nothing of consequence: cannon lay plowed into the furrows under their boots. For this,
  a war. (HIGH; "half the flour" MED)
- Officers mostly behaved correctly in town — paid for food and drink, declined to torch buildings.
  But burning gun carriages set the **town house alight by accident; regulars joined the bucket line**
  to douse it. The militia on the hill across the river saw the smoke column and concluded the town was
  being burned — Lt. Joseph Hosmer's "Will you let them burn the town down?" is the traditional goad
  (MED — tradition). They formed and advanced. (HIGH on the accident + bucket line)

### 4d. The North Bridge (~9:30 a.m.)
- ~400 militia in column advanced on the ~90–100 regulars (3 light companies under Capt. Walter Laurie)
  holding the bridge; the regulars fell back across it and fumbled into a bad formation for the road.
  **The regulars fired first here** — first scattered shots, then a volley — killing Capt. **Isaac
  Davis** of Acton (whose company led because, as he'd said that morning, he hadn't "a man who is afraid
  to go" — MED, tradition) and Abner Hosmer. Maj. **John Buttrick** of Concord then gave the order —
  by tradition crying **"Fire, fellow soldiers, for God's sake, fire!"** (MED — tradition) — and the
  militia's return volley killed 3 regulars, wounded 9 (4 of 8 officers present), and broke them; they
  ran for town. **The first ordered American volley against the King's troops — ranks, officers,
  command — and the regulars ran.** That, not Lexington, is what Emerson's 1837 "shot heard round the
  world" commemorates. (HIGH on action/numbers; quotes tagged)
- **The hatchet killing and the scalping rumor's career (handle exactly):** a wounded regular trying to
  rise was struck in the head with a hatchet by a young Concord man (tradition names **Ammi White**) and
  died. That killing is REAL. Regulars marching past the bridge later saw the corpse and the story
  ignited: by the time it reached Boston the man had been "scalped," ears cut off, eyes gouged — and the
  claim entered Gage's official account. The Provincial Congress answered with depositions from the two
  men who buried the bodies (Zechariah Brown and Thomas Davis Jr.: "neither of those persons were
  scalped, nor their ears cut off"). So: one ugly blow by one man → a propaganda atrocity in both
  directions. Several historians read the regulars' rage at Menotomy partly through this rumor (MED —
  interpretive; attribute). (Killing HIGH; White's name MED — tradition; rumor career HIGH)
- Strange anticlimax: for ~2 hours afterward Smith kept the column in Concord — rounding up wounded,
  hiring carriages — while militia numbers on the hills kept growing. The column marched for Boston
  around **noon**. (HIGH)

### 4e. The road back — the real battle (noon–7:30 p.m.)
- **Meriam's Corner (~12:30 p.m.), 1 mile east of Concord:** where the road crossed a small bridge and
  the British flankers had to fold back into the column, the gathered companies (Reading, Billerica,
  East Sudbury, Framingham men among them) opened fire; 2 regulars killed. The pattern was set: every
  wall, woodlot, and pinch point for 16 miles. (HIGH)
- **The Bloody Angle / Elm Brook Hill, Lincoln (~1:30 p.m.):** the road bent twice through woods and
  swampy ground; militia fired from both sides of the bend into the compressed column — ~8 regulars
  killed, the day's sharpest stretch so far. (Despite the name, a 19th-century coinage — MED on the
  name's vintage, HIGH on the fight.)
- **Parker's Revenge (Lincoln–Lexington line, ~1:45 p.m.):** Parker's reformed Lexington company —
  morning's survivors — ambushed the column from a wooded rocky rise. **Evidence level: a single
  participant recollection 50 years later (Nathan Munroe), BUT the 2012–2016 NPS archaeological project
  recovered musket balls and fixed the engagement site** just inside Lexington; Smith's thigh wound is
  placed by sources in this stretch or at Fiske Hill just beyond. (Engagement HIGH via archaeology;
  details/placement MED)
- **Fiske Hill (~2:15 p.m.):** the column's discipline began to fail under exhaustion and casualties;
  Pitcairn's horse threw him here (the pistols, §3); officers were being picked off; the retreat was
  approaching rout when —
- **Percy's relief (Lexington, ~2:00–3:00 p.m.):** the 1st Brigade with two 6-pounders met the column
  east of Lexington green; Smith's men collapsed inside Percy's hollow square, "their tongues hanging
  out of their mouths, like those of dogs after a chase" (Percy's aide's family account — MED, attribute
  loosely or drop). Percy's cannon — the first artillery fire of the war — scattered the massed militia
  pursuing through Lexington; his men torched several houses being used (or usable) as sniper posts;
  Doolittle's Plate IV shows them burning. After a rest he resumed the march with a rotating rearguard
  and strong flanking parties sweeping the walls and houses. (HIGH; aide quote tagged)
- **The flank guards are the tactical key (both directions):** where the light infantry could sweep wide
  of the road, ambushes died before they fired; where terrain pinched the flankers back into the column
  (the Angle, village centers), the column bled. And the sweeps are also where the ugliness lived —
  houses "cleared" with bayonets, then looted. (HIGH as mechanics)
- **Menotomy (~4:30–5:30 p.m.) — the worst of the day:** fresh companies (Essex County men who had
  marched all day, including Danvers') waited in a town the road ran straight through; fighting went
  house-to-house, hand-to-hand. ~**25 Americans and ~40 British died in Menotomy** — half the British
  dead of the entire day. At **Jason Russell's house**, the 58-year-old Russell (lame, defending his own
  doorstep) and **11 other Americans** were shot or bayoneted in the house and yard — the single
  bloodiest spot of April 19; the house still shows the bullet holes. **78-year-old Samuel Whittemore**
  killed three regulars before being shot, bayoneted (~6–13 wounds reported), and left for dead — he
  recovered and lived to ~96 (his 1840s monument says he was 80 and lived to 98; modern genealogy says
  78 and 96 — give "his late 70s," DISPUTED on the exact ages). Civilians who stayed died too (e.g.,
  unarmed Menotomy men cut down in taverns/houses). (HIGH on the fight, Russell house, Whittemore core;
  ages DISPUTED)
- **Looting and burning — at evidence level:** this is not just American allegation; **British officers'
  own diaries attest it.** Lt. Frederick Mackenzie (23rd Foot, in Percy's brigade): "Many houses were
  plundered... I have no doubt this inflamed the Rebels... Some soldiers who stayed too long in the
  houses were killed in the very act of plundering." Lt. John Barker (4th Foot): the men "were so wild
  and irregular that there was no keeping 'em in any order... The plundering was shameful"; and of the
  cleared houses, "All that were found in the houses were put to death." Gage's written orders had
  forbidden exactly this (§2). American depositions add detail (ransacked houses, church communion
  silver taken at Menotomy — MED, local records/tradition). Keep it one even-handed beat: the regulars
  plundered and killed in the houses; the militia shot men from ambush all day; both sides' propaganda
  inflated both. (Diaries HIGH via published editions — see §7 caveats; silver MED)
- **Charlestown (~7:00–7:30 p.m.):** at Cambridge, Percy swerved the column off the expected road to
  the Great Bridge (planked for an ambush) and made for Charlestown Neck, reaching the peninsula at
  dusk — under the covering guns of **HMS Somerset** in the ferryway, whose presence (and boats) ended
  the pursuit. Heath's skirmisher ring broke off at the Neck; the militia camped in a ring around
  Boston. By morning it was a siege. (HIGH)

## 5. CASUALTIES (dossier stats — ranges)
- **British: 273** — **73 killed, 174 wounded, 26 missing**, of ~1,700–1,900 engaged (~15%). (HIGH —
  standard figures from Gage's returns; stable across sources.)
- **American: ~95** — **49 killed, 39–41 wounded, ~5 missing**, of ~3,500–4,000 engaged across the day.
  Wounded counts vary by 1–2 across sources; spine pack uses 93–95 — match it. (HIGH with small
  variance)
- Distribution is the story: ~handful at the green and bridge; the rest on the road, with Menotomy the
  single worst town (~40 British / ~25 American dead). (HIGH)

## 6. AFTERMATH & MEANING
- **The siege begins that night.** Militia from four colonies converged; the lines ran Roxbury to
  Chelsea; loose command settled on Gen. Artemas Ward April 20. Siege of Boston: April 19, 1775 –
  March 17, 1776. (HIGH — spine owns the full siege arc.)
- **The news war — ONE beat only (the spine owns the Quero story):** within days the Provincial
  Congress had ~100 sworn depositions and an address to Britain at sea on a fast schooner; the American
  "they fired first" account ran in London newspapers nearly two weeks before Gage's official version
  arrived. Both sides understood within hours that who fired first mattered as much as who won the
  road. (HIGH)
- **Myth-hygiene set (carry all four):** (1) Revere — one rider in an organized network; captured;
  Prescott reached Concord; "the British are coming" anachronistic (they were all British — "the
  regulars are coming out"); lanterns were the backup. (2) **"The shot heard round the world" is
  Emerson, 1837** ("Concord Hymn," for the North Bridge monument dedication) — about the BRIDGE volley,
  not Lexington; never contemporary. (3) The first shot at Lexington is irreducibly unknown (§4b).
  (4) No minuteman romance: the men of April 19 were organized, officered, repeatedly mustered town
  militia with a designated rapid-response tier (minute companies, roughly a quarter to a third of the
  rolls) — a system, not spontaneity. The famous Parker boulder line ("Stand your ground; don't fire
  unless fired upon...") is a much-later recollection/inscription, NOT his contemporary deposition —
  DISPUTED as verbatim; his real deposition (§7) is better anyway. (HIGH on all four as
  historiography)
- Percy's professional verdict (§7) became British policy-grade respect: the insurrection would not
  "turn out so despicable as it is perhaps imagined at home." Smith's sneering counter-verdict (§7)
  shows the other reading — both went to Gage's desk within days. (HIGH)
- Smith's regulars had executed Gage's orders almost to the letter and it didn't matter: the stores
  were gone, 273 men were down, and the army that had marched out to prevent a war marched back into a
  siege. (HIGH as framing)

## 7. VERIFIED QUOTES (5–7 + caveats)
1. **Gage's orders to Smith, April 18, 1775:** "...you will March... with the utmost expedition and
   Secrecy to Concord, where you will seize and distroy all Artillery, Ammunition, Provisions, Tents,
   Small Arms, and all Military Stores whatever... But you will take care that the Soldiers do not
   plunder the Inhabitants, or hurt private property." (HIGH — full text at teachingamericanhistory.org.)
2. **Capt. John Parker, sworn deposition, April 25, 1775:** "[I] ordered our Militia to meet on the
   Common in said Lexington, to consult what to do, and concluded not to be discovered, nor meddle or
   make with said Regular Troops (if they should approach) unless they should insult or molest us; and
   upon their sudden Approach, I immediately ordered our Militia to disperse and not to fire." (HIGH —
   National Archives facsimile; matches spine.)
3. **Lord Percy to a fellow general (commonly identified as Adjutant-General Edward Harvey), April 20,
   1775:** "Whoever looks upon them as an irregular mob will find himself much mistaken. They have men
   amongst them who know very well what they are about..." and "...nor are several of their men void of
   a spirit of enthusiasm... for many of them concealed themselves in houses and advanced within 10
   yards to fire at me and other officers"; "...the Rebels... are determined to go through with it, nor
   will the insurrection turn out so despicable as it is perhaps imagined at home." (HIGH on the wording
   via multiple reproductions; MED on the addressee — say "to a fellow general.")
4. **Lt. Frederick Mackenzie, 23rd Foot, diary, April 19, 1775:** "Many houses were plundered... I have
   no doubt this inflamed the Rebels... Some soldiers who stayed too long in the houses were killed in
   the very act of plundering." (MED-HIGH — published 1926 Allen French edition; wording per multiple
   secondary reproductions; re-verify against the edition at integration if quoted in full.)
5. **Lt. John Barker, 4th (King's Own) Foot, diary, April 19, 1775:** the men "were so wild and
   irregular that there was no keeping 'em in any order... The plundering was shameful"; "All that were
   found in the houses were put to death." (MED — diary genuine, published Atlantic Monthly 1877 and as
   *The British in Boston*, 1924; these lines verified only via secondary carriers (Boston 1775 / JAR
   orbit). The often-quoted Barker verdict "as ill plann'd and ill executed as it was possible to be"
   could NOT be verified verbatim against a primary scan this pass — DO NOT use without checking the
   1924 Dana edition; prefer the two lines above.)
6. **Rev. William Emerson of Concord, diary, April 19, 1775:** "This morning, between 1 and 2 o'clock,
   we were alarmed by the ringing of the bell, and upon examination found that the troops, to the number
   of 800, had stole their march from Boston... and were at Lexington Meeting-house, half an hour before
   sunrise, where they had fired upon a body of our men, and (as we afterward heard) had killed several."
   (HIGH — widely reproduced contemporary diary; the best contemporary Concord voice. Note his "800" is
   an estimate, the real figure ~700.)
7. **Lt. Col. Smith to Gage, April 22, 1775:** "Notwithstanding the enemy's numbers, they did not make
   one gallant attempt during so long an action..." — the losing commander's sneer, perfect against
   Percy's verdict. (MED-HIGH — quoted from Smith's report in standard accounts; re-verify full sentence
   at integration.) Smith also reported the fire "increased to a very great degree, and continued...
   for, I believe, upwards of eighteen miles" (NPS rendering — MED on exact intermediate wording).
- **[DO NOT USE AS FACT]:** Parker's "Stand your ground..." boulder line (later tradition); Pitcairn's
  exact words on the green (report "by colonial accounts"); Buttrick's "Fire, fellow soldiers..." (mark
  "by tradition"); Hosmer's "Will you let them burn the town down?" (tradition); the "tongues hanging
  out... like dogs" line (family memoir — attribute loosely or drop).
