## 2. Casualties

### Standard figures

| Side | Killed | Wounded | Missing/Captured | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Union** | 203 | 980 | 201 | **1,384** |
| **Confederate** | ~300 | ~1,000 | ~700 | **~2,000** (est.) |

- Union breakdown by division: 4th Division (Carr, at Elkhorn Tavern) — 682 casualties; 3rd Division (Davis/Osterhaus, at Leetown) — 344 casualties (Wikipedia)
- **Sources for Union total:** Wikipedia infobox 1,384; ABT facts page 1,384; NPS CWSAC 1,384; historynet.com ~1,300; Essential Civil War Curriculum 1,384. The Union figure is stable across sources.
- **Sources for Confederate total:** NPS CWSAC 2,000; ABT facts page 2,500; Wikipedia ~2,000; historynet.com ~2,100; scholarly estimate ~2,000 (300 killed / 1,000 wounded / 700 missing — warfarehistorynetwork.com citing scholarly consensus)

### Disputed Confederate figures [CONTESTED — flag for author]

Van Dorn's official after-action report gave his losses as **800 killed and wounded, plus 200–300 prisoners** — widely regarded by historians as a significant undercount. (Wikipedia; Essential Civil War Curriculum; search result summary)

Reasons the report is suspect:
1. It is implausible that the attacking force (outnumbered two-to-one in final effect) suffered fewer casualties than the defender (1,384 Union).
2. Van Dorn's report excluded the Indian troops who "retreated independently" and whose casualties were never formally tallied. (Essential Civil War Curriculum; ABT)
3. Confederate records for the Army of the West are incomplete. (warfarehistorynetwork.com)

The scholarly consensus is approximately **2,000 total Confederate casualties**, or roughly 12–15% of the effective force engaged. (NPS CWSAC; Wikipedia; warfarehistorynetwork.com; historynet.com)

### Two Confederate generals killed: March 7

Both McCulloch and McIntosh fell within approximately 15 minutes of each other near Leetown on the morning of March 7 — a leadership decapitation of the Confederate right wing. (Wikipedia; ABT; warfarehistorynetwork.com)

- **McCulloch:** Shot through the heart by a musket ball from Union infantry skirmishers (Osterhaus's men) while riding forward to reconnoiter personally near the tree belt on the north side of Oberson's field. Credit for the shot was later claimed by **Peter Pelican of the 36th Illinois Infantry**. McCulloch was wearing a distinctive black velvet civilian suit and Wellington boots at the time — he disliked Confederate uniforms. His death was initially concealed from his troops by staff officers to prevent demoralization. (Wikipedia — Benjamin McCulloch article; ABT biographies; warfarehistorynetwork.com; frontierpartisans.com)
- **McIntosh:** Assumed command upon McCulloch's death; was killed "less than fifteen minutes later" while leading a cavalry advance from the front, struck by a bullet from Osterhaus's skirmishers in the same Leetown area. (Wikipedia — James M. McIntosh article; ABT; warfarehistorynetwork.com)
- **Hébert:** Third-in-command Col. Louis Hébert led a 2,000-man infantry attack east of Leetown through Morgan's Woods but became disoriented in the woods and was captured in "confused fighting." He did not know McCulloch and McIntosh were dead and that he was now the ranking officer on the field. (NPS — Colonel Hébert page; Encyclopedia of Arkansas; ABT)
- **Command devolution:** After Hébert's capture, command should have gone to Col. Elkanah Greer (3rd Texas Cavalry, fourth in seniority), but due to "prevailing command confusion" he was not notified of his superiors' deaths or capture for several hours. This left the Confederate right wing in the hands of Brig. Gen. Albert Pike — a lawyer and poet with no combat experience — who proved ineffective and ordered the withdrawal from Leetown around 3:00 p.m. (Wikipedia; NPS — Colonel Hébert page; Encyclopedia of Arkansas; ABT)
- **Slack:** Brig. Gen. William Y. Slack (Missouri State Guard) was shot in the hip or groin (accounts differ on trajectory — see Section 10) on the morning of March 7 during fighting on Price's wing. He was removed from the field, transported to a private home, later moved 7 miles east to avoid capture, and died of wound infection on **March 21, 1862**. The Confederate government issued a posthumous promotion to Brig. Gen. (Confederate Army) dated April 12. (Wikipedia — William Y. Slack article)

## 3. What Came Before

### Wilson's Creek and the fight for Missouri (August 1861)

- **Battle of Wilson's Creek, August 10, 1861:** Confederate forces under **Brig. Gen. Benjamin McCulloch** (CS) and **Maj. Gen. Sterling Price** (Missouri State Guard) defeated a Union army under Brig. Gen. Nathaniel Lyon, who was killed in the battle — the first Union general killed in the war. The Confederate victory left "western and southwestern Missouri in the hands of the rebels." (Pea Ridge Campaign — Encyclopedia of Arkansas; Wilson's Creek factpack in this repo)
- After Wilson's Creek, Price's Missouri State Guard pushed north; Price captured the Federal garrison at **Lexington, Missouri** in September 1861. However, with no reinforcements from McCulloch and unable to hold Missouri alone, Price was forced south again by year's end. (ABT — battle-pea-ridge article)
- The two Confederate commanders — Price (a Missouri politician-general with a State Guard commission) and McCulloch (a Texas Ranger and frontier fighter with a Confederate commission) — feuded bitterly over command and strategy throughout the fall of 1861. Jefferson Davis resolved the command problem by appointing a third man above them both. (ABT; Essential Civil War Curriculum)

### Van Dorn takes command and plans the offensive

- **Earl Van Dorn** was appointed to command the new **Military District of the Trans-Mississippi** in January 1862, arriving in January and formally taking command of the Army of the West on **March 2, 1862**. (Essential Civil War Curriculum; Encyclopedia of Arkansas — Pea Ridge Campaign)
- Van Dorn's strategic vision was sweeping: capture Missouri, seize **St. Louis** as a commercial and military gateway, and coordinate with Gen. Albert Sidney Johnston's Army of the Mississippi for operations against Grant in Tennessee. He wrote to his wife: **"I must have St. Louis — then Huzza!"** (warfarehistorynetwork.com; historynet.com; ABT — battle-pea-ridge article) [Source: Van Dorn's personal correspondence, widely quoted]
- Van Dorn assembled approximately **16,000 troops** at Fayetteville, Arkansas: McCulloch's wing (~8,000 Texans, Arkansans, Louisianans) and Price's wing (~7,000 Missourians from the State Guard), plus Pike's Native American units. (historynet.com; warfarehistorynetwork.com)

### Curtis pushes Price out of Missouri

- **Brig. Gen. Samuel R. Curtis** was appointed to command the District of Southwest Missouri by Maj. Gen. Henry Halleck on **Christmas Day 1861**. (CivilWarOnTheWesternBorder.org)
- Curtis organized the **Army of the Southwest** with roughly 12,100 men (about 9,600 infantry, 2,500 cavalry, 50 guns) and launched an aggressive winter campaign, pursuing Price's Missouri State Guard southwest through Missouri and across the Arkansas border. (ABT — battle-pea-ridge; Wikipedia)
- By **mid-to-late February 1862**, Curtis had pushed Price's Missourians entirely out of Missouri and into northern Arkansas. At **Dunagin's Farm / Sugar Creek (February 17)**, a Confederate rearguard action under Col. Louis Hébert was fought before the Confederates burned **Fayetteville** on February 21 and fell back into the Boston Mountains. (Pea Ridge Campaign — Encyclopedia of Arkansas)
- Curtis established a strong defensive position along the **bluffs north of Little Sugar Creek**, two miles southeast of Pea Ridge, centered on the Telegraph Road. He had left detachments behind to protect his supply line, reducing his effective strength to approximately **10,250 troops** by the time of the battle. (Wikipedia; ABT; NPS CWSAC)

### Van Dorn's forced march

- Van Dorn held a council of war on **March 3** and decided on an immediate offensive, despite his army's poor condition. (warfarehistorynetwork.com)
- The Confederate army **departed at dawn, March 4**, in blizzard conditions, marching north through the Boston Mountains.
- Van Dorn himself was ill — described as suffering from flu or pneumonia effects — and traveled part of the journey lying in a wagon. (historynet.com; ABT — battle-pea-ridge article)
- The march covered approximately **50 miles in three days** through rough terrain in severe winter weather. Troops were ordered to travel light: **one weapon, 40 rounds of ammunition, three days' rations, no tents or heavy clothing**. (warfarehistorynetwork.com; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- **Sterling Price opposed the pace**: He reportedly told Van Dorn, **"For God's sake, let the poor, worn-out and hungry soldiers rest and sleep."** (warfarehistorynetwork.com) [VERIFY primary source — widely quoted in secondary sources]
- Confederate soldiers arrived exhausted and hungry. One recalled: **"We arrived almost frozen and starved, having only one biscuit for breakfast that morning, and no prospect of supper."** (warfarehistorynetwork.com) [VERIFY: attributed to unnamed Confederate soldier in warfarehistorynetwork.com — primary source unspecified]
- By the time the battle opened, effective Confederate strength had fallen from ~16,000 to approximately **~12,000 fit troops** due to exhaustion, straggling, and illness. (warfarehistorynetwork.com)
- **March 6:** Confederate cavalry located Curtis's defensive line at Little Sugar Creek; a skirmish with **Sigel's** rear guard — who had been slow to evacuate Bentonville — was won by the Confederates. That evening, Van Dorn ordered the critical night march. (Essential Civil War Curriculum; Wikipedia)

## 4. The Battle

### Van Dorn's plan: the wide flanking march

- Rather than assaulting Curtis's prepared defenses at Little Sugar Creek head-on, Van Dorn divided his army and sent it on a **night march around the Union right flank** via the **Bentonville Detour** — a road running northwest from Camp Stephens onto the Pea Ridge plateau — intending to come at Curtis from the **north**, cutting his supply line and attacking his rear. (Wikipedia; ABT; all sources)
- Van Dorn split his army into two wings separated by **Pea Ridge / Big Mountain**:
  - **Price's wing** (eastern): took the Bentonville Detour around the northern end of the ridge and descended to attack via the **Telegraph Road** toward **Elkhorn Tavern**
  - **McCulloch's wing** (western): skirted the western edge of the ridge to attack near **Leetown**
  - The two wings were to converge at Elkhorn Tavern and encircle Curtis from both sides. (Wikipedia; ABT; all sources)
- **Critical self-inflicted problem:** By marching around Curtis's position, Van Dorn severed his own supply and ammunition trains from the forward troops. The supply wagons were left far to the rear — at or near Camp Stephens and Bentonville — separated from the army by Union-held ground and rough terrain. This would prove fatal on Day 2. (ABT; historynet.com; warfarehistorynetwork.com; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- The night march across icy, rocky terrain was brutal. Price's men encountered a **dense cluster of felled trees** blocking the Bentonville Detour and spent hours clearing the road, causing serious delays. (warfarehistorynetwork.com)

### Curtis reverses his army (180° about-face)

- On the morning of **March 7**, Curtis discovered that Van Dorn's army was **behind him** — to the north — instead of in front. He executed one of the more remarkable tactical responses of the war's early years: he **turned his entire army around 180°** to face north, using the Little Sugar Creek bluffs as his new rear instead of his front. His engineering background (he was a trained engineer) informed both his original entrenchments and this rapid reorientation. (ABT — battle-pea-ridge; historynet.com; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- Curtis dispatched Col. **Eugene Carr**'s 4th Division (~1,400 men) to **Elkhorn Tavern** to hold the Telegraph Road against Price's approaching column, and Cols. **Osterhaus** and **Davis** to block McCulloch's wing near Leetown. (Wikipedia; warfarehistorynetwork.com; Encyclopedia of Arkansas)

### Day 1, March 7 — Leetown (McCulloch's wing)

**Morning:**
- McCulloch's wing attacked near Leetown. Confederate cavalry charged Union positions, routing Federal cavalry and capturing three artillery pieces from Capt. Gustavus M. Elbert's **1st Missouri Flying Battery**. Pike's Native American troops (with a Texas cavalry squadron) led this assault at **Foster's Farm** up Foster's Lane. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge; Wikipedia)
- At approximately **10:30 a.m.** to noon, **McCulloch** rode forward alone to reconnoiter Union positions near the belt of trees on the north side of **Oberson's Field**. He was shot through the heart by a bullet from Osterhaus's skirmishers and died instantly. **Peter Pelican of the 36th Illinois Infantry** later claimed the shot. McCulloch had told his staff to wait: "You boys remain here; our gray horses will attract the fire of the sharpshooters." He wore his distinctive black velvet civilian suit. (Wikipedia — Benjamin McCulloch; frontierpartisans.com; civilwartalk.com forum citing eyewitness accounts)

**Early afternoon:**
- **McIntosh** assumed command of the division and was killed "less than fifteen minutes" later in the same area, leading a cavalry advance from the front. (Wikipedia — James M. McIntosh)
- **Hébert** then led ~2,000 infantry forward through Morgan's Woods but became lost, disoriented in the dense timber, and was captured in the confused fighting. He did not know he was by then the ranking officer. (NPS — Colonel Hébert page; Wikipedia)
- Command on the Confederate right effectively collapsed. **Albert Pike** gathered the remnants and ordered a withdrawal from the Leetown battlefield around **3:00 p.m.** Native American troops, after their initial success at Foster's Farm, "adamantly refused" further frontal assaults and withdrew. (Wikipedia; ABT — battle-pea-ridge; historynet.com)
- The Confederate right wing had been destroyed as a fighting force by approximately mid-afternoon of Day 1.

### Day 1, March 7 — Elkhorn Tavern (Price's wing)

- Price's column attacked Col. Carr's 4th Division along the Telegraph Road near **Elkhorn Tavern**. Carr's force was outnumbered roughly 5-to-1 but formed **four successive defensive lines**, repeatedly reforming and fighting back. (warfarehistorynetwork.com; ABT)
- Col. **Henry Little**'s Missouri brigade pushed Federal forces steadily back during the afternoon. An artillery duel raged (14 Confederate guns vs. 4 Federal guns at this point). (warfarehistorynetwork.com)
- Van Dorn learned at approximately **2:00 p.m.** that McCulloch's wing was not coming and would not reach Elkhorn Tavern; he ordered a direct frontal assault. (warfarehistorynetwork.com)
- **Sterling Price was wounded** during the fighting on the 7th but remained in command. (Wikipedia)
- By **nightfall on March 7**, Confederate forces under Price controlled **Elkhorn Tavern** and the section of the Telegraph Road near it — the high-water mark of their advance. Curtis arrived personally at the Elkhorn Tavern front in the evening, ordered a counterattack with reserves and commanded troops to **"charge with bayonets."** The Confederates retreated slightly but held the tavern. (warfarehistorynetwork.com; historynet.com)
- Confederate troops spent the night without proper food or shelter, exhausted, and critically, **without access to their ammunition trains**. (warfarehistorynetwork.com; Essential Civil War Curriculum)

### Day 2, March 8 — Union artillery barrage and Confederate collapse

- Curtis spent the night reorganizing, feeding, and resupplying his troops. He concentrated his entire army — all four divisions in a continuous line — facing north. On his left, **Sigel** massed **21 cannons** on an exposed knoll west of Elkhorn Tavern. (Wikipedia; ABT)
- Van Dorn attempted a cannon barrage to open March 8 but it was unfocused and ineffective.
- **Van Dorn then made his fatal discovery:** his reserve ammunition trains were with the supply wagons — now **five to six hours away** via road, on the other side of Union-held ground. There was no way to resupply during battle. He had not issued orders to keep the supply trains closely behind the army during the night march. (Wikipedia; warfarehistorynetwork.com; historynet.com; ABT)
- **Sigel's artillery** opened what one participant described as "a continual thunder. A fellow might have believed that the day of judgment had come." (warfarehistorynetwork.com) [VERIFY: from participant memoir, source identity not specified]
- Union gunners systematically disabled Confederate artillery "one by one" as Confederate ammunition dwindled and their guns fell silent. Sigel's 21-gun battery dominated the field. (historynet.com; warfarehistorynetwork.com; ABT)
- By approximately **10:30 a.m.**, Sigel launched an infantry assault. Asboth's regiments drove Stand Watie's **2nd Cherokee Mounted Rifles** off **Big Mountain** on the right. Curtis simultaneously ordered a "precisely timed artillery barrage and properly synchronized infantry assault" across the entire front. (Wikipedia; ABT — battle-pea-ridge; warfarehistorynetwork.com)
- At approximately **11:00 a.m.**, Van Dorn ordered retreat. The Confederate army disintegrated, retreating in **three separate directions** via the Huntsville Road and Bentonville Detour — not as a coherent force. (historynet.com; ABT; Wikipedia)
- By **noon**, Sigel's and Davis's divisions met near Elkhorn Tavern. Curtis reportedly exclaimed: **"Victory! Victory!"** (historynet.com)
- Van Dorn's post-battle self-assessment: **"I was not defeated, but only foiled in my intentions."** (warfarehistorynetwork.com; historynet.com; Essential Civil War Curriculum — citing Van Dorn's official report / letter to Johnston)

## 5. The Native American Troops

### Organization

- **Brig. Gen. Albert Pike** (CS): a lawyer, poet, and journalist from Arkansas who had negotiated the Confederate treaties with the Five Civilized Tribes in the summer of 1861, and who commanded the Department of Indian Territory. He organized Indian regiments under treaty stipulations specifying they were **not required to leave Indian Territory** — a stipulation he reportedly believed in, but which his commanders overrode. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge; Search results — Confederate treaties portal)
- Pike's force marching into Arkansas on **March 5, 1862** totaled approximately **900 troops**:
  - **Col. John Drew's 1st Cherokee Mounted Rifles** (pro-Union/Unionist Cherokees who nevertheless served under Confederate command, with divided loyalties)
  - **Col. Stand Watie's 2nd Cherokee Mounted Rifles** (firmly pro-Confederate)
  - **Capt. Otis G. Welch's Texas Cavalry Squadron** (~100 men)
  - (The 1st Choctaw and Chickasaw Mounted Rifles and 1st Creek Regiment were organized but their pay arrived too late; they did not reach the battlefield in time.) (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge)
- **Total Native American troops at the battle:** Wikipedia infobox gives ~800; other sources cite up to 2,000 enrolled but only the above units arrived in time. The ~800 figure is the most commonly cited for actual participants at Pea Ridge. (Wikipedia; ABT)
- Pike described his own troops as "entirely undisciplined, mounted chiefly on ponies, and armed very indifferently with common rifles and shotguns." (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge)

### Stand Watie: who he was

- **Stand Watie** (DeGadoga, 1806–1871) was a prominent Cherokee Nation leader, one of the signers of the **Treaty of New Echota (1835)** — the treaty that compelled the Cherokee removal and the Trail of Tears. This made him a deeply polarizing figure within the Cherokee Nation, politically opposed to Principal Chief **John Ross**. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge; NPS articles)
- Watie commanded the pro-Confederate **2nd Cherokee Mounted Rifles** at Pea Ridge. He would later become the **last Confederate general to surrender** in the Civil War (June 23, 1865 — more than six weeks after Appomattox), and was the **only Native American on either side promoted to brigadier general** in the Confederate army. (NPS — pearidge.htm; ABT)

### The divided Cherokee Nation

- The Cherokee Nation was split: Principal Chief John Ross initially maintained neutrality but was pressured into a Confederate alliance; **John Drew's 1st Cherokee Mounted Rifles** were largely Unionist sympathizers pressed into Confederate service. Drew's men would begin deserting to the Union side at Pea Ridge itself. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge)
- The broader context: Of Indian Territory's ~100,000 inhabitants, approximately **14% were enslaved African Americans**. The Five Tribes had economic and cultural ties to the South (cotton agriculture in the case of Choctaw and Chickasaw; trade connections to New Orleans across all five tribes). The Confederacy's treaty offers also promised protections that the U.S. government had repeatedly failed to deliver — making Confederate alliance attractive to some tribal leaders even where it was not unanimously supported. (Search results — ICT news; Confederate treaty context)
- The Confederacy signed nine formal treaties with tribes in Indian Territory in summer–fall 1861 (Creek July 10; Choctaw & Chickasaw July 12; Seminole August 1; Cherokee October 7; plus Quapaw, Seneca, Shawnee, Osage). (treatiesportal.unl.edu)

### The Foster's Farm action and the scalping incident

**What happened:**
- Pike's force (Stand Watie's 2nd Cherokee plus Welch's Texas cavalry) attacked a small Union column — two companies of the **3rd Iowa Cavalry** under Lt. Col. **Henry H. Trimble** — that had been led north of the main Union position up **Foster's Lane**. The Cherokees and Texans surprised the Iowans, **seriously wounded Trimble**, sent his men fleeing, and contributed to the capture of **three artillery pieces** from Capt. Elbert's 1st Missouri Flying Battery. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge; Wikipedia)
- After this action, **at least eight of Trimble's cavalrymen were scalped** and several others mutilated. This occurred in the woods near Foster's Farm after the fighting; **no eyewitness left a direct record** of the act in the woodland, so the exact perpetrators are uncertain. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge) [UNCERTAIN — see Section 10]

**Scale and culpability:**
- Of approximately 800–900 Cherokee soldiers at Pea Ridge, the ABT estimates "probably fewer than a dozen" participated in the mutilations. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge)
- The ABT notes explicitly: "somebody scalped eight of the Iowa cavalrymen — **both whites and Indians engaged in scalping on the frontier**, so the culprits could have been either Cherokee or Texan." The woods location and lack of eyewitnesses make definitive attribution impossible. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge) [CONTESTED]
- Capt. **Oliver Hazard Perry Scott** of the 3rd Iowa Cavalry stated afterward: "There was quite a number of our men scalped by them, two of our company." (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge) [Attribution to Cherokee soldiers by a Union officer; no firsthand witness to the act itself]

**Aftermath and controversy:**
- Pike was "horrified" when he learned of the scalping after the battle and issued orders against future such conduct. He later court-martialed a soldier for killing a wounded Federal. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge; search result summary)
- The Northern press "pilloried Pike and excoriated his Indian troops." The Confederate government was embarrassed; the Indian regiments were subsequently largely confined to Indian Territory operations. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge; search result summary)
- Pike resigned his Confederate commission in **July 1862** (formal acceptance November 1862) and was later indicted in Federal court for inciting war atrocities — though never tried. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge; civilwarvirtualmuseum.org)
- The Pea Ridge scalping incident was "the first of four documented cases of such mutilations" in the Civil War. (search result summary — from ABT Cherokees article context)
- In Cherokee cultural context, ABT notes scalping had historically held ritualistic significance with two traditional patterns: exacting revenge for tribal members' deaths, or responding to European/colonial encouragement (e.g., British bounties during the French and Indian War). ABT contextualizes the Pea Ridge case as closer to the latter pattern — Native peoples coerced into Confederate service against their interests. (ABT — Cherokees at Pea Ridge)

## 7. What It Meant

### Missouri secured for the Union

- The Confederate defeat at Pea Ridge ended **the largest and best-organized Confederate effort to retake Missouri**. Van Dorn had assembled more relative manpower advantage (roughly 16,000 vs. 10,250) than any other Confederate force in the Trans-Mississippi for the entire war. He failed. (ABT; NPS; Wikipedia)
- **"The Confederates never again seriously threatened the state of Missouri."** (Wikipedia — direct quote from article) Missouri remained under Union control for the rest of the war, serving as a stable base for subsequent river offensives. (warfarehistorynetwork.com; NPS — pearidge.htm)
- St. Louis — Van Dorn's stated prize, the commercial gateway to the Mississippi and a Union arsenal — was never seriously threatened again. (warfarehistorynetwork.com; historynet.com)

### Arkansas stripped of defenders

- After Pea Ridge, General Albert Sidney Johnston ordered Van Dorn to bring his army east of the Mississippi to Corinth, Mississippi, to join the concentration for what would become the **Battle of Shiloh** (April 6–7, 1862). Van Dorn complied, marching the remnants of the Army of the West across Arkansas and across the Mississippi. His troops arrived at Corinth on **April 23, 1862** — too late for Shiloh, where Johnston was killed. Van Dorn reported to Gen. **P.G.T. Beauregard**. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas — Earl Van Dorn article; historyofwar.org — Earl Van Dorn; ABT — Road to Corinth article)
- This stripped **Arkansas of virtually all organized Confederate military force**, leaving it defenseless against Federal advance. Van Dorn's replacement, Maj. Gen. **Thomas C. Hindman**, inherited the enormous task of rebuilding Arkansas defenses from near-nothing. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas — Earl Van Dorn article; search result summary)
- Curtis's army subsequently advanced south and reached **Helena, Arkansas** on July 12, 1862 — the Union effectively held northern Arkansas as a result of Pea Ridge. The march caused tremendous economic disruption; Curtis "emancipated slaves on a large scale, virtually ending slavery in the eastern river counties" during his advance. (Pea Ridge Campaign — Encyclopedia of Arkansas)

### Trans-Mississippi strategic significance

- Pea Ridge was the **decisive battle for control of the Trans-Mississippi** in the war's first year. No subsequent Confederate effort in that theater ever matched the scale or near-success of Van Dorn's campaign. (NPS — pearidge.htm; ABT)
- The battle disrupted Van Dorn's plan to coordinate with Johnston's Tennessee offensive — the Confederate western strategy of early 1862 depended in part on Trans-Mississippi forces being available. Instead, Van Dorn arrived at Corinth after Shiloh was already lost. (Essential Civil War Curriculum; Encyclopedia of Arkansas)

### Framing note: the slave-state border context

- Missouri was a **slave state** that had not seceded, despite a significant pro-Confederate political faction (a rival Confederate state government had been established; Missouri sent representatives to the Confederate Congress). The battle's outcome foreclosed the Confederacy's last real chance to bring Missouri's territory, population, and Mississippi River access into the Confederate order. Van Dorn's campaign was explicitly aimed at adding Missouri to the slaveholders' Confederacy; Curtis's victory denied that. (ABT; NPS; Wikipedia on Missouri in the Civil War context)

## 9. Geography for a Map

The following elements should appear on a battle map. North up.

### Terrain and natural features

- **Pea Ridge / Big Mountain:** An east–west ridge/plateau in Benton County, Arkansas, the dominant high ground of the battlefield, running roughly E–W and forming a natural divider between the two Confederate attack wings.
- **Little Sugar Creek:** Ran roughly E–W (or NW–SE) approximately 2 miles southeast of the ridge; this was Curtis's **original defensive line facing south** — the position he had entrenched in anticipation of a frontal Confederate attack from the south. After Van Dorn's flanking march, the creek became Curtis's rear.
- **Morgan's Woods / Oberson's Field:** Woodland and clearing near Leetown where McCulloch and McIntosh were killed; Hébert's infantry was captured in the woods.

### Roads (the arterial network)

- **Telegraph Road (also called Wire Road):** The main north–south road; ran from Missouri south through Cross Timber Hollow, down to **Elkhorn Tavern**, then continued south past Little Sugar Creek toward Fayetteville. Price's wing used this road to attack from the north. The road was the operational axis of the entire battle.
- **Huntsville Road:** Branched east from Elkhorn Tavern; Van Dorn's retreat route on March 8.
- **Bentonville Detour:** The flanking road Van Dorn used for his night march; ran northwest from Camp Stephens (southwest of the Union position), looping around the western and northern edge of Pea Ridge to come in from the northwest/north. Both wings used portions of this road.
- **Ford Road:** Ran west from the Telegraph Road area, roughly bisecting the gap between the two Confederate wings.

### Key named places

- **Elkhorn Tavern:** A landmark two-story tavern at the junction of the Telegraph Road and Huntsville Road, on the eastern portion of the Pea Ridge plateau. Price's wing aimed to seize this junction; it was the tactical center of gravity on Day 1. Confederate forces held it at nightfall on March 7; Curtis retook it on Day 2.
- **Leetown:** A small hamlet northwest of the Telegraph Road, about midway between Curtis's original creek position and Ford Road; the western half of the battlefield where McCulloch's wing fought and where both McCulloch and McIntosh were killed.
- **Foster's Farm / Foster's Lane:** North of the Union position; where Pike's Native American troops and Texas cavalry attacked Trimble's Iowa cavalry column and the scalping incident occurred.
- **Camp Stephens:** The Confederate assembly area, west of Curtis's position, where supply/ammunition wagons were left during the night march.
- **Cross Timber Hollow:** The draw/valley on the Telegraph Road north of Elkhorn Tavern through which the road descended from Pea Ridge toward Missouri.

### The flanking march direction (critical for map orientation)

- Van Dorn's army marched from **south/southwest** (Fayetteville/Camp Stephens) **north/northwest**, then looped around the western and northern edge of Pea Ridge via the Bentonville Detour.
- The attack came from the **NORTH**, against what had been the Union rear.
- Curtis's army had to execute a **180° pivot** — what had been the front (facing south toward Little Sugar Creek) became the rear; what had been the rear became the new front facing north toward Pea Ridge.
- Price's wing: came from the **northeast / north**, descending Cross Timber Hollow on the Telegraph Road.
- McCulloch's wing: came from the **northwest**, skirting the western edge of the ridge toward Leetown.

## 10. Contested / Uncertain Facts

### Casualty figures (CONTESTED)

- **Confederate total:** The most contested figure in the battle's historiography. Van Dorn reported ~800–1,000 killed/wounded plus 200–300 prisoners; modern scholars consistently reject this as a significant undercount and estimate ~2,000 total. **Neither figure is fully verified** because Confederate records for the Army of the West are incomplete. Different reputable sources give figures ranging from 2,000 (NPS CWSAC, Wikipedia) to 2,500 (ABT facts page), and the ABT narrative article gives 2,500 while its facts page gives 2,500 — suggesting even the Trust's own numbers are not fully settled. **Recommended author framing:** "roughly 2,000 casualties, with Confederate records incomplete and Van Dorn's own report of ~1,000 widely regarded as a serious undercount." [CONTESTED]
- **Union total:** Stable across sources at 1,384 (203 killed, 980 wounded, 201 missing). This is the one solid number. [RELIABLE]
- **Native American casualties:** Effectively unknown. Van Dorn's report excluded them; they dispersed independently. [UNKNOWN]

### Force sizes (MINOR VARIATION)

- Union: NPS CWSAC 10,250; ABT 10,500; Wikipedia 10,500; Essential Civil War Curriculum ~10,500. Variation is small. **Recommended:** ~10,250–10,500.
- Confederate: NPS CWSAC 14,000 (likely effective combatants); most other sources 16,000–16,500 (total present including worn-out/ill). The warfarehistorynetwork article estimates Van Dorn's effective strength by battle's start had fallen to ~12,000. **Recommended:** "approximately 16,000 men, though exhaustion and straggling from the forced march reduced effective strength substantially."

### The scalping incident (CONTESTED)

- **Number scalped:** Most sources say **eight**; some say "quite a number" (Scott's testimony). The ABT gives "at least eight scalped."
- **Who did it:** No eyewitness directly observed the act. Perpetrators could be Cherokee or Texas cavalry soldiers — both groups were present and "both whites and Indians engaged in scalping on the frontier" (ABT). **Author should not state definitively that Cherokee soldiers did the scalping;** the historical record says it happened, notes the Cherokee and Texans were present, and acknowledges uncertainty about who specifically acted. The Union officer's attribution to "them" (presumably Cherokee) is not eyewitness testimony of the act itself. [UNCERTAIN — use carefully]
- **Broader scale:** Of ~800–900 Native American troops, "probably fewer than a dozen" are estimated to have participated. The incident, while real and significant in its consequences, should not be used to characterize the behavior of Cherokee soldiers at Pea Ridge generally.

### Slack's wound trajectory (MINOR)

- Confederate Col. Thomas H. Rosser said the bullet hit Slack in the **hip** (taking a downward path to exit the body); another soldier said the bullet "deflected off a tree limb and struck Slack in the **groin**." Both agree he was partially paralyzed and removed from the field. He died March 21 of wound infection. (Wikipedia — William Y. Slack) [MINOR INCONSISTENCY — note for completeness; not load-bearing]

### Battle start date in CWSAC vs. other sources

- CWSAC lists the battle as "March 6–7, 1862" (counting the Sigel skirmish on March 6 as part of the battle). Most narrative sources treat **March 7–8** as the battle proper, with March 6 as a preliminary skirmish. Both conventions appear in reliable sources. **Recommended:** March 7–8 (the main battle), noting March 6 skirmish as prelude.

### Van Dorn's Van Dorn's illness

- Some sources say he was ill with "flu or pneumonia effects" and traveled in a wagon (historynet.com; ABT). Others simply note the forced march's hardship. The illness detail is credible but the specific diagnosis is secondary-source inference, not a primary medical record. [MINOR — treat as color detail, not hard fact]

### Van Dorn quote ("I must have St. Louis")

- The quote is attributed to a letter to his wife; it appears in multiple reputable secondary sources (ABT, historynet, warfarehistorynetwork) but the original document has not been independently verified in this research. Likely authentic — Van Dorn's character is consistent with it — but should be cited as "reportedly wrote" or verified against the Official Records/personal papers. [VERIFY primary]
