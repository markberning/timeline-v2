### Section: Morning, July 21 — The hour it looked won

**"the brigades of Brigadier General Barnard Bee (South) and Colonel Francis Bartow (South)"**
- MUST-FIX — "brigades" introduced with no definition. A reader has now seen "division" (undefined) and now "brigade" (also undefined). These are core structural terms used throughout and never explained. Quote: "the brigades of Brigadier General Barnard Bee…"
- Suggest a one-time gloss when "division" first appears: "an army in this era was organized in layers — regiments of ~1,000 men stacked into brigades, brigades into divisions, divisions into corps" — or simpler, gloss each term on first use.

**"Colonel Nathan Evans (South), had barely 1,100 men"**

**"Matthews Hill"**

**"Colonel William T. Sherman (North) — the same Sherman who would become one of the war's most famous generals — found an unguarded ford upstream from the Stone Bridge, crossed with his brigade around 10:00 a.m."**

### Section: Meanwhile in the Shenandoah Valley

**"screened his departure behind a curtain of cavalry under Colonel J.E.B. Stuart (South)"**
- NEEDS-GLOSS — "screened" and "screen" as a military verb (using cavalry to hide army movements) is jargon. Quote: "screened his departure behind a curtain of cavalry"
- The phrase "curtain of cavalry" somewhat covers it but "screened" is still a military term. Suggest: "hid his departure behind a screen of cavalry — riders who spread out ahead of his army to block Union scouts from seeing what was moving."

**"his infantry"**
- NEEDS-GLOSS — "infantry" has not been defined. Quote: "marched them [his infantry]"
- Suggest gloss on first use: "infantry (soldiers who fight on foot)" — it appears with no definition in a section where cavalry has also appeared without definition.

**"cavalry"**
- NEEDS-GLOSS — used twice ("cavalry under Colonel J.E.B. Stuart"; "curtain of cavalry") before ever being defined. A 15-year-old may assume "cavalry" = a general army term.
- Suggest first use: "cavalry — soldiers who fought on horseback."

### Section: There Stands Jackson — Henry House Hill

**"the reverse slope of Henry House Hill — the back side of the hill, the side facing away from the enemy, where the crest of the ground itself shielded his men from incoming artillery fire"**
- Status: CLEAR (defined inline)

**"artillery"**
- NEEDS-GLOSS — used multiple times before the first inline definition. Quote: "13 cannon on the crest above them" — this is the first place "artillery" is clarified as cannon. But "artillery" appears earlier (Spectators section: "lots of cannon fire") without definition. Considered acceptable since "cannon fire" is used alongside — CLEAR at the cannon use, but "artillery batteries" below is a harder case (see next).

**"artillery batteries — the battery being a cluster of cannon, here about 11 guns under captains James Ricketts (North) and Charles Griffin (North)"**
- Status: CLEAR (defined inline) — this is a good gloss.

**"Wade Hampton's Legion"**
- MUST-FIX — "Legion" as a military unit is unexplained. A 15-year-old reads "Hampton's Legion" and has no idea if this is 10 men or 10,000. It was approximately a regiment-sized combined arms unit. Quote: "men of Wade Hampton's Legion guarded his flanks."
- Suggest: "men of Wade Hampton's Legion — a South Carolina regiment-sized force of infantry, cavalry, and artillery — guarded his flanks."
- Also: Wade Hampton (South) — no side-tag on first mention.

**"the brigade of Brigadier General Edmund Kirby Smith (South)… command passed to Colonel Arnold Elzey (South), who led the brigade in a charge that smashed into the Union right on Chinn Ridge"**
- NEEDS-GLOSS — "Chinn Ridge" named with no location context. Where is it relative to Henry House Hill? The reader is already lost geographically. Suggest: "…on Chinn Ridge, just to the west of Henry House Hill."

**"the Union right"**
- NEEDS-GLOSS — "right" in military context (the right side of a line, looking toward the enemy) is not obvious to a newcomer. The draft uses "flank" earlier (defined), but "right" vs. "left" in army positioning is still jargon. Suggest: "the right end of the Union line" at first use with "right" in this sense — or tie it back to "flank": "the right flank of the Union line."

**"Colonel Jubal Early's (South) brigade"**
- Status: CLEAR (side-tag present)

**"the rebel yell — a sound that witnesses on both sides remembered for the rest of their lives, somewhere between a scream and a howl"**
- Status: CLEAR (defined and characterized inline)

**"corps"**
- Status: NOT PRESENT in draft — good, the draft avoids the term. No flag needed.

**"regiment"**
- NEEDS-GLOSS — "regiment" appears in the draft ("the 33rd Virginia," "two Union regiments," "a regiment he had helped recruit") without definition. It's used as a size-unit with no gloss anywhere in the draft. Suggest first use (earliest is "two Union regiments" in the 90-day war section): add "(a regiment was roughly 1,000 men, the basic building block of the army)."

**"rear guard"**
- MUST-FIX — used twice ("McDowell tried to organize a withdrawal with a rear guard to cover it"; "attempt to push brigades forward to intercept the retreat ran into McDowell's rear guard") without definition. Quote: "McDowell tried to organize a withdrawal with a rear guard to cover it."

### Overwhelming / leap passages

**"two divisions under Hunter and Heintzelman"**
- LOST — both names appear once and vanish. No first name, no rank, no side-tag, no character note. At this point the reader has already met McDowell, Tyler, Beauregard, Johnston, Patterson, and the narrative is about to add Evans, Bee, Bartow, Sherman, Jackson, Bee again, Ricketts, Griffin, Kirby Smith, Elzey, Early — Hunter and Heintzelman are noise without a sentence of grounding. Minimal fix: side-tags + one-phrase ID ("two of McDowell's division commanders").

**The flanking-column geography passage (Sudley Springs section)**
- NEEDS-GLOSS — the spatial choreography (Stone Bridge / Warrenton Turnpike / Sudley Springs Ford / Matthews Hill / Henry House Hill / Chinn Ridge) accumulates without a single orientation sentence. A 15-year-old is tracking six named places on a battlefield they have never seen. Consider one sentence early: "Picture Bull Run as a north-south stream with the Confederate line running behind it, Stone Bridge in the middle, and open country to the north — that open country is where the flank march was headed."

**The Henry House Hill artillery confusion (33rd Virginia blue uniforms)**
- NEEDS-GLOSS — "a Confederate regiment in blue uniforms" is confusing without a sentence explaining that early in the war, uniform colors were not yet standardized and both sides wore various colors. Quote: "At one point a Confederate regiment in blue uniforms, the 33rd Virginia, advanced on the Union guns; Griffin took them for friendly troops and held his fire."
- Suggest add: "Early in the war, uniform colors weren't yet standardized — some Confederate units still wore blue, the same shade as the Union army."

## Verdict

**7 MUST-FIX items.** The worst single offender is **#3/4 — "brigade" and "division" are used as core structural terms throughout the entire draft but never defined anywhere.** A reader who doesn't know what a brigade or division is (and a 15-year-old cold reader does not) cannot follow the troop movements at Matthews Hill, Henry House Hill, or the Chinn Ridge flank attack — which are the spine of the battle story. The army-unit glossary (regiment → brigade → division) needs one single gloss on first use and then the rest of the draft reads cleanly. All other MUST-FIX items are individual missing side-tags or one undefined term each, fixable in a sentence.
