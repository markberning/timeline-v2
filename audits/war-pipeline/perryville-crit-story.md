# Perryville — STORYTELLING Critic Gate (war pipeline step 4)

Judged AS A STORY (#1 goal). Reference voice: Shiloh `section-narrative.tsx`.
Scope: hook & stakes · human dimension (cost FELT?) · play-by-play tedium · dual POV · voice · pacing.
Selective is GOOD — this draft is appropriately selective and is graded on that basis.

**Overall grade: GOOD** (with two MUST-IMPROVE items that, fixed, push it to STRONG).

The draft has a real spine and two genuinely memorable hooks. The biggest gap vs. the Shiloh reference is **texture at ground level** — Shiloh lets you hear Sherman's three dead horses and Grant under the tree with a cigar; Perryville narrates the human cost more than it makes you feel it. The two signature hooks (water-in-drought, acoustic shadow) land — but the acoustic-shadow section currently buries its own best image under a play-by-play of brigade timestamps. Details below.

---

## Section 1 — `the-invasion` · "Bragg's Bid for a Slave State"

**Grade: STRONG**

The hook is the right hook and it's well chosen: not "an army marched into Kentucky" but "the Confederacy tried something it had never tried before and would never manage again — it sent an army north to take a state away from the United States." That's a stakes-first opening in the Shiloh mold.

- Stakes land cleanly and slavery is named in the section's OWN prose (framing requirement met, ship-blocking): *"one army moving to widen slavery's reach, a federal government openly moving to end it."* That collision-sentence is the strongest single line in the section.
- The Frankfort inauguration is a gift of a detail and the draft knows it: *"The 'government' of Confederate Kentucky lasted about an afternoon."* That's house voice — wry, compressed, true. Keep.
- The drought turn is well placed as the section's button: *"It would do its fighting thirsty."* Good muscular transition into the next section's hook.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE (minor):**
- The Emancipation paragraph is told at altitude ("The war's purpose was visibly turning…"). It's correct and important but it's the one stretch that reads like exposition rather than scene. Compare Shiloh, which always anchors the abstract to a body or an object. Optional: one concrete beat — the artillery actually interrupting Hawes mid-sentence is *right there* in the next paragraph; consider pulling a sliver of that physicality up, or trust it and leave as is. Not blocking.

## Section 2 — `the-fight-for-water` · "A Battle That Began at the Creek"

**Grade: STRONG**

This is the first signature hook and it lands hard. The framing — *"Of all the things two armies kill each other over, water is one of the oldest, and at Perryville it was almost literally the trigger"* — is exactly the kind of essayistic-but-grounded opener Shiloh uses. And the payoff line is the keeper:

- *"Men were shooting at each other for the right to drink."* That is the whole hook in eight words. Excellent.
- The Sheridan/Gilbert "told to stop" beat is doing real narrative work — it plants the held-back-Union motif that the acoustic-shadow section pays off: *"It would not be the last time that day a Union commander held back a man who wanted to fight."* Good throughline engineering; this is dual-POV setup done right (we feel the Union dysfunction from inside).
- The Gilbert acting-rank parenthetical is necessarily there for accuracy but it's a speed bump mid-momentum. Acceptable — it's brief — but it's the one spot where the fact-ledger machinery shows through the prose.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE (minor):**
- The closing paragraph ("What none of this told either side…") is slightly over-explained — it tells us a major battle is coming three different ways (not where Buell thought / Bragg misread / about to throw a hard punch). Tighten to one clean statement of the irony. The drought→water→contact→fire chain at the very end is great; the lead-up to it is wordier than it needs to be.

## Section 3 — `the-acoustic-shadow` · "The Battle Buell Never Heard"

**Grade: NEEDS WORK** (the hook is STRONG; the section it's wrapped in is the draft's weakest for tedium)

The hook itself is the best in the draft and it lands like a thunderclap:

- *"while it raged for hours, the general commanding the larger army sat about two miles away and never heard a sound of it."* Perfect. And the explanation is clean and eerie: sound *bending* over the Chaplin Hills, Buell taking real gunfire for *"distant artillery drill."* This is the signature image of the whole battle and the draft nails its setup.

The PROBLEM: after that brilliant opening, the section dissolves into exactly the play-by-play tedium the brief asks me to flag. Roughly the middle third becomes a timestamped brigade ledger:

> *"Around two o'clock, Brigadier General Daniel Donelson's brigade… Forty-five minutes later another brigade… Then, around three o'clock… around twenty to four, Brigadier General Patrick Cleburne… Around four, another Confederate brigade…"*

This is the cadence of a tour-guide plaque, not a story. Five brigades enter on a clock. Most do one thing and exit. The reader cannot hold them, doesn't know why most of them matter, and — worst — **the acoustic-shadow hook gets buried alive under the timestamps it should be controlling.** The whole eerie point is that this slaughter is happening in silence two miles from idle thousands; the brigade-by-brigade clock makes us forget the silence instead of feeling it grow.

**MUST-IMPROVE (this is the one big storytelling fix in the draft):**
1. **Cut the brigade roll-call down to what carries the story.** Open Knob / Maney's three charges is the real event and it's genuinely good — *"They were thrown back, charged again, were thrown back again, and on the third assault they broke through"* is the right rhythm, and two generals dead "within sight of each other" (paid off in section 5) is a strong image. Keep that. Donelson's repulse can stay as the setup punch. But the 2:45 brigade, the 3:40 Cleburne ankle-wound vignette, and the 4:00 Sheridan repulse are three separate clock-stamped fragments that dilute the climax. Cleburne's wounded-ankle-and-kept-going is a nice human beat but it's orphaned — he never returns — so it currently reads as trivia, not character. Either give it a payoff or cut it.
2. **Keep the silence present.** The Shiloh model would periodically cut back to the eerie fact while the killing escalates — e.g., remind us, as Open Knob is being overrun, that Buell still hears nothing. Right now the acoustic shadow is stated at the top and at the bottom but goes dark through the actual carnage. Thread it through so the reader holds both the slaughter AND the silence at once. That juxtaposition IS the story.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE:**
- The force-asymmetry paragraph (~22k engaged vs ~16k vs 55–61k present) is essential and well argued — keep it — but it arrives *before* the fighting and reads statistical. Consider letting the reader feel the asymmetry through the silence first, then land the numbers. Minor sequencing.
- The Noe "high-water mark of the western theater… the Angle at Gettysburg" line is a strong stakes-anchor for a non-expert reader (it tells them how big Open Knob was in terms they know). Keep it.

## Section 4 — `the-victory-and-the-retreat` · "Winning the Field, Losing the State"

**Grade: GOOD**

The section's job is the central irony — won the field, lost the state — and it delivers it with the right turn:

- *"And then Braxton Bragg did the thing that turned his victory to ash."* Strong pivot sentence, house voice.
- The Polk-bluffs-his-way-out anecdote is a terrific bit of theater and is correctly hedged ("is said to," "as reported") without killing the fun. Keep — it's the kind of vivid, slightly-unbelievable detail Shiloh leans into (cf. Forrest's human shield).
- The closing line of the Meanwhile sidebar is the section's best: *"The hardest thing for that army to swallow was that the victory and the retreat were both true at once."* That's the theme in one sentence.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE:**
- The retreat is narrated more than felt. We're *told* Bragg "looked at his situation in the cold light of that night and saw how exposed he was," then given a tidy list of threats (Crittenden's fresh corps, Gilbert's center, the arriving army). It's logically clear but it's a staff-meeting summary, not a scene. The Shiloh reference would give us one concrete image — a body, a campfire, the gray column starting to move in the dark. The Meanwhile sidebar actually *does* this ("the long gray column trudging southeast," the 900 wounded left behind) better than the main prose does. Consider pulling a beat of that physicality into the body so the retreat is felt, not just justified.
- The "abandoning around nine hundred of his own wounded" fact is the most human, most damning detail of the retreat and it's currently a subordinate clause. It deserves more weight — leaving 900 of your own wounded behind is the cost of the decision made visible. Elevate it.

## Section 5 — `the-reckoning` · "The Bloodiest Afternoon for the Smallest Fight"

**Grade: STRONG**

This is the strongest closer in the draft and it's modeled well on Shiloh's "start with the number" reckoning.

- The outcome verdict is crisp and the gloss is excellent: *"The victory was the retreat it forced, not the field it gave up."* That's a thesis sentence.
- *"Start with the cost, because the cost is the part that doesn't fit the size of the fight"* — directly echoes Shiloh's "start with the number, because the number is the whole story," which is the right instinct. And the draft does the smarter thing: the headline isn't the total, it's the **rate** — *"roughly one in five of all the troops engaged became a casualty."* That's the genuinely notorious fact and the draft makes the reader feel why (the killing "packed into one corps' worth of men"). Strong.
- The acoustic shadow is paid off here as the strangest human fact, and the payoff is the best human-dimension writing in the whole draft: *"thousands of Union soldiers stood within a couple of miles of that slaughter, never hearing it, never called, while their comrades were cut down on a ridge over the next rise."* This is cost FELT. More of the draft should read like this.
- Slavery throughline restated and closed cleanly (framing requirement met a second time): *"Kentucky would not be a Confederate slave state. It would be a Union one, on its way, with the rest of the country, out of slavery altogether."* Strong final cadence.
- Commander reckonings (Buell relieved/"slept through his own battle," Bragg summoned to Richmond) land with the right edge, and the Stones River hand-off sets up the next battle without over-explaining.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE (minor):**
- The casualty-range parentheticals ("4,211 to 4,241… 'the 4,421 figure is a transcription error, not used'") let the fact-ledger machinery leak into the prose. The reader doesn't need the typo-disclaimer — that's a note for the fact-checker, not the page. Move it to the ledger and give the prose a clean rounded number.
- Two generals dying "within sight of each other" is reused verbatim from section 3. It's a great image but landing it twice slightly dulls it. Pick the stronger placement (probably here, in the reckoning) and vary the other.

## Cross-cutting notes

**VOICE:** Consistently on-target for the house style — wry, compressed, confident, plain-language-with-stakes. Standout lines ("lasted about an afternoon," "shooting at each other for the right to drink," "turned his victory to ash," "the part that doesn't fit the size of the fight") match the Shiloh register exactly. The voice is NOT sanded down. No fix needed.

**DUAL POV:** Present and well-executed on the Union side — the held-back-commanders motif (Sheridan→Gilbert→Buell's silence) is a real structural spine, and we feel the Union dysfunction from inside. The **Confederate interior POV is thinner.** We get Bragg's cold-night calculation in section 4, but the Southern soldier's experience — winning a hill at terrible cost only to be marched out of the state — is mostly delivered in the Meanwhile sidebars, not the body. Shiloh balances both armies' interior more evenly. SHOULD-IMPROVE, not blocking: give one body-level Confederate beat in the main prose (Open Knob from the attackers' side, or the gray column's bitterness at the retreat).

**HUMAN DIMENSION / COST FELT:** This is the draft's most consistent shortfall and the through-line of the SHOULD-IMPROVEs above. The draft *names* cost well but *renders* it less often than Shiloh does. The two places it truly makes you feel it — the acoustic-shadow payoff in section 5, and the 900 abandoned wounded — are the model; the rest narrates the cost at one remove. The town of 300 becoming "one vast field hospital" / "the wounded of both armies laid out by the thousand" is stated but never shown at the scale of a single body the way Shiloh shows Johnston's boot filling with blood. One or two concrete, body-level images planted in the main prose would close most of this gap.

**PACING / PLAY-BY-PLAY TEDIUM:** Only one real offender — the timestamped brigade roll-call in the middle of section 3 (flagged as that section's MUST-IMPROVE). Everywhere else the selectivity is good and the draft moves. Sections 1, 2, and 5 are well paced.
