## The job / arc
The first causes chapter. It picks up EXACTLY where the French & Indian War ended (that
war's last off-field chapters were the war debt, the Proclamation Line, and the road to
taxation — this is a direct handoff). Shape: **pressure** (a victorious Britain, broke,

(the end of "salutary neglect": the Proclamation Line, the Sugar Act, the Currency Act,

Stamp Act).

## The setup — victory, and the debt
- The Seven Years' War / French and Indian War ended with the Treaty of Paris, signed
  **10 February 1763**. Britain won enormously: it took all of New France (Canada) and all
  French territory east of the Mississippi, plus Spanish Florida. [confirmed]
- The war roughly **DOUBLED Britain's national debt** — commonly given as growing from
  about **£75 million before the war to about £130–137 million by 1763/1764**. [VERIFY the
  exact figures; the doubling is the safe, well-supported claim.]
- Servicing that debt ate over half the annual national budget. British taxpayers at home
  were already among the most heavily taxed in Europe (the land tax, the cider tax that
  helped topple the previous ministry). [VERIFY cider-tax detail]
- Britain decided to keep a **standing army in North America** after the war — on the
  order of **~10,000 regulars** [VERIFY exact number] — to garrison the new conquests,
  hold the frontier, and police Native relations. Estimated cost on the order of
  **£200,000–£350,000 a year** [VERIFY range]. London's logic: the colonies were the chief
  beneficiaries of the war and the garrison, so they should help pay for it. The colonists'
  later view: a standing army in peacetime, quartered among them, looked less like
  protection than like control.

## Salutary neglect, and its end
- For decades before 1763 Britain governed the colonies with a light hand sometimes called
  **"salutary neglect"** — trade laws (the Navigation Acts) existed but were loosely
  enforced, and the colonies ran their own internal affairs through their elected
  assemblies, which controlled taxes and the governors' salaries. Smuggling (especially of
  molasses) was routine and tolerated. [confirmed — standard]
- After 1763 the new ministry reversed this: enforce the trade laws, raise revenue
  directly from the colonies, and bring them under tighter Crown control. The man who drove
  it: **George Grenville**, First Lord of the Treasury and effectively prime minister
  **1763–1765**, tasked with the war debt. [confirmed]

## The Proclamation of 1763
- Issued by **King George III on 7 October 1763**, after the Treaty of Paris transferred
  French North America to Britain. [confirmed]
- It drew a line along the crest of the **Appalachian Mountains** and (at least temporarily)
  **forbade all new colonial settlement west of it**, reserving that land as an
  **Indian Reserve**. [confirmed]
- The immediate trigger was **Pontiac's War** (the 1763 Native uprising across the Great
  Lakes and Ohio Country — covered in the F&I war's aftermath chapter): London wanted to
  stop the cheap land-grab that was provoking Native war. [confirmed — links to F&I
  pontiacs-war]
- Effect on colonists: fury. Colonial charters claimed land to the Pacific; veterans had
  been promised western land; speculators (including a young **George Washington**, who
  privately called the Proclamation "a temporary expedient to quiet the minds of the
  Indians" and kept acquiring western claims) had fortunes staked on the Ohio Country.
  [VERIFY the Washington quote wording + that he kept buying western land]
- The line was poorly enforced and settlers crossed it anyway, but the principle stung: for
  the first time London was telling colonists where they could and could not go on their own
  continent. [standard]

## The Sugar Act (American Revenue Act) 1764
- Passed by Parliament **5 April 1764**. [confirmed]
- It revised the old **Molasses Act of 1733**, which had taxed molasses at **6 pence per
  gallon** but was never effectively collected because colonists evaded it. The Sugar Act
  **cut the rate in half, to 3 pence per gallon**, but paired the cut with **strict
  enforcement** — the aim was a duty low enough that paying it beat smuggling, so it would
  actually be collected. [confirmed]
- Its preamble said the quiet part out loud: "it is just and necessary that a revenue
  should be raised ... for defraying the expenses of defending, protecting, and securing"
  the colonies. This made it, in effect, **the first act of Parliament explicitly designed
  to raise revenue FROM the colonies** (as opposed to merely regulating their trade).
  [confirmed via preamble]
- Enforcement teeth: smuggling cases were moved to **vice-admiralty courts** — tried by a
  Crown-appointed judge, **with no jury**, in Halifax, Nova Scotia. To colonists used to
  local juries, this was a second alarm: not just a new tax, but justice without a jury of
  your peers. [VERIFY Halifax / no-jury detail]
- It hit New England hard, where the rum-from-molasses trade was central; it landed in the
  middle of a **postwar economic depression**. [standard]

## The Currency Act 1764
- Parliament extended/passed the **Currency Act in 1764**, restricting the colonies' power
  to issue **paper money as legal tender**. The goal was to protect British merchants and
  creditors from being repaid in depreciated colonial currency. [confirmed]
- Effect: it tightened an already-tight money supply in a postwar slump, squeezing colonial
  debtors and merchants. [standard]
- HONEST FRAMING (anti-overstatement, from the source): modern economic historians do NOT
  rank colonial debts to British merchants among the powerful causes of the Revolution — a
  1995 survey found ~92% of economists and ~74% of historians disagreed that those debts
  were a leading cause. So: the Currency Act fed the *sense* of grievance and squeeze, but
  the chapter should not overstate it as a prime mover. [confirmed — use to stay fair]

## The argument begins
- These measures, taken together, started colonists thinking about the CONSTITUTIONAL
  question, not just the money: did Parliament — in which the colonies had no elected
  members — have the right to tax them for revenue?
- **James Otis Jr.** of Massachusetts published *The Rights of the British Colonies
  Asserted and Proved* (**1764**), an early, influential statement of the case; the slogan
  it crystallized was **"no taxation without representation."** [VERIFY Otis title + 1764
  date; the phrase's attribution is debated — present it as the idea crystallizing, not as
  one man's coinage]
- The distinction colonists themselves drew at this stage: between **"external" taxes**
  (duties to regulate trade, long accepted) and **"internal" taxes** (a tax laid simply to
  raise revenue, which they argued only their own assemblies could levy). The Sugar Act sat
  uneasily on that line; the Stamp Act (1765) would land squarely on the "internal" side
  and blow the argument wide open. [standard — sets up next chapter]

## Fairness / framing notes (for the framing critic — Revolution re-point)
- This is NOT a story of pure villainy. Britain's debt was real, the war had partly been
  fought for the colonies, and asking the colonies to help pay was, from London, reasonable.
  Tell it as a collision of two reasonable-sounding positions hardening into a quarrel.
- Do not render independence as inevitable or pre-ordained in 1764 — almost no one wanted
  it yet; colonists were arguing for their rights AS BRITONS.
- Keep the Native dimension visible: the Proclamation Line was, in part, an attempt to keep
  faith with Native nations after Pontiac's War, and colonial fury at it was fury at being
  told they couldn't take Native land.
- The liberty-and-slavery contradiction belongs to later chapters; don't force it here, but
  don't write "liberty" language naively either.

### Grenville, the man (storytelling MUST-IMPROVE — humanize him)
- George Grenville (1712–1770): a trained lawyer and a financial specialist, twice
  Treasurer of the Navy, who became First Lord of the Treasury / prime minister on Bute's
  resignation in April 1763. Famous for mastery of fiscal detail and for parliamentary
  tedium (a meticulous, exhausting man of numbers). He came to power with the war debt as
  his defining problem and approached the colonies as an accounting problem to be solved.
  [verified — George Grenville]
- He did NOT act blind: before the Sugar Act he studied the colonial customs system and
  consulted colonial agents in London. Present this as: he gave the colonies notice and
  thought he was being reasonable. [VERIFY the agent-consultation specifics; the year-of-
  study characterization is standard. Do not name a specific agent unless the fact-checker
  pins it.]

### Virtual representation (comprehensiveness MUST-ADD — the British counter-doctrine)
- The British constitutional answer to "we have no MPs" was **virtual representation**: the
  doctrine that every member of Parliament represented the interest of the WHOLE nation and
  all its subjects, not just the district that elected him, so the colonists were
  represented like the many Britons (e.g. whole cities such as Manchester and Birmingham)
  who also elected no MP. Colonists rejected this and demanded **actual representation**
  (their own elected members, or no taxation). The fullest statement of virtual
  representation came from Grenville's circle (Thomas Whately, 1765); for a 1763–64 chapter,
  present it as the British theory the colonists were arguing AGAINST, without over-precising
  the date. This is the half of the debate the colonial side was answering — without it the
  British look merely arrogant rather than holding a real constitutional theory. [verified —
  Virtual representation]

### Vice-admiralty courts — why they were alarming (comprehensiveness MUST-ADD)
- Beyond "no jury": under the Sugar Act's regime the **burden of proof was reversed** — a
  merchant whose goods were seized had to prove they had been legally imported, rather than
  the Crown proving they had not. And customs officers / informers received a **share of the
  forfeited cargo**, a built-in incentive to seize. To colonists raised on common-law jury
  trial and the presumption of innocence, this felt less like a court than a rigged
  collection machine. [burden-reversal + officer's-share are standard; mark the precise
  judge's-personal-fee specific as [VERIFY] and use the officer/informer-share wording.]
