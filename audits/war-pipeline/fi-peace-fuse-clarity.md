## Unexplained Leaps (Comprehension Gaps)

### MUST-FIX

**1. WHY winning the war led to taxing the colonists — the logic gap**
"The Bill Comes Due" gives the debt number and then says the government decided colonists "ought to help pay for it." But WHY the colonists specifically? The draft doesn't explain that Britain taxed its own citizens heavily too, and that Grenville turned to the colonies only after a cider tax back home caused riots (mentioned in passing but not connected). The reader needs: "Britain had borrowed enormous sums to fight a war partly on colonial soil. It now kept troops in America to guard the new territory. Grenville argued the colonists were the direct beneficiaries, so they should share the cost — but the colonists had no seat in Parliament to agree or object." Without that scaffolding, the pivot to "no taxation without representation" feels abrupt.

**2. WHY the Proclamation Line angered colonists — the land-promise gap**
The draft says "ordinary veterans had been promised western land for their service" but never explains WHO promised it, when, or how formal that promise was. A 15-year-old reads this and wonders: was it a signed contract? A handshake? A colonial law? The fury makes more sense if the reader knows these were official provincial land grants, not vague hopes. One sentence would fix it.

**3. The Bourbon / Family Compact section — side-tags missing**
"France and Spain, both ruled by branches of the Bourbon royal family" — the reader has been told France is the enemy of Britain. Now Spain enters. The draft says Britain declared war on Spain, which is clear. But "the Third Family Compact" implies there were a First and Second — this confuses without adding anything. Just call it "a formal alliance" the first time, then mention the name parenthetically. SHOULD-FIX.

**4. HOW gift-giving connected to food / survival**
The draft explains Amherst cut gift-giving and also restricted gunpowder. It says "to the nations it was something far more serious, the tribute and reciprocity that signified an ongoing relationship, the rent paid for permission to occupy Native land." This is well-written but skips a concrete step: the reader doesn't yet know that Native hunters had shifted to using European guns and ammunition for food, so cutting ammo was literally cutting the food supply. The draft mentions this in the ammo sentence but it reads as a separate point, not as "this is why the gift-cutting was an existential threat, not an insult." Needs one connective sentence.

**5. WHY losing France changed everything for Native nations (the balance argument)**
"The balance that had let them hold their ground for generations, the ability to play France against Britain, died with French power on the mainland." This is stated but not shown. The reader hasn't seen how this balance worked in practice — the draft describes the aftermath (Amherst's contempt, the cuts) but not the mechanism of the old balance. A short "Before: with France in Canada, a nation could tell Britain 'we'll trade with the French if you mistreat us' — that threat gave them leverage" would make the loss tangible. SHOULD-FIX.

### SHOULD-FIX

**6. "No taxation without representation" — the concept**
The phrase is quoted and the protest is described. But a 15-year-old needs one sentence: "The colonists sent no elected members to the British Parliament in London, so Parliament had no right, they argued, to impose taxes on them — that was the job of their own colonial assemblies." The draft assumes this is understood. It mostly lands but could break for readers with no civics background.

**7. The "meanwhile" block at the end**
The "meanwhile" block (region: the colonies) is a summary, not new content — that's fine. But it uses "garrison" (still undefined at that point in the text) again. Confirm a gloss exists earlier before this block ships.

## Overwhelming / Wall-of-Text Passages

- **The Pontiac's War section** ("The Blankets at Fort Pitt"): dense but well-broken by subheads. No single paragraph is a wall. CLEAR.
- **"The Fuse" closing section**: ambitious synthesis — three-clause escalation chain. For a sharp teen it works. For a slower reader the "every solution fed the next grievance" chain is abstract. SHOULD-FIX with one concrete example sentence to anchor it.
- **The fort-name cascade in "Pontiac's War"** ("Fort Sandusky, Fort St. Joseph, Fort Miami…"): eight fort names in one sentence. A reader at speed will skim this, which is fine — the point is cumulative. CLEAR (structure is doing the work).

## Overall Verdict

The draft is well-structured and largely clear-voiced. The biggest comprehension risk is the taxation logic chain — a sharp teen can follow "Britain got into debt" and "Britain taxed the colonists," but the *why* of singling out the colonies specifically is skipped, and without it the colonists' fury reads as abstract. Second-biggest risk: "cede/ceded" is used at the most pivotal moment (the treaty paragraph) with no gloss.

The Native nations material (Neolin, Pontiac, the balance argument) is the strongest section in terms of clarity — it shows rather than tells. The closing "The Fuse" synthesis is ambitious but earns it.
