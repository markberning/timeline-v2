## Must-Cover Checklist

### 1. Pitt's Grand Strategy / Three-Arrow Design
- [x] **COVERED** — Pitt as non-soldier London architect; the three prongs named, targeted, and mapped to commanders; purpose (simultaneous pressure so French can't concentrate) is explicit. The "three arrows, one target" formulation is memorable and correct.

### 2. The Annus Mirabilis Frame
- [x] **COVERED** — Named, defined, and anchored to the correct Latin in the opening. Quiberon Bay is mentioned in the Sainte-Foy section as the reason no French ships came, which is the right structural placement.
  - **SHOULD-CONSIDER:** The draft treats Quiberon Bay (November 1759) only in the Sainte-Foy section as a retroactive explanation. A single bridging sentence in the opening or closing of "The Western Pillar Falls" noting that 1759's Atlantic victories (Minden, Lagos, Quiberon) sealed the North American ones would show the global-war interlocking without requiring a detour. Not blocking.

### 3. Fort Niagara: Strategic Stakes and the Siege
- [x] **COVERED** — "Cork in the bottle" framing is strong. Pouchot's undermanning (500 vs. earlier 2,500 sent away) is present. Prideaux's death by friendly fire is covered and appropriately hedged ("shell fragment" — correct; the "decapitated" ABT version wisely avoided). Johnson's command succession and the royal-commission authority argument are both here.

### 4. La Belle Famille and Iroquois Neutrality — Native Agency
- [x] **COVERED** — This is among the draft's best passages. The key beats are all present:
  - Iroquois-to-Iroquois message (kin over alliance)
  - Warriors melting away before the battle
  - The chokepoint ambush
  - Casualty asymmetry
  - Pouchot's disbelief upon learning his relief was destroyed
  - The chain-reaction abandonment of western forts (Fort Rouillé named)
  - **Minor gap:** The fact pack names **Eyre Massey** (British, 46th Foot) as the regular-army commander of the British force at La Belle Famille — the draft names only Johnson. Massey is a minor figure and his omission is not blocking, but he commanded the regulars while Johnson commanded the Iroquois. **SHOULD-CONSIDER** naming him.
  - **Minor gap:** The draft doesn't name **Michel Maray de La Chauvignerie** (French), third co-commander of the relief force. Omission is fine at this altitude.

### 5. Amherst's Lake Champlain Prong — Why It Stalled in 1759
- [x] **COVERED** — The French demolition of both forts, the almost-free British capture, the contrast with 1758's 2,000 casualties at the same ground, and Amherst's halt to build a new fort are all present. This section is appropriately brief (it's a narrative of non-battle), and the framing ("two of Pitt's three arrows") keeps it structurally connected.
  - **SHOULD-CONSIDER:** The draft says Amherst "stopped to build a new fort" but doesn't name it (Fort Amherst/Fort Crown Point). Naming it would help readers who later look it up. Not blocking.

### 6. The Quebec Bombardment — Human Cost
- [x] **COVERED** — Bomb count, cannonball count, carcasses (incendiary shells) explained with correct composition and the loaded-pistol-barrel detail. 503 houses named. Cathedral and Notre-Dame-des-Victoires named with timing. Jean-Félix Recher's eyewitness quote is present and correctly sourced. The women reciting rosaries is a strong human detail.
  - **SHOULD-CONSIDER:** The draft notes the British established a base on Île d'Orléans "in late June 1759" but doesn't give a precise date (fact pack: June 26–28). This is fine — precision here is a nice-to-have. More importantly, the draft doesn't mention that the bombardment began from Point Lévis after a failed French fireship attack (June/July 28 fireships). The fireships were the French's most dramatic active defense of the summer and their failure is a turning point. **SHOULD-CONSIDER** adding a sentence — it shows Montcalm wasn't passive.

### 7. The Scorched-Earth Countryside Campaign
- [x] **COVERED** — 1,400 stone houses/manor farms, the August timeline, Major George Scott named, all six ranger companies noted. Wolfe's written order quoted. The editorial judgment ("widely judged to have backfired") is present and correct. The sentence "Colonists were killed in the burning" is appropriately blunt.
  - **Minor note:** The draft says "the south shore" for Scott's raid, which is accurate. The fact pack specifies "Kamouraska to Pointe-Lévy." The geographic range would help readers understand the scale; **SHOULD-CONSIDER**.

### 8. The Night Landing at L'Anse-au-Foulon
- [x] **COVERED** — Cove location, cliff height (174 ft / 53 m), the sentry deception, the disputed identity of the French-speaking officer (correctly flagged as unresolved), Vergor's failure to raise an effective alarm. Force size on the Plains by dawn (4,400–5,000) is given.
  - **SHOULD-CONSIDER:** William Howe (British) led the light infantry up the cliff — the fact pack names him. The draft skips him. Not blocking at this narrative altitude.

### 9. Battle of the Plains of Abraham — Tactics and the Double Volley
- [x] **COVERED** — Two-rank line innovation, the 30-yard hold-fire, Montcalm's premature advance as his worst decision, Bougainville's failure to arrive in time (and why — he was upstream and missed the night movement), the minutes-long duration of the decisive exchange. All present.
  - **SHOULD-CONSIDER:** The fact pack notes Monckton (British) was wounded, and that Townshend took command in the battle's aftermath — the draft mentions Townshend as the person who accepted the surrender but doesn't trace the command chain through Monckton's wounding → Townshend taking over. Minor.

### 10. Deaths of Both Commanders
- [x] **COVERED** — Wolfe's wounds (wrist, body, chest), the traditional last words, Parkman sourcing, and the "varies in every telling" hedge are all present. The Gray's Elegy anecdote is correctly treated as plausible oral tradition with a named witness (the midshipman) — this is the right epistemic level. The draft skips both these details. The street quote in particular is a strong human moment — **SHOULD-CONSIDER** adding it. Not blocking.
  - **Minor:** Fact pack note about Montcalm's remains being relocated in 2001 to the Hôpital-Général — omitting this is fine; it would be a footnote-level detail.

### 11. Fall of Quebec / Ramezay's Capitulation
- [x] **COVERED** — September 18 date, Ramezay's role as acting governor (Vaudreuil having withdrawn), Townshend accepting the surrender and immediately sailing home, Murray left in command. Compact and accurate.

### 12. Sainte-Foy — French Victory That Changed Nothing
- [x] **COVERED** — Lévis's winter rebuild, the race to retake Quebec before the navy arrived, Murray's aggressive sortie (correctly called aggressive), the two-hour close fight, the British left rolling up, guns spiked and abandoned in retreat, casualties (833 French / 1,182 British), "one of the bloodiest battles on Canadian soil" (correctly uses "one of the" — not absolutized), Lévis's three cannons vs. Quebec's fortifications = no path to assault. All present and accurate.

### 13. Why Montreal's Fall = The End (Three-Army Convergence)
- [x] **COVERED** — All three armies named with commanders, routes, force sizes, and individual challenges: Amherst's Lachine rapids (84 drowned / 46 boats wrecked); Murray disarming riverside parishes and militia deserting; Haviland's Île-aux-Noix action and Bougainville's night evacuation. September 6–7 convergence. The mathematical impossibility of Lévis defending against three simultaneous armies is implicit in the force-size ratio. 
  - **SHOULD-CONSIDER (MILD):** The draft says "some 18,000 British troops" but doesn't explicitly say Lévis's remaining force was a fraction of that. The fact pack doesn't give a Montreal-defense French strength figure, but noting "Lévis had only a skeleton force left" would make the futility visceral. Not blocking.

## BLIND SPOTS in the Fact Pack (flagged for author)

1. **French civilian population of Quebec at the time of the siege** — How many people lived in Quebec City? The bombardment's human scale is vividly described but the population baseline is absent from both the draft and the fact pack. If Quebec had ~8,000–10,000 inhabitants, that context would help readers grasp the proportion of loss. *Research needed before finalizing.*

2. **The French fireship attack (late June/early July 1759)** — The French sent fireships against the British fleet shortly after it arrived; the attack failed. This is absent from both the fact pack and the draft. It matters because it shows: (a) the French were not passive; (b) the navy's resilience was tested early. *Research and add if confirmed.*

3. **Bougainville's Cap Rouge force size and why it was there** — The fact pack and draft both explain that Bougainville was upstream at Cap Rouge and missed the night movement. But neither explains WHY Bougainville's force was there (guarding against exactly this kind of upriver landing — the irony being that it was positioned to prevent Wolfe from landing above the city and failed anyway). *A sentence of context would close this gap.*

4. **Canadian militia's breakdown during the Montreal campaign** — The draft notes that "thousands of Canadian militia, seeing the end, simply deserted." The fact pack doesn't quantify this. The mass desertion is an important social fact: the colony's civilian defense structure dissolved before the armies arrived. *Exact figures would strengthen this; if unavailable, the "thousands" construction in the draft is honest.*

5. **Article 40 — what "no punishment" meant in practice for the nations that fought for France** — The draft correctly calls Article 40's promise "about to prove worthless," but doesn't specify that Native nations received no formal role in the peace negotiations (the Treaty of Paris 1763 disposed of their lands without consulting them). The fact pack doesn't cover this either. *SHOULD-CONSIDER adding a sentence — it's the most concrete way to show that Article 40 was empty.*

6. **Quiberon Bay casualties** — The fact pack gives: ~2,500 French sailors killed vs. ~300 British. The draft mentions Quiberon Bay but not the lopsided human cost. This detail would reinforce why France simply could not send a relief fleet — it wasn't a political choice; it was a physical absence of ships and trained crews. *SHOULD-CONSIDER.*

### MUST-ADD Items: None

The draft is comprehensively structured. Every major beat is present, the Native agency thread is given genuine weight throughout (not just in the closing section), and the human cost of the bombardment and scorched-earth campaign is appropriately foregrounded. No blocking gaps found.

### Most Important SHOULD-CONSIDER

**The French fireship attack** — the only moment of active French naval initiative in the entire Quebec campaign. Its failure is the hinge that made the bombardment unstoppable: once the fireships failed, the British battery at Point Lévis could operate for months without threat. Without this, the bombardment appears to happen in a vacuum where France never tried to stop it. One paragraph would close it.

**Second most important:** Bougainville's ironic positioning at Cap Rouge — the force placed upstream to prevent exactly this kind of landing, which then failed to respond in time. Adding a sentence explaining WHY he was there sharpens Montcalm's tragedy from "he was unlucky" to "his own precaution defeated him."
