# Chickamauga — NEWCOMER/CLARITY critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

> Read COLD as a sharp 15-year-old with ZERO Civil War knowledge. Every flagged
> item is a term, leap, actor, or passage that a smart newcomer hits and stalls on.
> Verdicts: **CLEAR** (a beginner gets it) · **NEEDS-GLOSS** (term/leap used without
> the explanation the reader needs) · **LOST** (a newcomer cannot follow without help).
> MUST-FIX = ship-blocking for clarity. SHOULD-FIX = improves but not blocking.
> This gate does NOT judge facts (that is the fact-checker) — only whether a
> zero-knowledge reader can follow.

---

## Headline verdict

The draft is **unusually beginner-friendly for war prose** — it inline-defines its
worst jargon (Spencer repeating rifle, log breastworks, the LaFayette Road's role,
"river of death") right where it uses them, and it sets the scene for the war's cause
up front. The voice carries a newcomer well. **No passage rates LOST.** But there is a
cluster of military-org words it leans on without ever defining — **corps, division,
brigade, wing, Reserve Corps** — plus a few unexplained leaps and one
side-tag/scene-set gap. Those are the MUST-FIX items below. Everything else is polish.

---

## MUST-FIX (clarity ship-blockers)

### 1. The unit-size ladder is never defined — NEEDS-GLOSS
`corps`, `division`, `brigade`, `wing`, `column`, `regiment` are used constantly and
never explained. A 15-year-old does not know a corps is bigger than a division is
bigger than a brigade is bigger than a regiment, or roughly how many men each is.
This is the single biggest comprehension gap in the draft because these words carry
the whole plot — "Wood pulled his entire **division** out of the line," "Longstreet's
**column** of roughly eight **brigades**," "Bragg reorganized into two **wings**,"
"Gordon Granger commanding the Union Reserve **Corps**." The reader feels the scale is
important but can't size it.
- Appearances: "Longstreet's two **divisions**" (line 25), "Longstreet's **corps**"
  (line 31), "reorganize his whole army into two **wings**" (line 39), "Right **Wing**
  under Polk" (line 47), "his entire **division**" (line 49), "roughly eight
  **brigades**, thousands of men stacked several lines deep" (line 53), "his **corps**
  commanders" (line 55), "broken **regiments**" / "a few **regiments** at a time"
  (lines 35, 37), "the Union Reserve **Corps**" (line 69).
- **Fix:** one short inline gloss on first use of each rung — ideally a single sentence
  early (the-maneuver or the-woods) that stacks the ladder: army → corps → division →
  brigade → regiment, with rough head-counts. The "eight brigades, thousands of men"
  phrase (line 53) is the one spot that *does* hint at scale; lean on that pattern
  everywhere. "Division" especially must be sized before line 49, because the size of
  the hole Wood opens is the hinge of the whole battle.

### 2. "in detail" / "defeat in detail" risk — CLEAR as written, keep it that way
The factpack/skill list flags "in detail" as jargon to watch. **Good news: the draft
never uses the phrase "in detail" or "defeat in detail."** It expresses the concept in
plain language instead — "each one too far from the others to help quickly if one got
hit" (line 23), "strike Rosecrans's strung-out, separated columns" (line 31). This is
exactly right. **No fix — flagging only so a later revision does not reintroduce the
jargon.**

### 3. "rolled them up" / "rolling his assault from north to south" — NEEDS-GLOSS
"rolled them up" (line 55) and "rolling his assault from north to south... the blow
traveling down the line" (line 47) are cavalry/infantry idiom. A newcomer half-pictures
it but does not know "roll up a line" means attacking along the length of a line so it
collapses sideways unit by unit, like dominoes. The "north to south... traveling down
the line" phrasing (line 47) partly self-explains, which helps — but "rolled them up"
on line 55 stands bare.
- **Fix:** one clause on first use, e.g. "rolled them up — collapsing the line
  sideways, one unit folding into the next." Low-cost, removes the last opaque idiom.

### 4. Scene-set gap: who Longstreet is, on first appearance — NEEDS-GLOSS
Longstreet is introduced cleanly in the-maneuver Meanwhile (line 25: "Lt. Gen. James
Longstreet's two divisions... sent on a long, roundabout rail journey") — that part is
fine. But by the-gap (line 53) he is "the wing commander who had brought his divisions
all the way from Virginia," which re-grounds him well. **This is actually handled.**
The real gap is smaller: **Gordon Granger (line 69)** appears for the first and only
time at the climax with no prior setup — "commanding the Union Reserve Corps off to the
north, marched his men toward the sound of the guns without waiting for orders." A
newcomer asks "who is this, and why was he sitting off to the north?" The "marched to
the sound of the guns without waiting for orders" is a great beat but the reader needs
one half-clause establishing that Granger was a fresh reserve force held back from the
fighting — otherwise the rescue feels like a character teleporting in.
- **Fix:** add a few words establishing Granger = fresh reserve troops not yet
  committed, posted north to guard the road. (Also fold in the "Reserve Corps" gloss
  from item 1 here.)

### 5. "second-growth forest" — NEEDS-GLOSS (minor but real)
"dense, thick second-growth forest" (line 33). A 15-year-old likely does not know
"second-growth" means forest that regrew after the original was cleared/logged — i.e.
young, dense, scrubby trees, which is *why* it's so impenetrable. The draft does
immediately describe the effect ("so choked with timber and brush that men could not
see across it"), which rescues comprehension of the *consequence* even if the term
itself is opaque. Borderline.
- **Fix (light):** either drop "second-growth" (the description after it does the work)
  or add two words: "second-growth forest (young, dense trees grown back over cleared
  land)." Lean toward just cutting the term since the effect is already spelled out.

## SHOULD-FIX (polish; not ship-blocking)

### 6. "Minié ball" — SHOULD-FIX
"hit by a Minié ball that shattered his right leg" (line 89). Undefined. A newcomer
guesses "a bullet" from context (it shatters a leg), so it's not LOST, but the specific
term is unexplained jargon. One parenthetical — "(the cone-shaped rifle bullet of the
war)" — would close it. Low priority because context carries it.

### 7. "mounted infantry" vs "cavalry" — SHOULD-FIX
"Union cavalry and mounted infantry" (line 35), "Wilder's 'Lightning Brigade' of
mounted infantry." A sharp newcomer wonders how mounted infantry differs from cavalry.
Not blocking — the reader gets "guys on horses with guns" and moves on — but a
five-word gloss (mounted infantry = foot soldiers who ride to the fight, then dismount
to shoot) would sharpen it and set up why the Spencer-rifle firepower mattered.

### 8. The Tullahoma callback assumes the reader retains it — CLEAR, watch on revise
"The threat worked exactly as Tullahoma had" (line 21) and "just as Tullahoma had"
land fine because Tullahoma was defined one paragraph earlier (line 17, "the Tullahoma
campaign... maneuvered the Confederate army out of Middle Tennessee almost without
bloodshed"). Good. **No fix** — noting only that the payoff depends on that earlier
sentence surviving any cut.

### 9. "levering" / "levering his enemy backward" — CLEAR
"levering his enemy backward by threatening his lines rather than charging his front"
(line 17). Slightly literary but self-explained by the contrast clause ("rather than
charging his front"). A newcomer gets it. No fix.

### 10. "Emancipation Proclamation" — CLEAR (just barely)
"after the Emancipation Proclamation, the North's war aim had become the destruction
of slavery itself" (line 15). The sentence itself tells the reader what changed (war
aim → destroying slavery), so even a reader who has never heard the term infers it's
the document that made ending slavery a war goal. It's not formally defined, but the
surrounding sentence does the job. SHOULD-FIX at most: a three-word appositive
("the Emancipation Proclamation, Lincoln's 1863 order freeing enslaved people in the
rebel states") would make it airtight, but it is not LOST.

### 11. "cotton gin" — CLEAR
"cotton had made slaveholding explode after the cotton gin" (line 15). Undefined, but
the sentence's logic (a thing that made cotton/slaveholding explode) lets a newcomer
coast past without confusion. Optional gloss only.

### 12. "chief of staff" — CLEAR
"Rosecrans's young chief of staff Brig. Gen. James A. Garfield" (line 55). A modern
15-year-old has a workable idea of "chief of staff" = top aide. Fine.

### 13. "breastworks" — CLEAR (well-handled)
"log breastworks — walls of stacked timber to fire from behind and force any attacker
to cross open ground to reach them" (line 41). This is the model the rest of the draft
should follow: term + inline definition + why it matters. No fix.

### 14. "Cracker Line" / "Brown's Ferry" — SHOULD-FIX (light)
"a new route into the city — the 'Cracker Line' opened via Brown's Ferry on October 27"
(line 99). "Cracker Line" is in quotes and contextually = the supply route that fed the
starving army ("until the supply crisis was broken in late October by a new route"), so
the function is clear. The *name* itself ("Cracker" = the hardtack crackers the troops
ate) is left unexplained — harmless, mildly mysterious. Optional. Brown's Ferry is a
place name the reader can let wash over them.

### 15. Geography orientation — CLEAR, strong
The draft handles place-relationships unusually well for a newcomer: Chattanooga as
"the rail gateway into the Deep South," the LaFayette Road's spine role explicitly
walked through (line 33), "twelve miles to the northwest" (line 79), Missionary Ridge
and Lookout Mountain introduced as "the heights that ringed the city." A zero-knowledge
reader stays oriented. No fix.

## Overwhelming-passage check

No passage is a wall. The densest is **the-reckoning's casualty paragraph** (lines
85-93): three different casualty tallies (34,000 total; 16,170/18,454 split; Cozzens's
6,000-9,000 + 7,000 single-day estimate) land back-to-back. It does not tip into LOST
because the draft pre-frames it ("Start with the number, because the number tells you
how savage it was") and explicitly labels the Cozzens figure as a single-day, not-total
estimate. A newcomer can follow it. SHOULD-FIX only if a reviewer wants the second/third
tallies softened — but the dual-number presentation is a deliberate fact-honesty choice,
so leave it to the fact-checker, not this gate.

The placeholder Rosecrans quote block (line 19) is already flagged for cut in the draft
and is not a clarity issue.

## Bottom line

**Conditionally CLEAR for a newcomer once the MUST-FIX items land.** Fix the unit-size
ladder (item 1 — the big one), gloss "rolled them up" (item 3), scene-set Granger
(item 4), and lightly handle "second-growth" (item 5). The rest is optional polish.
This draft's instinct to define-where-it-uses (breastworks, Spencer rifle, LaFayette
Road, river-of-death) is exactly right — the gaps are the handful of org/idiom words it
forgot to give the same treatment.
