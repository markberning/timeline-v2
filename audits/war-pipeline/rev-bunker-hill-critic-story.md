# STORYTELLING CRITIC — Battle of Bunker Hill (rev-bunker-hill)

Draft: `audits/war-pipeline/rev-bunker-hill-draft.md`
Bar: `audits/war-pipeline/fi-carillon-draft.md` (shipped, gated)
Scope: story only. Facts are the fact-critic's floor.

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is a strong draft. The arc works: section 1 builds real dread (the leak, the
silent dig within earshot of "All's well," Pollard's decapitation, Prescott on the
parapet), section 2 escalates the three assaults instead of repeating them (beach →
frontal → fire spectacle → second repulse → powder arithmetic → bayonets), and
section 3 lands the meaning and then keeps finding endings that earn their place
(Warren's teeth, the monument "completing the joke," Pollard's unmarked grave as
the closing callback). The both-sides humanity is genuinely there. Two things keep
it from PASS: one sentence of outright meta-narration, and a climax that summarizes
where the house bar (the Carillon "imagine being one of them" abatis passage)
demands we be inside the moment.

Em-dash check: CLEAN — no em-dashes anywhere in reader-facing prose (the ledger's
dashes are internal). Drama-bold check: CLEAN. Both verified line-by-line.

## BLOCKER

### B1. Meta-narration: the narrator announces what he is about to say
Section 2: "The British soldiers' courage deserves saying plainly."
This is the exact locked-rule violation (never narrate the telling — just tell it).
The sentence after it already does the work. Cut the announcement and let the prose
say it plainly, which it then does, beautifully.

Rewrite: delete the sentence. The paragraph becomes:
"...and Howe's own staff was being shot down around him as he walked, on foot, in
the front line. They re-formed and walked back up that hill, in order, into massed
fire from men behind walls, behind officers who fell at a rate that scandalized the
army."
(Optionally open "And still they re-formed..." to keep the beat the announcement was
reaching for.)

### B2. The climax is summarized, not inhabited — the draft never reaches the Carillon bar
The storming of the redoubt is the battle's climax and it gets three brisk sentences:
"The defenders fired their last volley at point-blank range, and then the regulars
came over the parapet. Most of the Americans had no bayonets. There was a brief,
ugly hand-to-hand scrum of clubbed muskets and thrown stones."
Nowhere in the draft do we get the one in-the-moment passage that is the house bar
("Imagine being one of them. You hit a belt of felled trees..."). Everything is
vivid third-person reportage — good, but the climax is the place the story must slow
down and put the reader inside the redoubt. All raw material is already in the
fact ledger (powder gone, one volley left, smoke, no bayonets, clubbed muskets,
thrown stones, Prescott parrying with his sword).

Concrete shape (using only ledger-backed material):
"Imagine standing in that fort. You have been awake for thirty-six hours. You have
one load of powder left, maybe, and no bayonet, and the wall of the redoubt — six
feet of dirt you dug yourself last night — is the only thing between you and an army
that has now dropped its packs. You hold the last volley until the men climbing
toward you are close enough to touch, fire it into their faces, and then the
parapet darkens with regulars coming over the top with eighteen inches of steel,
and what is in your hands is a club."
(Author to redraft in their own words — and WITHOUT the em-dashes used here for
clarity; the point is the move, not my sentences. One such passage in the whole
piece; the beach is the alternative site if the author prefers, but the redoubt is
the climax.)

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. Warren dies before the story ever introduces him
Section 2: "And in the last moments by the redoubt, covering the retreat, Major
General Joseph Warren (American), the president of the Massachusetts Provincial
Congress, who had refused command that morning and fought all day as a volunteer
with a musket, was killed by a musket ball to the head."
This is the first time the section prose has mentioned Warren — his entire
characterization arrives stuffed into the obituary sentence. The dossier bio can't
do this work; the story has to plant him so the death lands. One sentence in the
two-hour-pause paragraph does it (fresh men streaming across the Neck is the natural
hook): e.g. "Among the men coming onto the peninsula was Joseph Warren, the
president of the Massachusetts Provincial Congress, commissioned a major general
three days earlier; offered command twice that afternoon, he refused it both times
and took a musket in the ranks." Then the death sentence can shrink to its punch:
"...Warren, covering the retreat, was killed by a musket ball to the head."

### S2. "Whites of their eyes" reads as a fact-check footnote, not a pleasure beat
Section 2: "The famous order supposedly given that afternoon, 'don't fire until you
see the whites of their eyes,' is legend: it was attributed after the fact to
Putnam, Prescott, Stark, and Gridley, and it was an old European army formula long
before 1775. Nobody can show anyone said it here."
"Nobody can show anyone said it here" is a deflation — the reader hears the air go
out. The legend IS part of the story; tell it with relish. Same badging, different
energy, e.g.: "This is the spot where legend puts the most famous order in American
history: 'don't fire until you see the whites of their eyes.' Legend has never
decided who gave it — it has been handed to Putnam, to Prescott, to Stark, even to
Gridley — and the line was an old European army formula decades before anyone dug
into Breed's Hill. What the defenders verifiably did needs no improving: they held
their fire murderously close and, on order, aimed low." (Again: author's words, no
em-dashes.) Also consider moving it to AFTER the frontal-assault paragraph, so the
first assault's beach-then-front rhythm isn't interrupted mid-stride; as placed it
splits assault one in half.

### S3. The Section-1 MEANWHILE spoils Section 3's ending
MEANWHILE (sec 1): "...after buying the Charlestown peninsula at terrible cost the
British held it for nine months and then gave it back, evacuating Boston altogether
in March 1776."
That is the exact irony Section 3 builds to ("a position bought with a fifth of an
army and then handed over"). Pre-spending it here flattens the payoff two sections
later. Keep the Dorchester block but end it on tension instead of the answer: the
southern heights stayed empty that summer, the arithmetic never changed, and
Dorchester Heights would be heard from again in March 1776. Stop there.

### S4. Section-2 PILL lands on the climax's heels
The slavery-and-the-revolution PILL sits between the fall of the redoubt and the
retreat — the two paragraphs that most need to run uninterrupted. Its anchor (Salem
/ Poor) ends the third-assault paragraph, so the placement is defensible, but
consider moving it below the retreat/Warren paragraph (end of section, above the
MEANWHILE). The link loses a little adjacency; the story keeps its sprint.

### S5. "What they built in one night is the astonishing part."
Narrator labeling his own story ("the astonishing part") — brushes the same meta
rule as B1, though softer because it's voice, not throat-clearing. Easy fix that
keeps the energy: "In one night they built a fort." (Then the dimensions land as
the astonishment instead of being announced as one.)

## TRIM

### T1. The wrong-hill mystery is hedged twice in one paragraph
Section 1: "Why is genuinely unsettled. No participant left a clear account..."
then six lines later "...Blunder or dare, nobody can say for certain." Same beat
twice. Keep "Blunder or dare, nobody can say for certain" (it's the better line,
and it sits right before the name-fossil payoff); cut "Why is genuinely unsettled"
and let "No participant left a clear account" open the explanation. The mystery
badging itself is well woven — this is purely a doubling.

### T2. Opening-paragraph gloss drag
Section 1, sentence two carries a 24-word parenthetical: "(militia were part-time
citizen soldiers, farmers and tradesmen with muskets, as opposed to regulars,
Britain's professional full-time troops)." It stalls the hook ("the strangest army
in America had the king's troops trapped in a town" — a great opener that deserves
a fast second sentence). Tighten to "(part-time citizen soldiers, as opposed to
Britain's professional regulars)" — "farmers and tradesmen with muskets" is lovely
but can move into the flow of the sentence rather than the gloss, or go.

### T3. "inherited both halves of the inheritance"
Section 3: "Washington took command of the army at Cambridge and inherited both
halves of the inheritance." Inherited/inheritance in five words. "...and inherited
both halves of it: an army convinced of itself, and an army nearly out of
gunpowder" — the colon pair is the good part; keep that, lose the echo.

## NIT

### N1. Dossier blurb riddle
Section-3 blurb: "fooled the winners' enemies into overconfidence" — the reader has
to solve for who that is (Britain won → its enemies → the Americans... who lost).
"fooled the losers into overconfidence" is the same irony with zero algebra.

### N2. "isthmus"
Section 2 retreat: "HMS Glasgow and gun-barges raking the isthmus." The piece has
taught the reader "the Neck" (and glossed it); cashing in the taught term is better
than switching vocabulary at speed: "raking the Neck."

### N3. QUOTE setup punctuation inconsistency
Section 3's Clinton setup ends with a colon ("wrote that evening:") where the other
two setups and the Carillon house pattern end on a period ("...wrote home three days
later." / "...remembered it plainly."). Use the period form for all three.

### N4. "by tradition" twice
"by tradition, drove a stake about forty yards out" (sec 2) and "by tradition the
battle's last casualty" (sec 3). House rule is to vary uncertainty language;
swap one (e.g. "the story has him driving a stake...").

### N5. MEANWHILE (sec 3) "the war went on to grade their homework"
On the line for too-cute, but it's inside a MEANWHILE (the step-back register) and
it's the house's informal voice doing its job. Flagging for the author's ear, not
demanding a change.

## WHAT'S WORKING (do not sand down)

- "In June 1775 the strangest army in America had the king's troops trapped in a
  town." — earns the cold open.
- The dig scene: "close enough to the enemy to hear Boston's church bells and the
  harbor watch calling 'All's well' across the water" — the best dread image in the
  piece. Pollard's burial-without-ceremony and the parapet walk in the banyan are
  exactly right, and the Peter Brown QUOTE lands at the dread peak.
- "Clinton was right, and the choice that overruled him bought everything that
  happened that afternoon." — perfect section-1 button; foreshadow, not meta.
- Escalation across the assaults is real, not repetition: the beach as the dead
  plan, the burning of Charlestown as the spectacle between repulses, "the real
  crisis was not courage but arithmetic" as the turn into assault three.
- "The men who had won the battle described it like men who had lost one." — the
  best line in the draft, perfectly placed above the Clinton pull quote, which is
  itself the right pull quote in the right section.
- Both-sides humanity: Howe on foot with his staff destroyed, the Rawdon quote, and
  Laurie's "stuffed the scoundrel" venom (the British view of Warren, doing
  characterization work a paragraph of explanation couldn't) — all of it should
  survive revision untouched.
- Peter Brown bookending (the letter QUOTE at dawn, "balls flew like hail stones"
  in the retreat) — quiet structural craft, keep it.
- Section 3's chain of endings — teeth, monument-as-joke, Pollard's grave — each one
  could close the piece and the actual order is the right one.
- MEANWHILE (sec 2) "A city watching" ties the amphitheater idea to the Homer
  image choice; that's the blocks at their best.
