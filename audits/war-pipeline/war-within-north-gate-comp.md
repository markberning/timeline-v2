## SECTION I — INDEPENDENT MUST-COVER CHECKLIST

Built from first principles via web research (Wikipedia, Britannica, Essential Civil War Curriculum, Bill of Rights Institute, American Battlefield Trust, National Affairs, JALA/Michigan, Indiana Historical Bureau), before reading the draft. DEDUP exclusions applied: the draft/conscription mechanics, the NYC Draft Riots, and the war economy are excluded as Home Front territory.

### Core arc: Pressure → Turning Point → Consequence

**A. The Copperhead / Peace Democrat Movement**
- A1. Who they were — Northern Democrats opposed to the war, demanding negotiated peace; distinction from War Democrats
- A2. What they wanted — "Union as it was, Constitution as it is" slogan; implicit endorsement of slavery-intact status quo
- A3. Geographic strength — lower Midwest / Old Northwest (Ohio, Indiana, Illinois); Southern-roots families; Ohio/Mississippi river-trade dependency
- A4. The name's double origin — (a) Republican snake-slur (eastern copperhead, strikes without rattle); (b) Peace Democrats re-appropriate it as the Liberty head on the copper cent, worn as lapel badge
- A5. Other prominent Copperhead leaders beyond Vallandigham — Horatio Seymour (NY governor), Fernando Wood (NYC mayor), George Pendleton (VP nominee) as supporting cast; Vallandigham is the face and the draft's correct emphasis

**B. Clement L. Vallandigham**
- B1. Identity — Ohio congressman (1858–1863); lost seat to redistricting; Copperhead leader
- B2. Key charges — war hijacked into a war for Black freedom at the cost of white liberties; "King Lincoln" label
- B3. January 14, 1863 House speech — "war for the negro" line (2 weeks after Emancipation Proclamation)
- B4. General Order No. 38 (Burnside, April 13, 1863) — what it said; why it reached civilians and speech
- B5. Mount Vernon speech (May 1, 1863) — defiance of Order No. 38; plainclothes officers taking notes
- B6. Arrest (2:40 a.m., May 5, 1863, ~150 soldiers, Dayton home)
- B7. Military commission trial (May 6–7, 1863); challenge to jurisdiction; conviction; sentence
- B8. Lincoln's commutation to banishment into the Confederacy (May 19, 1863); martyr-avoidance rationale
- B9. Confederacy rejects him; Canada exile (Windsor, Ontario); run for Ohio governor in absentia
- B10. Ohio governor election result (Oct. 13, 1863); decisively defeated; read as national referendum
- B11. Return to Ohio (June 1864, heavy disguise); Lincoln ignores him a second time
- B12. Optional aside: 1871 death (self-inflicted courtroom demonstration)

**C. The Supreme Court and Ex parte Vallandigham (1864)**
- C1. Vallandigham's appeal to the Supreme Court — the Court refused jurisdiction, declining to review military commission proceedings (Feb. 1864, unanimous)
- C2. Significance: the Supreme Court ducked the core question during the war, leaving military commissions unreviewed until *Milligan* (1866)

**D. Lincoln's Defense — The Corning Letter**
- D1. Context: NY Democrats (Erastus Corning) formally protest; Lincoln replies publicly (June 12, 1863)
- D2. Core argument: arrested for acts against the army (obstructing enlistment, encouraging desertion), not for opinions
- D3. "Must I shoot a simple-minded soldier boy" passage
- D4. Fair presentation of both sides — Lincoln's case AND the critics' case (chilling precedent; congressman arrested by night soldiers; tried by officers not jury; banished for speech)

**E. Civil Liberties — Habeas Corpus**
- E1. Plain-language definition of habeas corpus and suspension
- E2. Lincoln's first suspension (April 27, 1861, Philadelphia–Washington line); constitutional controversy (Article I = Congress's article)
- E3. *Ex parte Merryman* (Taney, May 1861): president has no power to suspend; order Merryman released
- E4. Lincoln ignores Taney; July 4, 1861 message to Congress; "Are all the laws but one..."
- E5. Congress finally authorizes suspension: Habeas Corpus Act (March 3, 1863)
- E6. Scale of arrests: contested 13,000–14,000+ figure; Neely (*Fate of Liberty*, 1991) finding that 13,535 is unreliable, true total higher and unknowable; most arrestees were not Northern political dissenters
- E7. Newspaper suppression — *Chicago Times* (June 1863): Burnside suppresses it; 20,000 protest; Lincoln reverses it (June 4); fairness beat (Lincoln curbed his own commanders)

**F. Secret Societies**
- F1. Lineage: Knights of the Golden Circle → Order of American Knights → Order of Sons of Liberty; Vallandigham named supreme commander
- F2. The Northwest Conspiracy: alleged 1864 plan, Confederate agents from Canada, to spark uprising, free POWs (Camp Douglas etc.), pull lower Midwest from war
- F3. Real vs. exaggerated: membership claimed in tens of thousands; grand uprising never materialized; Republicans had strong incentive to inflate; the treason trials as 1864 campaign material
- F4. *Ex parte Milligan* (1866): Lambdin Milligan, military commission conviction overturned; civilians cannot be tried by military commission where civil courts are open; the post-war legal reckoning on the whole military-commission practice

**G. The Election of 1864**
- G1. Grim summer 1864 context: Grant at Petersburg, Sherman stalled before Atlanta, public exhausted, Lincoln expects defeat
- G2. The "Blind Memorandum" (Aug. 23, 1864): Lincoln's private pledge to cooperate with his successor; cabinet signed the sealed envelope sight unseen; revealed Nov. 11, 1864
- G3. Radical challenge: Frémont ("Radical Democracy" third party); his September withdrawal clearing Lincoln's left flank
- G4. The National Union ticket: rebranding, dropping Hamlin, selecting Andrew Johnson (War Democrat, Tennessee); cross-party symbolism
- G5. Democratic convention (Chicago, Aug. 29–31): peace plank written by Vallandigham ("after four years of failure..."); the "war is a failure" declaration
- G6. The McClellan contradiction: war Democrat on a peace platform; repudiation of the plank in his acceptance letter; the ticket at war with itself
- G7. Fall of Atlanta (Sept. 2, 1864): overtook the peace plank within a week of adoption; transformed morale
- G8. The soldier vote: first widespread absentee voting; Lincoln 30,503 of 40,247 army votes (~78%); the army that could have ended Lincoln voted to keep him
- G9. Result: 212–21 electoral; ~55–45% popular; McClellan carries only Kentucky, Delaware, New Jersey; turnout ~74%
- G10. Milestones: first reelection since Jackson (1832); first Northern president reelected; first wartime election since 1812

**H. The Meaning**
- H1. The core fact: a free, scheduled, contested presidential election held mid-civil-war — almost unheard of in world history
- H2. Lincoln's Nov. 10, 1864 framing (whether the government was too strong for liberties or too weak to maintain itself)
- H3. The paradox: the same North that suspended habeas, arrested thousands, banished a congressman, and shut down a paper also reversed its worst suppressions, held the election, and submitted military-commission practice to *Milligan*'s cold peacetime judgment

## SECTION III — FACT-PACK BLIND-SPOT AUDIT

Reviewing the fact pack (`war-within-north-factpack.md`) for gaps that create downstream risk.

**Finding 1 — Ex parte Vallandigham (1864) entirely absent from the fact pack.**
The fact pack covers *Ex parte Merryman* (1861) and *Ex parte Milligan* (1866) but nowhere documents the intermediate Supreme Court ruling: *Ex parte Vallandigham*, 68 U.S. 243 (Feb. 1864), in which the Court unanimously declined jurisdiction over military commission proceedings, leaving military tribunals unreviewable during the war. This is the legal bridge between Merryman and Milligan. The beat is not required for the current draft (which does not mention it), but if a future revision adds it — or if the author or reviewer tries to fact-check what happened when Vallandigham appealed — the pack offers no support. The case is clearly established: Wikipedia / LII / Justia / Constituting America all confirm the unanimous Feb. 1864 ruling.

**Finding 2 — Horatio Seymour and Fernando Wood absent from the fact pack.**
The fact pack correctly makes Vallandigham the face of the Copperheads but does not include any factual scaffolding on Seymour (NY governor, prominent peace Democrat, considered for the 1864 nomination) or Fernando Wood (NYC mayor, also pushed a peace platform). If a revision wanted to widen the picture of Copperhead leadership, the pack provides no support. Low risk for the current draft.

**Finding 3 — The full Corning letter context slightly abbreviated.**
The pack quotes the "wily agitator" passage and the "shoot a simple-minded soldier boy" line but excerpts before the longer passage about "getting a father, or brother, or friend, into a public meeting." The draft uses only the shorter excerpt and flags the longer one in VERIFIED QUOTES. No error in the draft; the pack itself contains the full passage in the VERIFIED QUOTES section. Not a gap, just noting that the full quote (preserved in the pack) is available and the draft correctly excerpts a manageable portion.

**Finding 4 — Camp Douglas named in the fact pack but not the draft.**
The fact pack mentions Camp Douglas (Chicago) as a target of the Northwest Conspiracy POW liberation plot. The draft mentions "Northern POW camps" without naming Camp Douglas. Minor compression; not a factual error.

**Finding 5 — 25 states (not 19–20) passed absentee legislation, per the American Battlefield Trust.**
The fact pack says "about 19–20 Northern states" passed absentee/field-voting laws; the draft uses the same figure. However, the Military.com and American Battlefield Trust sources found in web research suggest the figure may be closer to 25 states. The pack's "19–20" appears to come from the Wikipedia article on the 1864 election. This discrepancy should be verified: the difference is between states that passed new laws vs. states that had some form of soldier-voting provision. The "19–20" figure in the pack and draft is likely the more restrictive count (states with new or expanded laws); "25" may count all states allowing any absentee mechanism. The pack should flag this. Low risk — the draft's framing is appropriately hedged and the soldier-vote numbers themselves (30,503 / 40,247) are consistent across sources.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER (not ship-blocking, but worth the author's attention)

**SC-1 — Ex parte Vallandigham (Supreme Court, Feb. 1864): add a sentence.**
The legal thread in the draft runs: Merryman (1861, Taney rules against Lincoln) → Lincoln ignores it → Milligan (1866, Court curbs military commissions). The missing link is that Vallandigham *did* seek Supreme Court review — and the Court unanimously refused to hear it in February 1864, declining to review military commission proceedings at all during the war. That is why Milligan (1866) is the first time the Court actually weighed in on the merits. One sentence in the "Arrested in the night" or "Great Writ" section would close this gap and make the legal arc more coherent. It also adds a note of institutional cowardice (the Court refusing to engage while the war raged) that sharpens the contrast with the post-war Milligan ruling.

> Suggested placement: After the conviction paragraph in "Arrested in the night, tried by soldiers, banished to the enemy" — note that Vallandigham sought review from the Supreme Court, which in February 1864 unanimously declined jurisdiction, ruling it had no power to review military commission proceedings; the question of their constitutionality would wait until after the war.

**SC-2 — Absentee-voting state count: verify 19–20 vs. 25.**
The fact pack and draft say "roughly nineteen or twenty Northern states" passed absentee/field-voting laws. Web sources suggest the count may be closer to 25 states when all forms of soldier-voting provision are included. This is a minor precision issue, not a factual error, but worth confirming against a primary source (e.g., the American Battlefield Trust article on the soldiers' vote) before the section ships.
