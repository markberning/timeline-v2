# FACT PACK — Battle of Jumonville Glen (May 28, 1754)

*French and Indian War. The ONLY permitted source. Stakes = empire, the Ohio Country, trade, Native land. NO slavery angle (that's the Civil War vertical). Write contested items AS disputed.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- Date: May 28, 1754, at dawn after a rainy overnight march (May 27-28). Also "the Jumonville affair."
- Location: a rocky glen/ravine in present-day Fayette County, Pennsylvania, near Uniontown/Hopwood/Farmington; ~7 miles from Great Meadows/Fort Necessity, beyond Laurel/Chestnut Ridge. State = Pennsylvania. Coords ~39.85,-79.62 (disputed; Wikipedia ~39.879,-79.645).
- Significance: widely seen as the opening shots of the French and Indian War, which became the North American theater of the global Seven Years' War. Horace Walpole's famous line (attribution universal; exact wording/source UNVERIFIED, treat as paraphrase): "The volley fired by a young Virginian in the backwoods of America set the world on fire." The "young Virginian" = George Washington.

## 2. BACKGROUND
- Prize: control of the Ohio Country (upper Ohio valley) — land, fur/Indian trade, imperial position; both Britain (via Virginia) and France claimed it.
- Ohio Company (formed 1748 by wealthy Virginians incl. two of Washington's older brothers); British grant ~500,000 acres.
- French forts: Fort Le Boeuf (1753), Venango, and Fort Duquesne at the Forks of the Ohio (Allegheny + Monongahela = Ohio, present-day Pittsburgh).
- Washington's 1753 mission: Lt. Gov. Robert Dinwiddie sent Washington (then a 21-yr-old militia major) with Christopher Gist to warn the French off; reached Fort Le Boeuf Dec 1753; commander Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre politely refused ("as to the summons you send me to retire, I do not think myself obliged to obey it").
- 1754: Virginia tried to build a fort at the Forks; French drove them off, built Fort Duquesne. Washington marched west with the Virginia Regiment, camped at Great Meadows, was warned by Tanaghrisson of a nearby French detachment.
- NOTE: Washington is age 21 in 1753, **age 22** at Jumonville Glen (May 1754). Rank in May 1754 usually given as **Lieutenant Colonel** of the Virginia Regiment ("Major" was his 1753 rank).

## 3. FORCES & PEOPLE
- British/Virginia: George Washington, age 22, Lt. Col.; ~40 (some say ~47) Virginia provincials. Native allies: Tanaghrisson ("the Half King"), a Mingo leader, with ~12 warriors.
- French: Ensign Joseph Coulon de Villiers, Sieur de Jumonville (rank 2nd ensign; b. Sept 8, 1718, so age 35); ~35 men from Fort Duquesne (Jumonville, another ensign, three cadets, a volunteer, an interpreter, ~28 men).
- CONTESTED & CENTRAL — embassy or scouting party? French reading: a diplomatic party carrying a summons ordering the British to withdraw (an emissary). British/Washington reading: concealed spies/scouts (they hid in a ravine instead of openly approaching; orders also told Jumonville to report British positions back for reinforcements). Historian Fred Anderson: it was BOTH (summons + reconnaissance). Do not assert one as fact.

## 4. THE ACTION
- Washington marched through a rainy night, linked with Tanaghrisson, and surrounded the French in a rocky hollow at dawn. A shot was fired (disputed who fired first); ~15-minute firefight; French routed.
- Casualties (ranges): French ~10-14 killed (Washington said 10), ~21 captured, 1 escaped (Monceau, who carried word to Fort Duquesne). British: 1 killed, ~2-3 wounded.

## 5. JUMONVILLE'S DEATH — THE CONTROVERSY (write as disputed)
- Most-cited account: after the firing, Jumonville was wounded and a prisoner; Tanaghrisson approached and killed him with hatchet/tomahawk blows to the head, reportedly saying first "Tu n'es pas encore mort, mon père" ("Thou art not yet dead, my father"). Provenance: a single source (deserter Denis Kaninguen) gives the exact words. Anderson calls it "the only source providing Tanaghrisson's exact words."
- Corroborating but legend-flagged details: John Shaw's second-hand sworn statement (split the head, "washed his hands in his brains," scalped) — NPS/MV flag "washed his hands in his brains" as legend. Warriors reportedly killed/scalped additional French wounded before Washington intervened.
- Washington's own account compressed events to imply all died in the firefight: "We killed Mr. de Jumonville, the commander of that Party, as also nine others; we wounded one, and made Twenty-one Prisoners."
- French survivor Monceau: the French called a cease-fire to read "the Summons" and the killing came during/after a parley.
- The "assassination" charge: France branded it the assassination of an envoy. It was locked into the record by the Fort Necessity capitulation (July 3-4, 1754), whose French text contained "l'assassinat" of Jumonville; Washington couldn't read French and signed, unknowingly "confessing." A French propaganda coup. Britain/Washington held it was a legitimate fight against a scouting party.

## 6. CONSEQUENCES
- French retaliation: Jumonville's older brother, Louis Coulon de Villiers, led a revenge force (~600 French + ~100 Native) from Fort Duquesne and attacked Washington's hastily built Fort Necessity (July 3, 1754); Washington surrendered — the only surrender of his career.
- Jumonville Glen + Fort Necessity = opening engagements of the F&I War; escalated (Braddock 1755) and merged into the global Seven Years' War. Hence Walpole's "set the world on fire."

## 7. NATIVE PERSPECTIVE / STAKES
- Tanaghrisson: born Catawba, raised among the Iroquois, became a Mingo leader at Logstown; represented the Six Nations at the 1752 Treaty of Logstown. Title "Half King" itself contested (Six Nations office vs. possibly a British label).
- His grievance: the French had treated him dismissively and humiliated him (Joncaire mocked him as "more English than the English"); after the French took the Forks, the Delawares and Shawnees ignored him and his standing was collapsing.
- Anderson's reading of his motive: the killing was likely a deliberate political act — to reassert his standing before wavering Ohio nations, to force the French into retaliation and bind British + Ohio Indians against France, and a ritual repudiation: calling Jumonville "father" invoked Onontio (the French governor's title, the "father" mediator) and then killing that "father" denied French legitimacy over Native land.
- What was fought over: control of the Ohio Country — empire, the Indian/fur trade, and above all the LAND of the Ohio nations, caught between two empires; their stake was their own land and autonomy.
- Tanaghrisson died of pneumonia Oct 4, 1754, at Paxtang, PA, his hoped-for resurgence unrealized.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. Jumonville's death (firefight vs. hatchet execution after surrender) — majority/strong but not eyewitness-certain; "brains" detail = legend. 2. Embassy vs. scouting party — genuinely contested (both, per Anderson). 3. Casualty counts — French ~10-14 killed; use ranges. 4. Quote wordings (Walpole paraphrase; Tanaghrisson single-source; George II retort unverified; "bullets whistle" verified). 5. Washington's rank = Lt. Col. (not Major). 6. Glen coords disputed (~7 mi from Fort Necessity). 7. Louis Coulon = Jumonville's (older) brother.
