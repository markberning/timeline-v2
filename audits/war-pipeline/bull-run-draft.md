# on-to-richmond | On to Richmond

In the spring of 1861, a remarkable number of Americans believed the war that had just started would be over by autumn, and that they would win it in an afternoon. The Confederate States had fired on Fort Sumter, a U.S. fort in the harbor of Charleston, South Carolina, on April 12 and 13, and President Abraham Lincoln (North) had answered by calling for 75,000 volunteers. The thing to understand about those volunteers is the fine print: they signed up for **90-day enlistments**, meaning they'd promised the army only three months of their lives. That number wasn't an accident. It was a confession of how short everyone expected this to be. Three months was thought to be plenty of time to march south, smash the rebellion in one big battle, and send everyone home before the harvest. Nobody enlists for ninety days if they think they're starting a four-year war.

## The 90-day war | A clock that forced the issue

The trouble with a 90-day army is that the clock runs out. By the middle of July 1861, those three-month enlistments were starting to expire, which meant the United States was about to watch its only real army melt back into a crowd of civilians without having fought anything. (How real this was: two Union regiments, their time up, literally turned around and marched back to Washington while the battle was beginning — they went home rather than re-enlist for one more day.) So there was a deadline pressing on everyone. If there was going to be a quick, war-ending victory, it had to happen now, before the men who were supposed to win it walked off the job.

The newspapers were not helping anyone stay calm. Starting June 26, 1861, the *New York Tribune* — the most influential paper in the country, run by the famous editor Horace Greeley — ran a standing demand at the top of its columns: **FORWARD TO RICHMOND!**, printed day after day like a drumbeat. Richmond, Virginia, was the prize. The Confederacy had moved its capital there from Montgomery, Alabama back in May, which put the rebel seat of government barely 100 miles from Washington — close enough to feel like a quick march, and important enough that taking it might end everything. Between Greeley's drumbeat, an impatient Congress, and a public absolutely certain of victory, the pressure to attack became a wall.

> You are green, it is true, but they are green also; you are all green alike.

The general who would have to do the attacking was **Brigadier General Irvin McDowell** (North), and he did not want to go. McDowell knew exactly what his army was: a mob of enthusiastic amateurs who had never fought, never marched far, never done any of it. He wanted more time to train them. He was overruled. The famous reply about both armies being equally raw — *"you are green, it is true, but they are green also; you are all green alike"* — is usually put in Lincoln's mouth in popular retellings, but the careful scholarship attributes it to **General Winfield Scott** (North), the aging general-in-chief of the whole U.S. Army, and that's the safer attribution. (Whoever said it, the sentiment was true and it didn't save McDowell from the consequences.) How green were his men? On the march to the battlefield, soldiers broke ranks to pick blackberries and wander off looking for water, because nobody had taught them yet that you don't do that. McDowell's plan would require precise, coordinated movements by exactly this army. That was the problem in a sentence.

## The spectators | A battle as a day out

Here is the detail that tells you how nobody understood what was coming. When McDowell's army marched out of Washington toward Virginia, it was followed by a crowd. Hundreds of civilians — including United States senators and congressmen, with their wives and families — rode out from the capital in carriages, packing picnic baskets and opera glasses, planning to find a good hill and watch the United States win the war the way you'd watch a horse race. Senators Zachariah Chandler of Michigan, Ben Wade of Ohio, Henry Wilson of Massachusetts, and James Grimes of Iowa were among them. So was Representative Alfred Ely (North), a congressman from Rochester, New York, who'd come out partly to check on a regiment he had helped recruit. They were certain. It was going to be a show, and they had front-row seats. Almost none of them grasped that the thing they'd come to watch was men killing each other a few hundred yards away.

## Two armies and one railroad | Why Manassas mattered

The Confederates had not been waiting passively. They had two armies in northern Virginia, and the whole battle turns on the relationship between them. The first, under **Brigadier General P.G.T. Beauregard** (South) — the hero of Fort Sumter — sat at **Manassas Junction**, a spot whose entire importance was its railroads. It was where two rail lines crossed: the Orange & Alexandria Railroad and the Manassas Gap Railroad. Whoever held Manassas Junction held the overland gateway to Richmond, which is exactly why Beauregard, with around 21,000 to 22,000 men, was dug in along a winding stream called Bull Run to block McDowell's path.

The second Confederate army, around 11,000 to 12,000 men under **Brigadier General Joseph E. Johnston** (South), was off to the west in the **Shenandoah Valley**, the long fertile farming valley that runs down the western side of Virginia behind the mountains. Johnston was being watched there by a Union force under **Major General Robert Patterson** (North), and that watching was the linchpin of McDowell's entire plan. The whole scheme only worked if Patterson kept Johnston pinned in the Valley, unable to come help Beauregard. Because here was the danger nobody on the Union side had fully reckoned with: that crossing rail line at Manassas. The Manassas Gap Railroad ran from the Valley straight to Beauregard's junction. If Johnston ever slipped away from Patterson, those tracks could carry his whole army to the battlefield faster than any army in history had ever moved. The railroad wasn't just a supply line. It was a loaded gun pointed at McDowell's plan, and he was about to find out the hard way.

### Meanwhile in Washington | The carnival certainty

In Washington, the mood was a festival. The capital had spent the spring being told, by its newspapers and its politicians and its own nerves, that one good battle would end the rebellion — and now the army was marching out to fight it. People packed picnics. Senators ordered their carriages. The talk in the city was not whether the United States would win but how completely, and how soon everyone could come home. It was the high-water mark of innocence about this war: the last morning anyone could honestly believe it would be quick. The men riding out with their opera glasses were about to be the first Northern civilians to learn, in person, what they had been cheering for.

# the-flank-march | The Flank March

For all the talk of his green army and his missed deadlines, McDowell's plan was genuinely good. That's the cruel part of First Bull Run: it was not a stupid plan badly conceived. It was a smart plan handed to an army that couldn't execute it. On the morning of Sunday, July 21, 1861, McDowell set in motion a scheme that, with one more hour of speed or one more trained division, might have won the war that summer.

## Morning, July 21 | A punch and a hook

The plan had two parts, the way a good boxing combination does. The first part was a **feint** — a fake attack meant to grab the enemy's attention and hold it in the wrong place. McDowell sent a division under Daniel Tyler to make a loud demonstration at the **Stone Bridge**, where the main east-west road (the Warrenton Turnpike) crossed Bull Run. Lots of noise, lots of cannon fire, the look of a serious assault — all designed to convince Beauregard that the real blow was coming there, at his right and center.

It wasn't. While Tyler hammered at the Stone Bridge, the bulk of McDowell's army — two divisions under Hunter and Heintzelman — was swinging in a wide arc to the north and west to hit the Confederate **flank**, the exposed end of an army's line where it can be rolled up sideways like a rug, the most vulnerable spot a defending army has. To get there they had to cross Bull Run at a **ford**, a shallow spot where a stream can be waded rather than bridged. The ford they aimed for was **Sudley Springs Ford**, about 3 miles (4.8 km) north of the Stone Bridge — well past the end of the Confederate line, so the Union flanking column could cross unopposed, then turn south and come crashing down on the rebel left from a direction Beauregard wasn't watching. Smash the flank, roll up the line, and cut Beauregard off from Manassas Junction behind him. On paper it was beautiful.

The trouble was the marching. The flanking column had a long way to go on a hot July morning over bad back roads, led by officers who had never moved this many men this far, and the army that broke ranks for blackberries did not suddenly become punctual under pressure. The wide hook that was supposed to land at dawn instead dragged on for hours. The column didn't reach Sudley Springs Ford until around 9:30 a.m. — a delay of roughly two hours, and in that two hours the surprise leaked away. A sharp-eyed Confederate signal officer, Captain Edward Porter Alexander (South), spotted the sun glinting off the bayonets of the distant flanking column and used **wig-wag signaling** — a system of waving flags in coded patterns to send messages across distance, here used in combat for the first time — to flash a warning to the Confederate left: *"Look out for your left, your position is turned."* The element of total surprise was gone. But the flank attack was still coming, and it still had the weight to win.

## Morning, July 21 | The hour it looked won

The Confederate officer holding the threatened left, Colonel Nathan Evans (South), had barely 1,100 men, and he made a fast, smart decision: realizing the Stone Bridge attack was a bluff, he pulled most of his command away from it and rushed them north to **Matthews Hill**, planting them directly in the path of the oncoming Union flankers to buy time. Reinforcements scrambled up to help him — the brigades of Brigadier General Barnard Bee (South) and Colonel Francis Bartow (South) — and for a couple of hours Matthews Hill was a furious, stand-up fight, a handful of outnumbered Confederate brigades trying to hold back the entire weight of McDowell's hook.

They couldn't. There were too many Union troops, and worse was coming at the rebel flank: Colonel William T. Sherman (North) — the same Sherman who would become one of the war's most famous generals — found an unguarded ford upstream from the Stone Bridge, crossed with his brigade around 10:00 a.m., and slammed into the Confederate right on Matthews Hill, adding his weight to the pile. By around 11:30 in the morning, the Confederate line on Matthews Hill broke. Evans, Bee, and Bartow's men streamed back in retreat, southward across the Warrenton Turnpike and up the slope of the next high ground, a plateau called **Henry House Hill**. To the Union officers watching, and to the picnicking spectators craning through their opera glasses from the hills behind, it looked exactly like what they'd all come to see: the rebels running, the Union advancing, the short war ending right on schedule. By late morning, First Bull Run looked like a Union victory.

> It looked, for an hour around noon, exactly like the quick triumph everyone had marched out to watch.

_ITALIC_: It was the high point of the day for the United States, and it was about to become the turning point against it.

### Meanwhile in the Shenandoah Valley | The railroad as a weapon

While McDowell's hook was swinging toward Sudley Springs, the gun pointed at his plan was already being fired, 50-odd miles to the west. Patterson, the Union general whose one job was to keep Johnston bottled up in the Shenandoah Valley, had failed to do it. He never pressed Johnston hard, and Johnston took full advantage: he screened his departure behind a curtain of cavalry under Colonel J.E.B. Stuart (South), peeled his infantry quietly away, and on July 18 and 19 marched them to **Piedmont Station** (present-day Delaplane, Virginia) on the Manassas Gap Railroad. There, his soldiers climbed aboard trains. The Manassas Gap Railroad carried Johnston's army roughly 34 miles (55 km) east to the battlefield — the first time in history that railroads were used to rush troops to a major battle. Patterson, meanwhile, believed he had Johnston contained, and reported as much to Washington — even as Johnston's men were already riding the rails toward Beauregard. The army that was supposed to be stuck in the Valley was, that very morning, stepping off the trains behind the Confederate line. McDowell's plan had a fatal flaw, and it ran on a railroad timetable.

# stone-wall | There Stands Jackson

By midday on July 21, the broken Confederate brigades were streaming back from Matthews Hill in a state close to rout, climbing the slope of Henry House Hill with a victorious Union army coming on behind them. The battle had a few hours left to run, and everything that made it famous — the legend, the turn, the rebel yell, the only civilian death — happened on this one plateau. Henry House Hill is where First Bull Run stopped being a Union victory and started being a Confederate one.

## Henry House Hill | A man who would not move

The reason the Confederate retreat didn't become a collapse was a brigade of Virginians that hadn't run, because it hadn't yet fought. **Brigadier General Thomas J. Jackson** (South) had brought his fresh brigade up near noon and positioned it on the **reverse slope** of Henry House Hill — the back side of the hill, the side facing away from the enemy, where the crest of the ground itself shielded his men from incoming artillery fire. It was a clever, deliberate piece of tactics: his infantry waited in cover while he set 13 cannon on the crest above them to do the killing. Cavalry under J.E.B. Stuart (South) and the men of Wade Hampton's Legion guarded his flanks. While the rest of the Confederate left fell apart, Jackson's line stood on that hill like a fixed point, unmoving, waiting.

And that is where the most famous sentence of the battle was spoken. As Barnard Bee (South) — his own brigade shot to pieces and falling back from Matthews Hill — tried to rally his broken men, he pointed toward the Virginians on the hill and said the line that gave Jackson his name forever:

> There stands Jackson like a stone wall! Rally behind the Virginians!

Here is the thing the legend usually leaves out: nobody actually knows what Bee meant. The popular reading is pure praise — *look how Jackson stands firm, rally on him, be like that.* But there's a real and unresolved second reading. One Confederate officer, Major Chase Whiting (South), reported afterward that Jackson had refused to bring his brigade forward to support Bee's men while they were being slaughtered, and that Bee said the words in fury, not admiration — *Jackson just stands there like a stone wall doing nothing while we're cut to pieces.* We will never know which it was, because Bee was mortally wounded minutes after he spoke and died the next day, July 22, without ever explaining himself. The most famous compliment in American military history may not have been a compliment at all. The praise version became the legend; the rebuke version has serious historians behind it; the man who could settle it took the answer to his grave. Either way, the name stuck. From that hill on, he was Stonewall Jackson.

## Henry House Hill | The widow in the house

There was a farmhouse on the crest of that hill, and a person inside it. **Judith Carter Henry**, around 85 years old, was a bedridden widow who lived in the house that gave Henry House Hill its name — and now the worst fighting of the day was happening on her doorstep. Her family tried to carry her out of the house to safety, but the old woman begged to be taken back to her own bed, and they did. Union artillery, believing Confederate sharpshooters were firing from the house, opened up on it. A shell tore through her bedroom wall, severing one of her feet and inflicting other wounds, and she died that day. Judith Henry is considered the first civilian killed in the American Civil War. The first major battle of a war that would kill three quarters of a million people took its first civilian life in a widow's bedroom, on the hill that fight made famous.

## Henry House Hill | The trains turn the tide

For hours the two armies tore at each other across the plateau. Union artillery batteries — the **battery** being a cluster of cannon, here about 11 guns under captains James Ricketts (North) and Charles Griffin (North) — were pushed forward onto the hill, and the fighting got close and confused. At one point a Confederate regiment in blue uniforms, the 33rd Virginia, advanced on the Union guns; Griffin took them for friendly troops and held his fire, and the Confederates overran and captured both batteries. The guns changed hands again and again in brutal, point-blank fighting. The hill was a meat grinder, and neither side could finish it.

What finished it came off the railroad. The last of Johnston's brigades from the Shenandoah Valley — the men shuttled in by train — were still arriving on the battlefield through the afternoon, and they came in on the exposed Union flank at the worst possible moment for McDowell. The brigade of Brigadier General Edmund Kirby Smith (South) reached the field by rail around 3:30 to 4:00 p.m.; Kirby Smith was almost instantly shot down, wounded badly in the neck and shoulder, and command passed to Colonel Arnold Elzey (South), who led the brigade in a charge that smashed into the Union right on Chinn Ridge. At the same moment, Colonel Jubal Early's (South) brigade struck the same flank from another angle. Fresh Confederate troops, delivered by railroad to the exact spot where they'd do the most damage, slammed into a Union army that was running out of strength.

And as they charged, the Confederates made a sound. Through the afternoon's counterattacks, the rebel infantry raised a high-pitched, eerie, screaming battle cry that came to be known as the **rebel yell** — a sound that witnesses on both sides remembered for the rest of their lives, somewhere between a scream and a howl, terrifying to hear coming at you out of the smoke. First Bull Run is generally credited as its first appearance in a major battle. (The exact order that supposedly started it — a command from Jackson to "yell like furies" — comes only from post-war accounts and doesn't appear in Jackson's own official reports, so we can describe the yell but not vouch for the order.) Whatever set it off, the effect on exhausted Union troops, already bending under the flank attack, was real. The tide on Henry House Hill had turned.

### Meanwhile in the Henry house | The civilians on the field

For the spectators on the hills behind the Union lines, the morning's certainty was curdling into confusion and then into dread. They had come to watch a victory, and through their opera glasses the thing they'd cheered at noon was coming apart in the afternoon. And on the field itself, where the battle was actually being fought, there was Judith Henry's house — a reminder that this was not a sporting event staged on empty ground. People lived here. An old woman lay dying in her own bedroom on the very hill the two armies were fighting over. The gap between the picnic the politicians had imagined and the thing actually happening on Henry House Hill was about to close, violently, for everyone.

# the-rout | The Rout

A battle that has gone one way for six hours can change in twenty minutes, and that is what happened to the Union army on the afternoon of July 21. One moment McDowell's men were fighting a hard, even battle on Henry House Hill; the next, with fresh Confederates pouring onto their flank and that unearthly yell rising out of the smoke, they were a army no longer. Around 4:00 p.m. the Union line gave way, and the retreat began.

## The Great Skedaddle | From near-victory to panic

It did not start as a panic. An exhausted army that has been fighting all day and is finally pushed too far will often pull back in reasonable order at first, and McDowell tried to organize a withdrawal with a rear guard to cover it. What turned an orderly retreat into a stampede was, fittingly for this battle, an accident on a bridge. As the Union troops fell back toward their base at Centreville, they had to cross **Cub Run Creek** on a single bridge, and a Confederate artillery shell overturned a wagon right on the span. The road jammed. With the only easy way out suddenly blocked and rebels behind them, the tired retreat dissolved into a terrified mob — men throwing away their rifles, packs, and anything that slowed them down, all running the same direction at once. The Southern press gleefully named it **The Great Skedaddle**.

And here the day's other miscalculation came home. Those roads back toward Washington weren't empty — they were clogged with the carriages of the picnickers, the senators and congressmen and their families who had ridden out to watch a victory. Now the fleeing army and the fleeing spectators collided on the same narrow roads, civilian carriages tangled with army wagons, everyone shoving toward the capital at once, the panic feeding on itself. The politicians who'd come for a show were caught inside the rout they'd come to applaud. The crowd that had treated a battle like a day out got the most realistic possible education in what war was.

## The Great Skedaddle | A congressman in the bag

One of the spectators didn't make it back at all. Representative **Alfred Ely** (North), the New York congressman who'd come out to look in on a regiment he'd helped recruit, got swept up in the collapse and was captured by advancing Confederate troops. Brought before rebel officers, he announced himself — "the Honorable Alfred Ely, Representative in Congress from New York" — which is not a thing the Confederate Army hears every day. They took him to Richmond and held him in **Libby Prison** alongside captured Union officers. Ely had the strange distinction of being the only member of the United States Congress to reach Richmond that day — not in triumph, the way Greeley's headline had promised, but as a prisoner of war. He was finally freed in a prisoner exchange on Christmas Day, 1861.

## The Great Skedaddle | The victory they couldn't finish

The Confederates had won a stunning victory, and they almost entirely failed to cash it in. The road to Washington was, for a few hours, wide open — a fleeing, disintegrating enemy and a captured capital practically begging to be taken. The Confederate president, Jefferson Davis (South), arrived on the field and urged a pursuit. It didn't happen. The reason is the quiet truth at the heart of the day: the winning army was nearly as wrecked by victory as the losing army was by defeat. These were green Confederate troops too — exhausted, scattered, their units tangled, their officers as inexperienced as McDowell's. An attempt to push brigades forward to intercept the retreat ran into McDowell's rear guard and stalled out. The South had broken the Union army and then discovered it was in no condition to chase it. The same rawness that doomed McDowell's plan saved his army from total destruction.

## The reckoning | The day the short war died

Then came the counting, and the counting was the real battle. The standard tally puts the day's casualties at roughly 4,878 men killed, wounded, captured, or missing — about 2,896 on the Union side and 1,982 on the Confederate (and the scholarly figures vary, running somewhere in the range of 4,700 to 4,900 total, because the sources disagree on how to count the missing and the captured). To a public that had packed picnic baskets, the numbers were a hammer blow. Around 847 Americans were killed in this single battle — more than all the U.S. battle deaths in the entire Mexican-American War, which had taken two years to accumulate them. The war everyone had marched out to win in an afternoon had just killed, in one Sunday, more men than they'd lost in their last whole war.

> Today will be known as BLACK MONDAY. We are utterly and disgracefully routed, beaten, whipped by secessionists.

That was the New York diarist George Templeton Strong, whose journal is one of the great records of Northern feeling during the war, writing the morning after. The illusion of the 90-day war died on Henry House Hill and was buried in Washington over the following days. And the reaction split exactly along the two sides. The North was sobered into seriousness: the very next day Lincoln (North) signed legislation authorizing 500,000 three-year volunteers — *three years*, not ninety days, the federal government finally admitting in law what the battlefield had just proven. McDowell was relieved of command within days, and **Major General George B. McClellan** (North), fresh off small victories in western Virginia, was summoned to Washington to build and train a real army out of the wreckage — the force that would become the famous **Army of the Potomac**. The South, meanwhile, swung the other way, into a dangerous elation; the easy victory bred a confidence that some historians think did the Confederacy long-term harm, teaching it to expect wins that would not keep coming.

_ITALIC_: And the South came away from Bull Run with something more lasting than the field: its first hero, and a name for him. There stood Jackson like a stone wall — and Stonewall he would be until the day he died.

### Meanwhile in the North | The morning the long war began

The deepest thing that happened at Bull Run wasn't on the field at all — it was in people's heads, the morning after. For three months, North and South alike had been living inside the same comfortable story: one battle, a quick win, everyone home by fall. Bull Run took that story out and shot it in front of the whole country. The men in Washington who woke up on "Black Monday" understood, many of them for the first time, that this was not going to be quick and was not going to be cheap — that the casualty list from a single Sunday in Virginia was a down payment on something enormous. The picnic was over. The reorganizing began. Somewhere in those sobered Northern minds, on that gray morning, the four-year war that was actually coming finally started to feel real.
