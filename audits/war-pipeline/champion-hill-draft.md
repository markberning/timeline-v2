## the-crossroads-hill · eyebrow: Champion Hill · The hinge · title: The Hill That Held the Road Home

By the middle of May 1863, Major General Ulysses S. Grant (North) was loose in the
interior of Mississippi, and there was no good way for the Confederacy to put him back.
He had crossed the great river south of Vicksburg, cut himself free of his own supply
line, and marched inland, winning a string of fights — Port Gibson, Raymond, and then
the state capital at Jackson, which he wrecked and left smoking behind him (see
Vicksburg for the full campaign). Now he turned west. Twenty miles in front of him,
behind the last good defensive ground before the fortress city, waited the army that
was supposed to stop him.

The whole point of all this marching was to take Vicksburg, and the whole point of
taking Vicksburg was to break the Confederacy in half. Vicksburg held the last stretch
of the Mississippi River still in Southern hands; cut it, and the slaveholding states
west of the river — Texas, Arkansas, most of Louisiana — would be severed from the rest
of the rebellion. And the rebellion was, at bottom, a slaveholders' republic: the
Confederacy had broken away from the United States to protect the enslavement of four
million Black people, and Grant's campaign was a campaign to saw that republic in two.
The ground he was about to fight over made the point plainly. Champion Hill took its
name from the Champion family, whose plantation it sat on — land in a Mississippi county
worked by enslaved people, the very institution the South had seceded to defend. Every
mile Grant pushed west, that institution dissolved behind his army: thousands of
enslaved people had been freeing themselves along his line of march, walking off the
plantations toward the Union columns rather than waiting to be freed (see Vicksburg).

The man waiting for him was Lieutenant General John C. Pemberton (South), commander of
the Confederate army charged with holding Vicksburg. Pemberton had been handed
impossible, contradictory orders — told both to hold the city at all costs and to march
out and strike at Grant's supply line (a supply line Grant had already abandoned, so the
order chased a thing that no longer existed). Caught between the two, Pemberton had
marched his army *out* of Vicksburg's safe works and into the open, and now he was
strung along a low ridge with Grant bearing down on him.

The ridge ran for about three miles, and it covered three roughly parallel roads, all
running east to west, all leading back from the Jackson and Edwards area toward
Vicksburg. From north to south they were the **Jackson Road**, the **Middle Road**, and
the **Raymond Road**. Hold those three roads and you held the army's way home. And the
key to the three roads was a single piece of ground at the northern end: a bald crest
about seventy-five feet high, rising over the country around it. This was Champion Hill.
Grant, who saw the position that morning, judged it shrewdly chosen.

> "...where Pemberton had chosen his position to receive us, whether taken by accident or
> design, was well selected. It is one of the highest points in that section, and
> commanded all the ground in the range."
> — Grant, *Personal Memoirs* [verified quote, fact pack §7]

The hill mattered because of what sat just below it. Where the Jackson Road and the
Middle Road came together, perhaps a third of a mile down from the crest, there was a
crossroads — and that crossroads sat astride Pemberton's only good road back to
Vicksburg. Whoever held the crossroads held the road home. And the crossroads belonged
to whoever held the hill above it. That was the whole tactical problem of the day,
stated in one sentence: the battle for Champion Hill was a battle for a road junction,
and behind the road junction was a creek, Baker's Creek, that the Confederate army would
have to get across to escape. Lose the hill, lose the crossroads; lose the crossroads,
lose the way over the creek; lose the way over the creek, and you lose the army.

One more thing the headline numbers won't tell you, and it matters for everything that
follows. On paper Grant had the bigger army — something on the order of thirty-two
thousand men against Pemberton's twenty-two or twenty-three thousand. But almost none of
that edge would reach the field. Major General William T. Sherman's (North) corps was
still back at Jackson, out of range entirely. And Major General John A. McClernand
(North), whose corps was marching up on the two southern roads, would sit nearly idle
all day despite Grant's orders, barely firing a shot. That left the fighting to fall on
Major General James B. McPherson's (North) corps and Alvin P. Hovey's (North) division —
a force just about equal in strength to Pemberton's. So forget the picture of a much
larger army steamrolling a smaller one. Champion Hill was a near-even, savage fight, and
Grant won it not by weight of numbers but by where he landed the blow.

**Meanwhile — Jackson, twenty miles east.** The reason Grant could fight at near-even
odds was that he had spent the previous days making sure the two Confederate armies in
Mississippi could never combine against him. General Joseph E. Johnston (South) was
gathering a relief force near Jackson, and Pemberton's army was the one strung along the
ridge at Champion Hill — and Grant had wedged himself squarely between them. Johnston was
not on the field and could not get to it in time. The whole campaign, in a sense, came
down to keeping those two Southern forces apart for one more day, and on May 16 the gap
between them ran right through the crossroads below Champion Hill (see Vicksburg).

## the-jackson-road · eyebrow: Champion Hill · The blow falls · title: Up the Jackson Road, Onto the Crest

Skirmishing opened at dawn as Grant's three columns felt their way west along the three
roads. The first man to grasp what was happening was on the Confederate side. Brigadier
General Stephen D. Lee (South) — no relation to Robert E. Lee, despite the name — held a
brigade of Alabamians posted on top of Champion Hill, and from that high ground he could
see what the men down on the roads could not. Off to the north, on the Jackson Road, a
heavy Union column was coming on. Lee understood instantly what it meant: that column was
aimed at the very flank of the army, and if it got past, it would cut Pemberton off from
Vicksburg. He sent the warning back. The danger was exactly where he said it was.

Grant reached the field around ten in the morning and put his blow in where the
crossroads could be reached: straight up the Jackson Road, against the northern, exposed
end of the Confederate line. Hovey's (North) men and the division of Major General John
A. Logan (North) pushed up the road and into the timber. By about half past eleven they
were against the Confederate main line, and the fight became a climb — up the slope of
Champion Hill, into Carter L. Stevenson's (South) division holding the Confederate left.
This was the flank Grant meant to break, and around one o'clock he broke it. The Union
men carried the crest. Stevenson's left came apart and fell back off the hill in
disorder, and the Federals swept on down the far slope to the prize below: the
crossroads. For a moment, Pemberton's escape route up the Jackson Road was shut.

The men who took that hill paid a price for it that stayed with the division forever.
Hovey's soldiers bore the brunt of the day's killing, and afterward they gave the height
a name. They called it the hill of death. [The fact pack flags this as a paraphrased
attribution to Hovey's men, widely repeated in the secondary accounts, not a single
verbatim quotation from a named soldier — render as what Hovey's men called it. See Fact
ledger.]

It is worth stopping on what those men were fighting for, because the ground itself was
the argument. They were climbing a slaveholding plantation. The hill was named for the
family that owned it, and the county around it was worked by enslaved people — the same
human property the Confederacy had been founded to keep. The army Hovey's men were
breaking on that crest existed to defend that arrangement, and the campaign they were
fighting was a campaign to cut the slaveholders' republic in half at the river. The hill
of death was a hill on a plantation, and that was not incidental to the war; it was the
war.

**OUTCOME of the morning fight — Union seizes the crest and the crossroads.** Grant's
main effort up the Jackson Road broke Stevenson's division, carried the seventy-five-foot
crest of Champion Hill by about one o'clock, and pressed on to take the crossroads below
— briefly closing Pemberton's best line of retreat to Vicksburg. It was the right blow in
the right place: not the heaviest part of the army, but the part aimed at the flank that
held the road home. The cost fell hardest on Hovey's division, which is why the men
named the place as they did.

**Meanwhile — the southern roads, where the rest of Grant's army wasn't fighting.** While
Hovey and Logan were climbing the hill, McClernand's (North) two southern columns sat
nearly still on the Middle and Raymond Roads. Grant sent orders forward to press the
attack there; for most of the day the orders went unanswered, and McClernand barely
engaged. It was the great Union missed opportunity of Champion Hill — a whole corps
standing idle while a near-even fight was decided a mile to the north. Had those columns
pressed Pemberton's right at the same time, the Confederate army might have been crushed
on the field instead of merely beaten off it. As it was, Grant won the battle with half
the army he had brought to it.

## the-hill-changes-hands · eyebrow: Champion Hill · The counterattack · title: Bowen's Charge and the Crest Retaken

Pemberton's army had its own best weapon, and now he used it. Major General John S. Bowen
(South) commanded the finest division in the Confederate force, hard-fighting Missourians
and Arkansans, and Bowen threw them straight at the men who had just taken the hill.
Around two in the afternoon his line slammed into Hovey's (North) exhausted, bloodied
soldiers and rolled them back. The Missourians and Arkansans surged up and over the crest
they had just lost, drove the Federals back down the slope, and pushed them nearly to the
Champion house at the foot of the hill. For a savage half hour or so this was the most
furious fighting of the whole day, and at the height of it the crest of Champion Hill and
the crossroads below it were back in Confederate hands.

But Bowen (South) had too few men to keep what his charge had taken. He had broken the
Union line and seized the ground, and there was nothing behind him to hold it with — a
counterattack is only as good as the reserve that follows it, and Bowen had outrun his.
The crest he had retaken he could not garrison.

Grant (North) had what Bowen lacked: a fresh division in hand. He fed in the reserve,
Brigadier General Marcellus Crocker's (North) men, straight into the path of Bowen's
surge. The fresh Union line stopped the Missourians and Arkansans, then began to push
them back up and over the same slope a third time. The Federals retook the crest and
retook the crossroads, and this time they kept them. The hill and the road junction below
it had changed hands several times in a few hours — Union, then Confederate under Bowen,
then Union again — and the last hands on it were Grant's.

That was the battle. With the crossroads gone for good and the army's flank caved in — and
with McClernand (North) at last beginning to lean on the southern end of the line —
Pemberton (South) had no choice left but to get what remained of his army off the field
before it was surrounded. The decision was forced on him by the loss of a single crest
and the road junction it commanded. He ordered the retreat.

**OUTCOME of the counterattack — Union holds the hill, the Confederate army is beaten.**
Bowen's charge briefly retook the crest and the crossroads in the hardest fighting of the
day, but he could not hold what he had won without a reserve. Grant's fresh division under
Crocker stopped the surge and took the ground back for good; the hill and crossroads
changed hands several times before settling with the Union. With his only good escape
road in Federal hands, Pemberton had to abandon the field — the field battle for Vicksburg
was lost.

**Meanwhile — inside the Confederate command, a feud that cost an army.** While Bowen's
men were dying to retake the hill, the southern end of the Confederate line was held by
Major General William W. Loring (South) — and Loring would not move. Pemberton sent order
after order for Loring to march north and reinforce the collapsing left. Loring insisted
he faced a strong Federal force on the Raymond Road and could not stir, then moved far too
late to make any difference. The blame ran both ways and never stopped running, but the
effect was simple: a third of Pemberton's army stood idle on the wrong end of the line
while the other end was overrun. The same division that would not march to save the hill
was about to march out of the campaign entirely.

## the-lost-division · eyebrow: Champion Hill · The doom of the city · title: Across Baker's Creek, and the Division Lost Forever

A beaten army's worst moment is usually the retreat, and everything Pemberton (South) had
to fear was waiting behind his line: Baker's Creek, running north to south between the
battlefield and Vicksburg, with the army needing to cross it to escape. The crossing was
a bridge on the Raymond Road, and getting the army over it before Grant's men arrived was
now the only thing that mattered. The job of holding the door open fell to the rearguard,
Brigadier General Lloyd Tilghman's (South) brigade, posted at the creek to buy time with
their bodies while the rest of the army filed across. Tilghman bought it and paid for it:
a Union artillery shell struck and killed him at the crossing. He died holding the way
open for the men streaming past him.

Most of Pemberton's army got over. By late afternoon Grant's (North) troops had seized
the Baker's Creek bridge themselves, and by midnight they held Edwards, the little
station to the east. The Confederate army that had stood on the ridge that morning was
streaming southwest toward Vicksburg, broken but largely intact — except for one
division, and that exception decided the war in Mississippi.

Loring's (South) division — the one that had refused to march all afternoon — now could
not get across Baker's Creek with the rest of the army. By the time Loring tried to pull
back, the usable crossing was cut off by Union troops and the loss of the bridge route.
He could not follow Pemberton west across the creek. Rather than fight his way through, he
peeled off to the south and east, marched clear around the Union army, and went to join
Johnston (South) near Jackson instead. He never rejoined Pemberton. He never entered
Vicksburg. Pemberton would walk back into the fortress city having lost an entire division
he would need for the siege — thousands of men who had survived the battle and were simply
gone, a loss with no body count to put on a casualty return.

And that is why this one day on a plantation hill decided the whole campaign. Champion
Hill was the decisive field battle of the Vicksburg operation — the day Grant beat
Pemberton in the open. Of the inland fights, this is the one that settled the campaign
before the siege ever began. The numbers tell part of it. The Confederates lost something
like 3,840 men to the Union's 2,457, and the casualty shape is the story: the killed and
wounded were close on both sides, but the Confederate total is blown out by roughly 2,441
men missing or captured — the price of a broken army funneling back across a single creek.
Champion Hill is frequently called the bloodiest single day of the entire Vicksburg
campaign.

But the deeper cost wasn't on the casualty return at all. The day after Champion Hill,
Grant's men ran Pemberton's rearguard off the field at Big Black River Bridge (see
Vicksburg), and on May 18 the beaten army was shut up inside Vicksburg's works. The siege
that followed — forty-seven days, and finally the surrender of the city and its garrison
on the Fourth of July — ran straight out of this hill (see Vicksburg). And through all of
it Grant stayed wedged between Johnston and Pemberton, so the two Confederate armies could
never join, which had been the whole aim. When Vicksburg fell, slavery fell with it in the
city, and within days the river was open from Cairo to the Gulf and the Confederacy was
split in two (see Vicksburg). All of that was decided, in the open, on the sixteenth of
May — on the crest of a slaveholders' plantation, over a crossroads, in a near-even fight
that Grant won because of where he put the blow.

> Grant's own verdict on what the day secured — that he now stood between Johnston and
> Pemberton with no possibility of their forces uniting — is well attested in substance;
> the exact wording sometimes given is a secondary attribution and is flagged in the Fact
> ledger below. [VERIFY exact words]

**OUTCOME — Decisive Union victory; the day that doomed Vicksburg.** Grant beat Pemberton
in the open in a near-even fight, broke his army off the field, and cut an entire
Confederate division (Loring's) away from it forever. Pemberton fell back into Vicksburg
short a division and out of options; the siege and the July 4 surrender followed within
weeks (see Vicksburg). Champion Hill is the battle where the campaign to take Vicksburg —
and to split the slaveholders' republic in half — was actually decided.

**Meanwhile — a thread that runs from this hill to the surrender.** The Confederate
officer whose charge nearly won the crest back, Major General John S. Bowen (South),
would carry the thread of this battle all the way to its end. Seven weeks later it was
Bowen who helped carry the surrender flag out of Vicksburg, and days after that he was
dead of dysentery (see Vicksburg). The man who almost saved the hill lived just long
enough to give the city away. Champion Hill did not end at Champion Hill; it ended on the
Fourth of July, on the Mississippi River, with the Confederacy cut in two and a slave city
freed.
