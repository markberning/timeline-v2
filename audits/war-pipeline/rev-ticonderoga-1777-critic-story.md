# STORYTELLING CRITIC · Fall of Fort Ticonderoga, 1777 (rev-ticonderoga)

*Critic output, 2026-06-11. Draft: `rev-ticonderoga-1777-draft.md`. Bar: `fi-carillon-draft.md` (shipped, gated).
Lane: story only; accuracy is the fact critic's.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

The tragic-irony engine works. Section 1 plants the hill twice-ignored ("Remember the
hill.") and the page pays it off exactly where it should; the 1758 PILL sits at the echo
beat and genuinely opens a corridor between the two wars. Section 3 is the strongest
"what it meant" in the war vertical so far: "heads rolled upward," the George III legend
told *as* a legend and enjoyed for it, the stop-the-clock verdict, and the closing
MEANWHILE that puts a present-day visitor on the walls looking at Mount Defiance — the
geometry returning as the last image. St. Clair's thesis lands in one perfect sentence
("He was right about both halves."). What keeps this from a clean PASS is scene-craft in
the middle: the page's two natural carillon-bar moments (the night evacuation, Hubbardton's
dawn) are narrated efficiently rather than inhabited, and one sentence of explicit
meta-narration ("makes this page a hinge") breaks the no-meta rule outright.

## BLOCKER

**B1 · The night evacuation is reported, not lived — the page's held-breath beat is missing.**
The arc spec for section 2 calls for the evacuation as held-breath, and the carillon bar
sets the standard with its "Imagine being one of them" abatis passage. The draft gives us:

> "The evacuation was a desperate improvisation that mostly worked. Everything that could
> float, more than 200 bateaux..."

That is inventory cadence. An army tip-toeing out of the Gibraltar of the North in the
dark — sick men carried into boats, whispered orders, 2,500 men filing over a quarter-mile
floating bridge under an enemy ten minutes' rowing away — is the single most filmable hour
of the week, and the draft spends it on logistics. The Fermoy fire already has the perfect
image ("lit up the bay and the retreating columns like a stage"), but with no built-up
silence before it, the fire has nothing to shatter. Concrete fix (2–4 sentences, no new
facts needed beyond the pack): after "sent south up the lake to Skenesborough under
Colonel Pierse Long," add an in-the-column beat in the carillon register, e.g.:

> "Imagine the discipline that night asked for. An army that had been promised it was
> holding the strongest place in America was walking away from it in the dark, in file,
> across a quarter-mile of floating bridge, every man listening for the sound that would
> mean the British had noticed. The orders were whispered. The oars were kept quiet. It
> almost held."

Then let the existing Fermoy-fire sentences land as the gut-drop they were written to be.

**B2 · Explicit meta-narration in section 3 — names the page.**

> "And then the inversion began, the part that makes this page a hinge rather than a defeat."

"This page" is the narrator discussing the artifact the reader is holding — the exact
move the no-meta-narration rule exists to kill, and a regression the war voice sweep
already paid to remove elsewhere. The inversion needs no announcement; the ledger
structure that follows ("Start with what Britain actually won... Now count what America
actually lost, and saved") performs it. Rewrite: **"And then the inversion began."** Full
stop, straight into "Start with what Britain actually won."

## SHOULD-FIX

**S1 · Hubbardton arrives as the real battle but leaves as a summary — one paragraph,
~190 words, every beat in passing.** The shape is all present (dawn surprise, Hale's camp
overrun, Francis's stand, the singing Brunswickers, the break, Francis dead, the scatter
order), but it is compressed into a single block in roll-call cadence: "It became the
hardest small battle of the year" is a verdict where the bar wants a scene. The best beat
on the page gets one sentence:

> "Then, up the Castleton road, came Riedesel's Brunswickers, jägers (riflemen) and
> grenadiers, advancing into the battle singing a hymn to a military band, on Riedesel's
> order, to sound like an army."

Stage it from the American side of the woods — the sound arriving before the men. E.g.:
break the paragraph after "bending his left flank back," then:

> "Then the Americans on Monument Hill heard something no battle had prepared them for:
> singing. Up the Castleton road came Riedesel's Brunswickers, jägers (riflemen) and
> grenadiers, advancing into the fight singing a hymn, with a military band playing, on
> Riedesel's order, so that a few hundred winded men would sound like an army arriving.
> It worked. The American right broke."

Split the block into three paragraphs (dawn attack and Hale overrun / Francis's stand /
the singing flank and the collapse) so the only pitched battle of the week reads at
battle tempo, not retreat-itinerary tempo.

**S2 · Meta framing on the 1758 setup, section 1.**

> "One detail from that disaster needs carrying forward, because this whole story turns on it:"

"Needs carrying forward" and "this whole story turns on it" are the narrator pointing at
his own machinery. The beat itself ("Remember the hill.") is excellent and carillon-grade
direct address — keep it and let it do the work alone. Rewrite the lead-in to point at
the world, not the story, e.g.: "One man in that doomed army had seen how to avoid all of
it. Abercromby's engineer, Matthew Clerk, pointed at a bare hill overlooking the French
lines..." → "...attacked head-on, and lost two thousand men. Remember the hill."

**S3 · The Burgoyne-army paragraph (section 1, "Now came the third moment") is a
spec-sheet clot.** Exact-to-the-man figures ("about 3,981 British infantry... about 3,116
Germans"), the Hessian/Brunswicker gloss, the followers, the Baroness, the whole
three-prong strategy, AND the Howe assumption all share one mega-paragraph. The
four-digit precision belongs in the dossier (where it already lives); in prose it reads
as the fact pack showing through. Round in the narrative ("just under 4,000 British
regulars and a shade over 3,000 Germans") and split the paragraph at "The plan, approved
in London..." so the strategy gets its own breath before "Hold that thought."

## TRIM

**T1 ·** "The verdict on the week, **in other words**, depends entirely on where you stop
the clock." — cut "in other words" (essay connective; the stop-the-clock device is
strong enough to arrive cold).

**T2 ·** Section 2, Hubbardton block: "It was the only Revolutionary War battle fought on
Vermont soil." sits between Hale's capture and the casualty percentages, interrupting the
close of the fight. Keep the fact, move it to the end of the Hubbardton passage (it works
as the quiet historical-marker beat after the percentages, not mid-collapse).

## NIT

**N1 ·** Two planted imperatives in one section: "Remember the hill." and "Hold that
thought." (section 1). Both earn their payoffs, but it is the same trick twice in ~600
words, and the second is paid off by the MEANWHILE directly beneath it, which shortens
the fuse to almost nothing. Consider varying the second ("Nobody in London ever turned
that assumption into an order. That sentence will cost Burgoyne his army.") or trusting
the MEANWHILE to land the point without the wind-up.

**N2 ·** The checkmate beat ("Every man who looked up understood the geometry at once")
is good — the flash of scarlet shows rather than tells — but it could hold one more beat
of *why there is no reply* (the guns command both forts; nothing in the works can answer
fire from 850 feet up) IF the fact pack supports it. One sentence, pack-permitting;
otherwise leave as is.

**N3 ·** The Adams-quote staging ("since the line is usually misquoted, here it is
exactly as he wrote it, period spelling and all") flirts with meta but earns its keep —
it converts the period capitalization from an apparent typo into a pleasure beat. Keep.

  Defiance "close, high, and looking straight down into the fort") — the irony engine's
  final turn. The best section-ender in the draft.
