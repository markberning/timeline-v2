# FACT PACK — Battle of Camden, August 16, 1780

*American Revolution (Southern campaign). ONLY source for the battle page. The catastrophe battle: the hero of Saratoga marches a hungry, half-militia "grand army" down the wrong road, collides with Cornwallis in the dark, puts his rawest men opposite the best regulars in America, and loses the second southern army in four months in about an hour. ## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **Battle of Camden, August 16, 1780** — fought about 5–6 miles north of Camden, South Carolina (battlefield at 34.3646,-80.6139, Kershaw County), astride the Great Wagon Road, the flanks hemmed by Gum Swamp. Southern campaign. [HIGH]
- **Result: decisive British victory** — the most complete defeat of an American field army in the war. The whole fight lasted about an hour. [HIGH]
- **The catastrophe premise [HIGH]:** Three months after Charleston surrendered with the entire southern army (May 12, 1780), Congress sent Horatio Gates, the celebrated victor of Saratoga, to rebuild and command a new one — over Washington's preference for Nathanael Greene. Twenty-two days after taking command, Gates had lost that army too: militia put opposite British regulars broke at the first volley, many without firing; the Maryland and Delaware Continentals under Johann de Kalb stood almost alone and were destroyed; de Kalb fell with some eleven wounds; Gates was swept off the field with the militia and rode to Hillsborough, North Carolina, roughly 180 miles, in three days.
- **Signature elements [HIGH]:** (1) the **night collision** — two armies, each attempting a night march to surprise the other, ran head-on into each other on the Great Wagon Road around 2 a.m.; (2) the **militia-vs-regulars line assignment** — Gates posted his Virginia and North Carolina militia on the left, directly opposite Webster's veteran regulars (by European convention each army's best stood on its own right, so the rawest Americans faced the best British — historians treat the placement as Gates's central tactical failure); (3) **de Kalb's stand and death**; (4) **Gates's ride** — 60 miles to Charlotte by that night, Hillsborough by August 19, fixed for history by Hamilton's jeer.
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — the Gates "ride of shame":** the documented record is Charlotte (~60 miles) the same evening and Hillsborough (~180 miles total) by August 19, and the record also shows Gates and Caswell trying to rally the militia before being carried along in the rout (Williams, Pinckney, Magill, and Jefferson's official report all support the rally attempts). The mockery (Hamilton) and the defense (Henry Lee, Greene, the officers' verdict "more unfortunate than criminal") BOTH belong in the telling. Do not write the ride as simple cowardice; do not write it as blameless either. See Sections 4, 6, 7.

## 2. BACKGROUND — THE SOUTH AFTER CHARLESTON
- **Charleston, May 12, 1780 [HIGH]:** Benjamin Lincoln surrendered the city and the entire southern Continental army (~5,000+ captured) after a six-week siege — the largest American surrender of the war. Clinton sailed back to New York in June leaving **Cornwallis** with ~8,300 to hold the South; British posts were strung across South Carolina (Camden the most important interior depot, held by **Lord Rawdon**). Georgia was already royal again. On paper the conquest was complete. [HIGH]
- **The Waxhaws, May 29, 1780 — one-paragraph context beat (keep at the spine's locked evidence level) [HIGH on events; intent DISPUTED]:** In the Waxhaws settlements near the North Carolina line, Lt. Col. Banastre Tarleton's British Legion (~270, mostly Loyalist provincials), off a forced ride of more than a hundred miles, caught Col. Abraham Buford's ~350–420 Virginia Continentals, the last organized American force in South Carolina. Buford refused a summons; the Legion cavalry broke his single line (he held fire until ~10 yards — one volley, then contact). In the smoke the killing continued after resistance had stopped. Tarleton's horse went down, and by his own published account the report that he had been killed "stimulated the soldiers to a vindictive asperity not easily restrained." Result: ~113 Americans killed, ~150 wounded (most too badly cut to move), 53 captured, against a handful of British losses (5 killed, 12 wounded). Whether this was massacre by intent or a command breakdown after Tarleton fell is genuinely argued by historians; what is certain is the wound evidence (multiple saber/bayonet wounds per man) and what the story became: **"Tarleton's quarter,"** meaning no quarter at all — the backcountry battle cry that fed the partisan war from then on (shouted at Kings Mountain in October). [Framing locked to the spine version; Tarleton quote VERIFIED, see Quotes]
- **The partisan summer (texture, one beat each) [HIGH]:**
  - **Ramsour's Mill, June 20, 1780** (Lincolnton, NC): ~1,300 Loyalist militia beaten by ~400 Patriot militia in a neighbor-against-neighbor brawl — no uniforms (Loyalists wore green twigs, Patriots paper scraps in their hats), muskets swung as clubs, brothers on opposite sides. The Loyalist rising in that region never reorganized. [HIGH]
  - **Huck's Defeat (Williamson's Plantation), July 12, 1780** (York County, SC): ~250 backcountry militia destroyed a 115-man Legion/Loyalist detachment under Christian Huck (a Philadelphia Loyalist lawyer who had been burning farms and insulting Presbyterian congregations); Huck shot dead, ~only 24 of his men escaped. The win electrified the backcountry and swelled Sumter's bands. [HIGH]
  - Sumter struck Hanging Rock (Aug 6) hard enough to maul a Loyalist garrison. The point for the page: the "conquered" province was already at war again BEFORE Gates fought — Camden did not start the southern fire and its loss did not put it out. [HIGH as framing; Hanging Rock details MED — keep to a clause]
- **Gates arrives with his Saratoga laurels [HIGH]:** On June 13, 1780, Congress — without consulting Washington, who wanted Greene — voted the southern command to Maj. Gen. Horatio Gates, the victor of Saratoga (and the man a faction in Congress had once floated to replace Washington). The anecdote that Charles Lee warned Gates "take care lest your Northern laurels turn to Southern willows" is a famous story recorded in early histories — use only as "the story goes" [MED-LOW, see Apocrypha]. Gates joined the army at Coxe's Mill on Deep River, North Carolina, on July 25, 1780. [HIGH on date/place]
- **The de Kalb question [HIGH]:** the army Gates inherited was built and led by **Maj. Gen. Johann de Kalb**, who had marched the Maryland and Delaware Continental division (~1,400 starving, ragged veterans — the cream of Washington's infantry) south from Morristown in April 1780, too late to save Charleston, and had then held the force together for months on next to no food ("I have struggled with a good many difficulties for Provisions," de Kalb wrote). De Kalb, a French-army professional, had judged an immediate move on Camden too risky without supplies and planned to swing west through the friendly, well-fed Salisbury–Charlotte country if he moved at all. Gates superseded him on arrival; de Kalb took it professionally and commanded the right wing under Gates without recorded complaint. [HIGH on the arc; "without recorded complaint" MED — keep low-key]

## 3. THE CAMPAIGN — THE MARCH THAT BEAT THE ARMY
- **The "grand army" [MED-HIGH]:** Gates's own orders dubbed the force the "Grand Army," and he acted on a paper strength of ~7,000. The reality (see Numbers) was less than half that, most of it militia. Standard accounts present the name with irony; safe to use with attribution to his orders.
- **The route choice [HIGH]:** On July 27, two days after taking command, Gates marched — by the DIRECT road to Camden, ~50 miles shorter, through pine-barren country ("by nature barren, abounding with sandy plains, intersected by swamps, and very thinly inhabited" — Williams) that was also politically hostile. His officers, backing de Kalb's earlier plan, urged the longer western route through Salisbury and Charlotte: Whig country, full of food and potential militia recruits. The officers put their objection in writing; Gates promised a council of officers to discuss it and never called one. [HIGH] **Fairness note [MED-HIGH]:** modern reassessment (JAR, "The Mysterious March of Horatio Gates," 2020) adds Gates's real reasons: he believed supply assurances from North Carolina officials, and moving fast was the way to cover Caswell's exposed NC militia (junction with Caswell was achieved Aug 7). The page may present the choice as defensible-in-purpose, disastrous-in-effect; do not write it as pure stupidity.
- **The hunger [HIGH]:** the army marched through stripped country on green corn, unripe peaches, and lean cattle found in the woods; flour almost nonexistent. The detail that officers thickened their soup with their own hair powder appears in the standard accounts derived from Williams [MED — verify wording before quoting; fine as reported detail]. Dramatic, documented, and central: the army that fought at Camden had been sick and hungry for weeks.
- **The famous "dysentery" framing — at its honest evidence level [HANDLE-WITH-CARE]:** the documented core (Williams's Narrative) is that on the evening of August 15, with no rum to issue, **a gill of molasses per man** was served out with cornmeal and fresh beef ("one gill of molasses per man, and a full ration of corn meal and meat, were issued to the army previous to their march" — Williams), and the quick-baked bread, half-cooked meat, and molasses disordered the men's bowels through the night march — soldiers falling out of the ranks all night. [HIGH that Williams reports the meal and its effects] The popular escalation — "the army fought Camden with mass dysentery" / "molasses lost the battle" — is embellishment; keep it at Williams's level: a hungry army given exactly the wrong meal on the wrong night. [Framing: HIGH; do not over-claim]
- **The numbers (the 3,052 scene) [HIGH on substance; exact dialogue MED]:** at Rugeley's Mill (Clermont), ~13 miles north of Camden, deputy adjutant general **Otho Holland Williams** brought Gates the field return: **3,052 rank and file present and fit for duty** (against the ~7,000 Gates supposed). Gates: "there are enough for our purpose" (wording per Williams's 1822 Narrative — frame as Williams's recollection). The plan ordered for that night: march at 10 p.m. to seize a defensible position at Saunders Creek north of Camden — Gates was NOT intending a head-on dawn assault on the town. [HIGH that the night march aimed at a position, not an attack]
- **Gates weakens himself further [MED-HIGH]:** on August 15 Gates detached ~400 men (100 Maryland Continentals, ~300 NC militia, 2 guns, under Lt. Col. Woolford) to support Sumter's raid on the British supply line on the Wateree — subtracting regulars from the main body the day before battle. Standard in the literature (Boatner et al.); fact-checker should confirm the exact composition.
- **Cornwallis races up from Charleston [HIGH]:** warned by Rawdon of Gates's approach, Cornwallis left Charleston on August 10 and reached Camden on the night of August 13, taking personal command of ~2,200 fit for duty (his post held ~800 sick, plus stores and prisoners he refused to abandon). Rather than retreat, he resolved to attack — and marched at 10 p.m. on August 15 to surprise Gates at Rugeley's Mill. **Both armies set out the same hour, on the same road, in opposite directions.** [HIGH]
- **The night collision (~2–2:30 a.m., Aug 16) [HIGH]:** the heads of the columns ran into each other in the dark on the Great Wagon Road ~5 miles north of Camden. Tarleton's dragoons at the British point; at the American point — unusually — Gates had put **Armand's Legion** (~120 horse, the remnant of Pulaski's corps under French volunteer Col. Charles Armand, who by standard accounts protested that cavalry leading a night march in column was improper [MED]). In the firefight Armand's horse recoiled into the infantry behind; the flanking light infantry under Lt. Col. **Charles Porterfield** of Virginia steadied the line, and Porterfield was mortally wounded (he died of the wound in British hands months later [MED on death date; HIGH on mortal wound]). Both armies pulled apart to wait for light — each now knowing exactly where the other was.
- **"Gentlemen, what is best to be done?" [HIGH-MED]:** prisoners told Gates he faced Cornwallis and ~3,000 (in fact ~2,200). Gates called his general officers and asked the question; only Brig. Gen. Edward Stevens of the Virginia militia answered: **"Gentlemen, is it not too late now to do any thing but fight?"** No one — de Kalb included, whatever his private doubts — argued retreat aloud. (Both quotes via Williams's Narrative; frame as Williams's account.) Retreat at that point, in the dark, with militia, in contact, would itself have been dangerous. [HIGH]

## 4. THE BATTLE (~daybreak to ~7 a.m. — about an hour)
### The field and the fatal deployment [HIGH]
- Open, park-like longleaf pine wood astride the Great Wagon Road, both flanks resting on the boggy margins of **Gum Swamp** — a narrow front that protected the flanks of the SMALLER army (Cornwallis's) and neutralized the American advantage in numbers.
- **American line:** right — de Kalb with Gist's 2nd Maryland Brigade + the Delaware Regiment; center — Caswell's ~1,800 North Carolina militia; left — Stevens's ~700 Virginia militia (arrived in camp only Aug 14), with Armand's horse behind the left; reserve — Smallwood's 1st Maryland Brigade on the road, ~200 yards back. 7–8 guns. Gates took post behind the reserve, ~600 yards from the line [MED on exact distance].
- **British line:** right — Lt. Col. **James Webster** with the light infantry, 23rd (Royal Welch Fusiliers), and 33rd Foot (~530 in the two numbered regiments); left — **Lord Rawdon**, 25 years old, with his Volunteers of Ireland, the Legion infantry, and Loyalist units; reserve — both battalions of the 71st (Highlanders) and Tarleton's ~180 dragoons. 4 guns forward. [HIGH; British gun count MED — some accounts add reserve pieces]
- **The placement error [HIGH as historiographical consensus]:** by the European convention both commanders followed, each army's senior/best troops stood on its own right — which automatically put Gates's best (the Continentals) opposite the British WEAKER wing, and his rawest militia opposite Webster's regulars. Cornwallis followed the same convention; Gates, with the larger militia mass, paid for it. Hamilton skewered the disposition itself within weeks ("His best troops placed on the side strongest by nature, his worst, on that weakest by nature" — see Quotes). Historians' consensus: a commander who knew his militia (as Greene and Morgan later showed they did) would not have asked them to stand in open pinewoods against a bayonet charge by the 23rd and 33rd.
### The collapse [HIGH]
- At first light Williams (by his own account) urged Gates to strike Webster's wing while it was still deploying; Stevens's Virginians were ordered forward. Webster's regulars answered the way regulars did: a volley, a cheer, and a bayonet charge. **Williams: the British advance "threw the whole body of the militia into such a panic that they generally threw down their loaded arms and fled in the utmost consternation"** — *loaded* arms: most of the Virginia militia never fired. The North Carolina militia in the center went with them (one regiment — Dixon's, of Gregory's brigade, next to the Continentals — stood and fought [MED-HIGH]). The Virginians' total loss was reported at three wounded — the measure of how instantly they broke. [HIGH]
- Militiaman **Garret Watts** (1834 pension statement): "I confess I was amongst the first that fled… It was instantaneous. There was no effort to rally, no encouragement to fight." [HIGH that he said it; trim ellipses honestly]
- **Gates swept away [HIGH]:** the rout of ~2,000 militia poured back over and around the reserve and the command post. Gates and Caswell tried to rally them — Magill (aide): Gates "rode about twenty yards in the rear of the line, to rally them," then tried again further back; Jefferson's official report to Congress described "utmost and unremitting exertions… to rally them" that "proved ineffectual" — and then were carried along in the flood. Gates says he meant to re-form at Clermont (Rugeley's); the mass never stopped. From there he rode for Charlotte. [HIGH on attempts + being swept; see Section 6 for the ride]
### De Kalb's stand [HIGH]
- On the right, nobody told the Continentals the battle was already lost. De Kalb and Gist's ~600–900 Marylanders and Delawares threw back Rawdon's first attack and counterattacked into his line — by several accounts nearly breaking it and taking ~50 prisoners; Cornwallis rode in personally to steady the Volunteers of Ireland. [HIGH]
- Webster, instead of chasing militia, wheeled his regulars left onto the Continentals' open flank. Smallwood's 1st Maryland reserve, attacked while coming up, was driven apart from the 2nd Brigade and could never close the gap (Smallwood himself was carried off in the rout [MED]). The Continentals fought on in the smoke-filled pines, flanked on both sides, for the better part of an hour. [HIGH]
- De Kalb's horse was killed under him; his head was laid open by a saber cut; on foot, he kept his men at it and led a final bayonet counterattack [HIGH on unhorsed/kept fighting; the saber-cut detail MED-HIGH]. Cornwallis then released **Tarleton's dragoons** against the Continentals' rear, and the last formation broke. "Rout and slaughter ensued in every quarter" (Tarleton). [HIGH]
- **De Kalb fell with eleven wounds — eight bayonet, three musket balls** (the count is from his aide, the Chevalier du Buysson, who threw himself over the fallen general, shouting his rank, and took wounds himself — the "eleven wounds" tradition is unusually well-sourced). British soldiers were stripping him when officers intervened; Cornwallis had him carried into Camden and treated by British surgeons. He died there on **August 19** and was buried with full military honors, British officers attending. Du Buysson: "Lord Cornwallis and Rawdon treated us with the greatest civility." [HIGH via du Buysson's letter; see Quotes + Apocrypha for the unverified deathbed quotes]
- **The escapes [HIGH]:** Gist took ~100 men off in a body through Gum Swamp, where cavalry could not follow; small parties under officers like John Eager Howard and Robert Kirkwood (names that return at Cowpens) cut their way out. Roughly half the Continentals engaged got away; no formed American unit left the field.
### Tarleton's pursuit [HIGH]
- The Legion cavalry rode the wreck down the Wagon Road for ~22 miles, to beyond Hanging Rock, sabering fugitives and taking wagons. Charles Stedman (British commissary officer and historian, present in the campaign): "The road for some miles was strewed with the wounded and killed who had been overtaken by the legion in their pursuit. The numbers of dead horses, broken waggons, and baggage scattered on the road formed a perfect scene of horror and confusion." [HIGH-MED — Stedman 1794; spelling per original where used]
- The army's entire train was lost: all 7–8 guns, ~200 wagons, the baggage, the papers. [HIGH]
- **Fishing Creek, August 18 (the coda) [HIGH]:** two days later Tarleton surprised Sumter's camp at Fishing Creek on the Catawba — ~150 of Sumter's men killed, ~300 captured, the Woolford detachment's gains all retaken; Sumter escaped half-dressed on an unsaddled horse and rode into Charlotte alone. In 72 hours the British had wiped out both organized American forces in South Carolina.

## 5. CASUALTIES (dossier ranges — returns are chaotic; use ranges)
- **American [HIGH on the chaos; ranges as follows]:** killed and wounded commonly put at **600–900** (the documented floor: Rankin's count of ~240 known dead — 162 Continentals, 63 NC militia, 12 SC, 3 VA — plus 290 wounded prisoners carried into Camden; Cornwallis claimed "between 800 and 900" rebels killed, certainly high). **About 1,000 captured** (Cornwallis's Aug 21 report). Total loss ~1,900 including the captured; beyond the numbers, the army **ceased to exist as a force** — what re-assembled at Hillsborough weeks later was ~700–800 Continentals. All guns and the train lost. Among the dead/dying: de Kalb; among the mortally wounded from the night action: Porterfield. [Match the spine's "600–900 + ~1,000 captured."]
- **British [HIGH]:** 68–69 killed, ~245 wounded, ~11 missing — **~324** total, about 15% of Cornwallis's force (heaviest in Rawdon's wing, the measure of how hard the Continentals fought).
- **The Virginia militia's reported loss of 3 wounded [HIGH-MED]** — usable as the single starkest number of the day, with its sting stated plainly.

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **The second southern army destroyed in four months [HIGH]:** Charleston (May 12) took ~5,000+; Camden annihilated the replacement. South Carolina garrisoned, Georgia royal, North Carolina apparently open — Camden is the British high-water mark in the South. Yet the partisan war the summer had ignited did not stop: Marion freed ~150 Maryland prisoners from a Camden-bound escort at Great Savannah within days [MED — verify date/figure before use], and seven weeks later the over-mountain men destroyed Ferguson at Kings Mountain (Oct 7) — the page should end Camden where the spine does: the field army was gone; the war in the South was not.
- **Gates's disgrace [HIGH]:** Gates reached Charlotte (~60 miles) the evening of the battle and Hillsborough — where the North Carolina government sat, and where he set about collecting the wreck and calling for supplies — on August 19. His letter to Congress (Aug 20, to President Samuel Huntington) opens: "In the deepest Distress and Anxiety of Mind, I am obliged to acquaint your Excellency with the Total Defeat of the Troops under my Command…" [HIGH-MED on exact wording — widely reproduced; confirm initial "the" at draft time]. Hamilton's mockery (see Quotes) fixed the ride for history. The defense, equally documented: eyewitnesses and Jefferson's report confirm he tried to rally the militia before being swept; Henry Lee later judged Hillsborough the right place to rebuild; Greene wrote to Gates that the southern officers thought him "more unfortunate than criminal" [MED on exact phrase]. Tell both honestly.
- **The inquiry that never tried him [HIGH]:** Congress resolved on **October 5, 1780** to order a court of inquiry into Gates's conduct and authorized Washington to appoint a successor. Washington named **Nathanael Greene** — his choice all along (Oct 22); Greene reached Charlotte and took command on **December 3, 1780** (~2,300 on the rolls, ~1,500 fit). The court of inquiry was never actually convened — Greene and the southern officers declined to treat Gates as a criminal, and Congress repealed the inquiry resolution in 1782; Gates rejoined the main army briefly and never held a field command again. (His only son Robert died in October 1780, weeks after the battle — usable as one humanizing clause [MED].) [HIGH on resolution/appointment/dates; "never convened/repealed 1782" HIGH-MED]
- **De Kalb's grave and monuments [HIGH]:** buried at Camden with military honors in 1780; Congress voted him a monument on October 14, 1780 [MED on exact date — verify if used]. In **March 1825** his remains were moved to the yard of **Bethesda Presbyterian Church, Camden**, under a monument designed by **Robert Mills**; **Lafayette — who had crossed the Atlantic with him in 1777 — laid the cornerstone** (March 9, 1825, during his American tour). A bronze statue of de Kalb (Ephraim Keyser, 1886) stands at the Maryland State House in Annapolis — Maryland honoring the commander of its division [MED on sculptor/date — verify if used; no Commons photo found this session]. The Washington-at-the-grave quote is apocrypha — see blacklist.
- **What Camden taught (set up, don't tell) [HIGH as historiography]:** the lesson was not "militia are useless" — it was that militia in open ground, in a main line, against a bayonet charge, was a sentence of rout. The man who absorbed it was **Daniel Morgan** (who joined the southern army that autumn): at **Cowpens, January 17, 1781**, he would put the militia OUT FRONT, ask them for exactly two volleys, and promise them they could then retire behind the Continentals — turning Camden's failure into the war's most perfect small battle. The Camden page should plant this (militia asked to do the impossible) without narrating Cowpens.
- **Camden battlefield today [MED]:** several hundred acres of the pine-woods battlefield are preserved (American Battlefield Trust / SC Battleground Preservation Trust, a Liberty Trail site); traces of the Great Wagon Road still run through it; markers stand where de Kalb is believed to have fallen. Keep general unless figures are re-verified.

## 11. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)
- **Date:** August 16, 1780 · **Where:** ~5–6 miles north of Camden, South Carolina, astride the Great Wagon Road
- **Forces:** ~3,000–4,000 American fit for duty (3,052 rank-and-file by Williams's Aug 15 return; about two-thirds militia; Continentals ~900–1,500 — see Uncertainties) vs ~2,200 British (2,239 fit by Cornwallis's return)
- **Duration:** about one hour at first light, after both armies' night marches collided at ~2 a.m.
- **Casualties:** American ~600–900 killed and wounded + ~1,000 captured, all guns and the train lost, the army destroyed; British ~324 (68–69 killed, ~245 wounded)
- **Winner line material:** Decisive British victory — the most complete rout of an American army in the war, and the second southern army gone in four months. But Cornwallis had won a province that was already burning behind him: within two months Kings Mountain, within five Cowpens — fought by men who had learned at Camden exactly what militia could not be asked to do, and what they could.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. **American strength:** Gates believed ~7,000; Williams's return = 3,052 rank-and-file fit (Aug 15, before ~400 detached to Sumter); totals "engaged" appear as 3,000–4,100 across sources. Use "between three and four thousand," anchored to the 3,052 return.
2. **Continental count:** ~900 regulars (Wikipedia order-of-battle) vs ~1,500 (ABT, the division at campaign start). The Sumter detachment + sickness explain part of the gap. DISPUTED — write "the nine hundred or so Continentals" for the battle line only with a hedge, or stay at "fewer than a thousand effectives in the two Maryland brigades and the Delawares" [safest].
3. **American casualties:** documented floor ~240 known dead + 290 wounded prisoners (Rankin) vs Cornwallis's claimed 800–900 killed; ~1,000 prisoners is solid. Use the spine's "600–900 killed and wounded, ~1,000 captured."
4. **British guns:** 4 forward is standard; some accounts count more with the reserve. Say "four guns."
5. **De Kalb's wounds:** 11 (8 bayonet + 3 ball) per du Buysson — unusually well-sourced for such a tradition, but write "some eleven wounds" (spine wording) and credit the aide. Du Buysson's "two days after the action" vs Aug 19 death date: reconcile as died Aug 19 (battle Aug 16); do not write "two days later."
6. **Hamilton quote:** wording HIGH-attested via PAH reproductions; Founders Online unreachable this session — re-confirm byte-exactness before shipping it inside quotation marks.
7. **The council + numbers dialogue ("enough for our purpose," "too late… but fight"):** all via Williams's 1822 Narrative — attribute as his account, not neutral fact.
8. **The hair-powder soup detail:** widely repeated from Williams; wording not pinned this session — use as reported detail, no quotes.
9. **Dixon's regiment standing:** standard but MED — "one North Carolina regiment beside the Continentals stood" is safe; name Dixon only with a hedge.
10. **Armand's protest at leading the column / his men's conduct:** standard accounts (from Williams) but partisan — Gates blamed the legion, Armand felt slandered. Keep to "the cavalry recoiled into the column behind"; skip the blame fight or flag it as disputed.
11. **Porterfield's death:** mortally wounded in the night action [HIGH]; died in British hands weeks-to-months later — exact date varies (autumn 1780–Jan 1781). Say "died of the wound in captivity."
12. **Gates's rally attempts vs the ride:** both documented; the page must carry both (Magill/Pinckney/Jefferson for the attempts; Charlotte-that-night/Hillsborough-Aug-19 for the ride; Hamilton for the mockery; Lee/Greene/the never-held inquiry for the verdict).
13. **Sumter detachment (Aug 15, ~400 men incl. 100 MD Continentals under Woolford):** standard but composition MED — fact-checker to confirm.
14. **Monument details:** Mills design + Lafayette cornerstone March 1825 HIGH; the exact day (Mar 8 vs 9), the Congress-votes-a-monument date (Oct 14, 1780), and the Annapolis statue (Keyser, 1886) are MED — verify any that ship.
15. **Battlefield preservation acreage:** figures vary by year; keep to "several hundred acres preserved" or verify current ABT number at draft time.
