## FLAGGED ITEMS — EXPLICIT RESOLUTION

### 1. "You are green, it is true; but they are green also" — Scott or Lincoln?

**Draft attribution:** General Winfield Scott (with a parenthetical note that Lincoln is the popular misattribution).

**Verification result:** ✅ CONFIRMED — Scott attribution is the more defensible scholarly position.

Sources confirm the controversy. The popular version attributes it to Lincoln; however, historian John J. Hennessy (*The First Battle of Manassas: An End to Innocence*) and Encyclopedia Virginia both credit Winfield Scott. The CivilWarMonths analysis notes that McDowell refers to it as "the answer" — language consistent with a conversation with Scott, his direct superior, not Lincoln. Wikipedia's article on the battle uses the Lincoln attribution in the text but acknowledges the Scott attribution elsewhere. The draft's handling — attribute to Scott, note Lincoln is the popular misattribution, flag as contested — is exactly the right call. No change needed.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED (Scott; draft handles correctly)

### 2. Patterson's telegram / belief he was holding Johnston

**Draft treatment:** Describes (does not quote) Patterson's belief and report that he had Johnston contained.

**Verification result:** ✅ CONFIRMED — substance verified; non-quotation is correct.

The exact Patterson telegram wording — "I have succeeded, in accordance with the wishes of the General-in-Chief, in keeping General Johnston's force at Winchester" — is confirmed by Wikipedia and corroborated in multiple secondary sources. The draft correctly chose not to quote this verbatim (per the fact-pack's [VERIFY] flag), describing only the substance. This is the safer approach and is accurate.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED (substance; non-quotation was the right call)

### 3. The rebel yell — no "yell like furies" quote

**Draft treatment:** Describes the rebel yell, credits Bull Run as its first major-battle appearance, explicitly notes that Jackson's "yell like furies" order comes only from post-war accounts (1865 London *Times*; 1866 Dabney biography) and is not in Jackson's official reports.

**Verification result:** ✅ CONFIRMED — this is exactly right.

Encyclopedia Virginia and multiple secondary sources confirm the "yell like furies" attribution: it derives from an unsigned January 17, 1865 London *Times* article and Robert Lewis Dabney's 1866 Jackson biography — not Jackson's own reports. The draft's explicit caveat is accurate and appropriate. No change needed.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED (draft's caveat is accurate)

### 4. "~50-odd miles" — Patterson's distance from the field

**Draft text:** "the gun pointed at his plan was already being fired, 50-odd miles to the west."

**Verification result:** ⚠️ ACCEPTABLE but imprecise — see below.

Patterson's force was near Winchester in the Shenandoah Valley when Johnston slipped away on July 18. Winchester to Manassas Junction is approximately 57 miles by road/rail route. The actual rail distance from Piedmont Station to Manassas Junction (which the draft also describes) is ~34 miles. The "50-odd miles" locator applies to Patterson's position (Winchester area) relative to the battlefield, not the rail haul. At ~57 miles, "50-odd miles" understates the actual distance by roughly 7 miles. However, because this is used as a loose narrative locator ("50-odd miles to the west") rather than a precise claim, and 57 falls within the spirit of "50-odd" in colloquial usage, this is a borderline case. The rail haul (~34 miles) is separately and accurately stated. This is flagged as a SHOULD-FIX for precision, not a MUST-FIX.

**Status:** ⚠️ BORDERLINE — closer to "~55–60 miles" would be more accurate

### 6. Casualty figures — ~4,700 total; US/CS split; scholarly range

**Draft claims:**
- "roughly 4,878 men killed, wounded, captured, or missing — about 2,896 on the Union side and 1,982 on the Confederate"
- "scholarly figures vary, running somewhere in the range of 4,700 to 4,900 total"
- "Around 847 Americans were killed in this single battle — more than all the U.S. battle deaths in the entire Mexican-American War"

**Verification result:** ✅ CONFIRMED with one precision note.

The standard ABT/Wikipedia figures of Union 2,896 / Confederate 1,982 / Total ~4,878 are confirmed. The scholarly range (4,700–4,878 across sources) is confirmed — Wikipedia itself gives alternate Union figures of 2,708, Encyclopedia Virginia gives Confederate as 1,897. The draft's acknowledgment of the range is appropriate.

The 847 killed figure (combined) is consistent with 460+387=847 from the ABT split. However, note that Wikipedia's article cites an alternate Union killed figure of 481 (not 460), which would change the combined killed to 868. The ABT figure (460+387=847) is the most commonly cited; the draft correctly uses it. The Mexican War comparison: Clodfelter's figure of 1,733 battle deaths is confirmed as the scholarly reference used for this comparison. The draft's claim that 847 single-battle deaths exceeded "all U.S. battle deaths in the entire Mexican-American War" overstates this — 1,733 (Mexican War) is more than 847 (Bull Run killed). The draft is comparing single-day *killed* at Bull Run to total Mexican War battle deaths — this is an accurate comparison (847 < 1,733), but the prose says "more than all the U.S. battle deaths in the entire Mexican-American War," which is **WRONG** on its face. 847 is NOT more than 1,733.

**This is a factual error in the draft.** The correct framing is: *the number killed at Bull Run in a single day (~847) approached — or represented roughly half of — all U.S. battle deaths in the Mexican-American War over two years*, which is still striking. Alternatively the comparison should be to a different metric (e.g., killed in any single battle of the Mexican War — which Bull Run does exceed). The fact pack makes the same error in the "contextual shock" section, so this originated in the pack. The fact-checker is calling it regardless.

**Status:** ❌ WRONG — "more than all the U.S. battle deaths in the entire Mexican-American War" is false (847 < 1,733). See MUST-FIX #1.

**Draft claims:** Johnston screened with Stuart, marched July 18–19 to Piedmont Station (present-day Delaplane, VA), shuttled ~34 miles east by Manassas Gap RR, "first time in history that railroads were used to rush troops to a major battle."

**Verification result:** ✅ CONFIRMED.

All confirmed: Stuart's cavalry screen, the July 18–19 departure, Piedmont Station / Delaplane, the Manassas Gap Railroad, the ~34-mile rail haul, the first-strategic-rail-use claim. Multiple authoritative sources (Encyclopedia Virginia, Essential Civil War Curriculum, Wikipedia) confirm. Troops began arriving July 19; Johnston himself July 20; Kirby Smith's brigade July 21 during the battle.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED

### 8. Judith Henry — age, sole civilian death, cause of injury

**Draft claims:** "Judith Carter Henry, around 85 years old, was a bedridden widow… A shell tore through her bedroom wall, severing one of her feet and inflicting other wounds, and she died that day. Judith Henry is considered the first civilian killed in the American Civil War."

**Verification result:** ✅ CONFIRMED (with one minor nuance).

Age ~85: confirmed (Wikipedia, Smithsonian, Find-a-Grave give birthdate 1776 and death 1861 = 85 years old).
Bedridden: confirmed.
Family tried to carry her out, she begged to return: confirmed by Smithsonian.
Union artillery fired on house targeting suspected sharpshooters: confirmed.
Shell severed a foot: confirmed by Wikipedia ("tore off one of the widow's feet").
Died that day: confirmed.
"First civilian killed": the Smithsonian's headline calls her "the Civil War's First Civilian Casualty," but the body text uses "first known" (the fact pack flagged this correctly). The draft uses "considered the first civilian killed," which appropriately hedges. No change needed on the framing.

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED

Exchanged for former U.S. Minister to France Charles James Faulkner: confirmed.

One small note: The draft describes Libby Prison without specifying it as a tobacco warehouse, which is accurate (Libby Prison was a converted tobacco warehouse, but the draft doesn't claim otherwise).

**Status:** ✅ CONFIRMED

## MUST-FIX LIST

### ❌ MUST-FIX #1 — Mexican-American War casualty comparison (FACTUAL ERROR)

**Location:** Section "The Rout" — "Around 847 Americans were killed in this single battle — more than all the U.S. battle deaths in the entire Mexican-American War"

**Problem:** The claim is false. U.S. battle deaths in the Mexican-American War are approximately 1,733 (Clodfelter). The 847 killed at Bull Run is substantially *less than* that figure, not more than it.

**Correct framing options (pick one):**
- "Around 847 Americans were killed in this single battle — roughly half of all U.S. battle deaths in the entire Mexican-American War, which took two years to accumulate them." (Accurate and still striking.)
- Or anchor the comparison to a different metric: "more men killed on a single Sunday than in any single battle of the Mexican-American War." (Would need verification of that specific sub-claim.)
- Or drop the Mexican War comparison and replace with the raw shock framing: "more Americans killed in one Sunday afternoon than the United States had lost in any previous engagement on its own soil."

**Note:** This error originated in the fact pack itself (Section 2: "~847 Americans killed in a single day — more than all U.S. combat deaths in the entire Mexican-American War"). The pack's framing conflates "combat deaths" with "battle deaths" and the numbers don't support it under any reading. The fact-checker is overriding the pack on this point.

---

### ❌ MUST-FIX #2 — "Griffin held fire" is incomplete / misleading

**Location:** Section "There Stands Jackson" — "At one point a Confederate regiment in blue uniforms, the 33rd Virginia, advanced on the Union guns; Griffin took them for friendly troops and held his fire"

**Problem:** Multiple sources confirm it was **Major William F. Barry** (McDowell's chief of artillery) who ordered Griffin to hold fire — not Griffin's own judgment. Griffin reportedly *wanted* to fire and was overruled by Barry. The draft implies it was Griffin's error in recognition; the historical record shows Griffin was explicitly ordered to hold fire by his superior. This is a meaningful factual distinction: Griffin's inaction was ordered, not voluntary.

**Correction:** Change to something like: "Griffin's battery commander, Major William F. Barry (North), ordered him to hold fire, mistaking them for Union troops; the Confederates overran both batteries at close range."

## SHOULD-FIX LIST

### ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX #1 — Flanking column delay: "roughly two hours" vs. "almost three hours"

**Location:** Section "The Flank March" — "a delay of roughly two hours"

**Problem:** The draft says the flanking column was roughly two hours late. Multiple sources, including the detailed battle account on the American Battlefield Trust site, describe the delay as almost three hours behind schedule. Wikipedia says "a two-hour delay" in some passages, but the operational timeline supports closer to 2.5–3 hours (scheduled ~6:30 a.m., arrived ~9:30 a.m.). The draft's "roughly two hours" is not incorrect but may understate the delay.

**Suggested fix:** "a delay of two to three hours" or simply "a delay of several hours."

### ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX #2 — Patterson's distance locator: "50-odd miles to the west"

**Location:** Section "The Flank March" — "50-odd miles to the west"

**Problem:** Patterson was near Winchester when Johnston slipped away. Winchester to Manassas is approximately 57 miles. "50-odd miles" understates this by ~7 miles. Not egregiously wrong but the actual figure is closer to 55–60 miles. Given that the rail haul (~34 miles from Piedmont Station) is accurately stated separately, this loose locator is double-counting approximate distances in a potentially confusing way.

**Suggested fix:** "~55–60 miles to the west" or simply drop the distance and keep the narrative framing of "off to the west."
