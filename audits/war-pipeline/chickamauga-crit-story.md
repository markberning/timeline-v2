# Chickamauga — STORYTELLING critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

> Judged AS A STORY (the #1 goal). Compared against the shipped Shiloh sections
> (`src/app/war-civil-war/western/shiloh/s/[section]/section-narrative.tsx`) for
> voice, pacing, human dimension, and dual-POV. Facts are NOT this gate's job —
> the fact-checker owns the `[VERIFY]` flags. This gate asks only: does it grip,
> does the cost land, does the voice match, and where does it go slack?
>
> Grades: STRONG / GOOD / NEEDS WORK / REWRITE. "Selective is GOOD" — a section
> that compresses the play-by-play to chase the story is rewarded, not penalized.

## Overall: **GOOD** (ships after the MUST-IMPROVE list)

This is a genuinely strong draft that already thinks like a story, not a battle
report. Its spine is excellent: the campaign is framed as *a gate* (the rail
gateway to the Deep South), and that single image is set up in section 1, paid
off in the rout, and inverted in the reckoning ("the side that won the fight lost
the doorway"). That through-line is better story architecture than Shiloh's,
which is more episodic. The dual-POV is real and structural (Rosecrans's elegant
maneuver vs. Bragg's turning trap; Longstreet's column vs. Wood's vacated line;
Thomas's stand vs. Bragg's squandered win). Three of the five OUTCOME verdicts
are genuinely sharp.

Where it falls short of Shiloh is **felt human cost** and **scene-level
intimacy**. Shiloh repeatedly drops the camera to a single human at a single
moment — Sherman with three horses shot out from under him, Johnston's boot
quietly filling with blood, Grant under a tree with the cigar. Chickamauga argues
its stakes more than it *stages* them. The casualties in `the-reckoning` are
mostly delivered as a list of names + injuries rather than as moments. Fixing the
human dimension is the main work between this draft and a ship.

Voice is on-model overall (see notes), with two recurring tics to sand: a
slight over-reliance on the "Here is the X" / "Now the strange part" essayist's
hinge, and one section (`the-maneuver`) where the slavery framing, while
correct and required, is front-loaded as a paragraph-long block that stalls the
hook.

## the-maneuver — *The bloodless prize* — **GOOD**

**Hook & stakes — GOOD, but the hook is buried.** The opening line is excellent:
"the Union won a city without firing the shot that should have been needed to
take it." That is a real hook — a paradox, a promise of a story. Then the
paragraph immediately stops the forward motion for six sentences of
slavery-as-cause exposition before returning to Chattanooga. The framing is
*required and correct* (name-the-cause rule), and it's well written — but
structurally it smothers its own hook. Compare Shiloh's `the-surprise`, which
threads the same mandatory slavery framing *through* the river-strategy narrative
("Grant's campaign down the rivers was, at bottom, a campaign to strangle a
slaveholding republic") so it rides the momentum instead of halting it.

> MUST-IMPROVE: Keep the hook sentence, then let one or two sentences of campaign
> momentum breathe before the slavery block — or weave the cause into the gateway
> image the way Shiloh weaves it into the rivers, so the reader never feels the
> story pause to deliver a civics lesson. Same facts, threaded not stacked.

**Human dimension — NEEDS WORK.** This section is all generals and maps; no human
moment lands. That's acceptable for a setup section, but the Bragg
characterization ("a hard, quarrelsome general who could never quite hold the
loyalty of the officers under him") is the one piece of human texture and it's
good — lean into it, because it's load-bearing for why his army botched Sept 20.

**Play-by-play — clean.** Selective and well-compressed. Tullahoma is summarized
in two sentences and used only for what it teaches (the levering technique that
recurs at Chattanooga). Good restraint.

**Dual POV — STRONG.** Rosecrans's elegance set directly against the "danger
hidden inside a bloodless victory" — and the Meanwhile flips to the Confederate
rail movement out of Virginia. That's the POV pivot doing real work.

**Voice — GOOD.** "Levering his enemy backward by threatening his lines rather
than charging his front" is house-voice — mechanism explained, not just outcome.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE: The placeholder Rosecrans quote block (line 19) must be CUT, not
> just flagged — as written it's an empty slot in the reading experience. The
> author already flags this; confirming it here as a story-gate issue, not only a
> fact issue. An empty pull-quote is worse than no pull-quote. (If the
> fact-checker finds no sourced confident-Rosecrans line, the section reads fine
> without one.)

## the-woods — *A battle nobody could see* — **STRONG**

**Hook & stakes — STRONG.** "This was a battle fought, for the most part, by men
who could not see the battle." That is the best single line in the draft and the
organizing idea of the whole section. The terrain-as-character setup ("The ground
itself is the key to everything that happened here, so it is worth stopping on")
earns its pause because the blindness it establishes pays off three times: Sept
19's blind brawl, the unseen Sept 20 breakthrough, and the friendly-fire cruelty
in the reckoning.

**Human dimension — GOOD.** Wilder's Lightning Brigade with the Spencer
repeaters is the right kind of concrete human detail (and the mechanism is
explained house-voice: "fired round after round without stopping to reload...
giving a few men the firepower of many"). Still mostly unit-level rather than
single-soldier-level, but for a "nobody could see it" section that's defensible —
the anonymity IS the point.

**Play-by-play — STRONG / selective.** Sept 18 is correctly called "the
curtain-raiser" and dispatched in one paragraph. Sept 19, which could have been a
tedious four-mile brigade-by-brigade slog, is handled as *texture* ("Lines
collided in the timber, fired blind, and lost cohesion almost as fast as they
formed") rather than enumeration. Exactly the right call — it refuses the tedium
trap.

**Dual POV — GOOD.** Bragg gathering and turning vs. Rosecrans quietly shifting
north — and the section plants the seed ("That northward shift would matter
enormously in the morning") that pays off as both rescue and disaster next
section.

**Voice — STRONG.** The "river of death" handling is a model of the house voice's
honesty reflex: "Treat the grim name as a story the war wrote onto the place
afterward, not a Cherokee prophecy." That's the same instinct as Shiloh's
"Sunken Road" / "Bloody Pond" myth-busting.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE: The Kelly Field Meanwhile is good foreshadowing but slightly
> mechanical ("walls of stacked timber to fire from behind and force any attacker
> to cross open ground"). Minor — the definition is fine; could be one beat
> warmer.

## the-gap — *The hole in the line* — **STRONG**

**Hook & stakes — STRONG.** "the cruel part is that it was nobody's idea and
everybody obeyed it." This is the dramatic engine of the entire battle and the
section nails the framing. The tragedy is set up as inevitability: a wrong report,
a literal-minded obedience born of a prior reprimand, and a coincidence "so
precise it reads like fiction." That's storytelling, not chronology.

**Human dimension — GOOD, could be GREAT with one beat.** Wood is well drawn —
"who had recently been dressed down for not obeying an order quickly enough,
obeyed this one to the letter." That single clause makes a man's catastrophic
decision *understandable*, which is the heart of the section. But the rout itself
is reported from altitude ("Half the army was routed off the field in minutes").
Shiloh would have given us one soldier in that flood.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE: Garfield (future president) is name-checked in the rout list but
> his moment is saved for the next section. Fine — but consider one concrete image
> of the rout itself (men, dust, the road choked) so "swept clean off the field"
> is *seen*, not just stated. The Rosecrans detail — riding all the way back to
> Chattanooga "convinced the battle was lost" — is the human beat that lands here;
> it's good, keep it forward.

**Play-by-play — STRONG.** Polk's late attack, the Wood order, Longstreet's
column — three moving parts, sequenced for cause-and-effect, no tedium. The
timestamp specificity (9:30, 11:10) is used for *suspense* (the column was
already moving as the gap opened), not as a log.

**Dual POV — STRONG.** This is the best POV section: the Union mistake and the
Confederate hammer converging on the same patch of ground at the same minute,
told as two halves of one trap. The Meanwhile (the LaFayette Road becoming "the
channel for the rout") is a sharp ironic inversion of the road Rosecrans had
worked to protect.

**Voice — STRONG.** "a battering ram of infantry," "by a coincidence so precise
it reads like fiction" — confident, vivid, on-model.

## the-rock — *The man who would not leave* — **GOOD**

**Hook & stakes — GOOD.** "Someone did." as a one-word answer to the prior
section's cliffhanger ("whether anyone could hold the field") is a strong,
confident hinge. The Thomas-as-Virginian-slaveholder irony ("a son of the
slaveholding South fighting against it") is the kind of cross-cutting human
contradiction the house voice loves, and it ties the stand back to the war's
cause without preaching.

**Human dimension — NEEDS WORK — this is the section that most under-delivers on
its own promise.** The whole section is *about* one man's defiance, and yet
Thomas barely acts as a person on the page. We're told "He set them on the ridge
and told them to hold. And from around one in the afternoon until dark, they
held." — but we never *see* Thomas. Compare Shiloh's Sherman, who "rode up and
down his line under fire... was wounded twice... had three horses shot out from
under him." The Rock of Chickamauga deserves at least that much physical presence.
The stand is summarized ("Wave after wave... each time Thomas's improvised line
beat it back") rather than dramatized.

> MUST-IMPROVE: Give Thomas one concrete sensory/physical beat on the ridge — the
> gathering of broken units, a moment of him under fire or refusing to yield
> ground, the ammunition running low, anything that makes the reader *stand on
> Snodgrass Hill* rather than read a plaque about it. Granger "marching to the
> sound of the guns without waiting for orders" is the one alive moment in the
> section — that's the register the Thomas material should be in. The factpack
> presumably has texture here (improvised line, low ammo, Granger's bayonet
> charge); pull it forward.

**Play-by-play — GOOD.** Correctly compressed; the stand is treated as endurance,
not a charge-by-charge log. Good.

**Dual POV — GOOD but lighter.** This is deliberately a Union-side section (the
rescue), and that's a legitimate choice. The Confederate side is mostly the
faceless "wave after wave." The Meanwhile pivots well to the strategic frame
(Chattanooga turning from conquest to trap).

**Voice — GOOD.** Handles the nickname provenance with exactly the right honesty
("the cleanest version of it is too tidy to fully trust... do NOT assert Garfield
invented it in the moment"). The "Whatever the exact words and whoever first spoke
them, the stand was real and the name was earned" recovery is house-voice gold —
same move as Shiloh's "whether or not Lincoln ever spoke those exact words, he did
keep Grant."

## the-reckoning — *A victory thrown away* — **GOOD**

**Hook & stakes — STRONG.** "Start with the number, because the number tells you
how savage it was" is a direct, intentional echo of Shiloh's `the-reckoning`
("Start with the number, because the number is the whole story") — appropriate
template-matching, and it works. The "bitter arithmetic" — winner lost more men
than the loser — is a genuinely arresting framing.

**Human dimension — NEEDS WORK — the casualties read as a roster.** This is the
section where cost should be felt hardest, and instead the maimed/dead generals
arrive as a bulleted-feeling list: Hood (leg amputated), Lytle (poet, killed),
Helm (Lincoln's brother-in-law), Deshler and Preston Smith (killed). Each is a
potentially devastating human story compressed to a clause. Hood losing the leg
ten weeks after losing the arm at Gettysburg is staged decently. But the Helm /
Lincoln connection — *the war reaching into the First Family on the winning-
Confederate side* — is the emotional peak of the whole battle and it's delivered
almost in passing ("Lincoln reportedly mourned the young man personally"). Shiloh
gave Mary Todd Lincoln's brother Samuel Todd a quiet, devastating beat ("The
Lincolns, like thousands of less famous families... learned what this war was
going to cost"). Helm deserves at least equal weight — arguably more, because the
irony is sharper.

> MUST-IMPROVE: Slow down on Helm. This is the section's strongest human moment and
> it's rushed. Give Lincoln mourning a Confederate general — his own kin — its own
> couple of sentences. And consider whether the dead-generals material reads less
> like a list and more like the dense woods *claiming* men (tie it to the section's
> own "the dense woods... made it uniquely cruel" frame — the wounded in burning
> underbrush is a haunting image that's currently stated and dropped).

**Play-by-play — GOOD.** The Bragg-squanders-it turn and the siege→Grant→
Missionary Ridge sequence is compressed well; the two-month inversion is told as
story, not chronology.

**Dual POV — STRONG.** "the side that won the field lost more men than the side it
drove off" is the dual-POV thesis of the whole battle in one line. The
tactical-win/strategic-failure split is sustained and lands.

**Pacing — GOOD, but the close is slightly over-summarized.** The final two
paragraphs + OUTCOME + Meanwhile all restate the same inversion ("the South won
the field, lost more men... and within two months had handed the Union the very
gateway"). The idea is excellent but it's delivered three times in a row (closing
para, OUTCOME, Meanwhile). One of those is redundant.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE: Trim the triple-restatement of the gateway inversion at the end.
> Let the prose close, let OUTCOME verdict it once, let Meanwhile point forward —
> right now all three say the same sentence. Pick the sharpest and cut the echo.

**Voice — GOOD.** "Now the strange part." is a slightly worn essayist's hinge
(it's the second "now the X" / "here is the X" turn in the draft — see also
the-maneuver's "But there is a danger hidden inside a bloodless victory"). Minor,
but worth varying.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE (voice, draft-wide tic): The draft leans on the explicit
> essayist's hinge — "Here is the bitter arithmetic," "Now the strange part," "But
> there is a danger hidden inside." Used once it's voice; used four times it's a
> verbal crutch. Vary or cut two of them.

## Consolidated MUST-IMPROVE (ship-blocking for the story gate)

1. **`the-maneuver`: unbury the hook.** Thread the (required) slavery-as-cause
   framing *through* the campaign momentum the way Shiloh threads it through the
   rivers, instead of stacking it as a six-sentence block that halts the opening
   paradox. Same facts, woven not piled.
2. **`the-maneuver`: cut the placeholder quote block (line 19)** — an empty
   pull-quote is a hole in the reading experience, not just a fact flag.
3. **`the-rock`: make Thomas physically present on the ridge.** The section is
   about one man's defiance and never shows the man. Add at least one concrete,
   sensory beat of Thomas under fire / the improvised line / ammunition running
   out — match the Sherman-at-Shiloh register the app has already set.
4. **`the-reckoning`: slow down on Helm + the Lincoln connection.** The emotional
   peak of the battle is currently a passing clause. Give it the weight Shiloh
   gave Samuel Todd. And turn the dead-generals roster into felt cost (lean on the
   "burning underbrush / uniquely cruel woods" image you already have).

## Consolidated SHOULD-IMPROVE

- **`the-gap`:** one concrete image of the rout itself (the choked road, the dust)
  so "swept clean off the field" is seen, not just asserted.
- **`the-reckoning`:** trim the triple-restatement of the gateway-inversion at the
  close (prose + OUTCOME + Meanwhile currently all say it). Keep the sharpest.
- **Draft-wide voice:** vary/cut the repeated essayist's hinge ("Now the strange
  part," "Here is the bitter arithmetic," "But there is a danger hidden inside,"
  "The ground itself is the key... so it is worth stopping on"). Once = voice,
  four times = crutch.
- **`the-woods`:** warm up the Kelly Field Meanwhile definition by one beat.

## What to PROTECT (do not let revision sand these off)

- The gate/doorway through-line — it's the draft's best asset; carry it to ship.
- "a battle fought... by men who could not see the battle" (the-woods).
- "it was nobody's idea and everybody obeyed it" + "a coincidence so precise it
  reads like fiction" (the-gap).
- The honesty moves on "river of death," the Rock nickname provenance, and the
  Wood-order gist — all model house-voice and must survive fact-checking intact.
- "the side that won the field lost more men than the side it drove off."
