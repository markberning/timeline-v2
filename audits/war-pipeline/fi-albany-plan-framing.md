## FIRST-CHECK (ship-blocking)

### (a) Native nations as first-class actors with their own aims — **PASS / FAIR**
The draft does not merely include the Iroquois; it structures the whole section around them. Findings:

- The congress's *primary* purpose is correctly led with as Native diplomacy, not union. Section "Why London called the meeting" states plainly that "The reason the colonies met at all was that the Native nations were drifting away, and the empire needed them back." (lines 13–17) This inverts the textbook framing (Albany = Franklin's union) and centers Native leverage, exactly as the brief demands.
- The Haudenosaunee are explicitly named as a sovereign power, not a tool: "They were not anyone's subjects. They were a power, and they negotiated like one... playing the British and French empires against each other as it suited them." (line 37)
- Theyanoguin (Hendrick) is given his own voice at length, and is framed as a partner rebuking a failed partner, not a supplicant: "He was not pleading. He was telling a partner it had failed to hold up its end, and reminding it who needed whom." (line 53)
- The renewal of the Covenant Chain is framed as conditional and on Iroquois terms — "the Iroquois accepted the renewed alliance, but on their own terms" and "kept where their authority lived" (Onondaga) (lines 61–63). Agency preserved through the outcome.

No reduction of Native nations to backdrop or instrument. This is a model of the first-class-actor standard.

### (b) Human cost / dispossession named in the section's own prose — **PASS / FAIR**
Named plainly and at length, in the section's own voice, not euphemized:

- An entire section ("The land taken in the next room," lines 67–80) is dedicated to the dispossession beat. Opening sentence: "While one part of the congress was renewing a friendship, another part was quietly stealing land, and the difference between the two is the whole truth of the event." (line 70) — uses the word *stealing*; no euphemism.
- The Wyoming Valley deed is correctly called fraudulent, secret, liquor-and-bribe greased, and sold by Mohawk who "did not rightfully control" it (line 72) — matches fact pack §4.
- The excluded occupants (Delaware/Lenape, Shawnee) are named as the people whose home was sold without them, with the Walking Purchase (1737) backstory and the consequence (drift to the French, frontier war) (line 74) — matches fact pack §4.
- The dual-truth instruction is met head-on: "The same gathering that renewed the Covenant Chain also stripped land from Native nations who were not even present to object. Both things are true." (line 78)
- The war-scale cost is named in the closer: "When the war finally ended, it would end as a catastrophe for the Native nations of the eastern woodlands, who lost the leverage of two competing empires and, with it, the ground itself." (line 133) — names the long catastrophe AND the mechanism (loss of empire-vs-empire leverage). This is the strongest possible version of the cost beat for a 1754 prelude theme.
- Hendrick's own line naming the stakes is quoted: "lands which belong to us... such a quarrel as this may end in our destruction." (line 59)

The contest-was-over-Native-land truth is stated in the section's own prose (line 57: "He also named the thing the whole war was actually about. The empires were fighting over ground that belonged to Native nations, and Native nations stood to lose either way.").

### Naming convention — **PASS / FAIR**
The brief's third clause (the "French and Indian War" name centers the British-colonial POV) is addressed directly and well in the final paragraph (line 133): "The British colonists called this the French and Indian War, naming it for the two enemies they expected to fight... The name centers their point of view and hides a larger truth: Native nations were not one side's tool. They fought on all sides and, more than anything, for themselves." This is verbatim the standard the brief sets.

**Both first-check gates PASS.**

## OTHER FRAMING FINDINGS

### 1. False balance / both-sidesing — **FAIR**
No false balance. The draft does not present empire dispossession as morally symmetric to Native resistance; it names theft as theft. The "both things are true" construction (line 78) is dual-*truth* (diplomacy AND land fraud happened in the same town), not dual-*blame* — appropriate and accurate.

### 2. Romanticization of empire — **FAIR**
No romanticization of British or French imperial purpose. Britain is shown as neglectful, self-interested, and rejecting union to keep colonies subordinate (line 108). The Crown's land-purchase power in the Plan is reported neutrally as a power "to take out of thirteen quarreling hands" (line 99) — the draft does not celebrate it. Franklin is given his due but not hagiographized; his "might-have-been" is clearly marked as *his* retrospective opinion (lines 110, 123), not the narrator's.

### 3. Erasure of Native agency — **FAIR**
The opposite of erasure. The Iroquois are shown choosing neutrality-or-France as leverage (line 15), accepting renewal on their own terms (line 61), and going home "only partly satisfied" (line 65). Even the dispossession section preserves agency where due: the Mohawk signers are noted as selling land that was not theirs to sell (line 72), which is an honest complication, not an erasure — it correctly distinguishes Iroquois diplomatic primacy from Delaware/Shawnee dispossession rather than flattening "the Indians" into one undifferentiated bloc.

### 4. Presentism — **FAIR**
No anachronistic moralizing imposed from the present. The judgments rendered (theft, fraud) are sourced to the period record and to long-standing characterization ("called fraudulent ever since," line 72), and the gift-giving diplomacy is explained in its own terms ("gift-giving was not a courtesy in this diplomacy, it was the diplomacy," line 15) rather than judged by modern norms.

### 5. Sanitized or gratuitous violence — **FAIR**
Theme section with no battle content; violence is referenced only as consequence ("fire and blood along the Pennsylvania frontier," line 74; "bloody contest," line 125 — the latter a Franklin quote). Neither sanitized nor gratuitous; proportionate to a politics/diplomacy theme.

### 6. Quote provenance honesty — **FAIR (and a strength)**
The draft handles the mediated nature of Hendrick's oratory exactly as the fact pack requires: "Hendrick spoke in Mohawk; what survives is the speech as written down by interpreters and clerks... We have it as recorded, filtered through other men's pens, not as a recording." (line 53) This is an honesty move that *protects* against putting words in a Native leader's mouth — a framing virtue, not just a fact-check one. All Hendrick quotes are tagged "as recorded at Albany, 1754."

### 7. Minor observation (not a finding, no action required)
Line 72 attributes the deed to "the Mohawk signing it" — consistent with fact pack §4 ("an Iroquois (chiefly Mohawk) delegation"). This correctly avoids implicating the Confederacy as a whole or the absent Delaware/Shawnee in their own dispossession. No distortion.
