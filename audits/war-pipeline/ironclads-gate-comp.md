## Findings

### THIN (present but underweighted)

**T1 — The Confederate government's attempt to regulate cargo — mentioned but not completed.**
The draft correctly names the luxury-over-military problem and frames it as a "rot" in the operation. What it does NOT include: the Confederate government tried (and largely failed) to force runners to reserve cargo space for military supplies — first requiring one-third, then by March 1864 requiring half — and even those mandates were widely evaded. This matters for the story's completeness: the South was not passive in the face of the corruption. The fact that even legal mandates couldn't fix it underlines how bad the structural problem was. The draft presents the rot without the attempted cure or its failure. One sentence would close this. (Web research confirms: Confederate regulation of cargo was imposed in two steps, 1863 and March 1864, and was widely evaded. No new facts requiring web verification beyond what research already supports.)

**T2 — The European ironclad race was already running before Hampton Roads; the draft slightly overstates Hampton Roads as the originating event.**
The draft's final section correctly names HMS *Warrior* and *Gloire* and says Hampton Roads "poured fuel on an ironclad arms race already smoldering across the Atlantic." That phrasing is fine and honest. But the framing of March 8 ("every wooden warship on the planet was now junk") and the close of the March 9 section ("the whole world was watching… every navy on Earth now needed iron") implies Hampton Roads was the moment of revelation rather than confirmation. The European navies already knew they needed iron — *Warrior* was commissioned in August 1861 and *Gloire* in 1860, before Hampton Roads. Hampton Roads confirmed and accelerated what Europe already suspected; it did not invent the lesson. The draft's Beat 7 nuances this correctly, but the March 8 and March 9 prose overstates it. A single phrase ("confirmed what Europe already suspected") in one of those sections would lock the framing. This is a light corrective — not a missing beat, just a framing tightening.

**T3 — The Virginia's armament is never mentioned.**
The draft describes the *Virginia*'s armor, dimensions, ram, and casemate design in detail, but never tells the reader what guns she carried. Her ten guns (six 9-inch Dahlgren smoothbores, two 6.4-inch Brooke rifles, two 7-inch Brooke rifles on pivot) were what did the actual destruction on March 8. A reader who's never heard of this ship gets armor specs but no firepower picture. This is a light gap — one clause would cover it — but it's the weapon she used to kill two ships. The fact pack also omits her armament entirely (a pack blind spot).

## Fact Pack Blind Spots

**FP1 — Virginia's armament missing from the pack.** The pack details armor, dimensions, draft, and displacement but does not list the *Virginia*'s guns. Web research confirms she carried ten guns: six 9-inch Dahlgren smoothbores (three per side), two 7-inch Brooke pivot rifles (bow and stern), and two 6.4-inch Brooke rifles. The draft inherits this gap. If this fact is used, verify against NHHC or Mariners' Museum sources (both reliable on Virginia specs).

**FP2 — Confederate cargo regulation is absent from the pack.** The pack covers the luxury-over-military problem (Beat 3) but says nothing about the Confederate government's attempts to mandate military cargo fractions (1/3 in late 1863, raised to 1/2 in March 1864, widely evaded). This is a documented historical fact. If added to the draft, cross-check against the Wikipedia article "Blockade runners of the American Civil War" which covers the 1864 regulation clearly.

**FP3 — Mallory's broader four-point strategy is not in the pack.** The pack presents Mallory as a one-bet man (ironclads). In fact, his strategy had four prongs: commerce raiders to destroy Union merchant shipping, ironclads for coastal defense, ironclads for offensive high-seas use, and new weapons/naval technology. The commerce raiders (CSS *Alabama* and her sisters) were the other major Confederate naval weapon. The pack omits this context entirely, which slightly flattens Mallory's portrait. The draft inherits this omission. Note: commerce raiders are almost certainly a separate section — this is only a blind spot if the section is intended to frame Mallory's full strategic thinking, which its scope suggests it is not. Flag rather than require.

**FP4 — The numbers on blockade attempts need a light flag.** The pack cites "roughly five out of every six attempts succeeded" (sourced from Wikipedia, "Union blockade"). Web research found a slightly different framing: "historians estimated 2,500–2,800 total attempts, at least 80% success rate." These are consistent (80% = roughly 4-in-5, close enough to "5-in-6") but the pack's sourcing is not as tight as the casualty figures. The draft uses the pack's framing correctly and presents the debate honestly, so this is a watch-item, not a stop.

## MUST-ADD

**MA1 — Confederate cargo regulation: one sentence on the failed attempt to fix the luxury problem.**
The draft says private runners "increasingly filled their precious cargo space with luxuries instead of war materiel, which slowly compromised the whole point of the enterprise." This is correct but incomplete. The South tried to fix it — mandatory cargo fractions (one-third, then half by 1864) — and the mandates were still widely evaded. That detail closes the story: it wasn't just owners being greedy; the Confederate government tried to stop it and couldn't. One sentence in the "cat-and-mouse off Nassau" section. No new facts requiring web verification — web research confirms this (Wikipedia, "Blockade runners of the American Civil War").

**MA2 — Virginia's armament: one clause on her guns.**
The *Merrimack*/Virginia section names her armor, dimensions, ram, and draft but never mentions what weapons she carried. She had ten guns — six heavy Dahlgren smoothbores, four Brooke rifles — and it was those guns that burned the *Congress* and pounded the *Cumberland* before ramming her. Naming them (even just "ten heavy guns") gives the reader a complete weapon, not a floating bunker with no teeth. Verify gun details against NHHC or Mariners' Museum (not currently in the fact pack — a pack blind spot).

## SHOULD-CONSIDER

**SC1 — Note that Hampton Roads confirmed a European arms race already running.**
In the March 8 and/or March 9 sections, one phrase ("confirmed what Europe already suspected" or similar) would lock the framing against the slight overstatement that Hampton Roads originated the ironclad era rather than accelerating it. The Beat 7 section handles this correctly; just pull one phrase forward. Not required — the Beat 7 wording does the corrective work — but it would prevent the earlier prose from feeling contradictory to attentive readers.

**SC2 — Consider flagging Mallory's commerce-raider prong (CSS Alabama etc.) with a brief forward pointer.**
Mallory's section presents him as a one-weapon man (ironclads). He was also the architect of the Confederate commerce-raider campaign that destroyed hundreds of Union merchant ships. If that campaign has its own section, a one-line forward bridge from Mallory's introduction would sharpen both sections. If it doesn't have its own section, this is more than a should-consider. Check the section roster. (Not a blind spot in this section's scope — just a character-portrait enrichment.)

**SC3 — The draft frames the effective blockade debate entirely in terms of cotton volume. Consider adding one line on what the runners did succeed in delivering militarily.**
Web research confirms blockade runners delivered half a million rifles, hundreds of cannons, and thousands of tons of gunpowder to the Confederacy. That context slightly sharpens the debate: the blockade was genuinely porous in military-supply terms, not just in luxury goods. The current framing ("it didn't matter because cotton collapsed") is not wrong, but noting that the South did receive meaningful arms through runners makes the "chokehold, not wall" framing more precise — it was still squeezing even while letting some blood supply through. One clause.
