# FACT PACK — Vincennes (Clark's recapture of Fort Sackville), February 23–25, 1779

*American Revolution (the frontier / western theater). ONLY source for the battle page. The story's spine is the MARCH: ~170 men, ~180 miles in 18 days across flooded winter prairie, wading the drowned lands of the Wabash, then bluffing a fort into surrender. The hard surface is the tomahawk execution of 4–5 captured Native warriors in sight of the fort — it is in this pack, documented precisely, and the page does NOT sanitize it. Confidence tags: HIGH / MED / LOW / DISPUTED; HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags inline. Primary-source hierarchy for this battle (use it): (1) Maj. Joseph Bowman's journal — contemporaneous, terse, reliable; (2) Henry Hamilton's journal — contemporaneous, detailed, hostile witness (published 1951); (3) Clark's letter to George Mason, Nov 19, 1779 — months after, vivid, mildly self-serving; (4) Clark's Memoir, written 1789–91, a DECADE later, openly reputation-salvaging — every theatrical detail sourced only to the Memoir gets tagged as such.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **Siege of Fort Vincennes (Fort Sackville), February 23–25, 1779** — Vincennes, on the east bank of the Wabash River (present-day Vincennes, Indiana; the fort site is under/near the George Rogers Clark Memorial, 38.6792,-87.5356). [HIGH]
- **Result: American victory.** Lt. Gov. Henry Hamilton surrendered the fort and 79 men on the morning of Feb 25, 1779. [HIGH]
- **The premise [HIGH]:** In December 1778 the British lieutenant governor of Detroit, Henry Hamilton, had retaken Vincennes and planned to sweep Clark out of the Illinois Country in the spring. Clark, 26 years old, with no hope of holding Kaskaskia against a spring offensive, chose the one move Hamilton considered impossible: a midwinter march across ~180 miles (290 km) of flooded prairie to attack first. "Great things have been effected by a few men well conducted" (Clark to Gov. Patrick Henry, Feb 3, 1779 — see Quotes).
- **Signature elements:** (1) THE MARCH — 18 days, the last week through icy floodwater at times shoulder-deep, the final 2–3 days with almost no food; (2) THE BLUFF — Clark paraded his ~170 men behind rises with enough flags for an army several times the size, so the town and fort read his force as hundreds strong; (3) THE RIFLEMEN — frontier marksmen firing through the fort's ill-fitted gunports silenced Hamilton's cannon within hours; (4) THE EXECUTIONS — Clark had 4–5 captured Native warriors tomahawked in full view of the fort's gate, a calculated atrocity that helped break the garrison's will (Section 5c — mandatory surface). [HIGH on all four as events]
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — "the Hair-Buyer":** the American frontier called Hamilton "the Hair-Buyer General." Treat the epithet as the propaganda it partly was. What the record supports: Hamilton organized and supplied Native war parties against the frontier from 1777 on orders from London, and rewarded returning parties (which presented scalps as proof of success) with goods. What the record does NOT support: documentary evidence that he paid per-scalp bounties; he denied it, Jefferson's government eventually dropped the scalp-buying charge, and the standard scholarly treatment (Bernard Sheehan, "The Famous Hair Buyer General," *Indiana Magazine of History* 79, 1983) treats the epithet as Clark's propaganda theme. Meanwhile AMERICAN governments paid actual, written scalp bounties in the same war (Pennsylvania, Apr 22, 1780: $2,500 Continental per Native scalp, $3,000 per prisoner). Both framings belong on the page together. [HIGH on raids/rewards-in-goods; HIGH that no per-scalp payment evidence exists; HIGH on the Pennsylvania bounty]
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — "Clark won the Northwest":** the traditional claim (the campaign "won the Old Northwest for the United States" and delivered the 1783 boundary) is DISPUTED and on the wrong side of modern scholarship. Most historians now hold that Clark's successes were not a factor in the Paris negotiations; Lowell Harrison calls the conquest "little more than a temporary occupation of territory"; Randolph Downes (1940): "It would be more accurate to say that he assisted the French and Indian inhabitants of that region to remove themselves from the very shadowy political rule of the British." The page may say the campaign gave the US a real presence in the Illinois Country and an enduring national legend; it may NOT say it won the 1783 boundary. [HIGH as historiographical consensus]

## 2. BACKGROUND — the Illinois Country campaign, 1778
- **The frontier war [HIGH]:** From mid-1777, on orders from Lord Germain, Hamilton at Detroit recruited, supplied, and dispatched Native war parties (with British/Loyalist officers attached) against the Kentucky and Pennsylvania–Virginia frontiers. Hundreds of settlers, including women and children, were killed or carried off; Kentucky's stations were under near-siege. Hamilton himself wrote to Germain wishing the "storm" could fall on "the guilty heads of those wretches who have raised it, and pass by the miserable many" [MED-HIGH, quoted via the Hamilton literature]. Attaching officers to restrain raids did not change what raids were.
- **Clark's plan [HIGH]:** Clark, a 25-year-old Virginia surveyor-turned-Kentucky-militia leader, sold Gov. Patrick Henry on striking the source: the lightly held British posts of the Illinois Country. He received a public commission to defend Kentucky and SECRET instructions (Jan 2, 1778; the lieutenant-colonel's commission is dated by some accounts to Dec 1777) to seize Kaskaskia. [HIGH on the secret-orders mechanism; MED on the exact commission date — say "that winter"]
- **Down the Ohio [HIGH]:** Clark left Redstone (on the Monongahela) May 12, 1778 with ~150 recruits, based at Corn Island at the Falls of the Ohio (the founding nucleus of Louisville) from May 27, and shot the falls downriver on June 24, 1778 — by well-attested accounts under a solar eclipse the men read as an omen (an eclipse did occur June 24, 1778) [MED-HIGH on the eclipse anecdote; the eclipse itself is astronomically verified]. Total force ~175 after desertions.
- **Kaskaskia, July 4, 1778 — without a shot [HIGH]:** after a ~120-mile overland march from the Ohio, Clark's men crossed the Kaskaskia River at night and took the town and its British-appointed commandant Rocheblave without firing a shot. Cahokia followed days later (Bowman's detachment), equally bloodless. The lever was news Clark carried: France — the habitants' old mother country — had allied with the United States (treaty signed Feb 6, 1778). The French-speaking villagers swore the American oath.
- **Vincennes the first time — taken by a priest [HIGH]:** Father Pierre Gibault, the Kaskaskia priest, volunteered to win Vincennes over. In mid-July 1778 he and a small party (no soldiers) brought the town's habitants to the American oath; Capt. Leonard Helm later took command of the run-down Fort Sackville with a handful of men and the local French militia. Virginia, astonishingly, organized the whole region as "Illinois County, Virginia" that winter. [HIGH]
- **Hamilton strikes back — December 17, 1778 [HIGH]:** Hamilton left Detroit Oct 7, 1778 with a mixed force — a core of regulars of the King's 8th Regiment (~33), Detroit volunteer militia, Indian Department officers, and Native allies who joined en route; modern summaries give totals from ~230 up to ~500 by arrival (see KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES #4). He moved ~600 miles by the Maumee–Wabash route in 71 days. Helm's local militia melted away; on Dec 17 he surrendered the fort with only a few men, receiving the honors of war. The habitants of Vincennes swore BACK to the British crown. [HIGH on the arc; numbers MED]
- **Hamilton's fatal economy [HIGH]:** rather than push on against Kaskaskia in winter, Hamilton dismissed most of his militia and nearly all the Native warriors until spring and settled in to rebuild Fort Sackville with a garrison of roughly 90–100 (about 79–80 of whom would surrender). The spring plan: retake the Illinois towns and scour Kentucky.
- **Vigo [HIGH]:** Francis Vigo, a Sardinian-born fur trader of St. Louis (a Spanish subject sympathetic to the Americans), was detained by Hamilton's scouts at Vincennes, released under pressure from the habitants, and reached Kaskaskia on Jan 29, 1779 with exact intelligence: Hamilton's reduced winter garrison, his cannon, and his spring intentions. Clark's choice crystallized: "we must either quit the country or attack Mr. Hamilton" (Feb 3 letter — Quotes). [HIGH]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
### Strengths (dossier stats)
- **American: ~170 men** on the overland march — Virginia/Kentucky frontier riflemen plus French volunteers from Kaskaskia and Cahokia, who were nearly HALF the force [HIGH; one popular account says ~130 — use ~170, the Bowman-derived standard]. Plus the armed row-galley **Willing** (Capt. John Rogers, ~40 men, two 4-pounders, four swivels, a 9-pounder per one account), sent down the Mississippi/up the Ohio and Wabash Feb 4–6 — it arrived Feb 27, two days too late. [HIGH on the boat and lateness; armament detail MED]
- **British: ~79–96 in the fort** — 33 regulars of the 8th (King's) Regiment, ~36–40 Detroit militia/volunteers, plus Indian Department officers; 79 surrendered as prisoners. Hamilton counted only "35 or 36" of the garrison as reliably loyal — the French-Canadian militia would not fight their kin in town. [HIGH on 33 regulars + 79 prisoners; the rest MED ranges]
- **Duration:** firing from ~8 p.m. Feb 23 through Feb 24; terms agreed evening Feb 24; formal surrender ~10 a.m. Feb 25. [HIGH]

### American commander bios (60–90 w material, verified)
- **George Rogers Clark (1752–1818) [HIGH]:** Virginia-born surveyor and Kentucky militia leader, a lieutenant colonel at 26. Tall, red-haired, persuasive, and ruthless when he judged ruthlessness useful — the February march was his idea, his gamble, and his masterpiece of will (he reportedly waded in front, and had the youngest men sing to keep the column moving — march details partly Memoir-derived, tag). Vincennes made him a national legend, but Detroit, his real objective, never came; his later life collapsed into debt, drink, a stroke, and an amputated leg. He never wrote that he had "won the Northwest" — others wrote it for him.
- **Maj. Joseph Bowman (c. 1752–Aug 14, 1779) [HIGH]:** Frederick County, Virginia officer; Clark's second-in-command and the campaign's contemporaneous diarist. Led the company that took Cahokia bloodlessly in July 1778. His journal is the march's most trustworthy record ("No provisions yet. Lord help us!"). Horribly burned on Feb 25 when captured gunpowder cartridges exploded during the victory salute; he died that August — the campaign's highest-ranking American fatality, killed by celebration, not combat.
- **Capt. Leonard Helm (c. 1720s–1782) [MED-HIGH]:** Virginia frontiersman who held Vincennes for Clark in fall 1778 with a token force, surrendered it to Hamilton on Dec 17 when his militia evaporated, and spent the siege as a prisoner INSIDE Fort Sackville — then helped talk Hamilton into surrendering. Days later he led the boat party upriver that captured the British supply convoy from Detroit. (His famous cannon-and-whiskey surrender scene is tradition — see APOCRYPHA.)
- **Father Pierre Gibault (1737–1802) [HIGH]:** the Kaskaskia priest who swung the habitants of Vincennes to the American oath in July 1778 without a soldier present, and blessed Clark's column out of Kaskaskia on Feb 5. The British marked him for it.
- **Francis Vigo (1747–1836) [HIGH]:** Sardinian-born St. Louis fur trader and Spanish subject whose January intelligence made the march possible (and who helped finance Clark's operation). The debt Virginia owed him was paid to his heirs in 1876, forty years after he died poor.

### British commander bios
- **Lt. Gov. Henry Hamilton (c. 1734–1796) [HIGH]:** Irish-born career officer; lieutenant governor and Indian agent at Detroit from 1775. A capable administrator and amateur artist (his portrait sketches of Great Lakes Native leaders, now at Harvard's Houghton Library, are the earliest such collection), he executed London's frontier-raid policy and so became, to Kentucky, "the Hair-Buyer." His December 1778 counterstroke was bold and well run; his February choices (wintering with a reduced garrison, trusting the habitants' re-sworn oath) handed Clark the opening. His journal of the campaign is candid, observant, and unsparing about Clark's executions — and about his own side's collapse.
- **Maj. Jehu Hay (d. 1785) [MED-HIGH]:** Detroit Indian Department officer, Hamilton's deputy and the man American frontier opinion most associated (with Hamilton) with directing raids. Helped persuade Hamilton to accept terms on Feb 24. Sent to Virginia in irons with Hamilton; exchanged 1781; later appointed lieutenant governor of Detroit, where he died.
- **Capt. Guillaume LaMothe (d. ?) [MED]:** Detroit volunteer/Indian Department captain. Outside the fort with a scouting party when Clark arrived; on the night of Feb 23–24 he and his men slipped through the American lines and scaled the stockade to rejoin the garrison (two were captured). Put in irons after the surrender; paroled Oct 1779.

## 4. THE MARCH (Feb 5–23, 1779) — the spine
*Chronology per Bowman's journal + Clark's 1779 Mason letter; Memoir-only color flagged.*
- **Feb 5 [HIGH]:** Clark's ~170 leave Kaskaskia in the afternoon, after Mass and Gibault's blessing. Weather: unseasonably wet winter; the prairies between the Kaskaskia and the Wabash are sheets of rain-flood. Clark (to Mason): "a great part of the plains under water several inches deep." First week: passable going, game plentiful, the French companies in good humor. [HIGH]
- **Feb 13 [HIGH]:** the **Little Wabash** — normally two channels three miles apart, now ONE flooded crossing ~5 miles wide. They build a pirogue, ferry baggage, and wade; ~2 days lost.
- **Feb 17 [HIGH]:** the **Embarras River**, 9 miles from Vincennes — too deep to ford, banks drowned. They cannot reach dry ground on the Wabash side; camp wet, food running out.
- **Feb 20–22 — the hunger days [HIGH]:** provisions effectively gone. Bowman, Feb 20: the men "very quiet but hungry; some almost in despair"; Feb 22: "No provisions yet. Lord help us!" A captured Vincennes hunting party (5 men, by canoe) gives intelligence — the town does not suspect; the fort's garrison numbers confirmed — and a quarter of a buffalo from another captured canoe yields a thin broth served first to the weakest. Crossing the Wabash by ferried canoe-loads Feb 21–23, then wading the drowned bottomlands beyond, water at times to the chest and shoulders, ice at the edges. Men too weak to walk are carried in the canoes; the strong haul the weak. [HIGH on all of it except the broth-to-the-weakest detail, MED, Memoir-flavored]
- **Feb 23 [HIGH]:** the column reaches **Warriors Island** (a timber rise above the flood ~2 miles from town) and SEES Vincennes. Clark sends the captured duck hunter ahead with an open letter to the inhabitants: friends of the United States, stay in your houses; anyone in the fort or under arms will be treated as an enemy. The letter is read in the public square. Nobody warns the fort. [HIGH; letter text survives in close paraphrase — author may paraphrase, not invent verbatim]
- **Memoir-only color [MED, tag in prose as Clark's later telling]:** Clark blackening his face with wet powder and raising the war whoop to get the column into the last flood; the drummer boy floating across on his drum; "land the tallest men first." None of this is in Bowman or the 1779 letter. Usable ONLY as "Clark later told it…".
- **Why nobody watched the east [HIGH as interpretation]:** Hamilton knew the floods; he judged an approach across the drowned lands impossible in February. The march's whole value was that it could not be believed.

## 5. THE SIEGE (evening Feb 23 – morning Feb 25)
### 5a. The bluff and the first night [HIGH]
- At dusk Feb 23 Clark marched his ~170 toward town in full view at maximum theatrical scale: companies counter-marching behind a low rise so only their flags showed — the column carried far more colors than a force its size rated (NPS: "flags sufficient for an army of 500"; Clark's Memoir implies ~1,000 — keep the number soft: "enough flags for several times their numbers"). The town read an army; Hamilton's hope of habitant warning or militia rally died before a shot. [HIGH that the deception worked; the specific numbers are Memoir/NPS-derived — MED]
- ~8 p.m.: the Americans occupy the town. Habitants welcome them — and dig up gunpowder they had hidden from the British, replacing Clark's rain-soaked stock. Tobacco's Son, the Piankeshaw chief whose town adjoined Vincennes, offers ~100 warriors; Clark declines (politely — he wants no confusion in the dark, and the optics of the fight are part of the point). [HIGH on the powder and the offer; the "100" figure and Clark's reasoning MED, Memoir-derived]
- Firing on Fort Sackville begins ~8 p.m. Hamilton's journal: he took the first shots for "some drunken frolic of the inhabitants, but going upon the parade heard the balls sing." A sergeant (Chapman) is saved by a metal button over "the pit of the Stomach." [HIGH — Hamilton's journal]
- **The riflemen [HIGH]:** ~14 picked riflemen under Capt. Bayley, with the rest firing in relays from houses, fences, and a natural bank, pour fire through the fort's gunports — the stockade was unfinished and "so ill set up" that the ports barely shielded the crews. Hamilton's gunners are hit at the guns (several of his seven wounded regulars fell there); his 3- and 6-pounders fire high and accomplish nothing against covered riflemen. By morning the fort's artillery is effectively silenced. American loss in the entire siege: none killed, one man slightly wounded. [HIGH]
- During the night LaMothe's scouting party, stranded outside, slips through and scales the stockade (two captured). Clark lets it happen rather than alarm the prize. [HIGH on the event; Clark's intent MED]
### 5b. Demand and defiance (mid-morning Feb 24) [HIGH]
- Clark to Hamilton, under flag: "I expect you shall immediately surrender yourself with your Garrison prisoners at discretion… you may expect no mercy, for by Heavens you shall be treated as a murtherer." Hamilton's reply: he and his garrison were "not… to be prevailed on by threats to act in a manner unbecoming the character of British Subjects" (wording per the IHB journal text; a variant form circulates — see Quotes #4). Firing resumes.
- Inside, Hamilton assembles the garrison: the regulars stand firm; the Detroit French militia "hung their heads," their sergeants muttering "it was hard they should fight against their own Friends and relations" in the town. Hamilton privately resolves to get honorable terms if he can. [HIGH — Hamilton's journal]
- Bowman's journal records the Americans beginning to tunnel from the riverbank toward the powder magazine — threat as engineering. [MED — attested via NPS/secondary accounts of the journal; keep brief]
### 5c. THE EXECUTIONS — the hard surface (Feb 24, ~midday) [HIGH; DO NOT SANITIZE]
- A British-allied war party — ~15–16 men, mostly Odawa (Ottawa) and other Lakes warriors with a few French partisans, returning to Vincennes from a raid toward the Falls of the Ohio, reportedly carrying scalps — walked into the town unaware it had changed hands. Clark's men (Capt. John Williams's company prominent) ambushed them: two killed on the spot, three wounded, six captured (Bowman: "killed two on the spot, wounded three, took six prisoners"). [HIGH]
- Of the captives, a French partisan was released to the townspeople's custody, and one young warrior was spared when the French volunteer lieutenant **St. Croix recognized his own son** under the war paint and begged his life. (Hamilton later wrote the spared youth was a son of Pontiac — false; see APOCRYPHA.) [HIGH on the spared-son episode as the standard account; MED on details]
- **The remaining four (some accounts five — see KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES #1) were taken to the main street before the fort's gate, in full view of the garrison, made to sit or kneel in a circle, and tomahawked to death; the bodies were thrown into the Wabash.** Bowman, flatly: "brought the Indians to the main street before the Fort gate, there tomahawked them, and throw them into the river." [HIGH]
- Hamilton's journal, the fullest account: the victims "began to sing their Death song, and were butcherd in succession"; one, the young Odawa war chief **Macutté Mong**, "having received the fatal stroke of a Tomahawk in the head, took it out and gave it again into the hands of his executioner who repeated the Stroke a second and third time, after which the miserable being, not entirely deprived of life was dragged to the river, and thrown in with the rope about his neck where he ended his life and tortures." Hamilton adds, acidly: "The Blood of the victims was still visible for days afterwards, a testimony of the courage and Humanity of Colonel Clarke." [HIGH as Hamilton's testimony — label it his]
- **Why Clark did it, in his own words [HIGH, his later account]:** "I had now a fair opportunity of making an impression on the Indians… that of convincing them that Governor Hamilton could not give them that protection that he had made them to believe he could," and so "Ordered the Prisoners to be Tomahawked in the face of the Garrison." It was aimed at two audiences at once: Native nations weighing British protection, and the garrison watching from the walls. It worked on both.
- **DISPUTED — did Clark kill any of them himself?** Hamilton wrote that one prisoner "was tomahawked either by Clarke or one of his Officers" and that Clark came to the parley "all bloody and sweating… while he washed his hands and face still reeking from the human sacrifice in which he had acted as chief priest, he told me with great exultation how he had been employed." Lt. Jacob Schieffelin (British Indian Department, eyewitness-adjacent) stated flatly that Clark wielded the tomahawk. Clark and Bowman gave no such details. Both accusers were enemies with every motive to blacken Clark; the silence on the American side proves nothing either way. The page must present it exactly this way: hostile eyewitnesses say Clark's own hand; American records are silent; historians have not resolved it. [DISPUTED — this is the honest state of the evidence]
- **Also documented from the same hours [HIGH via Hamilton's journal, HANDLE-WITH-CARE]:** the captured partisan François Maisonville was set in a chair and an American soldier ordered to scalp him — Hamilton thought it "probably was meant only in terrorism"; the man was partially cut before one of Clark's relatives intervened and he was released. If the page includes it, include the outcome.
### 5d. Surrender (Feb 24 afternoon – Feb 25 morning) [HIGH]
- Hamilton proposed a three-day truce; Clark refused and the two met (accounts differ: the church of St. Francis Xavier per Clark/Bowman; the parade before the gate per Hamilton). Clark, still washing off blood by Hamilton's account, again threatened that if he had to storm the fort "not a single man would be spared," and made his view of "Indian partisans" plain. Hamilton at first refused surrender at discretion — he and his officers would "prefer to die" — but his garrison arithmetic was hopeless: seven regulars wounded, guns unusable, militia he privately called "treacherous cowards," no relief possible. Helm (the prisoner) and Maj. Hay with Bowman shuttled the compromise. [HIGH; the "treacherous cowards" phrase is Hamilton's, HIGH]
- **Terms [HIGH]:** the garrison to "deliver themselves up Prisoners of War and March out with their Arms and Acoutriments"; officers keep personal baggage. At ~10 a.m. Feb 25 Hamilton's 79 marched out and grounded arms; the Stars and Stripes went up over the renamed **Fort Patrick Henry**. After the surrender Clark put four men associated with directing Native raids — Dejean-circle figures Chabert, Réaume, LaMothe, Maisonville — in irons; Hamilton protested it as a breach of the capitulation and offered himself for the irons instead. [HIGH — Hamilton's journal]
- **The salute that killed Bowman [HIGH]:** during the 13-gun victory salute on Feb 25, ~26 captured 6-pounder cartridges exploded in the battery, terribly burning Maj. Bowman, Capt. Worthington, and four privates. Bowman died of the burns Aug 14, 1779. Clark's reports omit the accident.
- **Mop-up [HIGH on capture, MED on figures]:** the Willing arrived Feb 27. Helm led ~50 men in boats upriver and captured the British supply convoy descending the Wabash from Detroit — seven boats, ~40 prisoners (including Detroit's notorious magistrate Philip Dejean), and goods worth a small fortune.

## 6. NATIVE NATIONS & THE FRENCH HABITANTS — actors, not props
- **Hamilton's December coalition [HIGH on composition, MED on numbers]:** his expedition's Native contingent (rising from ~60 at Detroit toward several hundred by Vincennes) drew on the Lakes nations long tied to the British alliance system — Odawa (led by the war chief **Egushawa**, Pontiac's political heir, who advised Hamilton and ran his scouts), Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Wyandot, and Miami, with Wabash-valley recruits gathered en route. These nations were not mercenaries; they were defending a world in which the British crown — via the Proclamation Line and the Detroit supply system — looked like the only brake on American settlement of their lands. [HIGH as framing]
- **The Wabash nations' recalculation [HIGH]:** Vincennes sat in Piankeshaw country (the Miami-related Wea, Kickapoo, and Piankeshaw held the Wabash). Their stake was sovereignty between empires. **Tobacco's Son** ("Young Tobacco"), the Piankeshaw chief at Vincennes, had already sided with Clark in 1778 — by Clark's account reasoning that "the Big Knife was in the right" and promising to "bloody the land no more for the English" [MED — the speech survives via Clark's telling]; he offered warriors for the assault itself (declined). After the fort fell, Wabash towns up to Ouiatenon and the Kickapoo made or kept peace with the Americans. The captured war party that Clark butchered was from the LAKES nations — Odawa men far from home — not from the Wabash towns watching from next door; the executions were staged precisely to teach the watching nations that British protection was worthless. [HIGH on the geography of who was killed; the staging-logic is Clark's own stated rationale]
- **What the fall actually meant for Native nations [HIGH as scholarly framing]:** less than the legend says. Hamilton's capture removed one organizer and dried up one supply point; Detroit, the real engine, never fell — Clark's Detroit expedition "never materialized." Egushawa escaped Vincennes with his war band intact and fought on (into the 1790s). The frontier war got WORSE after 1779: Bird's invasion of Kentucky (1780), La Balme's destruction (1780), Blue Licks (1782), Gnadenhutten (1782) all lie ahead. And American victory carried, for the Wabash and Lakes nations alike, the longer-run catastrophe of unrestrained settlement. Joshua Shepherd's summary is the safe register: the Indian threat to Kentucky was "blunted… but by no means eliminated." [HIGH]
- **The habitants of Vincennes — the twice-switched town [HIGH]:** French villagers who had sworn to Britain (under the Quebec settlement), then to Virginia (July 1778, via Gibault), then back to the crown (December, under Hamilton's guns), then welcomed Clark and dug up hidden gunpowder for him (February). Read it as survival politics, not fickleness: a Catholic, French-speaking community trading oaths to keep its town intact — and decisive twice, once when Gibault delivered the town without a shot, and again when the Detroit militia inside the fort refused to die fighting their "Friends and relations." [HIGH]

## 7. CASUALTIES (tiny numbers — get them right)
- **The siege proper [HIGH]:** American: 0 killed, 1 lightly wounded. British: 0–1 killed in the firing (accounts differ on whether any garrison death occurred at the walls; Hamilton records sergeants and gunners wounded), ~5–7 wounded (six/seven regulars), **79 captured**.
- **The ambushed war party + executions [HIGH]:** 2 warriors killed in the ambush, 3 wounded, 6 captured, of whom **4 (some accounts 5) were executed**; 1 youth and 1 French partisan spared.
- **The accident [HIGH]:** 6 Americans burned by the exploding cartridges on Feb 25; Maj. Joseph Bowman died of his burns Aug 14, 1779 — the campaign's only American officer death.
- **Wikipedia's infobox** ("American: 4; British: 11 killed, 5 wounded, 79 captured") bundles these streams; do NOT copy it raw. Use the three-stream breakdown above. [HIGH on the warning]

## 8. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **Hamilton in Virginia [HIGH]:** Clark sent Hamilton, Hay, LaMothe, and ~24 others under escort ~1,200 miles to Williamsburg (arrived mid-June 1779). Gov. Thomas Jefferson and the Virginia Council, treating Hamilton as a war criminal ("the Hair-Buyer") rather than a prisoner of war, had him jailed in irons. He refused a parole whose terms gagged him. Washington (and British threats of retaliation against American prisoners) pressed Virginia to relent; the scalp-buying charge was eventually dropped; Hamilton was paroled to New York in October 1780 and exchanged in March 1781. He ended his career a colonial governor (Quebec, Bermuda, Dominica), dying in office in 1796. The captivity is itself a framing surface: Virginia justified extraordinary harshness with an accusation it could not document. [HIGH]
- **What followed in the West [HIGH]:** Clark was promoted and lionized, but Virginia could never resource the Detroit expedition; "my chain appears to have run out," he wrote. The war in the West intensified through 1782 (see Sec. 6). At the 1783 Treaty of Paris, Britain ceded the territory northwest of the Ohio — for reasons negotiated in Paris (Shelburne's strategy, fisheries-and-boundaries horse-trading), which most historians agree did not turn on Clark's 1779 conquest. The campaign DID give the infant US administration-in-fact at Kaskaskia, Cahokia, and Vincennes, a legal fig leaf (Illinois County, Virginia), and the founding of Louisville — and it built the legend that justified the Northwest Ordinance generation's self-image. State the real and the claimed separately. [HIGH]
- **Clark's fall [HIGH]:** the hero of Vincennes at 26 was, by his 50s, a debt-ruined, hard-drinking, half-forgotten man; stroke, leg amputation (1809), death 1818. His Memoir (1789–91) — the source of the march's most cinematic details — was written partly to salvage that reputation. The page should let the arc land without moralizing.
- **The Memorial [HIGH]:** the George Rogers Clark Memorial in Vincennes — a 16-column granite-and-marble classical rotunda by architect Frederic Charles Hirons — was built from 1931 on the presumed fort site and dedicated June 14, 1936 by President Franklin D. Roosevelt; inside, a bronze Clark by Hermon Atkins MacNeil under seven Ezra Winter murals (c. 1933) of the campaign. National Historical Park since July 23, 1966. The fort's exact footprint has never been archaeologically fixed; it lies "undoubtedly within the park's boundaries." Note what the 1936 building is: high-imperial commemoration of the conquest narrative this pack flags as contested — usable as a closing "what we made of it" beat.

## 12. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)
- **Date:** February 23–25, 1779 · **Where:** Vincennes, on the Wabash River (present-day Indiana)
- **Forces:** ~170 American (Virginia/Kentucky riflemen + French volunteers, nearly half the force) vs ~79–96 British (33 regulars of the 8th Foot + Detroit militia and Indian Department officers; 79 surrendered)
- **Duration:** ~38 hours of siege after an 18-day, ~180-mile (290 km) march through winter floods
- **Casualties:** American 0 killed, 1 wounded in the siege (6 burned in the post-surrender accident; Maj. Bowman later died); British ~5–7 wounded, 79 captured; plus the ambushed war party — 2 killed, 6 captured, 4 (or 5) prisoners executed
- **Winner-line material:** American victory — the war's longest-odds march delivered the British governor of the western frontier into Virginian irons without the loss of a single attacker in the fighting. Keep the dark ledger visible in the same breath (the executed prisoners were how the bluff got its teeth), and keep the "won the Northwest" claim at its honest level: it made the legend; the negotiators in Paris made the boundary.

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE (summary register)
1. **Who swung the tomahawk:** Hamilton ("either by Clarke or one of his Officers"; Clark bloody at the parley) and Schieffelin (Clark personally) vs. American silence. Present as unresolved, with the witnesses' hostility named. [DISPUTED]
2. **4 vs 5 executed:** detailed accounts (Bowman, Hamilton, modern reconstructions) support FOUR executed from six captured (one youth + one Frenchman spared); NPS and the Clark Wikipedia article say five. Write "four — five by some counts." [DISPUTED, minor]
3. **"Hair-Buyer":** raids organized and rewarded, yes; per-scalp payments, no documentary evidence; charge dropped by Virginia itself; American governments paid literal scalp bounties in the same war. The epithet is part of the story AS propaganda. [HIGH on each component]
4. **Hamilton's December force:** ranges across sources (33 regulars constant; militia 85–160; Native allies 60→350, growing en route; ~500 total by some counts). Use "a mixed force that grew to several hundred." [MED]
5. **"Won the Northwest for the United States":** traditional claim, contradicted by most modern scholarship on the 1783 negotiations. Never assert; always frame. [HIGH]
6. **Memoir-only theatrics** (face-blackening, war whoop, drummer boy on his drum, "flags for 1,000," the broth to the weakest): usable only as "as Clark later told it." The 18-day flood march itself needs no inflation — Bowman's journal carries it. [MED, provenance-tagged]
7. **Helm's December cannon-and-whiskey surrender scene:** beloved tradition, no contemporary source; his honors-of-war surrender with ~a handful of effectives is the verified core. [Tradition — do not state as fact]
8. **The spared youth:** St. Croix's son, spared at his father's plea = standard account; Hamilton's claim he was a son of Pontiac = false. [HIGH/false respectively]
9. **Casualty bundling:** never copy aggregate infobox numbers; use the three-stream breakdown (siege / war party + executions / accident). [HIGH]
10. **Hamilton's captivity:** the irons and criminal treatment are fact; the justifying accusation (scalp-buying) is the same unproven epithet — the page should let that irony stand without editorializing. [HIGH]

- **PC1 — Vigo repayment year and arithmetic (overrides §2 and §3 "1876, forty years").** The debt
  Virginia owed Francis Vigo was NOT paid until **1875**, and the payment went to his **estate**, not
  "his heirs." Vigo died March 22, **1836**, so 1875 is **nearly forty years (thirty-nine years)**
  later, not "forty years," and not 1876. The pack's "1876, forty years after he died" is wrong on
  both the year and the span. Ship: "paid to his estate... in 1875, nearly forty years after he died
  poor." [HIGH — Wikipedia, Francis Vigo; fact critic spot-check #3]

- **PC2 — Philip Dejean's title (overrides §5d "magistrate").** Dejean was a **British-appointed
  judge**, not a "magistrate." The standard secondary description is "a British appointed judge,
  Philippe DeJean." Ship: "Detroit's notorious British-appointed judge Philip Dejean." [HIGH —
  Wikipedia, Siege of Fort Sackville / Illinois campaign; fact critic spot-check #2]

- **PC3 — the salute accident mechanism (overrides §5d / §7 "~26 captured 6-pounder cartridges
  exploded").** The "**26 cartridges**" figure is unsupported and is NOT to be shipped. What is
  supported: during the celebratory victory salute on February 25, a **captured British 6-pounder
  cannon touched off the loose gunpowder charges** at the battery, **wounding six men including Maj.
  Joseph Bowman**, who **died of the burns five months later** (August 14, 1779). Ship the mechanism
  and the six-wounded / Bowman-dies-five-months-later facts; do NOT ship any cartridge count or imply
  the cartridges themselves were the primary charge. [HIGH on mechanism + casualties — follow-up
  web-verification pass per the reconcile brief; the "~26" caliber/count was also flagged
  un-confirmable by fact critic SF3]

- **PC4 — where Philip Dejean was captured (clarifies §5d; the pack lists him among the convoy
  prisoners but the FINAL must not imply a fort-surrender capture).** Dejean was captured
  **separately, on the Wabash, with the supply convoy coming down from Detroit**, by **Capt. Leonard
  Helm's boat party** — NOT at Fort Sackville's surrender on February 25. The prose places his capture
  with the convoy stream accordingly. [HIGH — follow-up web-verification pass per the reconcile brief]
