## Checklist Evaluation

### 1. The week-long retreat to the James
**COVERED — well.**  
The draft opens with the full-week context: McClellan backing away every day, Gaines's Mill decision, Glendale near-miss, and the southward march across the Peninsula toward the gunboats. The retreat's cause (saving a slaveholding republic / Richmond) is named explicitly. The Seven Days scope (June 25–July 1, six battles) is laid out before the hill is introduced. No gap.

---

### 2. The commanding plateau + Hunt's massed artillery
**COVERED — well.**  
The draft describes the plateau's height (~100 ft), its true strength being fields of fire rather than elevation, the open bare north face, the tiered batteries, the 32-pdr howitzers on the right flank, the Artillery Reserve in close support behind, and the definitional ambiguity around gun counts (~250 total). All core geography (boggy creeks, ravine flanks, James to the south) is present.

**Minor thin spot:** The draft does not name the Crew house or West house/farm as the two anchor points of the Union artillery line, nor does it name the U-shaped infantry formation Porter built around the guns. These are supporting detail-level, not mandatory for a selective telling — but they are the "named landmarks" that make the position concrete on a map. Flag as SHOULD-CONSIDER, not MUST-ADD.

---

### 3. The gunboats on the James
**COVERED — adequately.**  
The USS Galena is named. Jacob Bell and Aroostook are named in the fact ledger but not in the prose (only "three Union gunboats — including the USS Galena"). The draft correctly notes their limited accuracy at range. No mandatory gap; SHOULD-CONSIDER adding Jacob Bell for concreteness if space allows.

---

### 4. Lee's faulty plan + garbled orders + Magruder's delay
**COVERED — but THIN on the mechanism of how the assault actually triggered.**

What's there: the two-part plan (suppress artillery, then infantry), the orders designating Armistead's yell as the signal for fifteen brigades, the map mislabeling Willis Church Rd as "Quaker Road," Magruder's hours-long delay.

**What's THIN/MISSING — the actual trigger cascade:**  
The draft says the assault "did not come as one great coordinated wave" because of the yell-signal flaw, but it does not explain *how* the shooting actually started: Armistead made a limited local advance against skirmishers (~3:30 PM), which was *not* the intended signal; D.H. Hill's men, who had been building bivouac shelters assuming no battle would happen that day, heard the yelling from Magruder's sector around 5:30 PM and mistook *that* for the general advance signal. The cascade of misunderstanding — not one planned assault at all, but a chain of individual commanders each thinking someone else had already started the ball — is the real story of why the attack was piecemeal.

The draft renders this as "the assault did not come as one great wave" without capturing the accidental/cascading nature: nobody meant to trigger the battle when it triggered. That's a meaningful narrative gap. **MUST-ADD (brief — 2–3 sentences in "it-was-murder" section).**

---

### 5. The signal-gun attack order (Armistead's yell)
**THIN — see item 4 above.**  
The draft names the yell-as-signal as "hopeless" but describes it as a design flaw without showing the actual consequence: the attack essentially started by accident, and then each subsequent brigade joined because they heard the brigade ahead of them and assumed the signal had sounded. The "signal-gun" framing in the checklist may refer to this same yell-signal mechanism. **Covered as a concept, thin on the cascade. Same MUST-ADD as item 4.**

### 6. The piecemeal uphill assaults slaughtered by artillery
**COVERED — well.**  
~20 brigades at different times, canister and shell, charges stalled well short of the crest, some divisions losing 20–25%, individual brigades ~40%, D.H. Hill's five-brigade charge (~8,200 men), no Confederate soldier reached the crest. The draft covers this extensively in the "it-was-murder" section.

**COVERED — well.**  
Quoted directly, attributed to his *Battles and Leaders* account written 20+ years later, presented in context. The morning warning ("better let him alone") is also present as paraphrase. No gap.

### 8. The repulse
**COVERED — well.**  
Every charge broken, field belonging to the North by dark, dead and wounded covering the slope. Clear.

### 9. Union tactical victory
**COVERED — well.**  
Casualty asymmetry (~3,000 US vs. ~5,300–5,650 CS), one-sided character of the result, "the kind of defensive win generals study for a century" framing. Clear.

### 10. McClellan absent + ordering continued retreat over subordinates' protests
**COVERED — adequately, but THIN on one specific detail.**

What's there: McClellan left before the main fighting, boarded USS Galena, inspected Harrison's Landing, Porter in de facto command, subordinates objected and McClellan overruled, army marched at ~11 PM in heavy rain, 1864 campaign mockery.

**Thin spot:** The draft does not name *which* subordinates protested. The fact pack correctly flags this as unverified ("specific subordinates not consistently identified") and keeps them generic — that is the right call given the verification state. No MUST-ADD on names.

**What IS thin:** The draft does not capture that McClellan actually *returned briefly* to the field at Porter's urging during the battle (per Wikipedia / fact pack §10), remaining on the right away from main action. This nuance matters because it complicates the simple "sat on a gunboat" narrative without undermining it. However, since the pack flags McClellan's exact arrival/return timing as unverified, keeping it generic is defensible. **SHOULD-CONSIDER adding a brief note that he briefly returned but did not direct the battle, if the timing is verified before print.** Not a MUST-ADD.

### 11. The Seven Days' end + Richmond saved as a strategic Confederate triumph
**COVERED — well.**  
The closing section explicitly frames the small-story/big-story split: tactical Union win, strategic Confederate triumph; ~23 months before Union forces approached Richmond again; Peninsula Campaign effectively over; Lee's army never left. The "we won the battle, lost the campaign" formulation is crisp and well-developed.

---

### 12. The artillery lesson
**THIN — MUST-ADD.**

The draft's "Richmond Saved" section calls Malvern Hill a "textbook demonstration" (in the fact ledger commentary) but the prose itself never actually states that lesson. The "richmond-saved" section closes on strategic outcomes, Lee's quote, and the Richmond Dispatch — it does not tell the reader what the battle *taught* the armies about artillery doctrine.

**What's missing from the prose:**
- Hunt's Malvern Hill deployment became the template for Union massed-battery doctrine (rotating fresh batteries in as others exhausted ammunition; converging fire across open ground; the Artillery Reserve as a flexible pool rather than parceled-out brigade assets).
- The Confederate failure exposed the weakness of their system of parceling batteries with individual brigades — only 6–8 of ~45 Confederate guns engaged simultaneously. This led directly to Lee's post-campaign reorganization: Confederate artillery was restructured into battalion-sized units rather than brigade-attached batteries, giving it massing capability going forward.
- These are the reason Malvern Hill appears in artillery textbooks. The draft gestures at a "wall of iron" but never closes the loop on what the battle *changed*.

**MUST-ADD: 2–4 sentences in "richmond-saved" section covering (a) Hunt's deployment as a template for Union artillery doctrine, and (b) Confederate brigade-artillery failure → battalion reorganization. This is a checklist item and a signature consequence of the battle.**

---

### 13. Lee's own command failure and absence from the battlefield
**THIN — SHOULD-CONSIDER.**

The draft says Lee believed one more blow could destroy McClellan, and covers his faulty planning. But it does not note that Lee himself was *also absent from the main battlefield* during much of the fighting — he was off searching for a non-existent route to flank McClellan and cut him off from the James, which is why the piecemeal assault proceeded without anyone at the top checking it. The symmetry with McClellan (both commanders absent, different reasons) is a potentially interesting resonance.

The fact pack notes this (§4, §6, §10) but the draft does not use it. Given the draft's tight focus on the assault's failure, incorporating this briefly would sharpen Lee's culpability. **SHOULD-CONSIDER, not MUST-ADD** — it would strengthen the "faulty plan" section without requiring much space.

The draft gestures at this ("bled-white army") but never quantifies the week. **SHOULD-CONSIDER adding the Seven Days total in one sentence in "richmond-saved."** Not a MUST-ADD since the draft's framing is still coherent without it.

## MUST-ADD Items (2 items / 1 closely linked pair)

### MUST-ADD A: The accidental trigger cascade — how the battle actually started
**Location:** "it-was-murder" section, paragraph on the flawed orders.  
**What to add (2–3 sentences):** The draft says the yell-signal was "hopeless" but stops there. Add: Armistead made a limited local push against Union skirmishers around 3:30 PM — not the intended signal, just a local tactical move. Magruder's brigades, arriving late, launched their main charge around 5:30 PM. D.H. Hill's men, who had been building bivouac shelters assuming no battle would happen, heard the commotion from Magruder's sector and took *it* as the yell-signal — and charged. The assault did not cascade from a plan; it cascaded from a misunderstanding on top of a misunderstanding. That is the real story of why it was piecemeal, and it makes the "hopeless" yell-signal more vivid and specific.

### MUST-ADD B: The artillery lesson — what the battle changed
**Location:** "richmond-saved" section, closing paragraph or dedicated closing beat.  
**What to add (3–4 sentences):** Hunt's deployment — rotating fresh batteries forward as others exhausted their rounds, converging fire across open ground, the Artillery Reserve as a mobile pool rather than guns tied to individual brigades — became the template for Union massed-battery doctrine. The Confederate failure exposed the opposite problem: their practice of parceling batteries out to individual brigades meant only 6–8 of roughly 45 Confederate guns engaged simultaneously at any point. Lee reorganized: after the Seven Days, Confederate artillery was restructured into battalion-sized units that could mass fire. Malvern Hill is in the textbooks not just as a slaughter but as the lesson that changed how both armies thought about cannon.

It belongs in MUST-ADD B above and should be verified against the Essential Civil War Curriculum / Warfare History Network sources before insertion.

Not relevant to the draft but is a flag for the fact-checker: if O'Reilly's book is now published (title: *Retreat from Victory: The Battle of Malvern Hill and the End of the Seven Days, July 1, 1862*), it is the best single-source check for the contested facts (McClellan's exact movements, subordinate protests, etc.).

## Verdict

**2 MUST-ADDs.** The draft is solid on the core story — the slaughter, the repulse, the paradox of winning the battle and losing the campaign. Its two gaps are (A) the accidental cascade that turned a bad plan into an even worse execution — the specific mechanism of how the piecemeal attacks actually triggered — and (B) the artillery doctrine lesson, which is a named checklist item and the battle's most durable historical consequence. Both gaps are concentrated and fixable with 5–7 sentences total, one passage in "it-was-murder" and one in "richmond-saved." The SHOULD-CONSIDERs are genuine improvements but none of them change the story.
