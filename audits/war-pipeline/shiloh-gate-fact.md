## SHOULD-FIX

### SF-1 ⚠️ Ruggles as "artillery officer" — rank imprecision
**Draft says:** "a Confederate artillery officer, Brig. Gen. Daniel Ruggles (South)"
**Verification:** Ruggles was a division commander in Bragg's corps, not specifically an "artillery officer" by specialty. He happened to organize the artillery concentration but his role was infantry/division command.
**Fix:** "Confederate division commander Brig. Gen. Daniel Ruggles (South)" — or simply "Brig. Gen. Daniel Ruggles (South), a Confederate division commander"

### SF-2 ⚠️ "62 cannons" vs. "over 50 pieces" — acknowledge the uncertainty
**Draft says:** "roughly 62 cannons"
**Verification:** Wikipedia uses "over 50 pieces"; ABT and the Ruggles Wikipedia article give 62. The two figures come from different counts of the same event; 62 is the more specific and commonly cited number from ABT.
**Verdict:** Draft's "roughly 62" is defensible and properly hedged. No mandatory change, but authors should know this specific count is disputed between sources (Wikipedia floor = 50+, ABT = 62). If a note is helpful: "roughly 62 cannons (sources range from 'over 50' to 62)" would eliminate any fact-checker objection.

### SF-3 ⚠️ Johnston's 1837 duel — nerve damage not uniformly confirmed
**Draft says:** "possibly because an old 1837 dueling injury had left the area numb"
**Verification:**
- ABT Johnston biography: confirms 1837 duel caused nerve damage and may have prevented him from recognizing severity
- NPS Johnston biography: confirms the wound and that Johnston "thought the wound minor" — does not specifically say "nerve damage" but implies diminished sensation
- Wikipedia Johnston article does not mention nerve damage from the 1837 duel in what was retrieved
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED at the pack level (ABT + NPS both note the 1837 duel and the diminished perception of severity). Draft's "possibly because" hedge is correctly applied. No change needed.

### SF-4 ⚠️ "four million enslaved" — verify precision
**Draft says:** "to protect and preserve the enslavement of four million Black people"
**Pack says:** `[VERIFY]` — standard figure, not in pack
**Verification:**
- 1860 US Census: 3,953,760 enslaved people (Wikipedia "1860 US Census")
- Wikipedia "American Civil War": "freeing four million African Americans" / "more than 3.5 million of the 4 million enslaved people in the country"
- Wikipedia "Slavery in the United States": "The total slave population in the South eventually reached four million"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED — "four million" is the standard widely-cited round figure, consistent with the 1860 census count of ~3.95 million. The draft's use of "four million" is accurate and matches the standard historical shorthand. No change needed.

### SF-5 ⚠️ W.H.L. Wallace and Lew Wallace "no relation" — CONFIRMED, wording could be tightened
**Draft says:** "no relation to Lew Wallace, despite the matching surname"
**Verification:**
- Wikipedia "W.H.L. Wallace": explicitly states they were not related; the article notes Lew Wallace joked about the shared surname "causing great profanity in the army post office"; they happened to have both commanded their respective states' 11th regiments — a coincidence Wallace found amusing
- Wikipedia "Lew Wallace": no family connection mentioned; W.H.L. Wallace is mentioned only in a military context
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED — They were genuinely unrelated. Draft's "no relation to Lew Wallace, despite the matching surname" is accurate. The fun detail about postal confusion is available but not required.

### SF-6 ⚠️ Johnston "second-highest-ranking officer in the whole Confederacy"

**Verification:**

**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Accurate. No change.

**Verdict:** ✅ DRAFT PASSES — The draft's hedging is more rigorous than most secondary sources. The McClure/1892 attribution and the "likely apocryphal" label are correct and appropriate. The gate specifically required this treatment and the draft delivers it precisely.

### SF-8 ⚠️ "more casualties than all previous American wars combined" — confirm draft does NOT make this claim
**Draft says:** "You'll sometimes hear the claim that Shiloh produced more casualties than all of America's previous wars combined. That's a famous line, but it's false — the Revolutionary War alone killed far more Americans in battle."
**Verification:**
- 1860 Census / Peckham study: ~8,624 Americans killed in battle in the Revolutionary War vs. ~3,482 killed at Shiloh
- ABT long-form (contradictory): "more casualties than the Revolution, War of 1812 and Mexican War combined" — ABT's own long-form article makes the contested claim
- Wikipedia: does not make the "all previous wars" claim — compares only to earlier Civil War battles
- Pack §2 and §10: flags this as DISPUTED
**Verdict:** ✅ DRAFT PASSES — The draft correctly identifies and refutes this claim. Notably, ABT's own long-form article makes the contested "all previous wars" claim while ABT's "Shattering Myths" does not. The draft is more accurate than one of its own source articles. No change needed.

### SF-10 ⚠️ Grant's April 3 quote attribution — "letter" vs. correspondence
**Draft says:** "Grant, in a letter on April 3, 1862, three days before the battle"
**Pack says:** NPS "Was Grant Surprised" cites this as "Grant correspondence" dated April 3
**Verification:** NPS source describes it as correspondence; calling it "a letter" is a reasonable characterization of correspondence. Three days before April 6 is correct.
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. No change needed.

### SF-11 ⚠️ Beauregard halts at dusk — time discrepancy
**Draft says:** "As darkness came on, Beauregard...called off the attacks"
**Pack says:** ~6:00 pm for the halt
**Verification:** Wikipedia: "~6:00 pm — Beauregard called off all Confederate attacks near dusk." April 6 sunset in Tennessee would be around 7:15 pm CDT; 6:00 pm is before true dark but in declining light. "As darkness came on" is atmospheric but slightly imprecise.
**Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor imprecision — "as darkness came on" slightly overstates the dimness at 6:00 pm; "as the light faded" or "as dusk approached" would be more precise. Low priority.
