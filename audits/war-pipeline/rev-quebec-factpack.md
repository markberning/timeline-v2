# FACT PACK — Battle of Quebec, December 31, 1775

*American Revolution battle page (id `rev-quebec`, size M). ONLY source for the author. Baseline = `rev-outbreak-factpack.md` §7 (the Canada section) — this pack goes DEEPER and must not contradict it. Tone guards: NO doomed-expedition melodrama (the assault was a rational calculation — enlistments expiring at midnight + no siege guns); Carleton's defense respected as the disciplined thing it was; habitant neutrality told straight, not caricatured in either direction. ## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- Battle of Quebec, December 31, 1775, pre-dawn (~4–5 am to ~10 am), in a blizzard. Quebec City (46.81, -71.21), the walled capital of the British Province of Quebec, on the cliff above the St. Lawrence. The climax of the American invasion of Canada — the two-prong gamble to make Canada the "fourteenth colony." (HIGH)
- The first major American defeat of the war, and the war's first death of an American general officer: Richard Montgomery, killed in the opening minutes of his attack. Benedict Arnold wounded; Daniel Morgan captured with ~400+ men. Winner: Britain. (HIGH)
- Resonance: this is the SAME city Wolfe took on the Plains of Abraham sixteen years earlier (Sept 1759, deep in this app's F&I vertical). The 1775 defender, Governor Guy Carleton, had been Wolfe's quartermaster general in that campaign and was wounded on the Plains — in 1775 he defended the city he had helped conquer. (VERIFIED — see §3.) Montgomery too was a F&I veteran, but NOT of Quebec 1759: he served at Louisbourg 1758 and on Amherst's Lake Champlain prong (Ticonderoga/Crown Point, 1759). Do NOT put Montgomery on the Plains of Abraham. (HIGH)

## 2. BACKGROUND
- **Why Canada (terse — outbreak chapter owns the spine).** Ticonderoga's capture (May 1775) opened the Lake Champlain corridor; Congress authorized an invasion in late June, hoping Canada would join as a fourteenth colony and that the British back door would be closed. Two prongs. (HIGH)
- **Prong 1 — Montgomery up the lake.** Brig. Gen. Richard Montgomery (Irish-born ex-British regular) took the main force north: the siege of Fort St. Jean ate mid-September to early November (the campaigning season); Montreal fell without a fight November 13; Carleton escaped downriver to Quebec — by one well-attested account slipping past American positions disguised as a civilian/habitant in a small boat (whaleboat with muffled paddles; standard account, MED on the costume detail — keep light). (HIGH on the spine)
- **Prong 2 — Arnold through the Maine woods (one paragraph of documented horror, no more).** Arnold left Cambridge in mid-September with ~1,100 men to reach Quebec via the Kennebec and Chaudière rivers — far longer and crueler than his maps claimed. The attested inventory: green-timber bateaux that leaked from day one, drowning food and powder; the Height of Land bogs and freezing river fordings; Lt. Col. Roger Enos turning back with his rear division — roughly a quarter to a third of the force (~300 men) — and most of the remaining food; the survivors boiling shoe leather, cartridge boxes, and candles, and eating a dog (Capt. Dearborn's Newfoundland is the famously attested one). About 600 scarecrow survivors reached the St. Lawrence opposite Quebec in mid-November. Numbers: ~1,100 out, ~600 arrived is the baseline figure; some accounts say ~650–675 — use "about 600" or "~600 of 1,100" to match the outbreak chapter, never a precise count. (HIGH on items listed; numbers as ranges)
- **The siege that wasn't.** Arnold crossed the river November 13–14, paraded before the walls, and had to withdraw to Pointe-aux-Trembles (~20 mi upriver) — he had no artillery that could dent the walls, ammunition for maybe five rounds a man, and a third of his muskets ruined. Montgomery joined him December 2–3 with ~300 men and captured supplies. The combined "siege" was a blockade in name: a few light guns in ice-and-snow batteries that the walls shrugged off; smallpox already appearing in the camps; and the clock — most of Arnold's men's enlistments expired at midnight December 31. Attack before New Year's or watch the army dissolve. That, plus no guns and no prospect of reinforcement before spring, is the whole calculation — rational, not romantic. (HIGH)
- **Carleton's preparation.** Quebec's garrison was a scratch force but a real one: Lt. Col. Allan Maclean's Royal Highland Emigrants (~200) got in ahead of the Americans in mid-November; Carleton arrived from Montreal November 19; his November 22 proclamation ordered anyone unwilling to bear arms out of the city within days — and the militia rolls grew. By December: ~1,800 defenders (regulars + Royal Highland Emigrants + marines + ~400-500 sailors off the ships in the harbor + English- and French-speaking militia) behind walls, with ~150+ cannon, food for months. Carleton's plan was simple and correct: never come out. (HIGH; garrison breakdown approximate)

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
- **American: ~900–1,200 effectives** (use the range; sickness, expiring enlistments, and detachments make any precise count false). Maj. Gen. Richard Montgomery (overall command; Congress promoted him Major General December 9, 1775 — he died without knowing it, a verified and usable beat). Col. Benedict Arnold (the Kennebec corps, ~600). Capt. Daniel Morgan (Virginia riflemen). Feint commanders: Col. James Livingston (1st Canadian Regiment, ~200 Canadian volunteers) and Maj. John Brown (~160). Aaron Burr present as a 19-year-old volunteer aide to Montgomery. (HIGH)
- **British/Canadian: ~1,800 defenders.** Gov. Gen. Guy Carleton commanding; Lt. Col. Allan Maclean (Royal Highland Emigrants) his backbone professional; Lt. Col. Henry Caldwell (British militia); Col. Noël Voyer (Canadian militia); Capt. John Hamilton (sailors). Composition: a few regulars (7th Foot detachments), ~200 Royal Highland Emigrants, marines and ~400+ sailors, and a majority of militia — French-Canadian and English. The point to keep: the city was substantially defended BY Canadians, which is itself the answer to the "fourteenth colony" hope. (HIGH on total; breakdown approximate)
- **Commander bios (cards):**
  - **Richard Montgomery (1738–1775).** Irish-born; British regular 1756–1772 (17th Foot): Louisbourg 1758, Ticonderoga/Crown Point 1759 under Amherst, the Caribbean 1762 — then sold his commission after being passed over, emigrated to New York 1772–73, married Janet Livingston of the powerful Livingston clan, and wanted to farm. Accepted a Continental brigadier's commission reluctantly ("the will of an oppressed people... must be obeyed" — see §7). At parting he told Janet, by her own later account, "You shall never blush for your Montgomery." Dead at 37, the cause's first martyred general; Congress voted him a monument January 25, 1776 — the first national monument the United States ever commissioned (carved in Paris by Jean-Jacques Caffiéri, installed at St. Paul's Chapel, NYC, 1788). (HIGH; the parting line is Janet's recollection — attribute)
  - **Benedict Arnold (1741–1801) — as 1775 saw him, ZERO traitor-foreshadowing.** New Haven merchant-captain, co-captor of Ticonderoga, and after the Kennebec march one of the most admired soldiers in America: the march made his name ("the American Hannibal" in period press — usable as period praise, MED). Wounded in the left leg in the first minutes of his column's attack; promoted Brigadier General January 10, 1776 for Quebec; kept the blockade going from a hospital bed. Write him as exactly what he then was: brave, driven, ambitious, indispensable. (HIGH)
  - **Daniel Morgan (1735/36–1802).** Virginia frontier teamster — a wagoner on Braddock's 1755 campaign. The 499-lashes story: Morgan struck back at a British officer who had hit him with the flat of his sword and was sentenced to ~500 lashes; he claimed ever after that the drummer miscounted and the British still owed him one lash. EVIDENCE LEVEL: Morgan's own oft-told tale (he showed the scars; no contemporary record of the sentence survives) — tell it as Morgan's own story, with the wink, not as documented fact. At Quebec: led the riflemen through the first barricade, took command when Arnold fell, fought deepest into the Lower Town, captured; when forced to surrender he handed his sword to a priest rather than to a British officer (well-attested anecdote from participant accounts — MED-HIGH). Exchanged 1776–77 (paroled Aug 1776, exchanged Jan 1777). One forward line only: six years later, at Cowpens, he handed Britain one of its worst small-battle defeats of the war. (HIGH on spine; lashes story = legend-with-evidence-level)
  - **Guy Carleton (1724–1808).** Anglo-Irish professional; Wolfe's quartermaster general in the 1759 Quebec campaign (Wolfe insisted on him over the King's objection — standard account), fought on the Plains of Abraham and was wounded (head wound) there. Governor of Quebec from 1768; architect of the Quebec Act's conciliation of the French Canadians. In 1775: lost Montreal, escaped downriver, and then conducted the defense of Quebec exactly right — refused every temptation to sortie, armed the townspeople, expelled the unwilling, and waited for the river to open. Respect the generalship: he beat the invasion with patience. (HIGH)

## 4. THE BATTLE
- **The plan.** Originally a multi-point storm; after deserters betrayed earlier versions (a deserter did reveal the outline — Carleton was warned an attack would come on a stormy night), Montgomery settled on: two FEINTS against the western walls of the Upper Town — Livingston demonstrating at the Porte Saint-Jean (St. John's Gate), Brown firing on the Cape Diamond bastion — while the two real columns converged on the LOWER TOWN, the commercial strip between cliff and river: Montgomery from the south along the riverside path under Cape Diamond, Arnold from the north through the Sault-au-Matelot. Meet in the middle, then force the Upper Town from below (or use the merchants' captured property as leverage). Signal: rockets fired by Brown's men ~4–5 am. They waited for foul weather; the night of December 30–31 delivered a nor'easter blizzard. (HIGH)
- **Montgomery's column and his death (Près-de-Ville).** Montgomery led ~50 men of the New York advance (the column ~300) down from Wolfe's Cove along the narrow drifted path between the cliff and the frozen river. Carpenters sawed through two palisade stockades; Montgomery himself helped pull the pickets aside and went through with the lead group. Just beyond stood a fortified house/blockhouse at Près-de-Ville — a battery of small cannon manned by a mixed picket of ~30–50 Canadian and British militiamen and sailors (Canadian militia captains Chabot and Picard; sailors working the guns; standard tally ~39 militia + 9 seamen). The defenders held fire until the lead group was within tens of yards, then fired a single blast of grapeshot. Montgomery was killed instantly (struck through the head; body also hit), and most of the men beside him went down — his aide Capt. John Macpherson and Capt. Jacob Cheesman among the dead. WHO FIRED: tradition credits Hugh McQuarters, a Royal Artillery sergeant/gunner serving the piece (sometimes told as the man who touched the match); the evidence is local tradition + later memoirs, not a contemporary document — write "the defenders' first blast of grapeshot," and if naming McQuarters, name him as tradition. The senior surviving officer, Lt. Col. Donald Campbell, ordered the column back to Wolfe's Cove — much criticized afterward, but the command group was dead in the snow. The southern attack was over in minutes. (HIGH on sequence; McQuarters = TRADITION; the alleged last exhortation "Push on, brave boys—Quebec is ours!" is from later accounts — attribute or omit, see §7)
- **Arnold's column and his wound (Sault-au-Matelot).** Arnold's ~600 filed through the snow under the Palace Gate walls, taking fire and grenades from above; the sled-mounted brass 6-pounder bogged in a drift and was abandoned. At the first Sault-au-Matelot barricade Arnold took a musket ball (likely a ricochet) in the left leg below the knee while organizing the assault; he was carried bleeding to the rear, urging the men on. Morgan took command, scaled the first barricade himself (knocked back on his first try — he rolled under a cannon's muzzle to escape the bayonets on the second), and the riflemen took it with trivial loss; the ~30 defenders surrendered. (HIGH)
- **The street fight and the trap.** Then the attack stalled — wet powder, no scaling ladders forward, prisoners to guard, officers urging a wait for Montgomery's column (which would never come). The pause — roughly half an hour to an hour in the narrow street — let Carleton act. He fed Caldwell, Voyer's Canadian militia, a 7th Foot company, Maclean's men and sailors to the SECOND Sault-au-Matelot barricade (~12 ft, with guns), which stopped Morgan's storming attempts cold; house-to-house flanking attempts were thrown back at bayonet point. Meanwhile Carleton sent Capt. George Laws with ~200–500 men OUT the Palace Gate to retake the first barricade behind the Americans. The column was sealed in the street. Henry Dearborn's rearward company, powder soaked, was forced to surrender near the Palace Gate; the rest, packed in the Lower Town with ammunition gone, gave up by ~10 am. Morgan, backed against a wall in tears and refusing to hand his sword to the British, surrendered it to a priest. ~400–430 Americans captured. (HIGH; Laws' sortie strength varies ~200–500 — range)
- **Carleton's defense, characterized.** He never considered coming out to fight in the open — the lesson of 1759 read from the other side (Montcalm came out and lost everything; Carleton stayed in and lost nothing). He used the feints' failure quickly, concentrated at the real points, counter-sortied only INSIDE the problem (Laws, behind the trapped column), and treated the prisoners decently. Calculated waiting as generalship. (HIGH on actions; the 1759-lesson framing is fair interpretation, label as such in voice not as quote)

## 5. CASUALTIES (ranges)
- **American:** ~50–60 killed or wounded in the assault (commonly cited splits: ~30–51 killed, ~30–36 wounded; Arnold's own report to Congress said ~60 killed and wounded) plus **~400–431 captured** (Carleton counted 431 prisoners; use ~400–430 per the outbreak baseline). Several of the dead were not recovered until the spring thaw. (HIGH as ranges; never a precise single figure)
- **British/Canadian:** Carleton's official return: ~5 killed, ~13–14 wounded (≈20). A few witness accounts run higher (up to ~50); use ~5–20 with the official return firmest. (HIGH as ranges)
- Duration: ~4–6 hours of action (signal ~4–5 am, last surrenders ~10 am). Winner: Britain, decisively. (HIGH)

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **The blockade in name (Jan–Apr 1776).** Arnold — wounded, promoted Brigadier General January 10 — kept perhaps 600 men in lines outside a city whose garrison now outnumbered them, maintaining the fiction of a siege through a subarctic winter. Carleton, knowing relief would sail when the ice broke, declined to sortie even now. Reinforcements trickled north; smallpox did the real killing in the American camp — the disease, fed by crowding and (against orders) self-inoculation, broke the army faster than the British ever did. ONE cross-ref line allowed: the memory of smallpox wrecking this army is what later pushed Washington to secretly inoculate the whole Continental Army (Feb 1777) — the army chapter tells that story. Hard money ran out; paper Continental dollars bought nothing from the habitants; requisitioning at bayonet point turned welcome into hostility. Arnold left for Montreal in April; Wooster, then John Thomas, took over. (HIGH)
- **The collapse (May–June 1776).** May 6, 1776: HMS Surprise and two consorts reached Quebec with the vanguard of ~9,000+ under Burgoyne; Carleton immediately marched out — now, with the odds reversed — and the American lines dissolved into a near-rout (guns, papers, even the sick abandoned). Gen. Thomas died of smallpox during the retreat (June 2). After the rear-guard defeat at Trois-Rivières (June 8), the army fell back up the lake to Ticonderoga. The Canadian adventure was over. (HIGH)
- **Montgomery's body.** Found in the snow on New Year's Day, identified (his brother-in-law's accounts say by features/his sword; identification by people in the city who knew him, incl. by some accounts a captured officer or the widow Thompson's lodger — keep simple: "identified by those in Quebec who had known him"). Carleton's government gave him a decent, honorable burial: Lt. Gov. Cramahé paid for the coffin and a simple military funeral, January 4, 1776, inside the city. Genuinely respectful treatment of a fallen enemy — say so. In 1818 New York State brought the remains home: carried down the Hudson by steamboat, and by arrangement with Gov. Clinton the boat passed his widow Janet's house (she watched from her veranda as the band played the dead march); reinterred under his monument at St. Paul's Chapel, Manhattan, July 8, 1818. Janet — his wife of two years, his widow for 53 — never remarried. (HIGH; identification detail varies — keep generic)
- **The martyr.** News reached Philadelphia mid-January 1776; Congress's mourning, the Jan 25 monument vote (the first national memorial), and a wave of orations made Montgomery the Revolution's first hero-martyr — he was mourned in BRITAIN too, where opposition Whigs eulogized him in Parliament as a fallen countryman of principle (standard account — usable at one-line level). (HIGH)
- **What it meant.** Canada stayed British — the fourteenth colony that never was; there would be no northern annex, and the war's northern frontier became Lake Champlain (Carleton's 1776 counter-thrust, Valcour Island, and 1777's Burgoyne campaign all flow from this door staying open — at most one forward clause). The habitants: the Quebec Act (1774) had already secured the Church and French civil law, so the clergy and seigneurs mostly held to Britain; the mass of habitants stayed pragmatically NEUTRAL — willing to sell to whoever paid silver, unwilling to die for either empire; some hundreds did serve on each side (Livingston's 1st Canadian Regiment on the American side; Voyer's militia inside the walls). The standard framing — the Quebec Act bought enough Canadian acquiescence to save Canada, and American hard-money failure + requisitioning squandered the rest — is sound; tell it as interest, not as fickleness. (HIGH on the framing as standard historiography)

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES / HANDLE-WITH-CARE
1. **The "I am in the way of my duty, and know no fear" line is ARNOLD's, not Montgomery's** (post-battle letter from the camp, early Jan 1776) — the assignment brief misattributes it; do not let it migrate.
2. **Montgomery's alleged last exhortation ("Push on, brave boys...") = weak chain** (later accounts; the lead group died with him). Tradition-tag or omit. Same for naming **Hugh McQuarters** as the man who fired the fatal gun — local tradition, not document; "the defenders' grapeshot" is the safe register. Defender headcount at Près-de-Ville varies (≈30–50; common tally 39 militia + 9 seamen).
3. **Morgan's 499 lashes** — his own much-repeated story (scars shown, count included); no contemporary record. Tell as Morgan's tale, evidence level explicit.
4. **Numbers all as ranges:** attackers ~900–1,200; defenders ~1,800; American killed/wounded ~50–60 (reports vary 30–60 killed) + prisoners ~400–431; British ~5 killed/~13–14 wounded (official) vs up to ~50 (witnesses); Arnold's march ~1,100 out / ~300 back with Enos / ~600 (some say 650–675) arrived — match the outbreak chapter's "~600."
5. **Montgomery's F&I service: Louisbourg 1758 + Amherst's Champlain prong 1759 — he was NOT at Quebec/Plains of Abraham 1759.** The 1759 resonance belongs to CARLETON (Wolfe's QMG, wounded on the Plains — verified) and to the geography (Wolfe's Cove). 
6. **Carleton portrait identification dispute** (common likeness possibly his brother Thomas) — caption conservatively.
7. **Smallpox warfare allegation** (Carleton deliberately sending infected civilians/women out) — appears in American accusations and some accounts; NOT established fact.
