# Lookout Mountain — NEWCOMER / CLARITY critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

**Read cold as a sharp 15-year-old with ZERO Civil War knowledge.**
Source: `audits/war-pipeline/lookout-mountain-draft.md`.
Each item: term/passage → location → verdict (CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST) → fix priority.

**Overall verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS.** The draft is unusually clear for a war section — the
prose is patient, the stakes and the central "small fight, giant name" idea land hard, and
side-tags + scene-setting are nearly complete. But a newcomer hits a cluster of military
organization jargon (corps/division/brigade) used heavily and never defined, plus a handful
of geography/engineering terms (palisade, bench/plateau, picket, works, flank/turn) that are
mostly defined — except a few load-bearing ones that aren't. With ~5 MUST-FIX glosses it is
clean. As-is, a true newcomer would be lost in §1 para 6–7 (the order-of-battle wall) and
fuzzy on "turning a position" the whole way through.

---

## MUST-FIX (a zero-knowledge reader is genuinely blocked or misled)

### 1. corps / division / brigade — the army-organization ladder. NEEDS-GLOSS.
- Locations: §1 ("two corps," "about three divisions, scraped together from three different
  corps," "XII Corps," "XV Corps," "IV Corps," "three brigades"); §2 ("Mississippi brigade,"
  "an Alabama-and-Mississippi brigade," "reinforcing brigades"); throughout.
- Problem: This is the single densest jargon load in the draft and it is **never defined
  once.** A 15-year-old does not know a brigade is bigger than a regiment, that a division
  is several brigades, a corps several divisions. §1 para 6 ("Three corps, three commanders,
  one mountain") is built entirely on a size/structure intuition the reader doesn't have, so
  the intended point — that Hooker's force was a patchwork scraped from unrelated big units —
  doesn't land. The Roman-numeral corps names (XII, XV, IV) read as random code.
- Fix: one short inline gloss at first use in §1. e.g. on "two corps": *(a corps was the
  army's largest building block — tens of thousands of men; it broke down into divisions,
  then brigades, then regiments)*. One definition carries the whole section.

### 2. "turning" a position / flank / "turning movement" vs "stormed." NEEDS-GLOSS.
- Locations: §2 ("rolling up the thin Confederate picket line **from the flank**"; "the
  mountain was being **turned**, not **stormed**"); §3/§4 lean on this contrast repeatedly;
  Outcome verdict and Meanwhile sidebars too.
- Problem: "Flank" (the side/end of a line) and "turning" a position (getting around the end
  of the enemy's line so you hit it from the side, forcing it to give way without a head-on
  assault) are THE core military idea of the whole battle — the draft's thesis is "turned,
  not stormed." But neither "flank" nor "turn" is ever defined. "Stormed" (a direct frontal
  rush) is also assumed. A newcomer reads "turned, not stormed" — the italic hinge of §2 —
  and cannot tell what distinction is being drawn. This is the load-bearing concept; it must
  be glossed.
- Fix: gloss "flank" on first use (§2 para 2): *attacking from the side or end of the enemy's
  line rather than head-on.* Then "turned, not stormed" pays off. Consider one clause
  contrasting "stormed" (charged straight up the front) explicitly.

### 3. "the palisade." NEEDS-GLOSS — but note it IS partly handled.
- Locations: §2 para 2 ("the base of the palisade — the vertical rock cliff-line that crowns
  Lookout Mountain near its summit, a wall of stone no infantry could storm"); reused §2,
  §4.
- Verdict: **CLEAR on first use** — the em-dash gloss is exactly right and tells the reader
  why it matters (can't be climbed → that's why nobody fought for the top). This is the model
  for how the others should be handled. Flagging only because the prompt named it: it is
  defined well. **No fix needed** — keep as written. (Listed under MUST-FIX bucket only so the
  reconciler sees it was checked and passed.)

### 4. "bench" / "plateau" / "shelf of ground." NEEDS-GLOSS (one of three uses defines it).
- Locations: §1 outcome verdict ("the Cravens House bench" — undefined, first appearance);
  §2 para 1 in fig caption ("the Cravens House bench"); §2 "The Stand at the Cravens House"
  ("a small flat shelf of ground, a bench cut into the mountainside ... where the slope
  leveled enough for men to dig in and stand" — **this one is CLEAR**); later "the Cravens
  plateau."
- Problem: The good definition arrives in §2's Cravens-House paragraph, but the term "bench"
  is used **earlier** (the outcome verdict at the very top, and casually) before it's defined,
  and then "plateau" is used as a synonym in §2 para "And then the fog..." without signaling
  it's the same flat shelf. A newcomer meeting "Cravens House bench" in the opening verdict
  has no idea a "bench" is a flat ledge on a slope.
- Fix: either (a) move/echo a 4-word gloss to the first "bench" in the outcome verdict, or
  (b) on first body use add "(a flat ledge on the slope)". And use ONE word — pick bench OR
  plateau OR shelf — or explicitly note "the Cravens bench (or plateau)" once so the reader
  knows they're the same place.

### 5. "siege" / "lift the siege." NEEDS-GLOSS.
- Locations: outcome verdict ("helped lift the siege"); §1 cross-ref "(see the siege of
  Chattanooga)"; §4 ("one piece of lifting the siege," "the siege of Chattanooga would truly
  be broken").
- Problem: The whole strategic payoff is "lifting the siege," but "siege" is never defined.
  The draft DOES dramatize the underlying situation beautifully in §1 ("living at the bottom
  of a trap," "caged it," "nearly starved," enemy commanding the river and roads) — so the
  *idea* is fully present. But it never connects that vivid picture to the word "siege," and
  "lift/break the siege" is then used as the strategic verdict. A newcomer gets the trap, but
  may not realize "siege" = that trap, or what "lifting" it means.
- Fix: cheap — at the first "siege" (§1, the Cracker Line para or the cross-ref), tie the word
  to the picture already drawn: *the siege (the Confederate ring of heights penning the army
  in and starving it)*. Then "lift the siege" lands.

## SHOULD-FIX (a newcomer stumbles or loses nuance, but recovers)

### 6. The order-of-battle paragraph (§1, "The force was a patchwork"). NEEDS-GLOSS / risk of
overwhelm.
- Location: §1 para "The force was a patchwork" through "Three corps, three commanders, one
  mountain."
- Problem: Even with #1 fixed, this paragraph fires four unfamiliar names (Geary, Osterhaus,
  Cruft + their corps) plus a digression on "Forty-Eighter" émigrés and the 1848 European
  revolutions, all at once. It's the closest thing to an overwhelming passage in the draft.
  Each man is side-tagged (North) and the Osterhaus aside is *charming* and well-explained —
  but a reader still has to hold three new generals + three corps numbers in working memory
  with no payoff yet. Borderline.
- Fix: probably survives once #1 (corps gloss) lands. If trimming, the Forty-Eighter aside,
  while delightful, is the most cuttable cognitive load. Keep if voice wins; just don't add
  more here.

### 7. "picket" / "pickets" / "picket line." NEEDS-GLOSS.
- Locations: §2 ("screen the crossing from the Confederate **pickets** above"; "rolling up the
  thin Confederate **picket line** from the flank").
- Problem: A picket = soldiers posted forward of the main line as lookouts/early warning. The
  word appears twice, undefined. A newcomer can half-infer "guards on the slope" from context,
  so not blocking — but "rolling up the picket line from the flank" is doing real work (it's
  HOW the thin defense collapsed) and the reader shouldn't have to guess.
- Fix: 3-word gloss on first use: *the pickets (forward lookouts)*.

### 8. "works" / "built their best works." NEEDS-GLOSS.
- Location: §2 "The Stand at the Cravens House" ("the Confederates had built their best works
  here").
- Problem: "Works" = field fortifications (dug-in trenches, log/earth defenses). Undefined.
  Context ("strongest position," "dig in") lets a reader approximate it, but it's a bare
  jargon noun a newcomer won't recognize.
- Fix: *their best works (dug-in defenses)* on first use.

### 9. "pontoon" — flagged in the prompt but NOT PRESENT. n/a.
- Note: the prompt asked to watch for "pontoon." The word does not appear in the draft. §2
  says the swollen creek "had to be bridged and forded" — generic verbs, no pontoon jargon.
  CLEAR. No action; flagging so the reconciler knows it was checked.

### 10. "Cracker Line" / "Brown's Ferry." CLEAR (with a tiny caveat).
- Location: §1 ("forced open a new supply route, the 'Cracker Line,' seizing a river crossing
  at Brown's Ferry").
- Verdict: CLEAR. "New supply route" defines Cracker Line inline; "river crossing" defines
  Brown's Ferry. Cross-ref "(see the siege of Chattanooga)" parks the detail correctly. Good.

### 11. "reconnaissance." NEEDS-GLOSS (minor).
- Location: §1 Meanwhile sidebar ("in what was billed as a **reconnaissance**").
- Problem: "Reconnaissance" (a scouting probe to test the enemy) is college-level vocabulary
  for a 15-year-old and it's load-bearing to the joke (a "scouting mission" that quietly seized
  a hill). Undefined.
- Fix: *billed as a reconnaissance (a probe to test the enemy)* — or substitute "a scouting
  probe."

### 12. "quartermaster general" (Meigs). SHOULD-FIX (minor).
- Location: §3 ("the Union quartermaster general, Brig. Gen. Montgomery C. Meigs").
- Problem: A reader won't know a quartermaster general runs army supply — but here it doesn't
  matter much; the point is just "a Union officer watching coined the name." Survives. Optional
  4-word gloss only if smooth.

### 13. Rank abbreviations: Maj. Gen. / Brig. Gen. / Gen. / Col. / Sgt. Maj. NEEDS-GLOSS (very
minor / cosmetic).
- Locations: throughout.
- Problem: A 15-year-old may not parse "Maj. Gen." vs "Brig. Gen." as a seniority order, or
  read "Col." A reader gets that these are all officers, which is enough for the story; the
  precise pecking order rarely matters here. Lowest priority — only Grant's "overall command"
  vs the brigade commanders needs the reader to sense Grant outranks everyone, and §1 says
  "overall command" in plain words, so it's covered. Leave unless trivially expandable.

### 14. "summit" / "crest" / "bench" / "slope" / "shoulder of the mountain" — geography stack.
SHOULD-FIX (consistency).
- Locations: throughout — "summit," "crest," "shoulder of the mountain," "the point" (§4/§5).
- Problem: Mostly CLEAR individually (summit = top is inferable). But "the point of Lookout
  Mountain" (§4, where the flag goes up) is a specific feature (the prow/northern tip) used as
  if known. A newcomer reads "the point" and may think "what point?" Minor.
- Fix: first "the point" → "the point (the mountain's northern tip)" once.

## Things a newcomer GETS right away (no fix — strengths to preserve)

- **The cause (slavery) is stated in the section's own plain prose** (§1 para 2, §4 closing) —
  clear, unmissable, well-tied to "the rail gateway to the slaveholding heartland." CLEAR.
- **The central irony** ("a small fight wearing a giant's name," romance vs. modest fight) is
  stated up front and reinforced — a newcomer cannot miss the thesis. CLEAR.
- **The trap/starvation setup** (§1) is dramatized in plain language before any jargon. CLEAR.
- **Side-tags** (North)/(South) on first mention are thorough and let a newcomer always know
  whose side everyone is on — exactly what a zero-knowledge reader needs. CLEAR.
- **The fog as both screen and wrecker** is told in plain cause-effect. CLEAR.
- **Cross-refs** (Chickamauga, Chancellorsville, Missionary Ridge) are parked, not dumped —
  a newcomer isn't asked to know them. CLEAR.
