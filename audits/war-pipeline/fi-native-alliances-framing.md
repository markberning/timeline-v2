# FRAMING CRITIC REPORT — "The Contest for Native Alliances"
## French & Indian War · theme section (kind=theme, SOCIETY) · web-enabled framing critic

**Verdict: PASS**

Both ship-blocking first-checks pass cleanly, no DISTORTED finding stands. A

## FIRST CHECK (a) — Are Native nations first-class actors with their own aims, not props? → PASS

This is the strongest part of the draft. Native nations are kept in the subject
position from the title forward, and their aims are named as *their own* at every
turn, never derived from British or French interest.

- The thesis sentence states it outright: "Native nations were not a sideshow to
  this war. They were the thing the war turned on." (¶4)
- France's early victory at the Monongahela is explicitly *de-centered from the
  empire*: "France did not beat Braddock at the Monongahela. France's Native
  allies did" — backed by the force-composition numbers (~640 Native of ~900). (§"How
  Native allies won France its early war")
- The Iroquois section refuses the "Britain's allies" filing in so many words: "It
  is tempting to file the Iroquois under 'Britain's allies' and move on. They
  wouldn't have recognized the description." Their strategy is named as theirs:
  balance, leverage, don't be swallowed by either empire. (§"The Iroquois play their
  own game")
- Easton is framed as a Native decision, not a gift: "The Ohio nations were not
  given peace at Easton. They chose it, because it served their interest better than
  the French alliance now did." (§"Easton")
- The dedicated "Allies, not pawns" section makes the agency claim the explicit
  spine, and lists Tanaghrisson, Hendrick, Teedyuscung, and Pontiac each "pursuing a
  Native aim, not a French or British one."
- "They were never 'Britain's Indians' or 'France's Indians'" directly executes the
  fact pack's framing directive against the possessive construction.
- The Mohawk-fought-Mohawk thread (Kahnawake for France, valley Mohawk for Britain)
  is used twice precisely to show nations as communities of self-determining people,
  not blocs: "The nations were not blocs. They were communities making their own
  choices."

Mark: **FAIR** (in fact, exemplary). No props framing anywhere.

## FIRST CHECK (b) — Is the human cost / dispossession named in the section's OWN prose? → PASS

Named plainly and repeatedly, in the section's own voice, not euphemized:

- **Dispossession as the underlying contest** is stated as the bottom-line thesis:
  "The contest for Native alliances was, at bottom, a contest over Native land, and
  the people whose land it was would pay for the outcome no matter who won."
  (§"Allies, not pawns")
- The specific frauds are itemized in the section's own prose: the Walking Purchase,
  the ~800,000 acres of Mohawk land Hendrick named, the Ohio Company's
  200,000-acre paper grant over Delaware/Shawnee homeland.
- **Disease cost** is named twice, including that the warriors carried smallpox home
  and "sparking epidemics that devastated their own communities… They had been
  denied their due, then sickened for their trouble." (§"The victory that cost France
  its allies")
- **War and captivity:** "War and captivity emptied communities."
- **The catastrophic outcome** — the loss of the second empire as a counterweight and
  the collapse of leverage — is the whole closing arc: "Winning the war, losing the
  peace," Amherst's deliberate severing of the alliance, the documented smallpox-
  blanket intent, and Pontiac's War. The Royal Proclamation promise is correctly
  flagged as one that "like most such promises to Native nations… would not hold."

The contest-was-over-Native-land claim and the war-was-a-catastrophe claim are both
present and explicit. Mark: **FAIR**.

## NAMING THE WAR — PASS

The fact-pack/framing requirement to acknowledge that the war's name centers the
British-colonial POV is executed in the opening paragraph and done unusually well:
"The British colonists who fought this war named it after the people they were most
afraid of… The label is a point of view. It names the enemy from a British porch
looking west." The draft also makes clear "Indians" fought on all sides and for
themselves (the Mohawk-vs-Mohawk thread; Native warriors on the French side as the
decisive force). Mark: **FAIR**.

## OTHER DISTORTION HUNT

### False balance / both-sides-ism → FAIR
The draft does NOT flatten France and Britain into morally equivalent "courting"
empires. France's middle-ground reciprocity is explained as a *mechanism* (why the
alliances held), not romanticized — and it is immediately complicated (gift-giving
read correctly as obligation, not generosity; Kahnawake origins traced to French
raids on Mohawk villages in the 1660s). Britain's land hunger and fraud are named
without hedge. Crucially, the draft does not let France off the hook: Fort William
Henry is framed as France *breaking faith* with the allies who won its battles. This
is the correct asymmetry — both empires are instrumentalizing Native power; neither
is the hero.

### Romanticization of empire → FAIR
No empire is romanticized. Johnson's intimacy with the Mohawk is presented as
*leverage Britain depended on* ("Britain's whole northern alliance ran through one
man's relationships"), not as benign friendship. Molly Brant is correctly given
standing as "a power broker in her own right," honoring the fact pack's caution —
and the draft correctly avoids asserting a formal marriage, using "household partner"
(matches the §3 CAUTION).

### Erasure of Native agency → FAIR (actively resisted; this is the draft's thesis)

### Presentism → FAIR
The draft explains Native diplomatic logic (gift-giving as obligation; Onontio as
reciprocal father) in period terms and explicitly corrects the European misreading
("To European eyes that looked like bribery. It wasn't.") rather than imposing modern
categories. The Amherst smallpox material is presented as documented intent with the
effectiveness correctly hedged, not as anachronistic moralizing.

### Sanitized OR gratuitous violence → FAIR
The Fort William Henry "massacre" is the highest-risk passage and the draft threads
it correctly: it (1) explains the warriors' grievance as legitimate before describing
the attack (avoids the "savage" trope), (2) uses the modern scholarly toll (~69–184,
"under eight percent," disease killed more than weapons) against the 1,500 legend
exactly as the fact pack's CONTESTED directive requires, (3) presents Montcalm's
culpability as a genuine debate (Parkman vs. Jennings) without picking a side, and
(4) flags the Frye quote's period word "savages" by attribution rather than adopting
it in the narrator's own voice. Violence is neither minimized nor luridly dwelt on.
Mark: **FAIR**.

### "Slur in narrator voice" check → FAIR
"Savages" appears only inside the directly-quoted Frye eyewitness account (quote #4
in the ledger), never in the draft's own narration. The narrator consistently uses
"warriors," "Native nations," and proper nation names. This is the correct handling.

## SHOULD-FIX (polish, non-blocking — not framing defects)

1. **Tanaghrisson is named in "Allies, not pawns" without ever being introduced in
   the body.** He appears in the fact pack (§3) as the Mingo "Half King" who leaned
   British at Logstown over the heads of the Delaware — a useful agency example — but
   the draft's closing list is the first and only mention, with a parenthetical gloss
   ("the Mingo headman the British called the Half King"). It reads slightly as a name
   dropped for the list. Either give him one earlier beat in the Ohio/Easton setup
   (his top-down Iroquois claim over the Delaware is part of *why* the Delaware made
   their own peace at Easton) or accept the parenthetical as sufficient. Minor.

2. **"The Walking Purchase that defrauded the Delaware"** is named in the closing
   section and again referenced at Easton ("an earlier fraud") but never dated or
   one-line-explained on first use, where the writing rules want inline definition.
   The reader meets "Walking Purchase" cold in the final section. Consider a four-word
   gloss (the 1737 Pennsylvania land swindle). Minor; the surrounding sentence carries
   enough that it isn't a comprehension wall.

3. **Tuscarora are listed among the Six Nations without the usual "(joined ~1722)"
   note.** Not a framing issue and arguably out of scope for this section's altitude;
   noting only for completeness. Safe to leave.

None of these is ship-blocking and none touches the two first-checks.

## CONCLUSION
The section treats Native nations as the protagonists of the war they are named after,
names the dispossession and the human cost in its own prose as the thing the war sat
on top of, and acknowledges the British-colonial point of view baked into the war's
name. It resists every distortion the F&I framing rule targets — false balance,
imperial romance, agency erasure, presentism, and both sanitized and gratuitous
violence. **No MUST-FIX. Verdict: PASS.**
