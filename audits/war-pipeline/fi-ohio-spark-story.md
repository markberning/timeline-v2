# Story Critic Gate — The Ohio Spark
> Gate: STORYTELLING · Reviewer: Story Critic · Draft: `fi-ohio-spark-draft.md`

---

## Overall Grade: GOOD (borderline STRONG in places)

The draft is genuinely readable — it has a clear spine, the Tanaghrisson thread is the best material in it, and the prose is mostly clean. But it has two structural habits that blunt its punching power: (1) it tells you what to think about a scene before the scene earns it, and (2) the pacing sags in the middle third because the draft switches into logistics mode and forgets it's a story. One locked rule violation flagged (em-dash).

---

## Section-by-Section

### Opening (lede paragraph)
**Grade: STRONG**

"What he set off instead was a war that would burn for nine years across four continents" is a good hook. The pivot — "the man who turned that skirmish into a killing was not Washington and not the French" — is the strongest single sentence in the draft. It announces the thesis and creates genuine forward tension.

Minor flag: "a damp Pennsylvania ravine, in an engagement that lasted about fifteen minutes" — the specificity is good, but "damp" feels reach-y, a tonal color asserted rather than earned. Fine to keep; just notice it.

---

### The Land in the Middle
**Grade: GOOD**

"A French fort was a trading partner with cannons. A British farm was the leading edge of dispossession." — This is exactly the target register. Sharp, concrete, carries weight. The Ohio Company paragraph is solid too: the Dinwiddie conflict-of-interest detail ("he was also defending his own investment") is the kind of human-rot specificity the voice needs more of throughout.

**Issue — pill placement:** The pill `[The Ohio Country & the Ohio Company]` sits at the very bottom of the section, after the OC detail has already been fully told. In the reference (`improvising-a-war`), pills interrupt the prose at the *moment* the topic opens, not after the section has explained it. Move the pill to right after "the Ohio Company of Virginia" is first named (after sentence 2 of that paragraph), not after the section ends.

---

### The Forts and the Ultimatum
**Grade: GOOD**

The scene of Washington at Fort Le Boeuf works — Saint-Pierre's quote lands, and the almost-drowning on the way back is a good vivid beat. "A twenty-one-year-old's travel diary had become a piece of imperial propaganda, and Washington, almost overnight, had a name" is a solid closing sentence.

**Issue — pacing/flat passage:** The middle of the section, the roster of French forts (Presque Isle → Le Boeuf → Machault/Venango), reads like a gazetteer entry. Compare to the reference, which compresses infrastructure ("21,000 to 22,000 miles of track" vs. the South's "patchwork of different gauges") through the lens of human consequence. Here we get: "Fort Presque Isle at modern Erie, Pennsylvania, then Fort Le Boeuf to its south, and Fort Machault (also called Venango) at the junction of French Creek and the Allegheny River, built on a seized British trading post." The reader doesn't need to know all three fort names to feel what the French were doing. One vivid anchor — the seized trading post is actually the best detail, because it shows aggression, not just construction — could carry the weight of three names.

**Suggested tightening:** Cut to Fort Le Boeuf (the destination) and Fort Machault (because it was a grab), drop Presque Isle. Frame the chain as what it was strategically: "By the time the column was done, the French had nailed a lid on the Ohio from the south shore of Lake Erie down to the Allegheny River, the last one built on top of a British trading post they'd simply seized." Something like that — the coordinator should source the actual language from the fact pack.

**Issue — telling before showing:** "He was a Native leader pursuing his own war, for his own reasons, using both empires as his instruments" is stated in the lede AND is the thesis of the entire Jumonville section. That's fine for a lede. But the Tanaghrisson intro inside "The Forts and the Ultimatum" says: "He had his own reasons for wanting to walk into the French forts, and Washington did not fully grasp them." This is a deliberate withhold — good technique — but it needs a small grace note to be felt rather than just noted. Right now it reads like a footnote. Something that makes the *reader* notice the gap, not just records it.

### The Forks Are Lost
**Grade: NEEDS WORK**

This section is the pacing sag. It's competent but inert. It tells us what happened in straightforward chronological order without any narrative charge. The most interesting detail — that Washington was already marching toward a fort that was being taken out from under him — is buried in the last paragraph and delivered flatly: "he learned the bad news on the road: the place he was marching to support no longer existed." That deserves more than an aside.

**Core problem:** The section is all outcome, no texture. 600 French soldiers arriving with cannon and demanding surrender of a handful of workers — that should feel like a gut-punch. Instead: "roughly 600 French soldiers and Native allies under Claude-Pierre Pécaudy de Contrecœur (French) arrived with cannon and demanded surrender. The handful of Virginians had no choice and were allowed to march away." This is encyclopedia prose. The reference voice would have made you feel the Virginians' absurdity — they're building a fort, these enormous reinforcements show up, and they just...leave. That's almost comic. Use it.

**Suggested lift:** The irony of the moment is the story: Britain's first, best strategic position on the continent — the spot that controlled every river highway — fell to an unanswered ultimatum before a single soldier arrived to defend it. A construction crew handed over a keystone. Lean into that.

**em-dash violation:** "the keystone of the whole French chain" — no em-dash found in that sentence, but check surrounding paragraphs. In the lede section: "a war that would burn for nine years across four continents" uses a comma, fine. **Confirmed em-dash:** not found in this section on review. (See full-doc flag below.)

### Fifteen Minutes in a Ravine
**Grade: STRONG**

This is the best section in the draft. The Tanaghrisson analysis — "he tried to use the British to win his own war for the Ohio" — is fully earned here because the draft has done the work of building his situation: born Catawba, adopted Seneca, influence "nearly gone," a personal hatred of the French. The "Tu n'es pas encore mort, mon père" line is exactly the kind of specific the voice needs, and the draft uses it well (while correctly hedging the sourcing).

The question of Jumonville as envoy or spy, and the conclusion "it was going to be settled by whoever could put it in writing," is smart analysis delivered with the right touch of cynicism.

**Washington's quote** ("I heard Bulletts whistle and believe me there was something charming in the sound") is great and well-placed.

**Issue — meta-narrator opener:** "To understand why Tanaghrisson did this, you have to set aside the idea that he was fighting Washington's war." The opener "to understand X, you have to Y" is a meta-narrator aside — it steps outside the story to coach the reader. The reference voice never does this. Rewrite: enter the explanation directly. Something like: "Tanaghrisson was not fighting Washington's war. He had his own." Then the analysis follows. The explanation itself is strong; just drop the lecturing frame.

### That Little Thing Upon the Meadow
**Grade: GOOD**

"Calling it a fort flatters it" — perfect sentence. The rain-soaked trenches, Villiers getting command of the revenge expedition specifically because it was his brother — these are good.

**Issue — one flat passage:** "The casualties told the story: roughly thirty Virginians and South Carolinians killed and seventy wounded, against a handful of French dead. It was Washington's first battle as a commander, and he had lost it badly." The first sentence is fine; the second is redundant with the casualties AND with everything the section has already shown. Cut "and he had lost it badly" — the reader sees it.

**Issue — the surrender document sequence:** This is the most narratively important event in the section — a French commander seeds the word "assassination" into a surrender document, a Dutch translator bungles it (or doesn't), and Washington signs away his reputation. This deserves more weight and more pace. Right now it reads almost procedurally: article VII, the word, the translation, the signing. The reference voice would slow down here. What did Washington think he was signing? What did "the death of Jumonville" versus "the assassination of Jumonville" actually mean diplomatically? The draft tells you the answer but doesn't make you feel the trap closing. The sentence "That document, very likely seeded with the word 'assassinat' on purpose, gave France exactly the justification it wanted" is the payoff, but it arrives too quickly after a section that compressed the trap into three sentences.

**Minor house-rule flag — em-dash:** No em-dash detected in this section; clean.

### A Union That Wasn't
**Grade: GOOD**

The JOIN, or DIE woodcut is well-used (published nineteen days before Jumonville Glen — that juxtaposition is good). The Albany Congress and Franklin's Plan of Union are handled briskly and clearly.

**Issue — thesis-statement writing:** "The two events ran in parallel through the summer of 1754, and the contrast is the point" — this is the meta-narrator again, telling the reader what the contrast is before delivering it. The reference voice trusts the reader to catch it. Compare: in "Improvising a War," the Scott/Anaconda section doesn't say "the contrast between Scott and the public mood is the point"; it just *puts Scott next to the public mood and lets the irony speak*. Here the draft should drop "the contrast is the point" and trust the juxtaposition.

**Issue — closing line too neat:** "France acted as one. Britain's colonies could not even agree to act as one, and the men in the field paid for it." This is a strong formulation, but "paid for it" is slightly vague. What did they pay? Washington's men at Fort Necessity paid in blood. The closing line should be *slightly* more specific — or if the abstraction is the choice, it's fine, just flag it as a choice.

**Pill placement:** Both pills in this section (`[The Ohio Country & the Ohio Company]` — which actually belongs in "The Land in the Middle" — and `[The Contest for Native Alliances]`) are correctly placed mid-section at topic-open. The Albany Congress pill works. The Native Alliances pill feels slightly orphaned; it arrives after the context has been delivered. Consider moving it to just before the Iroquois Confederacy sentence rather than after the Albany Congress section fully winds down.

### The Spark and the World It Burned
**Grade: STRONG**

"The attribution is doubtful, and probably wrong, so the line is best left to the side. The thing it describes, however, is sound." — This is exactly the target register: informal, honest, confident. Good.

The Tanaghrisson death coda — he "died of pneumonia near modern Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, on October 4, 1754, a few months after the killing in the glen that he, more than anyone, had made matter" — is the right ending. The structure (broad global scale → the name problem → the Ohio nations' bill → Tanaghrisson's death) is correct.

**Issue — mild over-explanation:** "There is a last thing worth saying plainly" — another meta-narrator marker. "Worth saying plainly" signals that the prose is about to announce its own insight. In the reference voice, the insight just lands. Cut "There is a last thing worth saying plainly, and it sits in the name." Open directly: "The name centers the British colonial view and flattens everyone else..." The transition from the global-scale paragraph works without the announcement.

## MUST-IMPROVE

1. **"The Forks Are Lost" — rewrite for narrative charge.** The fall of the Forks is the single most consequential strategic event in the chapter, and it reads like a summary. The irony (a construction crew surrendered the hinge of a continent) should be felt, not logged. The Washington-marching-toward-a-fort-that-no-longer-exists beat needs real weight.

2. **Meta-narrator openers — cut four instances.** "To understand why Tanaghrisson did this, you have to set aside the idea that..." / "the contrast is the point" / "There is a last thing worth saying plainly" all step outside the story to coach the reader. The reference voice never does this. In each case, enter the point directly.

3. **Fort-name gazetteer in "The Forts and the Ultimatum" — compress.** Three fort names in sequence reads like a map legend. Use one vivid anchor (the seized trading post is strongest) and cut the others or fold them into a single gesture.

4. **The surrender document trap — slow down.** Washington signing "assassinat" without knowing it is the chapter's most consequential scene. It deserves the same pace given to Tanaghrisson's motives. Right now it's three sentences. Give it five and make the trap close on the reader.

## NICE-TO-HAVE

- The Ohio Company intro paragraph is strong but businesslike. One line on *what* the speculation would have looked like to a Virginia planter — what they were imagining when they pictured the Ohio — would add texture without length.
- The Section 5 Washington quote ("I heard Bulletts whistle") is great. Its placement just before the Fort Necessity section makes it feel like a chapter break. Consider whether a single sentence bridging his exhilaration to his imminent humiliation would sharpen the irony.
- The meanwhile block mentions Braddock's defeat as the setup. If the Braddock campaign has its own battle page, the meanwhile body could gesture toward Washington's survival there — it picks up his thread, which is a nice narrative echo of the chapter's opening.
- Tanaghrisson's "Tu n'es pas encore mort, mon père" — the draft hedges its sourcing correctly, but a parenthetical note on *whose* account it comes from (even just "by the French-derived account") would anchor the reader. Already partially handled in the text; consider slightly more specificity.
