# Storytelling Critic Gate — The Conquest of Canada
> Draft: `fi-conquest-draft.md` | Voice target: `improvising-a-war/page.tsx`

---

## Overall Grade: GOOD (trending STRONG in places)

This is a well-shaped climax chapter. The three-front structure is clear, the pacing is controlled, and the Sainte-Foy irony lands correctly. The draft does not collapse into tactical play-by-play — a real success for a chapter covering this much battle material. The Native counterweight section is the strongest piece in the draft; it earns its place and lands with genuine weight. The main story problems are: (1) the bombardment section tells the human cost rather than making the reader feel it, (2) Montcalm's fatal decision is explained but not dramatized, and (3) the opening paragraph over-explains the chapter's own architecture in a way that stalls the momentum.

---

## Section Grades

### Opening / Introduction — NEEDS WORK

**Rating: NEEDS WORK**

The first paragraph is good — "Annus Mirabilis" is a nice touch, the Penn-glen callback earns its place. But the second paragraph is a structural map written for an editor, not a reader:

> *"This is the chapter where New France dies. It does not re-tell each siege and battle in full; it links down to those. What it carries is the shape of the conquest…"*

This is the narrator talking about what the narrator is about to do. The house voice doesn't do this — see how "Improvising a War" opens by planting the reader in the moment, then earns its shape through prose. Cut or drastically compress the meta-description. If you need the structural contract, make it one sentence buried inside a forward-motion paragraph, not a self-describing list.

**MUST-IMPROVE.** The meta-narrator aside ("This is the chapter where New France dies…") is a house-voice violation. A reader on a phone does not want to be told what they are about to read; they want to be inside it.

### Pitt's Three Arrows — GOOD

**Rating: GOOD**

"Three arrows, one target: the narrow corridor of towns that was all of settled New France." — this closing line earns its spot and sets up the section well. The structural explanation of the three-front plan is clear without being dry. The detail about Pitt never leaving London is a good human hook.

Minor flag: *"He asked the American colonies for 20,000 troops; their governors delivered around 17,000"* — this is a good textured fact and should stay, but the transition into the three-arrow list is slightly mechanical. Consider giving it a beat of dramatic irony first: Pitt designs a perfect campaign from a London office, 4,000 miles from the ice.

### The Western Pillar Falls — STRONG

**Rating: STRONG**

This is the best-structured battle section in the draft. The Iroquois kinship-over-alliance decision is handled exactly right — it's given its own paragraph, framed as a deliberate Indigenous choice, not a backdrop to European maneuver. The "smoking ruins almost for free, five killed and thirty-one wounded... against the 2,000 casualties the British had suffered storming the same ground the year before" — this is the kind of number that does real work. Strong.

### The Long Summer Under the Guns — NEEDS WORK

**Rating: NEEDS WORK**

This section has the right information and the right structure. The problem is that the human cost is stated rather than felt.

> *"A parish priest in the city, Jean-Félix Recher (French), wrote that the gunfire and the bombardment terrorized the whole town, that the women, with their children, were continually in tears, wailing and praying."*

This is the one moment of eyewitness testimony in the entire bombardment section, and it is compressed into an indirect clause. Recher is your witness. Give him a sentence of his own in something closer to direct register. Compare the treatment of Wolfe's deathbed words (which the draft correctly marks as tradition, not record) — you give the dying general more breath than the priest who was actually there watching families pray under shells. Invert that priority.

The incendiary shell detail ("loaded pistol barrels, so that anyone trying to put out the fire risked the barrels firing in their faces") is genuinely good and should be foregrounded, not buried in a subordinate clause.

The countryside-burning paragraph is solid. *"The Wolfe-and-Montcalm story that history remembers is a duel of two generals; the people who actually paid that summer were the farmers whose manors were torched and the families who prayed under the shells."* — this closer is exactly right. But it comes after the section does too little to earn it. The section tells you to feel it; the prose hasn't yet made you.

**MUST-IMPROVE**: Give Recher's testimony more breath. The human cost of the bombardment is the stated thesis of the section; the evidence for it is a subordinate clause.

### A Battle Before Breakfast — GOOD

**Rating: GOOD**

The Plains of Abraham section is well-controlled. The draft correctly does not blow up the battle into a long tactical sequence — it stays at the level of decision and consequence. The cliff-climbing setup is efficient without being thin.

Montcalm's fatal decision is explained analytically but not dramatized:

> *"What he did next is generally judged his worst decision of the war. Around ten in the morning, rather than wait for reinforcements, Montcalm ordered a general advance."*

The analytical framing ("generally judged") is fine for a civ chapter; it's slightly flat for a climax battle. What was Montcalm's state of mind? Did he think Bougainville was coming? Did he fear the French garrison inside the walls would see the battle forming and break out in a disorganized mess, forcing the issue anyway? There's room for one sentence of the man's thinking before the decision lands. Right now it reads as a command, then a critique, with no human inside it.

The Wolfe deathbed scene is handled with exactly the right skepticism — "These words come down through later reconstructions, not a contemporary transcript" — without dismissing the tradition. This is the house voice working well. The same care for the Gray poem tradition is good.

*"Montcalm was shot as he rode back toward the city with the fleeing army."* — strong. The burial in the shell crater in the convent floor is an extraordinary image. Make slightly more of it: the man who died defending New France buried in a hole a British shell made.

One voice flag: *"By the traditional account, again from Parkman"* — the second "again from Parkman" is a meta-narrator tick. First use is fine; the second suggests the narrator is cataloguing their own sourcing methodology. Fold it differently.

No em-dash violations in this section.

### The Wrong Ships — STRONG

**Rating: STRONG**

This is the best section in the draft for irony. The Sainte-Foy reversal is told exactly right:

> *"The battle of Sainte-Foy had gone to France; the campaign had been decided not on the Plains of Abraham or at Sainte-Foy but out in the Atlantic, by the Royal Navy. The land battles were almost beside the point."*

This is the irony landing as a thesis, not a punchline. Good. The winter attrition of Murray's garrison — scurvy, cold, effective force cut in half — makes the French hope feel real, not just procedural.

One thing missing: the reader should feel the suspense of both armies watching the river for the first sails. The draft has *"Both armies watched the water for the first sails of spring"* — this is the right sentence but it's given a single line. One more beat here (what do you do, in a ruined city, in the ice, waiting for a sail that may or may not be the right flag?) would deepen the irony payoff when the British sails come.

No voice violations.

### Three Armies and a Surrender — GOOD

**Rating: GOOD**

The three-pronged Montreal convergence is handled efficiently. The detail of Amherst losing 84 men drowned in the Lachine rapids before the city — that's evocative and true to the casualty texture of this war. The Canadian militia simply going home to their farms as Murray advances is a great human note.

The burning of the regimental colors is the right image to close on for the military story. *"The standards of the regiments of New France went into the fire."* — strong final sentence for that beat.

The capitulation terms paragraph is necessary context but runs long for the climax section. The slavery protection clause is important (and correctly included) but might be better served by a tighter sentence rather than the current subordinate-clause structure.

**NICE-TO-HAVE**: The moment of Vaudreuil signing deserves one sentence of texture — what does it feel like to sign away a continent? Even a small human beat makes the surrender land instead of just resolve.

### The Counterweight Removed — STRONG

**Rating: STRONG**

This is the strongest section in the draft. The Native counterweight analysis is sophisticated and the framing is right — this is not a surrender, it is a pragmatic adaptation by nations watching one leverage point disappear. Nimwha's line is excellent and correctly sourced:

> *"the British thought themselves masters of the country because they had taken it from the French, who had no right to it in the first place, because it was the property of the Indians."*

Neolin / the Delaware Prophet is given appropriate weight. The Pontiac setup lands correctly as consequence, not coda.

The closing:

> *"The war in North America ended in 1760. The reckoning over who would actually hold the continent was only beginning."*

Perfect closing line. Leave it exactly as is.
