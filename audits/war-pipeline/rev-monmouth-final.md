title: Monmouth
date: June 28, 1778
place: Monmouth Court House (modern Freehold) and the farms west of it, New Jersey
note: France had come into the war, the British were giving up Philadelphia and marching across New Jersey to New York, and the army that had drilled all winter at Valley Forge went after their column. The vanguard (the forward attack force sent ahead to strike the enemy) was led by Charles Lee, who had argued against the whole idea, and when his attack came apart he ordered a retreat without telling Washington. Washington rode into the retreating troops, turned them around, and built a line that stood all afternoon in heat near 100 degrees, against the best regiments in the British Army. The battle ended a draw. It read, on the American side, like the proof of everything the winter had built. It was the last major battle in the north, and it cost the army its most experienced general.

sides:
- AMERICAN | force: The main Continental Army | strength: more than 14,000 (Lee's vanguard ~4,500; the main body ~7,800; Morgan's ~600–800 riflemen off the flank; plus militia) | cmd: Gen. George Washington | note: The army that came off the Valley Forge winter remade by Steuben's drill. Roughly half the Continentals (the full-time paid regulars, as opposed to part-time militia) were recruits enlisted since January, so what held the army together in the open field was the training, not veteran seasoning. - BRITISH | force: Clinton's column on the march | strength: about 10,000 of Clinton's best troops turned to fight (Cornwallis's first division and the rearguard); the whole column, with its 1,500-wagon train, ran 17,700 to 19,700, and most of it kept marching | cmd: Gen. casualties:
- American: Washington's return listed 69 killed, 161 wounded, and 132 to 140 missing, a reported total of 362 to 370. Many of the missing later came back, a share of them heat casualties, so real losses including the heat dead are usually put at about 450 to 500 and more. American heatstroke deaths ran to dozens.
- British: Clinton's return listed 65 killed, 59 dead "of fatigue" (heatstroke), 170 wounded, and 64 missing, a total around 358. But American burial parties reported interring about 250 British dead on the field, a count later revised to just over 300, far above the 124 killed and heat-dead Clinton admitted. Estimates that fold in the buried, the wounded, and the campaign's roughly 256 German deserters run as high as 1,100 to 1,200 British losses for the whole march.
- footnote: The British numbers are genuinely contested. Both armies' official returns came to about 360, but the American burial count alone exceeds the British admitted dead, and no single figure reconciles the two. What is not in dispute is that on both sides the heat killed men who never saw an enemy.

outcome:
- verdict: A tactical draw · the day Washington's command inside the army stopped being questioned
- text: Clinton accomplished his mission and Washington held the field, and both things were true at once. The Continental Army had stood in open ground against British regulars for a full day and finished it attacking, the first time this army ever ended a battle advancing, and the Valley Forge winter was made good in front of the whole country. Congress voted its thanks; the press celebrated; inside the army the talk of replacing Washington stopped. It was the last major battle between the two main armies in the north. The price was Charles Lee, the army's most experienced general, ruined by a retreat, a confrontation, and a court-martial that historians still argue about.

## SECTION: a-bridge-of-gold
EYEBROW: Monmouth · Why fight at all
TITLE: A bridge of gold

In the winter of 1778 the war stopped being only an American war. On February 6, France and the United States signed treaties of alliance and commerce, and the news that reached London turned the whole conflict on its head. Britain was now facing a global war, in the Caribbean, in home waters, in India, against the one navy that could rival its own, and it could no longer pour everything into crushing the rebellion. London's orders followed: abandon Philadelphia, the rebel capital the British had taken with such effort the autumn before, pull back and consolidate the army at New York, and send troops off to defend the West Indies and Florida. The man who had to carry it out was new to the job. Sir Henry Clinton (British) had just succeeded Sir William Howe as commander in chief in May, after Howe resigned, and his first major act was a retreat.

PILL: /war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/french-alliance | The French alliance: the diplomacy that changed the whole war

It was not a simple one. Clinton did not have enough ships to move the whole army, its horses, and thousands of Loyalist refugees (Americans loyal to the King, who could not stay behind once the British left) by sea. So he sent the refugees and the heavy equipment by water and marched the army itself overland across New Jersey, the long way around the bottom of the colonies toward New York. The last British troops left Philadelphia on June 18. What started up the roads was less an army than a slow-moving town: roughly 20,000 people in all, with a baggage train of about 1,500 wagons that stretched the column to some twelve miles. Clinton put it in two divisions, Lord Cornwallis (British) with the elite first division of about 10,000 fighting men, and the German general Wilhelm von Knyphausen (German, in British service) with the second, escorting the train.

The march was wretched, and New Jersey made it worse on purpose. A heat wave settled in, with temperatures again and again above 90 degrees; the roads were sandy; thunderstorms broke over the column; and the local militia (part-time citizen soldiers) felled trees across the road, burned the bridges, and spoiled the wells ahead of the advance. At points the army made barely six miles in a day. Desertion bled it the whole way: some 256 of the German auxiliaries slipped off during the campaign, many of them men who had married or put down roots in Philadelphia and would not leave.

All of this was an opening, and Washington meant to use it. George Washington (American), commander of the Continental Army (the rebels' full-time regular army, as opposed to the part-time militia), brought his army out of Valley Forge as Philadelphia emptied, crossed the Delaware upstream at Coryell's Ferry, and moved east through Hopewell, shadowing Clinton's column from the north with more than 12,000 Continentals and militia. The army he was marching was not the army that had stumbled out of Brandywine and Germantown the previous fall. It had spent the winter at Valley Forge being remade.

PILL: /war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/army-from-nothing | An army from nothing: the winter at Valley Forge that built the force at Monmouth

That winter is the thing braided through everything that happened at Monmouth, so it is worth a moment. The army had nearly come apart at Valley Forge, cold and hungry and unpaid. What it gained there was drill. A former Prussian officer named Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben (American, by then), whom Benjamin Franklin's recruiting had quietly promoted from captain to "lieutenant general" on paper, arrived in February, drilled a single model company by hand, and then cascaded one standardized system of marching, maneuver, and bayonet work through the entire army. About half the men in the ranks now were recruits who had enlisted only since January. There were no seasoned veterans to carry them; there was only the training. Monmouth would be the first major battle this retrained army fought, and the question hanging over it, though no one put it quite this way at the time, was whether a year-old army of farm boys could stand in an open field against the best regular infantry in the world.

Not everyone thought it should try. On June 24, at Hopewell, Washington put the question to a council of war (a formal meeting of his senior generals; eighteenth-century commanders often polled their officers before a major decision, though the commander was not bound by the vote): attack the British column, or shadow it and let it go? The loudest voice against attacking belonged to the army's most experienced soldier. Charles Lee (American), newly returned from sixteen months as a British prisoner and the senior major general under Washington, argued hard against bringing on a general action. The army, he said, was no match for the British in open battle; France was in the war now, which meant time was on the American side and there was no reason to risk the army on a gamble; the right course was to let Clinton march away, to build him, in the phrase of the day, a "bridge of gold" to cross by, making the retreat so uncontested that the British would simply march off without another battle, and only nip at the edges of his column. Lee reportedly called a general action "criminal."

Lee was not a coward and not a fool, and this is the place to be fair to him, because the page that follows will not be kind. He was English-born, a professional to his bones: a British regular in the last war, a soldier of fortune afterward in Portugal and Poland, an aide to a Polish king, a man who had settled in Virginia in 1773 and brought to the Continental Army more formal military experience than anyone in it, Washington included. His caution at Hopewell was a professional's caution. He genuinely doubted the new army, and he genuinely thought the alliance had made big risks pointless. His preferred approach, a war of harassment, hitting the British from cover and letting time and France do the work, had real strategic logic, and a real strand of modern scholarship thinks he was at least half right. The council compromised: a strong harassing detachment, no general action.

QUOTE: would have done honor to the most honorab[le] society of midwives, and to them only

The younger generals were furious. Nathanael Greene (American), Anthony Wayne (American), the Marquis de Lafayette (American), and Washington's aide Alexander Hamilton all pressed to strike the column anyway. Hamilton wrote afterward, with the contempt of a young man for his elders, that the cautious council "would have done honor to the most honorab[le] society of midwives, and to them only," and named Lee the prime mover of the timidity. Within two days Washington came around to the young men. He enlarged the advance force into a real vanguard, with orders to attack the British rear once it moved.

And here the command arrangement turned into something close to farce. Washington offered the vanguard, the forward striking force, then about 4,000 men sent ahead to hit the British rear while the main army followed, to Lee, as the senior officer's right. Lee turned it down: it was beneath his rank, he said, a detachment, not a wing. So Washington gave it to the twenty-year-old Lafayette instead. Then the force grew, to four and a half or five thousand men, with Wayne's and other detachments folded in, and at that size it was no longer beneath a major general's dignity. Lee changed his mind and claimed it back by seniority. Washington, awkwardly, let him, and on June 27, near Englishtown, Lee superseded Lafayette. So the man who would lead the attack on the morning of June 28 was the one general officer on record as opposing the attack in the first place.

MEANWHILE: Off Sandy Hook | The fleet that was almost there | The whole point of Clinton's march was to reach the ships at Sandy Hook and ferry the army across to New York before the new French alliance could put a fleet in his way. He was right to hurry. The Comte d'Estaing, a French admiral, was already crossing the Atlantic with twelve French ships of the line, France's first fleet sent to the new alliance, a force that could have caught Clinton's army strung out on the road or in mid-evacuation. It anchored off Sandy Hook on July 11, days after the last British troops were safely across. The margin between a difficult retreat and a catastrophe was a matter of days, and almost nobody marching across New Jersey that week knew it.

## SECTION: the-confrontation
EYEBROW: Monmouth · The retreat and the rally
TITLE: "Whence arises this disorder?"

Clinton's column began pulling out of its camp at Monmouth Court House toward Middletown in the small hours of June 28, somewhere between two and four in the morning. That was the moment to strike the rear, and Charles Lee (American), commanding the vanguard (the forward attack force) of about 4,500 men, moved out from Englishtown, a village a few miles west where the vanguard had camped, to do it, with George Washington (American) and the main body of some 7,800 following four to six miles behind. The lead detachments skirmished near a brook around eight o'clock, and the battle opened in heat that was already climbing toward 100 degrees.

It came apart almost at once. Lee found what he took for a British rearguard of fifteen hundred to two thousand men near the courthouse and improvised a plan to swing around and cut it off, Wayne pinning it in front while the rest looped to the left. He did not explain the plan to his own subordinate generals, which mattered, because the rearguard was not a rearguard. Warned that his column was being attacked, Clinton turned Cornwallis's entire first division, ten thousand of his best troops, around and brought them back. As the British strength swelled in front of them, American generals who had been told nothing began to react on their own. Scott and Maxwell (two of Lee's brigade commanders, told nothing of his plan), uninformed and afraid of being cut off, pulled their men back without orders. Lafayette's part of the line shifted. Wayne's force was suddenly hanging in the air. And Lee, his plan in pieces and his command seemingly unraveling on its own initiative, ordered a general retreat westward, back past the courthouse.

The retreat was not a rout, and this is the part the legend always forgets. The vanguard fell back about three miles in generally good order, in heat now near 100 degrees, and lost only about a dozen men doing it. An army that had not spent the winter under Steuben would have dissolved; this one withdrew like soldiers. Steuben's drill was visible even in the failure. Lee's real sins were of a different kind. He communicated no plan, organized no stand at the strong defensive ground of the ravine lines he was falling back through, and, the worst of it, sent no word back to Washington, who was at that moment marching the main body forward into what he still believed was a developing attack. At one farmhouse, told the main body was still two miles off, Lee said, by the trial record, "that he really did not know what to say."

What Washington knew, riding ahead of his column, was nothing. The first he learned of any retreat was when he began meeting his own soldiers walking the wrong way. For Lee, the next minutes were the worst a soldier can face: he had read the British strength correctly and built a retreat that did not dissolve, and now, with his plan in pieces and Cornwallis's ten thousand somewhere behind him, he had to meet his commander in chief with no plan communicated and no explanation that would serve. Washington rode on and found him with the tail of the column at a low knoll just west of the Middle Brook crossing. What happened there is the most famous moment of the war and the most worked-over, and it is worth being careful about exactly what the evidence is, because two very different versions have come down.

Here is the version the evidence supports, the one set down within weeks by the men who were close enough to hear. Before seven staff officers, Washington demanded an explanation. By the sworn court-martial testimony of his aide Tench Tilghman, the words were these.

QUOTE: I desire to know, sir, what is the reason — whence arises this disorder and confusion?

Lee, caught flat, could manage only "Sir? Sir?" before he found his footing and began to explain: contradictory intelligence, Scott's withdrawal made without orders, his own openly stated belief that fighting a general action here was a mistake. Washington's reply, as the witnesses pieced it together, was that whatever Lee's private opinion, he had expected his orders to be obeyed, and that he was sorry Lee had taken the command at all unless he meant to fight. The witnesses described considerable heat, real anger. Not one of them recorded a single oath.

That last point matters, because the other version, the one most people know, is that Washington swore at Lee with such force, in Charles Scott's much-quoted line, that "he swore on that day till the leaves shook on the trees," and called him a "damned poltroon." Those stories are almost certainly invented. They first surface forty-six to eighty years later, from men who were not there: Scott was half a mile away with his brigade, and his line was first printed in 1860; Lafayette's "poltroon" version comes from table talk on his old-age tour of America, decades after the fact, and his own 1779 memoir has no swearing in it at all. Against them stands the sworn testimony of seven officers within weeks, recording anger and no profanity, and one witness more decisive than any of them: Charles Lee himself, who spent the rest of his life itemizing every grievance against Washington and never once claimed he had been sworn at. What Lee did say, in his own defense, was that "the manner in which he expressed them was much stronger and more severe than the expressions themselves." Careful modern scholarship has come down hard on the side of the record: Washington was furious, and Washington was not profane.

And then, contrary to the way the scene is usually staged, Washington did not order Lee off the field. He gave him a choice: command the delaying action right here, or go back and organize the rear. Lee chose to stay and fight, and he fought a creditable rearguard action of about half an hour at the hedgerow (a brushy fence line) with the troops at hand, buying time while Washington galloped back to throw the main body into a line. Then Lee withdrew across the bridge as ordered, was sent to organize the reserve at Englishtown, and was relieved there a couple of hours later by Steuben. The cashiering everyone imagines, the general publicly stripped of his command in the middle of the battlefield, did not happen. What happened was worse for Lee, and slower: he would do it to himself, in writing, over the next two weeks.

MEANWHILE: The main body | The line going up behind him | While Washington was reining in his vanguard, the rest of the army was arriving, and the winter's drill was about to pay off in the open. Behind the ravines, Lord Stirling was wheeling the guns up onto Perrine Ridge, Greene's wing was extending to the right, and the guns of Henry Knox, the Continental Army's artillery commander, were unlimbering. The thirty minutes Lee bought at the hedgerow were the thirty minutes the main line needed. By the time the British came on in earnest, where an hour earlier there had been only a road full of retreating men, now there was a gun line.

## SECTION: the-stand
EYEBROW: Monmouth · The afternoon
TITLE: The army that held

The ground west of Monmouth Court House is cut by three wooded ravines, swamp-bottomed and choked with brush, the beds of Spotswood's brooks. They run across the line of march, which makes them miserable to attack over and excellent to fight behind, and George Washington (American) put his line behind them. On the left, on a rise called Perrine Ridge, William Alexander, Lord Stirling (American) massed the artillery and the infantry of the left wing. On the right stood the wing of Nathanael Greene (American). Forward of the line, covering the bridge over the Middle Brook, Anthony Wayne (American) held a brushy fence line called the hedgerow, with the Point of Woods beyond it. The Marquis de Lafayette (American) formed the second line. And across the southern ravine, on a height called Combs Hill, Greene planted Henry Knox's guns where they could fire down the whole length of any British line that tried to form, the single decision that would settle the afternoon.

Against this came the best infantry in the British Army, and they came hard. Lord Cornwallis (British), who commanded the British first division, pushed his elites straight at the new line: the light infantry (fast-moving skirmishers who fought in loose order ahead of the main line) and the 42nd Foot, the Black Watch (a famous Highland regiment), against the American left; the Guards (the army's household regiment, its most socially prestigious troops) and the grenadiers (historically the men who threw grenades, kept on as the biggest, most experienced heavy infantry in each regiment) against the hedgerow and the center. Wayne's forward men were driven out of the Point of Woods. The fight at the hedgerow was close and bloody. The push against Stirling's wing on the left ran into massed guns and steady infantry volleys and stalled. By about two in the afternoon the British assault had spent itself against the ridge, and the battle settled into the thing Monmouth is most remembered for after the legends: a long, pounding artillery duel.

For roughly two hours, better than a dozen guns on each side hammered across the open plain, about 1,200 yards of it (nearly three-quarters of a mile), between Perrine Ridge and the British positions near the hedgerow. By tradition it was the largest field-artillery exchange of the war, though that is the kind of superlative that gets attached to a lot of battles, so call it one of the largest. What is certain is that for all its noise it killed fewer men than the weather did. The guns and the sun were working in parallel: men loaded and fired in heat that was killing other men thirty yards off who had simply stopped moving, on both sides at once. Because the real killer that day was not the cannon. It was the heat.

It was somewhere in the mid-90s, with claims that it touched 100 degrees, and no shade and no rest. Men dropped in the ranks and on the march and died where they fell, the wool-coated British grenadiers worst of all. Clinton's own casualty return listed 59 men dead "of fatigue," meaning heatstroke, not a wound among them. About a third of the German troops were overcome. American heat deaths ran to dozens. "More men died of the heat than of the fighting" is the kind of line that sounds like exaggeration and, by many accounts of this particular day, simply was not.

It is into this cannonade, exactly here, that the most famous legend of the battle belongs, and it deserves to be told straight, because the truth of it is better than the myth. The story everyone knows is Molly Pitcher: a woman named Mary Hays carrying water to the gun crews in the heat, who took her fallen husband's place at his cannon when he went down and served it for the rest of the day, and was thanked by Washington himself afterward. Almost none of that can be supported. No source from the time names any "Molly Pitcher," and no source from the time names Mary Hays. The name appears in print only decades later, and it was attached to a real Pennsylvania woman, Mary Hays McCauley, around 1876, more than forty years after she died; her own 1832 obituary says nothing about a cannon, or water, or any such name. Scholars treat "Molly Pitcher" as a composite, a kind of folk shorthand for the many women who followed the army and worked at the edge of its battles.

But women were at the guns' edge at Monmouth, and the near-contemporary evidence for it is real and vivid, and that is the part worth keeping. A surgeon named Albigence Waldo wrote in his diary, days after the battle, of "one of the camp women" whose man was shot down beside her: "she immediately took up his gun and cartridges and like a Spartan heroine fought with astonishing bravery, discharging the piece with as much regularity as any soldier present." And the soldier Joseph Plumb Martin, in a memoir published long after, described an unnamed woman who attended with her husband at the piece the whole time, serving at a cannon when an enemy round shot passed clean between her legs, carrying away the lower part of her petticoat, which she looked at, by his account, with apparent unconcern. Neither man names her. One describes a musket, the other a cannon. What they document is not a single heroine with a nickname but something truer and stranger: that on this murderous afternoon, women were in the fire alongside the men, and at least one of them, when her man fell, picked up his weapon and kept fighting. The Pennsylvania legislature, decades later in 1822, did pension Mary Hays McCauley by a special act that pointedly changed the bill's reasoning from a soldier's-widow's relief to her own service during the war. That much is on the record. A specific woman at a specific gun, commended by Washington, is not.

PILL: /war-revolution/two-armies | 1777: the two British armies and the surrender that bought the alliance now playing out across New Jersey

The thing that finally broke the British attack came from Combs Hill. Around a quarter to four, Greene's guns up on that height opened on the flank of the British line, firing down its length, and there was no answer to it. Clinton had to pull his own artillery back and his infantry into whatever cover they could find. The American edge in the duel was now decisive, and with it the initiative changed hands. Clinton began to disengage, and Washington did something this army had never done at the end of a battle: he attacked. Colonel Joseph Cilley (one of the American commanders on the field) took about 350 picked men up a ravine and drove the 42nd Highlanders out of an orchard in a sharp stand-up fight. Wayne crossed the bridge with roughly 400 Pennsylvanians and tore into the rearmost grenadier battalion at the parsonage farm, where the British Lieutenant Colonel Henry Monckton (British), leading the grenadiers, was shot dead, the highest-ranking British officer to fall at Monmouth. When a fresh British regiment counterattacked, the Combs Hill guns covered Wayne's men as they pulled back. As the light went, the armies stood about a mile apart, and Washington's evening probes were halted by darkness before they made contact.

His troops slept on their arms on the ground they had held, and Washington meant to renew the battle at dawn. He did not get the chance. Around midnight Clinton's division marched, screened and silent, and by morning had rejoined Knyphausen's train near Middletown. The British reached the Sandy Hook area in the first days of July and were ferried across to New York, safe in the city before the French fleet ever arrived off the coast. Washington, with an exhausted army and bad ground ahead, declined to chase, and turned north toward the Hudson. The Philadelphia campaign was over, and so, though no one announced it, was the era of big set-piece battles between the two main armies in the north.

The American side chose to read the draw as a triumph, and not without cause: Clinton's own secretary admitted that "the Rebels stood much better than ever they did." What was not over, though, was the matter of Charles Lee. Two days after the battle, Lee wrote Washington an extraordinary letter, demanding to know what exactly he was accused of, blaming the "dirty earwigs" he imagined whispering around the commander, insisting his own maneuvers had actually saved the army, and accusing Washington of "an act of cruel injustice." Washington replied coldly that the letter's tone was highly improper and that Lee would have the inquiry he was asking for. Lee fired back demanding a court-martial, and Washington granted it by placing him under arrest, the formal procedure for suspending an officer from command while charges were heard, not a cell. The trial ran from July through August on three charges: disobeying orders by not attacking, misbehavior before the enemy in making "an unnecessary, disorderly, and shameful retreat," and disrespect to the commander in chief in those letters. The court convicted him on all three, but it half-flinched on the one that stung most: it struck the word "shameful," which carried the charge of cowardice, and allowed that the retreat had been disorderly only "in some few instances." The sentence was suspension from command for one year. Congress confirmed it that December by a narrow vote.

Historians have argued about that verdict ever since, and the honest answer is that it was probably the army choosing Washington more than it was clean justice. The historian John Shy put the politics of it plainly: "Under the circumstances, an acquittal on the first two charges would have been a vote of no-confidence in Washington." The trial record itself shows a retreat that was mostly orderly and may well have saved the vanguard; Lee's genuine failures were his silence toward Washington and his prickly, plan-it-myself conduct before the battle, not cowardice and not treason. Some modern scholars think the disobedience charge had real substance; others think the whole thing was a political conviction. What is not in doubt is what it did to Lee. He kept attacking Washington in print, called the official account of the battle "a most abominable damn'd lie," was wounded in a duel by one of Washington's aides over it, and in 1780 got himself thrown out of the army for good with a final insulting letter to Congress. He died in a Philadelphia inn in 1782, broke and bitter. His will asked that he not be buried in any church or churchyard; his friends buried him in Christ Church's yard in Philadelphia anyway, in an unmarked grave.

After Monmouth, no one inside the army seriously questioned Washington's command again. The draw on the field read, on the American side, like a victory, and worked like one: Congress voted its thanks "for the important victory of Monmouth over the British grand army," the press celebrated, and the year ended with "Father of his Country" current in print. The strategic truth was a wash. Clinton had saved his army and his wagons and gotten them to New York, exactly as he set out to do. But the army Steuben had drilled all winter had stood for a full day in open field against the best regiments Britain had, finished the day advancing, and slept on the ground it held. Half of those men were recruits who had enlisted only since January, carrying nothing onto the field but the training; what the drill produced was an army that did not just hold but ended the day moving forward. That was the thing the winter had been for, and at Monmouth, in front of the whole country, it was made good. Whether it settled anything about who would finally win the war, no one on either side that July yet knew.

MEANWHILE: Monmouth Battlefield today | The ground that kept the story | The core of the field is preserved now as Monmouth Battlefield State Park, in Manalapan and Freehold: Perrine Ridge, Combs Hill, the hedgerow line, the old farmhouses that stood through the fighting. Old Tennent Church, which was a field hospital that day, stands at its edge, and Henry Monckton lies in its cemetery. The 1884 battle monument rises at the courthouse in Freehold. Every year the battle is fought again in reenactment on the park's open ground, with, somewhere in the crowd, a woman carrying a pitcher and playing the part the country invented.
