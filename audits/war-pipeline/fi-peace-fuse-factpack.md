## 2. THE MILITARY GOVERNMENT OF CANADA (1760–1764)

### Capitulation and division of Canada
- Montreal surrendered **September 8, 1760**; French Governor-General the Marquis de Vaudreuil (Pierre de Rigaud de Vaudreuil-Cavagnal, French) signed the capitulation to British commander-in-chief General Jeffery Amherst (British).
  - Source: Wikipedia: Montreal campaign; Pontiac's War
- The former colony was immediately divided into **three districts**, each administered by a British military officer answerable to Amherst:
  - **Quebec District** — Brigadier General James Murray (British)
  - **Trois-Rivières District** — Brigadier General Ralph Burton (British)
  - **Montreal District** — Brigadier General Thomas Gage (British)
  - Source: Quebec History Encyclopedia (Marianopolis); Search result from canadahistory.com
- On **September 22, 1760**, Amherst decreed that existing militia officers would maintain order in parishes, serving as intermediaries between the British government and the French-Canadian population — effectively co-opting the pre-existing French administrative structure.
  - Source: Quebec History Encyclopedia (Marianopolis) via search

### Conditions for French Canadians
- Murray described the French Canadians as "a conquered yet dignified people — quiet, pliant, hard-working, and humble."
  - Source: canadahistory.com (citing Murray's own account)
- The military régime lasted roughly **four years** — the longest period of British military rule over a civilian population in Canadian history up to that point.
- On **November 7, 1763**, Britain formally proclaimed civil government for the Province of Quebec. It was inaugurated **in Canada on August 10, 1764**, when the three districts were united into a single province of Quebec.
  - Source: Quebec History Encyclopedia (Marianopolis)

### Key figures — note for author
- **Jeffery Amherst (British)** — Commander-in-Chief, British North America; Governor-General of British North America from September 1760. Recalled to Britain in **1763** following outbreak of Pontiac's War; replaced by **Major General Thomas Gage (British)**. Side-tag: (British).
- **James Murray (British)** — Military governor of Quebec District 1760; first civilian Governor of Canada from August 1764. Side-tag: (British).
- **Thomas Gage (British)** — Military governor of Montreal District; successor commander in North America. Side-tag: (British).
- **Marquis de Vaudreuil (French)** — Last Governor-General of New France. Side-tag: (French).

## 3. THE GLOBAL WAR CONTINUES: CARIBBEAN, INDIA, SPAIN (1761–1763)

### Spain enters the war
- France and Spain signed the **Third Family Compact** in **August 1761**, formalizing a Bourbon alliance against Britain.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Anglo-Spanish War (1762–1763)
- Britain declared war on Spain on **January 4, 1762**, after Spain placed an embargo on British trade, seized British goods, and expelled British merchants.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Anglo-Spanish War (1762–1763)

### Capture of Havana (Cuba)
- **June 6 to August 13, 1762**: British forces under Admiral Sir George Pocock and Major General George Keppel, 3rd Earl of Albemarle (both British), besieged and captured Havana — Spain's richest colonial port in the Caribbean.
- Force: approximately 51 warships and 12,000 troops.
- Havana was the crown jewel of Spanish Caribbean commerce; its fall shocked Spain.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Anglo-Spanish War (1762–1763); britishbattles.com: Capture of Havana

### Capture of Manila (Philippines)
- **September 24 – October 6, 1762**: A British fleet of 8 ships of the line, 3 frigates, and 4 store ships (6,839 regulars, sailors, and marines) sailed from Madras and captured Manila.
- British could not extend control beyond Manila and nearby Cavite.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Anglo-Spanish War (1762–1763); britishbattles.com: Capture of Manila

### India
- Britain returned French trading posts (factories) in India — including **Pondicherry** and **Chandernagore** — to France at the Treaty of Paris. Britain had effectively neutralized French power in India but did not annex all French holdings.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1763)

## 4. THE TREATY OF PARIS (FEBRUARY 10, 1763)

### Signing
- Signed **February 10, 1763**, in Paris.
- **Principal negotiators:**
  - Britain: **John Russell, 4th Duke of Bedford** (British)
  - France: **César Gabriel de Choiseul** (French, cousin of the duc de Choiseul, the foreign minister)
  - Spain: **Jerónimo Grimaldi, 1st Marquis of Grimaldi** (Spanish)
  - Source: Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1763)
- Also see: history.state.gov notes **Lord Bute** (British Prime Minister) overseeing British position.

### Key territorial transfers

**France → Britain:**
- All of **Canada** and its dependencies
- **Nova Scotia/Acadia**, Cape Breton Island
- All French territory **east of the Mississippi River** (eastern Louisiana / the Illinois Country)
- **Dominica, Grenada, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Tobago** (Caribbean islands)
- Mobile and Gulf Coast territories (east of Mississippi)
  - Source: Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1763); history.state.gov

**Spain → Britain:**
- **Florida** (in exchange for return of Havana)
  - Source: Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1763); Anglo-Spanish War

**Britain → France (returned):**
- **Guadeloupe, Martinique, Saint Lucia** (Caribbean sugar islands)
- French fishing stations: **Saint Pierre and Miquelon** (retained for fishing rights near Newfoundland)
- Indian trading posts: **Pondicherry, Chandernagore** and the Gorée trading station (West Africa)
  - Source: Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1763)

**France → Spain (SEPARATE SECRET TREATY):**
- **Western Louisiana** (west of the Mississippi) and **New Orleans** — transferred by the secret **Treaty of Fontainebleau**, signed **November 3, 1762**, THREE MONTHS before the Treaty of Paris. Spain did not take actual possession until 1769.
- The Fontainebleau treaty was not publicly revealed until **1764**.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Treaty of Fontainebleau (1762); 64parishes.org; kids.kiddle.co

### Direct treaty quote (Article VII)
> "The confines between the dominions of his Britannick Majesty and those of his Most Christian Majesty...shall be fixed irrevocably by a line drawn along the middle of the River Mississippi."
- Source: Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1763) — drawn from the treaty text itself

### Strategic result
- France was effectively **ejected from mainland North America** as a territorial power. The continent east of the Mississippi was now British. West of the Mississippi was nominally Spanish (by secret treaty).
- For the first time in over a century, no European great-power rival threatened British colonies from Canada or the Mississippi valley.
  - Source: history.state.gov: Treaty of Paris, 1763

## 5. THE CANADA vs. GUADELOUPE DEBATE

### The debate
- During 1760–1763, at least **65 pamphlets** were published debating whether Britain should keep **Canada** or **Guadeloupe** (and potentially Martinique) at the peace.
  - Source: Academia.edu / ResearchGate: "Canada or Guadeloupe?: French and British Perceptions of Empire, 1760–1763"
- **The core choice:** Canada = vast territory but costly to administer; no major export commodity. Guadeloupe = a small sugar island that "produced more sugar than all Britain's Caribbean islands combined" and generated approximately **£6 million annually**.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1763); search results
- Pamphlet count: 11 favored Canada, 6 favored Guadeloupe.
  - Source: Academia.edu / ResearchGate academic paper cited in search

### Benjamin Franklin's role
- Franklin wrote **"The Interest of Great Britain Considered"** (April 17, 1760), arguing forcefully for keeping Canada:
  > "In a Word, you must keep Canada, otherways you lay the Foundation of another War."
  - Source: Founders Online, National Archives: founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-09-02-0029
- ⚠️ NOTE FOR AUTHOR: Franklin had no side-tag (he is one of the marquee names). His pamphlet was influential but not the decisive factor; British strategic reasoning weighted security of existing colonies over sugar revenue.

### Why Britain chose Canada
- The elimination of the French threat to the existing Thirteen Colonies through retention of Canada was deemed more strategically valuable than acquiring another sugar island.
- France was content: Choiseul (French foreign minister) viewed Canada as an economic drain, preferring Caribbean sugar islands. He reportedly believed Canada's loss would ultimately destabilize Britain's control of the Thirteen Colonies — a calculation that proved correct.
  - Source: history.state.gov; Academia.edu paper; Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1763)

## 6. THE ROYAL PROCLAMATION OF 1763

### Issuance
- Issued by King George III on **October 7, 1763**.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Royal Proclamation of 1763

### What it did
- Forbade all new settlements **west of a line drawn along the Appalachian Mountains**, designating the territory as an Indian Reserve.
- The boundary: roughly followed the Eastern Continental Divide / St. Lawrence Divide — land with rivers flowing into the Atlantic went to colonists; land draining toward the Mississippi remained reserved for Native Americans.
- Created four new British colonies: **Quebec, East Florida, West Florida, and Grenada**.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Royal Proclamation of 1763

### Why it was issued — the dual rationale
1. **Pontiac's War** (ongoing at issuance): the uprising demonstrated the danger of unmanaged colonial encroachment on Native territory.
2. **Pre-war planning**: "The proclamation line had been conceived before the onset of Pontiac's Rebellion, but the outbreak of this conflict hastened the process of making it law."
   - Source: Wikipedia: Royal Proclamation of 1763

### Impact on colonists and land speculators
- Directly voided or blocked **land grants that had been promised** to French and Indian War veterans — including grants west of the line promised by Virginia's colonial government.
- The **Ohio Company of Virginia** had extensive plans to settle and profit from trans-Appalachian land; its wealthy backers "protested the line to the governor of Virginia."
  - Source: Wikipedia: Royal Proclamation of 1763; americanrevolution.org
- **George Washington** held or was promised lands west of the line. He received **20,000 acres** for French and Indian War service (⚠️ specific figure: Washington's Mount Vernon site cited this figure for his total veteran bounty; the search result notes he actively petitioned Virginia government to release tracts promised to war veterans).
  - Source: mountvernon.org: Proclamation Line of 1763; americanrevolution.org
- Washington privately dismissed the Proclamation as **"a temporary expedient to quiet the minds of the Indians"** that "must fall, of course, in a few years, especially when those Indians consent to our occupying those lands."
  - Source: Washington letter quoted at mountvernon.org and americanrevolution.org
- In a **September 1767 letter to William Crawford** (Pennsylvania surveyor), Washington privately urged Crawford "to keep the whole matter a secret, rather than give the alarm to others or allow himself to be censured for the opinion I have given in respect to the King's Proclamation." Washington was arranging to have Crawford appointed "Surveyor of the Soldiers Land" to quietly scout and claim western bounty lands.
  - Source: americanrevolution.org; loc.gov Washington Papers

### Path to Revolution
- The Proclamation became one of "the first significant areas of dispute between Britain and the Thirteen Colonies."
- It allied large landowners, ordinary veterans, and frontier settlers in resentment of British authority — a cross-class grievance.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Royal Proclamation of 1763; encyclopediavirginia.org

## 7. PONTIAC'S WAR (1763–1766)

### Pontiac — identity and background
- **Pontiac** (also spelled Obwaandi'eyaag in Odawa) — Odawa (Ottawa) war chief. Born circa **1714–1720**, likely near present-day Detroit or in the Ohio region. Parentage uncertain; tradition holds his mother was Chippewa and father Odawa.
- Contemporaries consistently identified him as **Odawa**. Modern historians reject older "mastermind conspiracy" portrayals — he was a regional leader whose early successes inspired broader tribal action rather than a supreme commander of a coordinated pan-Native operation.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac (Odawa leader)
- **Side-tag for author:** Pontiac is tagged **(Odawa)**.

### Causes — the systematic destruction of Native agency

#### 1. Amherst's policy reversal
- General Jeffery Amherst cut **gift-giving to Native nations in February 1761**. He viewed it as bribery rather than as the essential diplomatic tribute it was: permission to occupy Native land in exchange for reciprocal relationship.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; mountvernon.org: Pontiac's Rebellion
- Amherst also **restricted ammunition and gunpowder sales** to Native peoples, fearing they would stockpile weapons for future uprisings.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; Lumen Learning US History I
- Amherst stationing only **~500 troops** across the conflict region despite commanding 8,000 total — he believed Indigenous peoples "incapable of concerted action."
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War

#### 2. Neolin the Delaware Prophet
- **Neolin** (meaning "the enlightened" in Algonquian), a Delaware (Lenape) religious prophet from the village of Muskingum in Ohio. Born c. 1740.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Neolin
- In **1761**, Neolin claimed to have been visited by the **Master of Life** (Keesh-she'-la-mil'-lang-up, "being that thought us into being") during a vision during fasting and dreaming.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Neolin; nativeamericannetroots.net
- His message: Native Americans must reject European goods, alcohol, and ways and return to traditional life. The Master of Life told him that Native peoples' greatest offense was tolerating European settlers on their lands; he promised to restore game and prosperity if they rejected "further European incursions."
  - Source: Wikipedia: Neolin; teachingamericanhistory.org
- In **1762**, Neolin claimed to receive a prayer from the Master of Life to be said every morning and evening, spreading his movement further.
- Pontiac adopted Neolin's message as a rallying framework, using it to unite warriors from "nearly twenty tribes from Lake Ontario to the Mississippi, including the Ojibwa, Ottawa, Potawatomi, Seneca, Huron, Miami, Shawnee, and Delaware."
  - Source: Wikipedia: Neolin; Lumen Learning US History I

#### 3. Loss of French counterweight
- With France defeated, Native nations lost the diplomatic counterweight they had used to play French and British interests against each other. The British now had no rival to restrain their behavior.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; Lumen Learning US History I

### Pontiac's Detroit council and opening moves
- On **April 27, 1763**, Pontiac held a council below Fort Detroit and urged followers to attack, contrasting French and British approaches: *"The French familiarized themselves with us, Studied our Tongue, and Manners...treated with Respect Our Kings"* versus British arrogance and exploitation.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac (Odawa leader)
- On **May 7, 1763**, Pontiac attempted a surprise seizure of Fort Detroit by approaching under the pretext of a diplomatic visit, planning to smuggle weapons under blankets. The plan was discovered; instead, Pontiac besieged the fort.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; Lumen Learning US History I

- By late June 1763, only **three major British forts remained**: Fort Niagara, Fort Detroit, and Fort Pitt.
  - Source: Lumen Learning US History I; Wikipedia: Pontiac's War

### Siege of Fort Detroit
- **May 7 – October 31, 1763**: Pontiac besieged Fort Detroit for nearly **six months** with up to **900 warriors** from multiple tribes.
- The fort held, commanded by Major Henry Gladwin (British).
- Pontiac eventually abandoned the siege and withdrew in October 1763 after receiving word that French reinforcements would not be coming.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War

### Siege of Fort Pitt and the smallpox episode

**The siege:**
- Delaware and allied warriors attacked and besieged **Fort Pitt** beginning approximately **June 22, 1763**; the siege continued through August 20.
- Nearly **550 people** crowded inside the fort, including more than **200 women and children**.
- Captain **Simeon Ecuyer** (Swiss-born British officer, British service), commanding officer, wrote: *"We are so crowded in the fort that I fear disease... the smallpox is among us."*
  - Source: Wikipedia: Siege of Fort Pitt; pastmedicalhistory.co.uk

**The Fort Pitt blanket incident (June 24, 1763):**

This is a documented event with a clear primary-source paper trail. The facts:

1. During a **parley at Fort Pitt on June 24, 1763**, Captain Ecuyer gave representatives of the besieging Delaware two blankets and a handkerchief that had been exposed to smallpox.

2. Militia commander **William Trent** (British colonial militia) recorded in his journal:
   > "we gave them two Blankets and an Handkerchief out of the Small Pox Hospital. I hope it will have the desired effect."
   - Source: Wikipedia: Siege of Fort Pitt; pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1126655/ (British biological warfare, PMC article)

3. This action was taken **by Ecuyer and Trent at Fort Pitt without prior orders from Amherst or Bouquet** — it preceded the Amherst-Bouquet correspondence on the topic.
   - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; colonialwilliamsburg.org research

**The Amherst-Bouquet correspondence:**

The timeline of letters is:

- **~July 7, 1763**: Amherst wrote to Colonel Henry Bouquet (British, Swiss-born):
  > "Could it not be contrived to send the Small Pox among those disaffected tribes of Indians? We must, on this occasion, Use Every Strategem in our power to Reduce them."
  - ⚠️ SOURCE NOTE: The exact date of this specific letter is variously given as "about June 29" and "July 7" across sources. The umass.edu analysis notes that historian Francis Parkman quoted this line, but confirms the letter itself is in the British Manuscript Project microfilms held at the Library of Congress (approximately 300 reels of Amherst papers). The PMC/NIH article gives the wording as above.

- **July 13, 1763**: Bouquet replied to Amherst, writing in a postscript:
  > "I will try to inoculate the [Indians] with Some Blankets that may fall in their Hands, and take Care not to get the disease myself."
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; umass.edu Amherst smallpox page; PMC article

- **July 16, 1763**: Amherst endorsed the plan:
  > "You will do well to try to inoculate the Indians by means of blankets, as well as to try every other method that can serve to extirpate this execrable race."
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; PMC/NIH article; explorepahistory.com

**Captain Ecuyer's own record (June 24, 1763):**
> "Out of regard for them we gave them two blankets and a handkerchief out of the Smallpox hospital."
- Source: PMC/NIH article (British biological warfare)

**What is and is NOT documented:**
- DOCUMENTED: Intent to use smallpox as a weapon — multiple letters and Trent's journal entry confirm this unambiguously.
- DOCUMENTED: The actual distribution of two blankets and a handkerchief from the smallpox hospital at Fort Pitt on June 24, 1763.
- CONTESTED ⚠️: Whether the attempt caused significant Native deaths. Historians disagree:
  - Ranlet (2000) argued the Delaware chiefs who handled the blankets were in good health a month later — "a total failure."
  - Peckham (1947), Jennings (1988), and Nester (2000) concluded the attempt successfully spread smallpox, causing numerous Native deaths and hampering the war effort.
  - Smallpox was spreading in the region during this period; attribution of any specific outbreak is difficult.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; Wikipedia: Siege of Fort Pitt

### Battle of Bushy Run
- **August 5–6, 1763**: Colonel Henry Bouquet (British) led a relief force of ~500 British regulars (Highland regiments — 42nd, 77th — and 60th Royal Americans) westward from Fort Ligonier toward Fort Pitt.
- A combined force of Delaware, Shawnee, Mingo, and Huron warriors ambushed the column at **Bushy Run** (western Pennsylvania).
- Bouquet's forces were outnumbered and initially pinned down on August 5; on August 6, Bouquet feigned a retreat, drawing the warriors into a prepared trap and routing them.
- **British casualties:** 50 killed (including 29 of the 42nd Highlanders, 7 of the 60th Royal Americans, 6 of the 77th Highlanders, 8 civilians/volunteers), plus ~60 wounded.
- **Native casualties:** approximately 60 killed, including two prominent Delaware chiefs (estimates by contemporaries).
- Bouquet relieved Fort Pitt on **August 20, 1763**, ending the siege.
- The victory was celebrated in Philadelphia with church bells ringing through the night; King George praised Bouquet.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Battle of Bushy Run; army.mil article

### Peace and outcome
- **July 25, 1766**: Pontiac made a formal treaty with British Indian Superintendent **Sir William Johnson** (British) at **Fort Ontario** (Oswego, New York).
- Terms: no land cessions, no prisoner returns, no hostages. Pontiac received a pardon.
- Described by historians as "hardly a surrender."
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; americanhistorycentral.com
- **Pontiac's assassination**: On **April 20, 1769**, a Peoria warrior assassinated Pontiac near **Cahokia, Illinois**, likely avenging a chief named Makachinga whom Pontiac had wounded in 1766.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac (Odawa leader)

### Casualty overview — Pontiac's War (full conflict)

| Category | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| British soldiers killed in action | ~400 | Wikipedia: Pontiac's War |
| British soldiers captured and tortured to death | ~50 | Wikipedia: Pontiac's War |
| Colonial civilians killed | ~450 | Wikipedia: Pontiac's War |
| Settlers displaced (Pennsylvania and Virginia) | ~4,000 | Wikipedia: Pontiac's War |
| Native warriors killed | 200+ (minimum; civilian casualties largely unrecorded) | Wikipedia: Pontiac's War |

### Strategic outcome and its significance for Native peoples
- Modern historians characterize Pontiac's War as a **military stalemate**: Native nations failed to expel the British, but the British could not conquer them militarily either.
- The uprising forced Britain to abandon Amherst's contemptuous policies and revert to French-style alliance diplomacy.
- Amherst was recalled in 1763 and replaced by Thomas Gage — his approach was effectively repudiated.
- The Royal Proclamation of 1763, however intended, was the **first British recognition of Indigenous territorial rights** in North America — a double-edged legacy, since it was also routinely ignored by colonists and eventually nullified by the Revolution.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Pontiac's War; Royal Proclamation of 1763

### The garrison decision
- Grenville's government decided to station **10,000 troops** permanently in North America to defend the new territories.
- Maintaining this garrison cost an estimated **£200,000–£225,000 per year**.
  - Source: NPS: Sugar and Stamp Acts; americanrevolution.org: Sugar Act
  - Note: NPS gives £200,000; americanrevolution.org gives £225,000. Both are plausible for different years/accounting methods; the author can use "roughly £200,000 per year."

### The Sugar Act (April 5, 1764)

| Field | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Official name** | American Revenue Act of 1764 | NPS; americanrevolution.org |
| **Date enacted** | April 5, 1764 | NPS; Wikipedia: Sugar Act |
| **Date effective** | September 29, 1764 | NPS |
| **What it taxed** | Molasses (cut from 6d to 3d/gallon), plus wine, coffee, textiles; banned direct lumber/iron export to Europe | Wikipedia: Sugar Act; NPS |
| **Purpose stated** | "just and necessary that a revenue should be raised...for defraying the expenses of defending, protecting, and securing" the colonies | Preamble of the act; Wikipedia: Sugar Act |
| **Key figure** | George Grenville (Prime Minister, Chancellor of the Exchequer) — introduced the act | Wikipedia: Sugar Act; NPS |

- **First time** Parliament explicitly aimed to raise revenue (rather than merely regulate trade) from the colonies.
- Colonial reaction: New England merchants and ports protested; Samuel Adams denounced it as violating colonial rights.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Sugar Act; NPS
- The Board of Trade estimated colonial smuggling at approximately **£700,000 in merchandise annually** — Grenville's justification that colonists should contribute to their own defense.
  - Source: NPS: Sugar and Stamp Acts

### The Stamp Act (March 22, 1765)

| Field | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Date royal assent** | March 22, 1765 | Wikipedia: Stamp Act 1765 |
| **Date effective** | November 1, 1765 | Wikipedia: Stamp Act 1765 |
| **Date repealed** | March 18, 1766 | Wikipedia: Stamp Act 1765 |
| **What it taxed** | Legal documents, newspapers, playing cards, dice, magazines, licenses, land grants; payment in hard currency only | Wikipedia: Stamp Act 1765; NPS |
| **Tax range** | 1 penny (single-sheet newspapers) to £10 (attorney licenses) | Wikipedia: Stamp Act 1765 |
| **Key figure** | George Grenville — designed act to address post-war debt; first direct parliamentary tax on colonies | Wikipedia; NPS |

- Grenville's rationale: rather than raise taxes in Britain (where the **1763 cider tax** had sparked violent protests), Parliament would tax the American colonies directly for the first time.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Stamp Act 1765
- **"No taxation without representation"** — the rallying cry. Colonists had no seats in Parliament.
- In Boston, **Andrew Oliver** (stamp distributor) was hanged in effigy on **August 14, 1765**.
- Mobs destroyed **Lieutenant Governor Thomas Hutchinson's** mansion on **August 26, 1765**.
- Twelve stamp distributors had resigned by November 16.
- Colonial lawyer **James Otis** (Boston): *"Taxes are not to be laid on the people, but by their consent in person, or by deputation."*
  - Source: Wikipedia: Stamp Act 1765; NPS

### Stamp Act Congress
- October 1765: representatives from **nine colonies** convened the Stamp Act Congress in New York City, issuing petitions denying Parliament's authority to tax the colonies.
  - Source: Wikipedia: Stamp Act 1765; Lumen Learning

### Currency Act (April 1764)
- Mandated payment in gold and silver rather than colonial paper money, compounding economic hardship alongside the Sugar Act.
  - Source: NPS: Sugar and Stamp Acts

## 9. REGULARS VS. PROVINCIALS — THE INSTITUTIONAL RESENTMENT

(Brief — this has its own theme page)

- Throughout the war, British regular officers were **contemptuous** of colonial (provincial) troops, viewing them as "undisciplined and unruly, little more than a rabble."
  - Source: warhistory.org; encyclopedia.com: British Army in North America
- British officers with royal commissions routinely placed **even their most junior officers above more senior provincial militia officers** in the chain of command — a standing insult.
- George Washington was personally humiliated by this: during the 1755 Braddock expedition, he became an **unpaid volunteer aide-de-camp** rather than assume his militia rank and be subordinated to junior British regulars.
  - Source: encyclopedia.com; warhistory.org
- Colonists in turn noted the incompetence of British commanders like **Major General Edward Braddock** and **Major General James Abercrombie** (both British), whose armies were routed — and drew the lesson that British regulars were poorly suited to frontier warfare.
- This mutual contempt fed colonial self-confidence and resentment simultaneously — both ingredients of revolutionary politics.
  - Source: warhistory.org; encyclopedia.com

## 10. FORWARD THREAD — THE FUSE TO THE AMERICAN REVOLUTION

The war that made Britain supreme in North America planted three causes that would cost it the Thirteen Colonies:

1. **Debt and taxation**: The war doubled Britain's national debt; the decision to garrison North America permanently and tax the colonies to pay for it (Sugar Act 1764, Stamp Act 1765) produced the first organized pan-colonial resistance and the slogan "No taxation without representation."

2. **The Proclamation Line**: By blocking colonial westward expansion, Britain antagonized the very veterans and land speculators who had fought and financed the war — men like Washington who now had personal grievances as well as political ones.

3. **Pontiac's War**: The uprising revealed how untenable British control of the interior was without French-style diplomacy — and demonstrated to colonists that Britain was capable of adopting policies (the Proclamation, continued garrison costs) that served imperial convenience at colonial expense.

- France's foreign minister Choiseul's calculation — that losing Canada would destabilize Britain's hold on the Thirteen Colonies by removing the French military threat that bound the colonies to Britain — proved accurate within a generation.
  - Source: history.state.gov; Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1763)

The American Revolution began in 1775 — twelve years after the Treaty of Paris. The fuse was the debt.

## Revision additions (verified) — round 2

### B. Declaratory Act — March 18, 1766

**Date confirmed:** March 18, 1766 — the exact same day Parliament passed the repeal of the Stamp Act.

**Key wording confirmed:** The Act asserted Parliament's authority to make laws binding the colonies "in all cases whatsoever." Full title: *American Colonies Act 1766* (Declaratory Act).

**Significance:** Parliament used the declaration to avoid appearing humiliated by the repeal. Colonial leaders initially paid it little attention — the repeal was the headline — but the phrase "in all cases whatsoever" was the constitutional time-bomb: it flatly rejected the colonial argument that Parliament could not tax without representation, and it hardened the impasse that would play out through the Townshend Acts (1767) and beyond.

**Safe draft language:** "On March 18, 1766 — the same day it repealed the Stamp Act — Parliament passed the Declaratory Act, asserting its authority to bind the colonies 'in all cases whatsoever.' The colonies celebrated the repeal and barely noticed the declaration. They would remember it later."

- American Battlefield Trust: "Declaratory Act" primary source (battlefields.org)
- History.com: "Parliament repeals the Stamp Act, March 18, 1766" (same-day passage confirmed)

### C. Currency Act (1764)

**Date confirmed:** Passed by Parliament April 19, 1764; took effect September 1, 1764.

**Substance confirmed:** Prohibited all colonies from issuing paper money as legal tender and from extending the retirement period of outstanding paper currency. Part of George Grenville's broader program to tighten Britain's fiscal control over the colonies and raise revenue to pay for the Seven Years' War debt and the new standing army in North America. Combined with the Sugar Act (also 1764), it cut off both the specie supply (via West Indies trade) and the paper money supply simultaneously, producing a genuine currency squeeze.

**Safe draft language:** "The Currency Act of 1764 banned the colonies from issuing paper money as legal tender — enacted April 19, 1764, taking effect September 1. Part of Grenville's squeeze, it came just as the Sugar Act was choking off the West Indies trade that had been the colonies' main source of hard coin."

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: "Currency Act" (date April 19 / September 1, 1764; substance)
- Britannica: "Currency Act, Great Britain [1764]"
- americanhistorycentral.com: "Currency Act, Summary, Facts, Significance"

**The draft phrase "conquered yet dignified... quiet, pliant, hard-working, and humble" is UNCONFIRMED and should not be used.** No source found attributing those specific words to Murray.

**What the Dictionary of Canadian Biography (DCB) actually gives (verbatim):**
