# FACT PACK — Battle of Bunker Hill, June 17, 1775 (rev-bunker-hill, size L)

*American Revolution battle page. Goes DEEPER than the verified spine baseline in `rev-outbreak-factpack.md` §5 — never contradicts it. Where the spine carries a range, that range governs here. HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags: the "whites of their eyes" legend; the wrong-hill question's honest murkiness; the Putnam/Prescott command dispute (present, don't resolve); Warren without martyr-kitsch; the British courage in the assaults respected — no faceless-redcoat waves.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **June 17, 1775**, on the **Charlestown peninsula** across the harbor from Boston — almost entirely on **Breed's Hill** (~42.3764,-71.0608), the lower hill closer to Boston, NOT Bunker Hill proper. The battle is named for the hill the Americans were *ordered* to fortify, not the one they fortified. Two months after Lexington and Concord, eight weeks into the Siege of Boston. State MA.
- Significance: the bloodiest single battle of the entire Revolutionary War for the British army. Britain **took the hill** — a tactical victory, and the dossier's win marker is theirs — at a cost (**1,054 casualties, 226 killed**) that horrified London, made Gage's recall certain, taught Howe a caution he arguably never shed, and convinced both sides this would be a real war. Clinton, that evening: "A dear bought victory, another such would have ruined us." (HIGH)
- Duration: naval cannonade from ~4 a.m.; the infantry battle ran roughly **3 p.m. to 5 p.m. — about two hours of combat** for three assaults. (HIGH on the shape; clock times approximate.)

## 2. BACKGROUND — THE INTELLIGENCE RACE
- After April 19 a New England militia army (~15,000–20,000, fluctuating) penned Gage's garrison (~6,000–6,500 after the May 25 reinforcement) inside Boston. Two sets of unoccupied heights commanded the town: **Dorchester Heights** (south) and the **Charlestown peninsula** (north). Whoever fortified them first would dominate the harbor. (HIGH)
- **The British plan:** Gage — pushed by the three newly arrived major generals Howe, Clinton, and Burgoyne (off HMS *Cerberus*, May 25) — finalized a plan **on June 12** to break out: seize Dorchester Neck and fortify Dorchester Heights first, march on Roxbury, then take the Charlestown heights. Execution set for **Sunday, June 18**. (HIGH — NPS, standard accounts.)
- **The leak:** the plan traveled fast. On **June 13** the **New Hampshire Committee of Safety** (Meshech Weare) passed word to the Massachusetts committee that a New Hampshire gentleman "of undoubted veracity" had overheard British officers discussing plans to seize Dorchester and Charlestown. On **June 15** the Massachusetts Committee of Safety voted that "the hill called Bunker's Hill in Charlestown be kept and defended." The besiegers would move first — by three days. (HIGH on the chain; the informant's identity unknown.)
- Gen. **Artemas Ward** (commanding at Cambridge) gave the order; the fortifying column assembled on Cambridge Common the evening of June 16 — prayed over by President Langdon of Harvard — and marched after dark. (HIGH)

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
- **American** (no clean command structure — the central fact and the later dispute): **Col. William Prescott** of Pepperell led the fortifying force, ~1,200 men (his own, Frye's, and Bridge's Massachusetts regiments, plus ~200 Connecticut men under Capt. **Thomas Knowlton** from Putnam's regiment), with **Col. Richard Gridley**, the army's chief engineer, to lay out the works. Reinforcements during the day — Col. **John Stark's** large 1st New Hampshire and Col. James Reed's New Hampshire regiment at the rail fence, assorted Massachusetts companies — brought total engaged to **~2,400–3,000 across the day**, men rotating in and out, many never crossing the shot-swept Neck. Brig. Gen. **Israel Putnam** (Connecticut) was the senior officer on the field but exercised no clear command over the Massachusetts or New Hampshire troops (see §8). Maj. Gen. **Joseph Warren** and Brig. Gen. **Seth Pomeroy** both came as volunteers and refused command. (HIGH; engaged strength as the range.)
- **British:** Maj. Gen. **William Howe** in field command (Brig. Gen. **Robert Pigot** leading the left wing against the redoubt), under Gage's overall direction, with Clinton and Burgoyne at the Copp's Hill battery; Clinton crossed late on his own initiative to rally the reformed third assault. Force: ~1,500 in the first lift, **~2,200–3,000 engaged across the day** with reinforcements (5th, 38th, 43rd, 47th, 52nd Foot, the grenadier and light-infantry companies, 1st and 2nd Marines — Maj. John Pitcairn of Lexington fame mortally wounded at the redoubt). Royal Navy + shore batteries in support. (HIGH; ranges.)

### Commander bio-card material (verified)
- **William Prescott** (1726–1795, Pepperell MA; veteran of the 1745 Louisbourg expedition): commanded in the redoubt all day. At dawn, with the warships firing and his exhausted men flinching, he **mounted the parapet and walked it deliberately**, conspicuous in a light-colored **banyan** (a loose linen gown) over his waistcoat, giving directions, to show the cannonballs could be ignored. In the final melee he parried bayonets with his sword and got out — his banyan and waistcoat pierced through, he untouched. **No authentic life portrait of him exists**; the 1881 Story statue at the Monument was modeled with help from a photograph of his grandson, historian William Hickling Prescott. (HIGH; banyan well-attested in 19th-c. family/veteran accounts — MED-HIGH on the costume details.)
- **Joseph Warren** (1741–1775): Boston physician, president of the Massachusetts Provincial Congress, the patriot movement's operating chief since April. Commissioned a **major general by the Provincial Congress on June 14** — but the commission not yet formalized/acted on. On June 17 he asked Putnam where the fighting would be heaviest, went to the redoubt, and when **both Putnam and Prescott offered him command, he refused, saying he came to serve as a volunteer** — and took a musket in the ranks. Killed in the final moments (§4). (HIGH — NPS, Britannica, ABT all carry the refusal precisely this way.)
- **William Howe** (1729–1814): led the assaults **in person, on foot, in the front line** — by the end of the day **every member of his personal staff had been killed or wounded** (his servant carrying wine among them, by tradition). Watching his line stagger back he wrote that "there was a moment that I never felt before." Many historians read Bunker Hill as the trauma behind his later caution — at Brooklyn Heights in 1776 he declined to storm fortified lines he might have carried. Present the caution thesis as **interpretation (MED), widely held but not provable**. (HIGH on personal lead + staff; quote HIGH as text, recipient varies in citations — see §7.)
- **John Stark** (1728–1822, NH; veteran of Rogers' Rangers): arrived with his big New Hampshire regiment at midday, walked his men across the shot-swept Neck at a deliberate pace, and took the open left flank — the rail fence and, crucially, the **Mystic River beach below it**, where he had a **stone wall thrown across the strand** and (by tradition) drove a stake ~40 yards out as a firing mark. His men's volleys annihilated the British light-infantry column on the beach (§4). His recollection: the dead "lay as thick as sheep in a fold." (HIGH on position/result; stake + quote from later recollection — MED.)
- **Israel Putnam** (1718–1790, CT; celebrated F&I veteran): the genuinely murky figure. Senior officer present; rode between Bunker and Breed's Hills directing reinforcements and trying to entrench Bunker Hill proper as a fallback; declined Prescott's offer of command in the redoubt ("he had business elsewhere" in tradition). Whether he was the battle's commander, its energetic odd-job man, or an obstruction became a **19th-century memory war** (§8). Honest card: state what he verifiably did, then name the dispute. (HIGH on the facts listed; everything beyond them contested.)

## 4. THE BATTLE
- **The night dig — and the wrong hill.** Prescott's ~1,200 crossed the Neck after dark on **June 16** with orders to fortify **Bunker Hill** (the higher, safer hill commanding the Neck). On the ground, after a debated council — tradition puts Prescott, Gridley, and Putnam in it — the work went up on **Breed's Hill** instead: lower (~62 ft vs ~110 ft), well forward, within cannon range of Boston. **Why is genuinely unsettled**: no participant left a clear account; the Committee of Safety's own report said the position was taken "by some mistake"; the most common modern reading is that it was deliberate provocation — a fort there *forced* a British response. Present as unresolved (MED), per the spine. Gridley staked the lines around midnight; the men dug in silence, hearing Boston's church bells and the harbor watch calling "All's well." By dawn they had a **redoubt roughly 130 feet on a side** (Peter Brown called it "ten rod long, and eight wide"), walls ~6 feet, with a breastwork running ~100 yards down the northern slope. (HIGH on the dig; dimensions as stated with the Brown variance; the why-Breed's question MED — leave it loose.)
- **Dawn — the ships open fire.** About 4 a.m. a watch officer (HMS *Lively* by most accounts) spotted the works and opened fire; the shore batteries and other ships joined. The cannonade's first kill was **Asa Pollard** of Billerica, **decapitated by a cannonball** while working outside the redoubt. Men crowded around the body; Prescott, fearing panic, **ordered him buried immediately, without ceremony** — and when a minister insisted on prayers, accounts have Prescott dispersing the crowd back to work (a chaplain said words over the grave anyway). It was after this that Prescott took his walk on the parapet. (HIGH on Pollard/burial — NPS, Boston 1775; which ship fired the killing shot varies, *Lively* vs *Somerset* — don't pin it.)
- **The British council — four generals, one room.** Gage convened Howe, Clinton, and Burgoyne at Province House that morning. **Clinton urged a landing at the Neck behind the rebels** to trap the whole force on the peninsula; he was overruled — the others would not put a force between two enemy bodies, and the shallow Mystic flats and militia in Cambridge argued against it. The decision: Howe would cross and take the position by **direct assault** — partly contempt for militia, partly the era's conviction that regulars beat irregulars in the open. The honest beat: Clinton was right, and the choice bought the carnage. (HIGH on the council and Clinton's rejected plan.)
- **The landings.** From ~1 p.m. the longboats put Howe's first ~1,500 ashore at **Moulton's Point**, the peninsula's eastern corner — unopposed, out of musket range. Seeing the line now extended by the rail fence and fresh men streaming over the Neck (Stark's and Reed's New Hampshire regiments among them), Howe paused, sent for reinforcements, and let his men eat while the navy worked. The pause gave the Americans two more hours to set the left flank: Knowlton's Connecticut men at a **rail fence** stuffed with hay, Stark extending it and walling the **beach** below. (HIGH)
- **First assault (~3 p.m.) — the beach is the hinge.** Howe's plan was an envelopment: Pigot to demonstrate against the redoubt while the grenadiers struck the rail fence and the **light infantry column charged up the narrow Mystic beach to turn the whole line**. The beach — often the untold key — was about as wide as a wagon; the column had no room to deploy. Stark's men held fire to ~50 paces, then delivered rotating volleys that wrecked the head of the column (the Welch Fusiliers' light company leading); the survivors recoiled. **96 British dead were counted on the beach** afterward. With the flanking move dead, the grenadiers' frontal push at the rail fence and Pigot's at the redoubt were shot flat — the attackers struggling uphill through thick unmown grass, over fences and brick kilns, in ~100°-feeling wool, while the defenders (many resting their muskets on the fence rails) fired low on order. (HIGH; the 96 from contemporary counts — MED-HIGH.)
- **Charlestown burns mid-battle.** Taking sniper fire from the village on Pigot's flank, the British fired **Charlestown with heated shot and carcasses (incendiary shells) from Copp's Hill and the ships**; some 300–400 buildings burned in one vast blaze while the second assault went in beside it. Burgoyne, watching from Copp's Hill, called the whole panorama "one of the greatest scenes of war that can be conceived." (HIGH; building count as a range.)
- **Second assault — same plan, same result.** After re-forming, Howe attacked again within the half hour — grenadiers and line at the fence, Pigot at the redoubt — and was repulsed at least as bloodily; some companies were reported at two-thirds losses, and Howe's own staff was being shot down around him. This is where the British courage belongs in the telling: re-formed twice, walked back uphill twice into massed fire. (HIGH on repulse; specific company-loss figures vary — keep general.)
- **Third assault — bayonets against empty horns.** Reinforced by the 47th Foot and fresh Marines (~400 more), with **Clinton crossing on his own initiative** to rally the shaken units, Howe changed method: **packs off, columns not lines, the redoubt the single objective, the bayonet the weapon**. Inside the redoubt the powder was nearly gone — Prescott's men had perhaps one volley left, fired at point-blank; then the regulars came over the parapet. The defenders, mostly without bayonets, clubbed muskets and threw stones in a brief hand-to-hand scrum (Pitcairn fell mortally wounded at the parapet — the tradition that the Black soldier **Peter Salem** shot him is Swett's 1818 account, MED-LOW, attribute don't assert; the commendation of **Salem Poor** — 14 officers petitioned that he "behaved like an experienced officer" — is documented, HIGH). Prescott ordered the retreat. (HIGH on the third-assault mechanics.)
- **The retreat — and Warren.** Most American casualties came in the retreat across Bunker Hill and over the Neck, under naval fire (HMS *Glasgow* and gun-barges raking the isthmus). The withdrawal was ragged but not a rout; Putnam tried and failed to rally a stand at the half-dug works on Bunker Hill proper. **Joseph Warren was killed in the last moments near the redoubt, covering the retreat — a musket ball to the head.** That is the documented death; resist the romanticized last-stand tableaux (Trumbull's painting is a heroic composition made eleven years later). British officers recognized the body; Capt. Walter Laurie wrote that he "stuffed the scoundrel with another rebel into one hole, and there he and his seditious principles may remain." The body was stripped and buried in a shallow mass grave on the field. (HIGH)
- **By ~5 p.m.** the peninsula was British. They fortified Bunker Hill and held the ground until they evacuated Boston on March 17, 1776 — the hill bought with a fifth of an army held nine months and then handed back. The Americans dug in a mile away on Winter and Prospect Hills the same evening; the siege resumed as if the lines had merely shifted one peninsula. (HIGH)

## 5. CASUALTIES (ranges)
- **British: 1,054 casualties — 226 killed, 828 wounded** (firm; Gage's return) of ~2,200–3,000 engaged: roughly **40–50% of those who fought**. The officer toll was the era's scandal — commonly given as **~100 officer casualties (19 killed, 62 wounded** in standard tallies; some lists run higher) — a measurable share of all British officers lost in the entire eight-year war. (HIGH on 1,054/226/828 — the spine's firm figure; officer split MED, cite as "about a hundred.")
- **American: ~420–450 total** — commonly **~115 killed (range 115–140), ~270–305 wounded, ~30 captured** (most of the captured wounded; 20 of ~30 prisoners died). Among the dead: Maj. Gen. Joseph Warren and Maj. Andrew McClary of New Hampshire (killed by a cannonball at the Neck after the action — the battle's last casualty by tradition). Use ranges; never invent precision. (HIGH on the range per the spine; McClary MED-HIGH.)
- **Stats block:** forces ~2,400–3,000 colonial engaged vs ~2,200–3,000 British across the day; duration ~2 hours of infantry combat (cannonade from dawn); **winner: Britain — took the hill; the marker is theirs, the framing is the cost.**

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **London's lesson:** Gage's report and private letters ("The loss we have sustained, is greater than we can bear" — to Barrington, June 26, 1775; and that the rebels showed a spirit "they never shewed against the French") ended his command — recalled within weeks, Howe succeeding him in October. The government accepted this was a war requiring a real army; the news fed the August 23 Proclamation of Rebellion (spine §9). (HIGH; Gage quote MED-HIGH per the spine — verify verbatim before quoting in full.)
- **The American lesson — dangerously seductive:** militia behind earthworks could maul regulars. It bred a faith in untrained men and dirt that 1776 (Long Island, Kip's Bay) would punish. Washington, taking command at Cambridge July 3, inherited an army convinced of itself but out of powder. (HIGH on the framing; standard historiography.)
- **The Howe-caution thesis:** contemporaries and historians have read Howe's later war — Brooklyn Heights declined, repeated unwillingness to storm fortified lines, the missing killer instinct — as Bunker Hill's long shadow. Widely held, unfalsifiable; present at MED as interpretation, not fact. (MED)
- **Warren's body — the Revere identification.** Ten months later, days after the British evacuation (late March/April 1776), Warren's family and friends exhumed the battlefield grave. The corpse was unrecognizable — except that **Paul Revere, who had made Warren's dental prosthesis (a false tooth/two-tooth bridge of ivory fastened with silver or gold wire) shortly before the war, identified his friend by his own handiwork** — commonly cited as the first forensic dental identification in American history. Warren was reburied with honors (Granary, later St. Paul's, finally Forest Hills Cemetery, 1856). Remarkable and documented — tell it plainly, no kitsch. (HIGH — NPS, National Museum of Health and Medicine, peer-reviewed forensic literature.)
- Charlestown was a burned ruin; its people were refugees for years. The Bunker Hill Monument (1825–1843) stands on Breed's Hill — the name confusion fossilized in granite. Pollard's unmarked grave is presumed still under its shadow. (HIGH)

## 8. MYTH SET / HANDLE-WITH-CARE
1. **"Whites of their eyes" — legend at this battle.** Attributed after the fact to Putnam, Prescott, Stark, AND Gridley; the formula predates the Revolution in European armies (Prussian/earlier usage). Scholarly consensus: never put it in anyone's mouth at Bunker Hill. Tell the myth AS a myth — the page can enjoy it honestly. (Spine-locked.)
2. **The wrong hill.** Real, but the *why* is unsettled — order, council decision, or error ("by some mistake," said the Committee of Safety). The provocation reading is the most common modern one; present it as the leading explanation, not the answer. The battle's NAME is the fossil of the confusion.
3. **The Putnam/Prescott command question** is a genuine 19th-century historiographical war (partisan memoirs, dueling orations, the Dearborn–Putnam controversy of 1818). Verifiable floor: Prescott commanded in the redoubt throughout; Stark ran his own flank; Putnam, senior officer present, rode the field moving men and tools and declined command in the redoubt. Who "commanded the battle" has no clean answer — say so.
4. **Warren without martyr-kitsch.** The documented death is a musket ball to the head in the closing minutes near the redoubt; the Trumbull canvas is an eleven-years-later heroic composition by an artist who watched from across the harbor (caption caveat MANDATORY, per spine). His real distinction is the verified refusal of command — a major general choosing to fight as a private.
5. **British courage respected.** They re-formed twice and came up the hill three times into the worst infantry fire of the era; ~100 officers fell leading from the front; Howe walked every assault. No faceless-redcoat waves.
6. **Peter Salem / Pitcairn:** Swett's 1818 tradition (MED-LOW) — attribute, never assert. **Salem Poor's** commendation petition is solid (HIGH) and is the better-documented beat about Black soldiers at the battle.
7. **Numbers discipline:** all strengths/casualties as the spine's ranges (§5); British 1,054/226/828 is the one firm figure.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. **Why Breed's, and who chose it** — no clear participant account; "by some mistake" (Committee of Safety) vs. deliberate provocation (leading modern reading). Unsettled; present loose (spine-locked MED).
2. **Command** — Prescott in the redoubt is firm; everything above him (Putnam's authority, who "commanded the battle") is a real dispute; present, don't resolve.
3. **American casualties** as ranges only (~420–450; killed 115–140; ~30 captured); engaged strengths both sides as ranges.
4. **Which ship killed Asa Pollard** (*Lively* vs *Somerset* in accounts) — don't pin; the burial-without-ceremony is solid.
5. **Howe quote addressees** vary by citation — quote the words, fuzz the recipient. The Gage "greater than we can bear" line: verify verbatim against the Barrington letter before quoting in full (spine flag).
6. **"Frighted out of his wits"** — NOT in Peter Brown's MHS-transcribed letter; do not attribute. Use his verified lines (§7.4).
7. **Stark's stake at ~40 yards, the 96 beach dead, "sheep in a fold"** — from counts and recollections; MED-HIGH/MED, attribute as such.
8. **Peter Salem killing Pitcairn** — Swett 1818 tradition, MED-LOW; Salem Poor's commendation HIGH.
9. **The Howe-caution thesis** — interpretation (MED), never stated as fact.
10. **Redoubt size** — "roughly 130 ft square" standard vs. Brown's "ten rod long, eight wide" (165×132 ft); say "roughly 130 feet on a side."
11. **Building count in Charlestown's burning** — ~300–400; use "some three to four hundred" or "hundreds."
