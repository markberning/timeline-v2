## FINDINGS

### 1. Slavery as cause — named and load-bearing. **FAIR**
Covered above. The framing is not a single perfunctory sentence buried in the ledger;
it is asserted up front, restated mid-piece, and made the interpretive payoff of the
finale. Strong.

### 2. Emancipation Proclamation timing & significance. **FAIR** (web-verified)
The draft's central claim — Stones River was the first major Union victory of the
Emancipation Proclamation era, and a defeat would have undercut the new policy at the
moment it took legal effect — is historically supported (American Battlefield Trust;
NPS; ironbrigader). The American Battlefield Trust states the victory "helped
legitimize the Emancipation Proclamation that had gone into effect on January 1,
1863." The draft does NOT overclaim a causal link Lincoln intended day-of; it
correctly frames the Proclamation's effect as the larger meaning the soldiers on the
field did not yet know. The §the-new-years-pause line *"It freed no one instantly —
it applied to the very Confederate territory the Union did not yet control"* is an
honest, non-triumphalist caveat that prevents the emancipation framing from tipping
into Northern self-congratulation. Good restraint.

### 3. Casualty-rate claim. **FAIR** (web-verified)
The draft handles the "highest percentage casualty rate" claim with exactly the
qualification the sources demand: *"By one common reckoning it ran higher than Shiloh
or Antietam; by another it stands second only to Gettysburg. The exact ranking
depends on how you count."* This matches the live source ambivalence (Battlefield
Trust / Statista variously say "greatest percentage of any major battle… more than
Shiloh and Antietam" vs. "second only to Gettysburg"). Not a framing distortion — and
notably the casualty emphasis serves the human cost, not romance.

### 4. Sympathy distribution between the sides. **FAIR**
This is the area to watch hardest in any Confederate-attack battle, and the draft
passes. The Confederate command is treated critically, not heroically: Bragg is "a
harsh disciplinarian whose own generals could barely stand him"; the Day-3 charge is
an "OUTCOME — Day three: A self-inflicted catastrophe for the South"; Breckinridge's
assault is "the whole ferocious gamble… accomplished nothing but the gutting of one
of Bragg's best divisions." The South's failure is framed as command folly and
internal friction, NOT as noble doomed valor. No "gallant Lost Cause" gloss.

### 5. The Orphan Brigade. **FAIR — but the one spot to watch.**
The Orphan Brigade (Kentuckians who fought for the Confederacy and couldn't go home
because Kentucky stayed Union) gets the draft's most sympathetic treatment — a
"Meanwhile" sidebar built entirely around their tragedy ("orphaned twice over"). This
is the classic vector for Confederate-soldier romanticization. Here it stays on the
right side of the line because: (a) the sympathy is for men cut down by their own
commander's ordered folly (anti-Confederate-command, not pro-Confederate-cause), not
for what they were fighting for; (b) the draft never valorizes their cause or implies
their devotion ennobles it; (c) the cause (slavery) is named flatly elsewhere and not
softened to make room for the pathos. **No change required.** Noting it so the
reconcile step is aware this is the single passage where the human-reality sympathy
leans Confederate; it is earned by the "killed by Bragg's bad order" framing, not by
romance. If anything in revision drifts toward "brave Kentuckians' sacrifice" language
divorced from the futility, it would tip TILTED — it does not currently.

### 6. The "Home! Sweet Home!" shared serenade. **FAIR**
The both-sides-singing-together episode is the textbook false-equivalence trap ("both
sides were just boys who loved home"). The draft earns it: it is framed as the human
reality of men *"who would be trying to kill each other at first light,"* not as moral
symmetry between the causes, and it is bracketed by the slavery-cause framing two
paragraphs earlier. It does not imply the war was a tragic misunderstanding between
equals. The [VERIFY] discipline (no soldier's words in quotes, "by most accounts") is
correctly applied. Not a both-sides distortion.

### 7. Human cost — sanitized or gratuitous? **FAIR**
Neither. The dead are concrete and unflinching (Garesché decapitated by a cannonball
at Rosecrans's side; Sheridan losing all three brigade commanders and a third of his
division; 1,800 Confederates "shredded… in roughly sixty minutes") but not
pornographic. The casualty figures are given as the human stakes, not as a box score.
The enslaved are present as the four million whose freedom the war was now, in
writing, about — not abstracted away. Civilians barely feature (Murfreesboro is mostly
terrain here), which is appropriate to this battle's record and not an evasion.

### 8. Presentism. **FAIR**
No anachronistic moralizing or 21st-century framing imposed on the actors. The
emancipation significance is drawn from what the Proclamation did in 1863, not from a
modern editorial stance. Lincoln's verbatim letter (web-corroboratable, factpack-
VERIFIED) carries the stakes in period voice.
