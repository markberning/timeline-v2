## the-breadbasket-burning · The Shenandoah · The valley they came to burn

### Why two armies were standing in the smoke of a ruined harvest

By October 1864 the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia had been the most fought-over ground in the East for three years, and the reason was written into the land itself. The Valley is a long, fertile corridor running southwest-to-northeast between two mountain ranges — which made it two things at once. It was the Confederacy's **"breadbasket,"** a rich grain belt whose wheat and cattle fed Gen. Robert E. Lee's (South) Army of Northern Virginia. And it was a natural invasion highway: because it points toward Washington, an army marching *down* the Valley (down meaning north, the way the rivers run) could threaten the U.S. capital while the mountains screened its movement. Food for the South and a knife at the North's throat, in one piece of geography. Both armies wanted it for both reasons.

It is worth being plain, before the cannons start, about what those two armies were ultimately fighting over, because a breadbasket is the *object* and not the *cause*. The Confederacy existed to preserve and extend slavery, and the Valley was a slave economy that proved it. The wheat that fed Lee's army was grown by enslaved people; between 1810 and 1860 roughly a fifth of the Valley's population was held in bondage, and in Frederick County — where this battle would be fought — close to a third of households enslaved people. So when the Union set out to wreck the breadbasket, it was not attacking a granary in the abstract. It was attacking the food supply of a slaveholding army, raised on land worked by the very people the war was being fought to free. Hold that thought; by the end of this story it will be standing in the middle of the battlefield, a house with a name, and impossible to miss.

That summer the Valley had nearly won the war for the South. Lt. Gen. Jubal Early (South) had marched his Army of the Valley down the corridor and right up to the outskirts of Washington in July 1864 — close enough to rattle the capital. Grant decided to end the threat for good and sent Maj. Gen. Philip Sheridan (North), a short, ferocious cavalryman, to do it. Through the fall Sheridan beat Early three times running: at the Third Battle of Winchester on September 19 (the biggest, bloodiest fight of the whole campaign), at Fisher's Hill on September 22, and at Tom's Brook on October 9, where Union horsemen routed Early's cavalry. Early had been driven up the Valley a beaten man.

And then Sheridan did the thing the Valley had been built to prevent: he burned it. Acting on Grant's order to leave the Valley "a barren waste," his army spent roughly thirteen days in late September and early October systematically destroying the breadbasket — about 1,400 barns torched, some 435,000 bushels of wheat burned, mills and furnaces wrecked, around 11,000 head of cattle driven off, across a swath roughly 70 miles long and 30 wide. Soldiers called it, simply, "The Burning." The goal was cold and explicit: starve Lee's army by destroying the slave-worked farms that fed it. The battle this story is about would be fought in the smoking ruin of that harvest — which, as it turned out, is exactly why hungry Confederate soldiers couldn't keep their eyes off the well-stocked Union camps.

Believing the beaten Early was finished, Sheridan's army went into camp on the north bank of Cedar Creek, a stream running roughly east-to-west across their front. The creek was the line they thought protected them. Early, reinforced and out of options, decided to bet the whole campaign on proving them wrong.

> **MEANWHILE — a trap set from a mountaintop.** Two days before the attack, on October 17, Early's second-in-command, Maj. Gen. John Brown Gordon (South), climbed the heights of Massanutten Mountain — the long ridge that looms over the southeast corner of the field — and looked straight down into the sleeping Union camps. From up there he could see what the Federals could not see about themselves: their *left flank* (the southeastern end of their line) hung "in the air," unanchored and unwatched. A flank is the vulnerable end of an army's line, where an attacker who gets around the side can roll the whole thing up like a carpet. Gordon came down the mountain with the layout of the trap in his head. The surprise at Cedar Creek was scouted from an overlook.

## the-dawn-surprise · October 19, dawn · The morning the Confederacy almost won

### Early's army comes out of the fog

Early (South) split his army into columns and sent them through the dark for a pre-dawn convergence on the camps. Gordon's (South) flanking column set out first, around eight on the evening of the 18th, threading a narrow trail along the North Fork of the Shenandoah River around the very base of Massanutten. The other two columns moved out around one in the morning. By about half past three, in fog and silence, all three were in position against the sleeping Union line.

It came at about five o'clock, and it came like the end of the world. Maj. Gen. Joseph Kershaw's (South) division struck first, smashing into the camps of Col. Joseph Thoburn's (North) division — part of Brig. Gen. George Crook's (North) Army of West Virginia, the corps holding that exposed left flank. (A *corps* is one of the big building blocks of a Civil War army, several divisions and many thousands of men under one general; this army had three infantry corps plus its cavalry.) Most of Thoburn's men were still asleep in their tents. There was no warning, no line, no time — just Confederate infantry coming out of the fog into a camp of men in their blankets. Fifteen minutes later Gordon's column hit the next division over. Around five-thirty the Confederate artillery opened on Brig. Gen. William Emory's (North) XIX Corps.

What followed was a *rout* — not an orderly retreat but a collapse, an army coming apart and running. Two whole Union corps, Crook's Army of West Virginia and Emory's XIX Corps, were driven in panic back through their own camps to the north. Thoburn was mortally wounded in the wreckage of the dawn. By around half past seven Kershaw and the Confederates were shoving two divisions of the VI Corps to the northwest as well. Maj. Gen. Horatio Wright (North) — who was running the army that morning because Sheridan was away (more on that shortly) — could do little but fight a delaying withdrawal, trading ground for time as his army fell apart around him.

By about ten in the morning, Early had done the seemingly impossible. He had attacked an army half again the size of his own — roughly 31,000 Union effectives against his own 21,000 — caught it in its sleep, and beaten it. He held the field. He held the captured camps. He had taken more than 1,300 prisoners and 24 cannon. A general who had been chased up the Valley three times in a month had, in five hours, apparently won a stunning victory and reversed the entire campaign.

And then he stopped. And the stopping is the great argument of this battle.

> **MEANWHILE — the cost of a rout.** Here is a fact that looks backwards until you understand it: by the end of this day the *winning* army would have lost roughly twice as many men as the loser — about 5,700 Union casualties against about 2,900 Confederate. The reason is the morning you just read. Most of the Union loss was paid right here, at dawn: men killed and wounded in their tents, and more than a thousand captured when their camps were overrun, all before the battle turned. The casualty math itself tells the story of a fight that ran in two opposite directions on the same ground.

## the-fatal-halt · Mid-morning · The battle that stopped

### Why Early's victory stalled — and what people have argued about it ever since

Around ten in the morning, with the Union army broken and streaming north, Early (South) halted his attack. For roughly three hours, into the early afternoon, the killing blow did not fall. By the time the Confederates resumed — pushing forward again around half past eleven, only to stall once more by about one o'clock — the momentum was gone. That pause is the most argued-over decision in the battle, and the honest thing is to lay out the explanations as competing interpretations rather than pretend the records have settled it.

Early himself gave reasons. He worried about Union cavalry — armed with fast-firing repeating rifles — hovering on his flanks, and he noted that his own troops had stopped to plunder the abandoned Union camps. That last point grew into the battle's most famous story, sometimes called "the battle that stopped for breakfast": the idea that Early's hungry, ragged men — fresh from watching their own Valley burned to ash — simply couldn't resist the rich, intact Union camps in front of them, and broke ranks to loot food, shoes, and clothing instead of finishing the fight. It is a vivid and widely repeated explanation, and it has the cruel logic of the whole campaign behind it: the same Burning that was meant to starve this army may have helped save the men it was used against, by tempting them off the attack at the decisive moment. But it is a leading interpretation, not a proven cause. Historians genuinely dispute how decisive the plundering really was, against the duller possibilities of sheer exhaustion and Early's own caution.

The harshest verdict came from inside Early's own headquarters. Gordon (South), who had devised and led the flank march that opened the battle, blamed the halt above everything else, writing after the war that the "fatal halting" had "converted the brilliant victory of the morning into disastrous defeat in the evening." It is a damning line — but Gordon was an interested party. He and Early feuded bitterly over who lost Cedar Creek, and Gordon's memoir is a brief for the prosecution as much as a record. Take it as Gordon's charge against Early, not as a neutral finding.

Whatever the cause — loot, fatigue, caution, or all three — the result is not in dispute. The Confederate attack lost its edge in the middle of the morning, and it never got it back. Which gave one absent Union general just enough time to ride into the story.

## sheridans-ride · The rally · Sheridan rides down the Pike

### The true core of a famous legend

Sheridan (North) was not on the field when his army fell apart. He had spent the night of October 18 at Winchester, a town down the Valley Pike to the north, returning from a meeting in Washington. The **Valley Pike** (the Valley Turnpike) was the great north–south road that ran the length of the Shenandoah, the spine of the whole campaign — and on this day it would be the road of both the rout running north and the rescue riding south.

Around six in the morning he heard distant artillery and dismissed it as nothing — a reconnaissance, the kind of probing fire that means little. By around nine he had set out south down the Pike on his horse, a powerful black gelding named Rienzi. Less than two miles south of Winchester the truth came up the road to meet him: not a probe but a flood of his own men, broken and fleeing north, the wreckage of a routed army streaming past him in the wrong direction.

What he did next is the reason everyone remembers Cedar Creek. Sheridan put spurs to Rienzi and galloped the rest of the way to the field — roughly a dozen miles — riding *against* the current of his own retreat, turning fugitives around as he went, gathering his broken army off the road by the force of his arrival. A *rally* is exactly this: stopping a fleeing army and re-forming it into a fighting line, and it is one of the hardest things a commander can do, because panic spreads faster than orders. One soldier said the effect of seeing him come down the Pike was like "an electric shock." He reached the field near Middletown, at the center of the battlefield, around half past ten, and began rebuilding the army that had spent the morning coming apart — re-forming a line through midday, and waiting until he was sure, around half past three, that no Confederate reinforcements were coming up from Front Royal before he turned and attacked.

Now, about the legend — because Cedar Creek is wrapped in one, and the honest way to tell it is to keep the true core and label the embroidery. The ride really happened, and it really turned the battle; that part is solid. But most of what people "know" about it comes from a poem. Within weeks the poet Thomas Buchanan Read wrote **"Sheridan's Ride"** (1864), whose refrain counts down the miles — "and Sheridan twenty miles away," then fifteen, then ten, then five — building to its galloping finish: "Here is the steed that saved the day, / By carrying Sheridan into the fight, / From Winchester, twenty miles away!" The poem made the ride immortal. It also stretched it: the real distance from Winchester to the field was about twelve miles, not twenty. And the poem put words in Sheridan's mouth — its rousing "Turn, boys, turn! We're going back!" is Read's invention, not anything Sheridan is recorded as saying. What Sheridan actually shouted to his men as he rode is reported in several conflicting versions, none of them cleanly verbatim; the gist is solid even where the exact words aren't — he told his men they were not retreating, that they would sleep in their own camps that night, and that they were going to turn around and attack. That is the true core. The poem is the legend that grew on top of it.

The legend, though, did real work in the world. Read's poem was printed on page one of Horace Greeley's *New York Tribune* on Election Day, November 8, 1864 — and the timing was not innocent.

> **MEANWHILE — three weeks before the election.** Cedar Creek fell three weeks before the presidential election of 1864, and that is what the whole campaign was *for*. Through the summer Lincoln's reelection had looked doubtful; a victory by his opponent threatened a negotiated peace that could have left slavery standing. Then came the autumn of victories — Mobile Bay in August, Atlanta in September, and now the Valley — and the doubt burned off. Lincoln's reelection was the referendum that guaranteed the war would be fought through to the end of slavery, and Cedar Creek helped clinch it. Read's poem, on the *Tribune's* front page on the very day the country voted, made the battle a campaign asset; one account suggests it "may well have affected Lincoln's margin" in a close race. That a battle should *deliver* votes is the kind of thing no one can really count, so leave it at "may have." But the connection is not a stretch: the Valley was burned to starve a slaveholding army, and the news of its conquest reached voters' hands on the morning they decided whether the war to end slavery would go on.

## the-counterattack · Late afternoon · Sheridan takes it all back

### How the morning's defeat became the evening's rout

Just before four in the afternoon, with his army re-formed and steady, Sheridan (North) launched a general counterattack — the whole line moving south, back down the ground it had lost at dawn. His XIX Corps under Emory (North) and his VI Corps formed the main infantry line. Brig. Gen. Wesley Merritt's (North) cavalry took the left, southeast of the Pike; Brig. Gen. George Armstrong Custer's (North) horsemen anchored the far right to the northwest, facing the Confederate cavalry under Maj. Gen. Thomas Rosser (South); Crook's (North) battered Army of West Virginia waited in reserve.

It did not break open at once. The infantry attack stalled against stone walls and Confederate artillery — the same hard arithmetic of frontal assault against a defended line that had cost both armies all war. The thing that cracked it was the cavalry. Merritt's troopers made three charges; the first two were thrown back, and the third broke through, with the VI Corps pushing in behind it. The Confederate line, thinned and tired and a long way from the morning's triumph, began to give.

The kill stroke was Custer's. He drove his division around the Confederate left — the western end of their line — and made for the Cedar Creek bridge, the crossing on the Valley Pike that was Early's (South) line of retreat. When Confederate soldiers looked back toward their only escape route and saw Union cavalry already curling around it, the army's nerve broke. Panic ran down the line the way it had run through the Union camps at dawn, only now it ran the other way. By about half past five the Army of the Valley simply disintegrated, abandoning everything — all the Union guns it had captured that morning, plus most of its own artillery and wagons. Union pursuit ran south until nightfall. The same army that had won a stunning victory before breakfast was a fleeing wreck by dark, on the same field, in a single day.

> **MEANWHILE — the West Point friends at Belle Grove.** The center of the battlefield — Sheridan's own headquarters — was a stone manor house called Belle Grove, built in the 1790s, just north of Middletown. It is the reason the battle's other name is "Belle Grove." On the Confederate left, near the Miller house, sometime between five and six in the evening, Maj. Gen. Stephen Dodson Ramseur (South †) was shot through both lungs — his third horse of the day going down under him, the first two already killed beneath him. Captured during the retreat, he was carried to Belle Grove, the enemy commander's headquarters, where he died the next day. And here the war did something the war rarely let it do: his old West Point friends, now wearing the other uniform, came to him at the end. Custer (North) and Merritt (North), who had been classmates and friends, came to his deathbed, along with Capt. Henry DuPont (North). DuPont remembered Ramseur grasping his hand. The day *before* the battle, Ramseur had learned that his wife had given birth to their first child, a daughter — a child he never met, and reportedly wore a flower into the fight to celebrate. He left a widow and a newborn he had never held, and he died in his enemy's house with his enemies, who had once been his friends, at his side.

## what-it-won · The meaning · What the victory secured

### The proof of the cause, in the house at the center of the field

**OUTCOME: Union victory — a decisive one.** A near-total Confederate triumph at dawn reversed into a crushing Union victory by dusk. Early's (South) Army of the Valley was destroyed as a fighting force. It never again threatened the North, retreated up the Valley toward Fisher's Hill and beyond, and was formally finished off the following spring when Custer (North) wrecked its remnant at Waynesboro in March 1865. Early's reputation collapsed; he lost the confidence of his own subordinates, and the army that had stood at the gates of Washington in July was a memory by winter.

The strategic payoff was enormous. Cedar Creek secured the Shenandoah Valley permanently for the Union and ended, in one day, the Valley's two functions for the Confederacy — Lee's (South) breadbasket and Lee's invasion route. The army that had marched on Washington could no longer maneuver down the corridor to threaten the capital or the northern states, and the farms that had fed the Army of Northern Virginia were ash. In the last winter of the war it was one more length of rope around Lee's neck. And it made Sheridan (North) a legend — his fame, in the period's judgment, "only eclipsed by Grant and Sherman." He would rise to command the entire U.S. Army; an equestrian statue of him stands in Washington today, and the horse that carried him down the Pike, Rienzi, was renamed "Winchester" for the ride.

But the deepest meaning of Cedar Creek was not strategic, and it was not on the Pike. It was in the house at the center of the field, and it is the answer to the question this story opened with — what these two armies were really fighting over.

Belle Grove — Sheridan's headquarters, the house where the dying Ramseur was carried — was a slave plantation. That is the most concrete proof on this entire battlefield of what the war was about. It was built by Isaac Hite Jr., who enslaved people to build it and to run it, and over its history the family that owned it enslaved hundreds of men, women, and children; census records from the early 1800s count roughly a hundred enslaved people living on the place at a time. Its product was the breadbasket's product — wheat, livestock, the output of mills and a distillery — all of it worked by enslaved hands. The plantation at the center of the battlefield was itself a piece of the very breadbasket Sheridan had spent the autumn burning. And it sat in a Valley where, as we began, a fifth of the people were enslaved and a third of the households in this county held human beings as property.

So stand for a moment in the middle of this field and see the whole war laid out on it. The breadbasket was the object; slavery was the reason the breadbasket mattered; and the proof of the reason was the manor house in the center, a working slave plantation that served as the Union commander's headquarters and the Confederate general's deathbed on the same terrible day. Sheridan burned the Valley to starve a slaveholding army of the food that enslaved people were forced to grow. He won the field three weeks before an election that would decide whether the war to end slavery went on. And the news of his victory — wrapped in a poem about a horse — reached voters on the morning they cast their ballots. The dramatic cavalry ride is the part everyone remembers. The slave plantation at the center of the field is the part that explains why anyone was riding at all.

> **MEANWHILE — what the burning was really for.** It is tempting to file Cedar Creek as a battle about a breadbasket, or a battle about a famous ride, and both the strategy and the legend invite it. But sit with what the breadbasket *was*. It was a slave economy — wheat grown by people held in bondage, feeding an army raised to keep them there. "The Burning" that frames this battle, the destruction of 1,400 barns and 435,000 bushels of wheat, was not vandalism; it was a deliberate strike at the food supply of a slaveholding republic, and Belle Grove, the house in the middle of the fight, was one of the farms that supply came from. That is the cruelty hiding in the geometry of this battle: the same plantation that helped feed the war to preserve slavery became the headquarters of the army sent to end it, and the deathbed of a general who fell defending it. The ride saved the day. But the day was worth saving because of what was at the center of the field — and what was at the center of the field was slavery.
