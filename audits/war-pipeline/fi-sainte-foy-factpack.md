## CONTEXT / STAKES
1. Quebec, the capital of New France, fell to the British on Sept 18, 1759, days after
   the Battle of the Plains of Abraham (Sept 13, 1759), in which both opposing generals,
   James Wolfe (British) and the Marquis de Montcalm (French), were mortally wounded. (S1, S2)
2. Montcalm's successor as French field commander was François Gaston de Lévis, the
   Chevalier de Lévis (S3). After the loss of Quebec, the surviving French field army
   withdrew upriver to Montreal (S3).
3. New France was not yet lost. To save it, the French had to retake Quebec before British
   reinforcements could come up the St. Lawrence in spring. The St. Lawrence freezes in
   winter, closing it to seagoing ships; whichever side's fleet arrived first when the ice
   broke up would decide the campaign. (S2, S3)
4. Sainte-Foy was the French attempt to retake Quebec. It was a FRENCH VICTORY (S1, S2, S3,
   S4) and is described as bloodier by casualty rate than the Plains of Abraham (S1). It
   still failed to save New France because British ships, not French ships, arrived first. (S2, S3)

## THE BRITISH WINTER GARRISON (1759–1760)
5. After taking Quebec, James Murray (British) held the city as commander/governor.
   Garrison strength at the start of winter: ~6,000–7,000 men. DISPUTED/RANGE: S4 says
   "approximately 6,000 able-bodied men"; S2 says "approximately 7,000 troops." (S2, S4)
6. The garrison was wrecked over the winter by scurvy, cold, lack of firewood, and lack of
   fresh food. The men were victualled almost entirely on salt provisions; fresh meat could
   not be procured; scurvy spread until nearly every soldier, even those reckoned fit, was
   affected to some degree. (S6, S7)
7. The city had been largely destroyed in the 1759 bombardment and siege, so housing was
   scarce; troops were in makeshift, exposed shelters in deep cold. (S2, S6)
8. Winter deaths: about 1,000 men died over the winter; roughly 2,000 more were sick. By
   spring 1760 Murray had only about 4,000 men fit to fight. DISPUTED/RANGE: S2 "a thousand
   of these troops had died, and two thousand more were ill," leaving ~4,000 combat-ready;
   S4 "fewer than 4,000 during the winter"; S7 (Knox, a garrison member) the garrison
   "buried a thousand men" in the first nine months with about an equal number sick daily,
   chiefly of scurvy. CONVERGES on ~1,000 dead and ~4,000 fit by spring. (S2, S4, S7)
9. Murray was also without funds (had to raise money on the personal notes of two senior
   officers, at 5%); drunkenness and theft were rife and required special measures. (S7)

## LÉVIS REBUILDS AND MARCHES (winter 1759–1760, April 1760)
10. Over the winter Lévis rebuilt the French army at/around Montreal: tightened discipline,
    distributed uniforms and equipment, arranged provisioning; habitants billeting soldiers
    were to keep eight days' rations ready for rapid mobilization. (S3)
11. Lévis left Montreal on April 20, 1760, before the river had fully thawed, and marched
    down toward Quebec. The march took about eight days; it was "a terrible march through
    slush and mud." (S3)
12. French strength in the 1760 campaign: about 7,000 troops, roughly half regulars and half
    Canadian militia and Indigenous allies. DISPUTED/RANGE on the army's total vs. the number
    actually engaged at Sainte-Foy:
    - S1 (battle, total force): Lévis commanded 6,910 soldiers (3,889 in eight regular
      battalions, plus two battalions of Compagnies Franches de la Marine, plus Canadian militia).
    - S1 (engaged) / fetch summary: ~5,000 present at the battle, with only 3 cannon deployed.
    - S3: ~7,000 troops (3,000 militia, 4,000 regulars).
    USE: French army of about 7,000; about 5,000 engaged at the battle; only ~3 cannon brought
    to the battlefield (siege guns came up later). (S1, S3)

## THE BATTLE OF SAINTE-FOY (April 28, 1760)
13. Location: just west of Quebec, near the village/heights of Sainte-Foy, on and around the
    same ground as the Plains of Abraham. (S1, S3)
14. Murray marched OUT of Quebec to give battle in the open rather than stand behind the
    city's weak, war-damaged walls with his reduced garrison. He judged the walls too poor and
    his force too small to mount a good defense, and (per S4) was temperamentally
    "hot-headed and impetuous"; he chose to attack. (S1 fetch summary, S4)
15. British field strength at the battle: about 3,800–3,900 men, with about 20 cannon (S1 says
    "over twenty cannon"; fetch summary "20 cannons and 2 howitzers") and 2 howitzers. So the
    British had FAR more artillery on the field than the French (~20+ guns vs ~3). (S1, S3)
16. Course of the battle: Murray attacked before the French had fully deployed. The British
    advanced and at first had success; but their own artillery became masked by their
    advancing infantry. The fight became close-range and lasted roughly two hours. French
    counterattacks (including a bayonet charge by d'Alquier de Servian's troops per S3, and a
    flanking move) overwhelmed the British left flank. The British artillery ran out of
    ammunition and the guns had to be abandoned. The British were beaten and fell back into
    Quebec. (S1, S3)
17. Lévis deployed his line more swiftly than Murray expected, avoiding Montcalm's 1759
    mistake of advancing in columns. (S3)

## CASUALTIES (Sainte-Foy)
18. British casualties: ~1,182 total — 292 killed, 837 wounded, 53 captured. The 15th Foot
    suffered about 34% casualties. (S1)
19. French casualties: ~833 total — 193 killed, 640 wounded. (S1)
20. Comparison: Sainte-Foy was bloodier than the Plains of Abraham (Sept 1759). S1 frames it
    as "notably bloodier" with 833 French casualties to 1,124+ British casualties. Often
    described as the bloodiest battle of the war on the continent by casualty rate. (S1)
    NOTE: figures 18–20 carry small internal range; S1 gives British total both as 1,182 and
    elsewhere ~1,124. Use ~1,100–1,200 British casualties; ~830 French casualties.

## THE SIEGE OF QUEBEC (April 29 – mid-May 1760)
21. Murray's beaten force fell back into Quebec; Lévis began the siege on April 29, 1760. (S2)
22. Lévis's siege was crippled by an inadequate siege train. S3: he "was obliged to lay
    siege, with a totally inadequate siege train." He brought only twelve artillery pieces
    from Montreal and wanted ~40 guns positioned before a real bombardment. (S2, S3)
23. Lévis delayed assaulting because his troops were exhausted and he was unsure of the
    reliability of some militiamen. (S2)
24. Ammunition shortage: Lévis ordered his guns to fire only ~20 rounds per gun per day. By
    contrast the British, once resupplied, fired massively — S2 cites 2,913 shots in a single
    day against only four French shells in reply. (S2)

## THE RACE UP THE ST. LAWRENCE — THE SHIPS
25. The whole campaign turned on which nation's fleet arrived first as the ice broke.
    (S2, S3)
26. May 9, 1760: a ship appeared in the river — HMS Lowestoffe (also spelled Lowestoft), a
    28-gun frigate, the first ship up after the thaw. Each side at first hoped it was theirs.
    It was British. Its arrival told Lévis he had to take the city fast, before the main British
    fleet came up. (S1, S2)
27. May 15–16, 1760: Commodore Robert Swanton arrived with more warships. British ships —
    HMS Vanguard, HMS Diana, and HMS Lowestoffe — attacked the French naval division upstream.
    Swanton forced the French to cut their cables and a running battle followed; the French
    flotilla, which was carrying the army's supplies and ammunition, was destroyed. (S1, S2)
28. The French supply fleet: a small division under Jean Vauquelin (French naval officer) —
    the frigate Atalante, the Pomone, the flute Pie, and light craft — had left Sorel on
    April 20 to follow and supply Lévis's army and had reached the Anse au Foulon on April 28,
    the day of the Sainte-Foy victory. (S5)
29. Vauquelin's last stand (May 16, 1760): pursued by Lowestoffe and Diana, Vauquelin drew
    them off toward Cap-Rouge to protect the army's supply depots, then ran the Atalante
    aground at Pointe-aux-Trembles (Neuville). He fired off his ammunition, nailed his flag to
    the mast under steady enemy fire, threw his sword into the St. Lawrence, and ordered his
    crew to abandon ship. He was wounded and captured; the British burned the Atalante the next
    day. DCB: "Vauquelin, it seems, had greatly impressed his enemies with his bravery." (S5)
30. With his supply fleet destroyed and the British fleet in the river, Lévis lifted the siege
    on the night of May 16–17, 1760, and withdrew to Montreal. (S2, S3)

## AFTERMATH — WHY A WIN STILL LOST THE WAR
31. The French victory at Sainte-Foy changed nothing strategically: it could not be cashed in
    without a fleet to deliver siege guns, ammunition, and reinforcements, and the Royal Navy
    arrived first. (S2, S3)
32. Lévis retired to Montreal "for a last stand." In summer 1760 three British armies converged
    on Montreal (under Jeffery Amherst, William Haviland, and Murray). (S2, S3, S4)
33. Final capitulation: Montreal surrendered to Amherst on Sept 8, 1760, ending the French
    rule of New France (the formal capitulation of New France). (S2, S3)
34. At the surrender, Lévis wanted to keep fighting to preserve French military honor; the
    governor, the Marquis de Vaudreuil, refused to prolong destruction for principle. Lévis
    had the regimental colours burned to deny them to the British. (S3)
