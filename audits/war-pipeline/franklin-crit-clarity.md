# Franklin — CRAFT PANEL: NEWCOMER / CLARITY gate

> War content pipeline, critic step. Cold read of `franklin-draft.md` as a
> zero-knowledge but sharp 15-year-old. Flags undefined terms, unexplained leaps,
> named actors who arrive without a side-tag or scene-set, and passages that
> overwhelm. Accuracy is a separate gate; this gate asks only "can a smart
> newcomer follow it without getting lost."

**VERDICT: PASS WITH ONE MUST-FIX.** The draft is unusually disciplined about
inline-defining its own terms — most war jargon is glossed at first use, which is
exactly the house rule. One MUST-FIX (an undefined corps/army-name leap in §1) and
a short list of SHOULD-FIX gloss/scene gaps. No LOST passages.

---

## Term-by-term (newcomer cold read)

### Things the draft glosses WELL (CLEAR — credit where due)

- **flanking march** — CLEAR. Defined inline at first use: "A flanking march, here,
  just means swinging wide around an enemy's side instead of hitting him head-on."
  Textbook execution of the house rule.
- **works / field fortifications** — CLEAR. "the soldiers' word for field
  fortifications, meaning a ditch fronting an earthen wall thrown up high enough that
  an attacker has to climb it under fire." Excellent — a newcomer now owns the word
  for the rest of the piece.
- **frontal assault** — CLEAR. "marching infantry straight at a fortified position
  head-on, rather than slipping around its flank." Defined exactly where it's first
  used as a term.
- **salient** — CLEAR. "a bulge in the line that juts toward the enemy and catches
  fire from three sides." Glossed at first use.
- **cotton gin** — CLEAR. "a barn-like building for cleaning cotton." A newcomer
  would not know this; the draft assumes nothing. Good.
- **retrenchment line** — CLEAR. "a fallback wall behind the first one." Glossed.
- **bridgehead** — CLEAR. "a defended semicircle guarding the southern approaches and
  the crossings to its rear." Defined in context.
- **no artillery preparation** — CLEAR. The Gettysburg contrast ("roughly a hundred
  and fifty guns hammered the Union line for about two hours first, trying to soften
  it up before the infantry stepped off") fully explains what artillery prep *is* and
  why its absence is lethal. A newcomer learns the concept by its absence. Strong.
- **"the Pickett's Charge of the West"** — CLEAR. The draft doesn't assume the reader
  knows Pickett's Charge; it explains it in the same breath (the Gettysburg
  bombardment + the "famous charge"), and even ranks Franklin as worse. Well handled.
- **U.S. Colored Troops (USCT)** — CLEAR. Glossed inline: "Black soldiers, the largest
  such force yet assembled on any Civil War battlefield, many of them formerly enslaved."
- **Side-tags** — CLEAR and consistent. Every named person carries (North)/(South) on
  first mention per section, and tags reset per section as intended. A newcomer can
  always tell who's on which side. This is the single biggest clarity win in the draft.

### MUST-FIX

- **"the Army of Tennessee" / "Army of the Ohio" / "an attached corps" — NEEDS-GLOSS
  (MUST-FIX, §1).** In §1 the draft says the "Confederate Army of Tennessee — one of
  the South's two great field armies." That much is glossed (good — a newcomer learns
  it's a named army, and that the South had two big ones). BUT on the Union side, the
  very next paragraph hits the reader with "his own Army of the Ohio plus an attached
  corps" and later "his own Army of the Ohio / XXIII Corps + the attached IV Corps"
  concepts, and a 15-year-old does not know what a **corps** is, or that armies are
  built out of corps, or why a force can be "his own army *plus* an attached corps."
  The term **corps** then recurs load-bearingly in §3 ("his corps would lead the main
  assault"). It is never defined. **MUST-FIX:** gloss "corps" once, at first use — one
  clause is enough (e.g., a corps = a major chunk of an army, tens of thousands of men
  under one general). Without it, "an attached corps," "Cheatham's corps," and "most
  of Lee's corps had not yet come up" (the latter isn't in the draft but the *concept*
  recurs) are opaque. This is the one place a sharp newcomer actually loses the
  org-chart thread. The Confederate "two great field armies" gloss shows the draft
  knows how to do this — it just didn't do it for *corps*.

  Note: the draft is otherwise careful — it even glosses "Army of Tennessee" as a
  proper-named army the first time. The gap is specifically the unit *below* an army
  (corps), which carries real weight in §3.

### SHOULD-FIX

- **"entrenched / entrenchments / dug in" — borderline, SHOULD-FIX (§1).** The draft
  uses "put his men to digging in" and later "entrenched" before it formally defines
  "works." By the time "works" is glossed in §2, the reader is fine — but in §1, "put
  his men to digging in" appears with no explanation of *why* digging matters or what
  it produces. A newcomer can infer it, so this is CLEAR-ISH, not LOST — but a
  four-word nod in §1 ("digging in — throwing up dirt walls to fight behind") would
  remove the inference burden and pre-load the §2 "works" gloss. SHOULD-FIX.

- **"corps" appears before "Army of the Ohio" is contextualized — SHOULD-FIX ordering
  (§1).** Tied to the MUST-FIX above: the phrase "a combined column — his own Army of
  the Ohio plus an attached corps, something on the order of twenty-seven thousand men"
  asks the newcomer to hold three unfamiliar org concepts (a named army, an attached
  corps, the idea that you combine them) in one sentence. Even after "corps" is
  glossed, consider simplifying the sentence so the *number* (27,000 men) is the
  takeaway and the org-chart is secondary. SHOULD-FIX.

- **"the Duck River east of Columbia" — NEEDS-GLOSS (light, §1).** "Columbia" appears
  with no scene-set — a newcomer doesn't know Columbia is a town south of Spring Hill,
  so "crossed the Duck River east of Columbia" floats geographically. The sentence
  still functions (the point is Hood swung wide and got behind Schofield), so this is
  SHOULD-FIX not MUST — but one half-clause locating Columbia (the town Schofield was
  retreating *from*) would anchor it. The draft *does* later locate Spring Hill ("about
  twelve miles" from Franklin) and Franklin (the destination), so Columbia is the one
  unanchored place name.

- **"Carter House" vs "Carter cotton gin" vs "the Carter family" vs "Tod Carter" —
  SHOULD-FIX, potential name-collision confusion.** In §2 the draft introduces the
  Carter House and the Carter cotton gin as two separate map features. In §4 it reveals
  the Carter *family* owned the house and that Tod Carter was their son. A sharp
  newcomer *can* track this, and the §4 reveal ("the son of the very family whose house
  anchored the Union center") is actually a deliberate, effective payoff. The risk is
  only that in §2/§3 the reader meets "Carter House" and "Carter cotton gin" as cold
  place-names and might assume they're unrelated to a family — then §4 retroactively
  connects them. This is mostly fine (and arguably good craft), but consider a
  one-word signal in §2 that "Carter" is a family name (e.g., "the Carter family's
  house"), so the §4 payoff feels like a deepening rather than new information.
  SHOULD-FIX, low priority.

- **"Cox's headquarters" — minor, CLEAR-ish.** Cox is introduced and side-tagged in §2
  ("the headquarters of Brig. Gen. Jacob D. Cox (North)") but then never appears again.
  A newcomer briefly wonders why this name matters. Not a clarity defect (it's
  scene-setting the house), but Cox is a dangling name; could be trimmed to "a Union
  general's headquarters" if he carries no later weight. SHOULD-FIX, optional.

- **"battery / a battery or two" — NEEDS-GLOSS (light, §2).** "Hood sent his men forward
  behind only a battery or two." A newcomer may not know a **battery** = a group of
  cannons (typically ~4–6 guns). The Gettysburg contrast ("a hundred and fifty guns")
  partly rescues it by implying batteries are made of guns, but "a battery or two" as a
  unit of artillery isn't defined. One-word gloss. SHOULD-FIX.

- **"the broken center" / "rally the broken center" — CLEAR in context.** A newcomer
  understands from the surrounding action (the breach at the Pike) that "the center"
  means the middle of the Union line where it broke. No fix needed; flagging only to
  confirm it reads CLEAR, which it does.

- **"the irreplaceable veteran officer corps" idea — CLEAR.** The draft does the work:
  "An army can replace privates. It cannot quickly replace the men who lead it." A
  newcomer fully gets *why* losing generals/colonels matters more than losing
  privates. Strong — this is the kind of mechanism-explanation the house rules want.

## Unexplained leaps (newcomer follow-the-logic check)

- **Why Schofield fortifies with his back to the river — CLEAR.** The draft explicitly
  explains it ("he needed to hold a perimeter long enough to get his wagon trains
  across the Harpeth"). A newcomer might otherwise think fortifying with a river behind
  you is insane; the draft heads that off. Good.

- **Why holding fire to avoid shooting their own men creates a lull the attackers
  exploit — CLEAR.** The cause-and-effect ("the last half-mile of the Confederate
  advance went largely uncontested") is spelled out. A newcomer follows it.

- **Why a tactical *victory* gets abandoned — CLEAR.** "Holding the town had never been
  his goal" plus the wagon-train logic makes the strange withdrawal legible. Good.

- **The jump from Franklin to Nashville (Dec 15–16) — CLEAR.** The draft sets up
  Nashville as "the sequel" and explains the gutted army limped there. A newcomer
  isn't dropped.

- **Cleburne's January 1864 proposal arriving in §4 — CLEAR, well-staged.** The
  ten-months-earlier flashback is clearly time-stamped and the logic (he named slavery
  as a weakness → his own side buried it → that proves the cause) is laid out step by
  step. No leap.

No LOST passages. No leap that strands the newcomer.

## Overwhelm check (passages that pile up names/clauses)

- **§1 paragraph 2–3 (the strategic math) — borderline OVERWHELMING.** Schofield's
  ~27,000, Thomas's ~25,000, the trap logic, Army of the Ohio + attached corps — four
  numbers/units in close succession before the reader has a grip on "corps." Combined
  with the MUST-FIX above, this is the densest passage for a newcomer. The storytelling
  gate also flags this paragraph for pacing; the clarity overlap is real — fewer org
  nouns here would help both. SHOULD-FIX.

- **§2 middle paragraph (works / retrenchment / Carter House / cotton gin / salient) —
  borderline.** Five named map features in one paragraph. Each is individually glossed
  (good), but the cumulative load is high. A newcomer survives it because every term is
  defined, but it's the second-densest passage. Trimming the retrenchment-line aside
  (which never pays off later) would lighten it. SHOULD-FIX, low priority.

- **§3 closing sidebar (six generals' names) — CLEAR.** Five names + a sixth in one
  breath is a lot, but it's deliberately a *list* (the toll), the reader isn't asked to
  remember them individually, and the point is the *number*. Reads fine as a roster.
  Not overwhelming because the draft signals it's a tally.

- **Everything in §4 — CLEAR.** Despite heavy emotional and argumentative content, §4
  introduces people one at a time with full scene-set (Tod Carter, the McGavocks,
  Cleburne's proposal, Gist, Davis), each side-tagged. A newcomer is never lost here.

## Summary for the integrator

**MUST-FIX (1):**
1. Gloss **"corps"** at first use in §1 (the one undefined org-unit term that recurs
   load-bearingly in §3). Everything else is defined; this is the gap.

**SHOULD-FIX (priority order):**
1. Thin the §1 strategic-math passage so "corps"/army org doesn't pile up (clarity +
   pacing overlap).
2. Add a half-clause locating **Columbia** (the one unanchored place name).
3. One-word gloss on **battery** (§2).
4. Light gloss / pre-load on **"digging in"** in §1 before "works" is formally defined.
5. Signal "Carter" is a family name in §2 so the §4 payoff deepens rather than surprises.
6. Optional: trim dangling **Cox** name; trim the **retrenchment-line** aside.

**No facts requested.** Every fix above is a gloss/scene-set/trim using material
already present. The draft's inline-define discipline is otherwise a model of the
house rule — the corps gap is the lone real hole a sharp newcomer falls into.
