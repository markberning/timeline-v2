# STORYTELLING CRITIC REPORT — "The Acadian Expulsion"

**Section:** French and Indian War, off-the-battlefield theme (kind=theme, no battle stat block)
**Draft:** `audits/war-pipeline/fi-acadian-expulsion-draft.md`
**Fact pack:** `audits/war-pipeline/fi-acadian-expulsion-factpack.md`
**Verdict:** PASS (storytelling) — no heading grades NEEDS WORK or REWRITE; house voice clean.

---

## Overall read

This is a strong piece of narrative history. It opens on a thesis with teeth ("The
clearest atrocity of the French and Indian War was not a battle. It fell on farmers."),
sustains stakes through every section, and lands the human cost without sensationalizing.
The structural choice to give the Mi'kmaq their own full section as actors (not backdrop)
is the right call and is executed with real care. The piece resists the two failure modes
this kind of aftermath theme is prone to: dry chronology and number-dumping. Where numbers
appear, they are anchored to people (Noël Doiron's 120 relatives; the women "carrying
their children in their arms"). The voice is plain, controlled, and morally clear without
preaching. The closing "Naming it" section handles the ethnic-cleansing/genocide debate
honestly without letting it soften the documented removal — exactly what the fact pack asked.

No play-by-play tedium. No list-of-facts stretches. Pacing is well managed: it slows down
deliberately at the two set-pieces (the Grand-Pré church, the December shipwrecks) and
moves briskly through context.

## Heading-by-heading

### (Opening / lede, lines 5–11) — STRONG
The three-sentence hook is excellent. "It fell on farmers." is a hard, short landing.
The paragraph at line 7 does a lot of work fast (who, where, what was done, that it could
not be undone) and the rhythm of the short closers ("Thousands died. The survivors never
got their farms back.") earns its weight. Line 9 explicitly promises the Mi'kmaq thread,
which sets reader expectation correctly. Nothing to fix.

### The marsh farmers — STRONG
The aboiteau explanation (line 19) is the kind of mechanism-not-outcome writing the house
rules want, and it makes the later burning land harder. "Do this for a few years and the
trapped marsh slowly rinses itself of salt and turns into rich black pasture and cropland"
is vivid and concrete. "They were, by the standards of the day, doing very well" is a quiet,
effective setup for the fall. Good restraint on the contested population number ("somewhere
between 13,000 and 14,000"). No changes needed.

### Caught between two empires — STRONG
The bind is dramatized exactly as the fact pack asked. "So they tried to thread a needle"
and "It was a genuinely awkward position, and it depended entirely on nobody forcing the
question. Eventually somebody did." — that last line is a clean pivot that creates dread
without melodrama. Full-rank-first satisfied ("Governor Richard Philipps"). The Lawrence
deportation-orders pull-quote at line 37 is well placed as foreshadowing. STRONG.

### The Mi'kmaq were fighting their own war — STRONG
This is the most important structural section and it delivers. It names the Mi'kmaq as
initiators ("on their own initiative and for their own reasons"), gives the three confirmed
chiefs by name, and is scrupulous about Le Loutre: "He was an instigator and an ally, but
he was not Mi'kmaq, and the Mi'kmaq did not need him to tell them their land was being taken."
That is precisely the fact-pack guardrail. The bounty paragraph (line 53) names British
brutality plainly without flinching: "This was a price on the heads of a people for living
where they had always lived." The handoff ("which is where the Acadians' story turns")
keeps the spine intact. STRONG.

### The pretext — GOOD
Tight and effective. "It also handed Lawrence the excuse he had been looking for" is the
right framing, and the section is honest about the pressed-into-service Acadians. The only
reason this is GOOD rather than STRONG is a small pacing dip: the section is almost entirely
procedural (siege → defenders found → oath → refusal → jail → decision) and the human stakes
recede slightly compared to its neighbors. Not a defect, just the valley between two peaks —
the prose at "the Acadians would be deported. All of them." (line 63) does enough to recover
the menace. No change required.

### The Grand Dérangement — STRONG
The set-piece. "They are chilling in their plainness" earns the quote that follows. The
Grand-Pré church scene is the emotional center and is paced beautifully: Winslow's
self-aware "very disagreeable to my natural make & temper," then "Then he delivered the
blow." The decision to preserve the 1755 spelling and flag it parenthetically is the honest
move the fact pack wanted. "the marsh farms the Acadians had built over a century became
smoke" is a strong, compressed image that pays off the opening section. The split-families
paragraph (line 81) lands hard and plainly. STRONG.

### Scattered — GOOD
Carries the "refused at every port" cruelty well: "People who had owned good farms a few
months earlier were now beggars in a strange country whose language they did not speak."
The destination numbers (line 89) are the densest data run in the piece, but the section
immediately re-humanizes them, so it does not tip into list-tedium. GOOD; no fix needed.

### The ships that sank — STRONG
The deadliest phase, handled with the right gravity. "It is worth slowing down on one family,
because the numbers go numb otherwise" is an earned authorial move (rare meta, but it is
about reading the story, not the narrator performing). The Noël Doiron paragraph — "About
120 people of one family. None of them survived." — is the single best human-cost beat in
the piece. The honest close ("thousands, and we cannot give the exact number") respects the
fact pack's uncertainty guidance instead of inventing precision. STRONG.

### What they became — STRONG
The turn from atrocity to survival is well judged and does not feel like a tidy bow.
"The dykes the Acadians had built, someone else now farmed" is a quietly devastating line
that keeps the loss present even in the recovery section. The Acadien → Cadien → Cajun
etymology lands with the right note: "A culture that the British government had set out to
erase did not disappear." The Evangeline coda is proportionate and doesn't overstay. STRONG.

### Naming it — STRONG
The hardest section to get right and it is right. "Strip away the poem and the propaganda
and the military memos, and what is left is simple to state and hard to look at" is a strong
opener. It presents the scholarly debate squarely (Faragher / Grenier / Basque / Johnston),
grants the British military logic honestly, and then refuses to let the debate soften the
fact: "But it does not change what was done, or to whom, or how many never came home." The
final return to the Mi'kmaq and the land closes the thematic loop opened in the lede. STRONG.

## House-voice compliance

- **No em-dashes in prose:** PASS. The only `—` characters are the standard attribution
  dashes in the two `> quote:` lines (`— Charles Lawrence`), which is the quote-attribution
  convention, not prose em-dashes. Body prose uses parenthetical appositives and commas
  throughout (e.g. line 7, line 17). Zero en-dashes.
- **No meta-narrator:** PASS. The two near-misses are legitimate: "This is the story of how
  that happened" (line 9, a scope-setting promise) and "It is worth slowing down on one
  family, because the numbers go numb otherwise" (line 107). Both serve the reader's
  experience of the material rather than the narrator performing about the telling. Neither
  is the "let me tell you a tale / dear reader" register the rule bans.
- **Little bold:** PASS. Zero bold (`**`) anywhere in the body.
- **Full-rank-first:** PASS. "Governor Richard Philipps," "Governor Edward Cornwallis,"
  "Lieutenant Colonel Robert Monckton," "Lieutenant Colonel John Winslow," "Lieutenant
  Governor Charles Lawrence" all carry full rank/title on first mention. Mi'kmaq chiefs and
  Le Loutre are tagged with their nation/origin on first mention per the war side-tag rule
  ("(Mi'kmaq)", "(French)", "(British)").
- **Cross-refs as standalone pill lines:** PASS. Both cross-refs are on their own lines —
  `[Louisbourg](/war-french-indian/pitts-turn/louisbourg)` (line 101) and
  `[The Treaty of Paris & the Proclamation Line](...)` (line 137) — not inlined into prose.

No house-voice violations.

## Optional polish (NOT blocking — shouldFix only)

1. **Line 99/101 — Louisbourg pill placement reads slightly abrupt.** "In July 1758 the
   British captured the great French fortress of Louisbourg, on Cape Breton Island." is
   immediately followed by the bare `[Louisbourg]` pill, then "With Louisbourg gone…" The
   sentence already names and describes Louisbourg, so the pill on its own line lands a touch
   redundantly. Consider whether the pill belongs after the "With Louisbourg gone" sentence
   instead, so the link sits at the natural "go deeper" beat rather than mid-thought. Purely
   a flow nicety.

2. **Line 47 — chief-by-chief sentence rhythm.** The three chiefs are introduced in three
   parallel short sentences ("Chief Jean-Baptiste Cope… Chief Étienne Bâtard… Chief Paul
   Laurent…"). It is clear and correct, but reads marginally like a roster after the strong
   narrative momentum of the paragraph above. Optional: weave Laurent into one of the first
   two rather than a standalone "is named among their war leaders," which is the one slightly
   flat clause in an otherwise excellent section.

Neither affects the verdict.
