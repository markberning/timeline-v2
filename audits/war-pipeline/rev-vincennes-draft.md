note: The longest-odds march of the war. With no hope of holding the Illinois Country against the British governor of Detroit's spring offensive, a 26-year-old Virginia colonel chose the one move the enemy thought impossible: a midwinter march of about 180 miles across flooded prairie, the last week wading icy floodwater to the chest, to attack first. The teeth in the bluff were four captured warriors tomahawked in sight of the fort gate.

## SECTION: the-fort-changes-hands
EYEBROW: Vincennes · The setup
TITLE: The fort changes hands twice

To understand the march, you have to understand what George Rogers Clark (American) was trying to save, and how he had won it the first time, almost without firing a shot.

The Illinois Country was the cluster of old French villages along the Mississippi and the lower Wabash rivers, in the heart of what the eighteenth century called the West: Kaskaskia, Cahokia, and Vincennes, small French-speaking towns left over from France's old North American empire and lightly held, since 1763, by the British. Their people were the habitants (the French Catholic farmers and traders who had lived there for generations, under whatever flag flew). These were "the posts": the scattered fortified villages that were the only European settlements for hundreds of miles.

From Detroit, the British lieutenant governor Henry Hamilton (British) ran the frontier war. On orders from London, beginning in 1777, he recruited, supplied, and dispatched Native war parties (with British and Loyalist officers attached) against the Kentucky and Pennsylvania-Virginia frontiers. Hundreds of settlers, women and children among them, were killed or carried off, and Kentucky's stations lived under near-siege. The American frontier had a name for him: the Hair-Buyer General. Hold that name; we will come back to what it does and does not mean.

Clark was a 25-year-old Virginia surveyor turned Kentucky militia leader, and his plan was to strike the source rather than keep absorbing the raids. He sold the governor of Virginia, Patrick Henry, on a stroke at the lightly held British posts of the Illinois Country, and got, that winter, a public commission to defend Kentucky and a secret one to seize Kaskaskia. In May 1778 he started down the Ohio River with about 150 recruits, based for a month at Corn Island at the Falls of the Ohio (the founding nucleus of Louisville), and shot the falls downriver on June 24 under a solar eclipse his men read as an omen (an eclipse did occur that day).

What happened next was almost bloodless. After a long overland march, Clark's men crossed the Kaskaskia River at night on July 4, 1778 and took the town and its British-appointed commandant without firing a shot. Cahokia followed days later, equally bloodless. The lever was news Clark carried in his pocket: France, the habitants' old mother country, had allied with the United States that February. The French-speaking villagers swore the American oath. Vincennes was won the same way, by a man with no soldiers at all: Father Pierre Gibault, the Kaskaskia priest, volunteered to ride over and talk the town around, and in mid-July he brought its habitants to the American oath. Virginia, astonishingly, organized the whole region as Illinois County, Virginia, that winter.

PILL: /war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/native-nations | The nations caught between two empires, told whole

Hamilton did not let it stand. On October 7, 1778 he left Detroit with a mixed force, a core of regulars of the King's 8th Regiment, Detroit volunteer militia, Indian Department officers, and Native allies who joined along the way, a force that grew to several hundred by the time it arrived. He moved 600 miles by the Maumee and Wabash rivers in 71 days. At Vincennes, the local militia holding the fort for the Americans melted away, and Capt. Leonard Helm (American), with only a handful of men, surrendered Fort Sackville on December 17 with the honors of war. The habitants of Vincennes swore back to the British crown. It was the second time the town had changed hands in six months, and it would not be the last.

Then Hamilton made the choice that handed Clark his opening. Rather than push on against Kaskaskia in the winter, he dismissed most of his militia and nearly all his Native warriors until spring, and settled in to rebuild Fort Sackville with a garrison of roughly 90. The plan for spring was to retake the Illinois towns and then scour Kentucky. It was sound, and it was unhurried, and it assumed he had until the weather turned.

He did not, because of a fur trader. Francis Vigo (American ally), a Sardinian-born merchant of St. Louis and a Spanish subject who leaned American, was detained by Hamilton's scouts at Vincennes, released under pressure from the town, and reached Kaskaskia on January 29, 1779 with exact intelligence: the size of Hamilton's reduced winter garrison, his cannon, and his intention to come for Clark in the spring. Clark now had the whole picture, and it left him one real choice. He could quit the country, or he could attack the British governor while the garrison was at its weakest and the season made the attack unthinkable. On February 3 he wrote the governor of Virginia what he meant to do.

QUOTE: I know the case is desperate; but, Sir, we must either quit the country or attack Mr. Hamilton... Great things have been effected by a few men well conducted.

MEANWHILE: The town of Vincennes | The twice-switched town | By February the habitants of Vincennes had sworn four oaths in a few years: to Britain under the old settlement, to Virginia in July via Gibault, back to the crown in December under Hamilton's guns, and they were about to swear to the Americans again. Read it as survival politics, not fickleness. A Catholic, French-speaking community far from any government traded oaths to keep its town in one piece, and twice it would be decisive: once when Gibault delivered the town without a shot, and again, in a few days, when the French militia inside the fort refused to die fighting their own friends and relations in the streets outside.

## SECTION: the-drowned-lands
EYEBROW: Vincennes · The march
TITLE: The drowned lands

On February 5, 1779, after Mass and a blessing from Father Gibault, about 170 men left Kaskaskia in the afternoon and turned east toward Vincennes, roughly 180 miles (290 km) away. Nearly half of them were French volunteers from Kaskaskia and Cahokia; the rest were Virginia and Kentucky frontier riflemen. It was an unseasonably wet winter, and the prairies between them and the Wabash River were not prairies anymore but sheets of rain-flood. Clark (American) wrote afterward that the plains lay "under water several inches deep." The first week was the easy part: the going was passable, game was plentiful, and the French companies were in good humor.

Then the rivers stopped them. On February 13 they reached the Little Wabash, normally two channels three miles apart, now a single flooded crossing about five miles wide. They built a pirogue (a dugout canoe), ferried the baggage, and waded, and lost about two days. On February 17 they hit the Embarras River, nine miles from Vincennes, too deep to ford and its banks drowned. They could not reach dry ground on the Wabash side. They camped wet, and the food began to run out.

The next days were the worst of it. By February 20 the provisions were effectively gone. Maj. Joseph Bowman (American), Clark's second-in-command, kept the campaign's plainest record; on February 20 he wrote that the men were "very quiet but hungry; some almost in despair," and on February 22, the whole of the entry: "No provisions yet. Lord help us!" A captured hunting party out of Vincennes, taken by canoe, gave them intelligence (the town did not suspect a thing, and the fort's numbers were confirmed) and a quarter of a buffalo from another captured canoe yielded a thin broth. They ferried the Wabash itself in canoe-loads between February 21 and 23, and then waded the drowned bottomlands beyond, where the water ran at times to the chest and shoulders and ice formed at the edges. Men too weak to walk were carried in the canoes; the strong hauled the weak. On February 23 the column reached Warriors Island, a timber rise above the flood about two miles from town, and saw Vincennes at last.

(Clark told some of this more cinematically a decade later, in a memoir written partly to salvage his reputation: blackening his own face with wet powder and raising a war whoop to drive the column into the last flood, the drummer boy floating across on his drum, an order to land the tallest men first. None of it is in Bowman's journal or in the letter Clark wrote months after the campaign, so it travels here only as Clark's later telling. The eighteen days in the flood need no embroidery; Bowman's terse journal carries the whole weight of them.)

This was the thing Hamilton had thought impossible. He knew the floods; he had judged that no one could cross the drowned lands in February, and he was very nearly right. The entire value of the march was that it could not be believed, which is why nobody in Vincennes was watching the east.

From Warriors Island, Clark sent the captured duck hunter ahead with an open letter to the inhabitants: friends of the United States should stay in their houses, and anyone found in the fort or under arms would be treated as an enemy. The letter was read aloud in the public square, and nobody warned the fort. At dusk on February 23 Clark marched his men toward town in full view, at maximum theatrical scale, the companies counter-marching behind a low rise so that only their flags showed, and the column carried far more colors than a force its size rated. The town read an army several times the real number. Whatever hope Hamilton had of a warning or a militia rally died before a shot was fired.

Around eight in the evening the Americans walked into the town, and the habitants welcomed them and dug up gunpowder they had hidden from the British, replacing Clark's rain-soaked stock. Tobacco's Son, the Piankeshaw chief whose town adjoined Vincennes and who had sided with Clark the year before, offered warriors for the assault; Clark declined, politely. He wanted no confusion in the dark, and the optics of the fight, an American force taking a British fort, were part of the point.

The firing on Fort Sackville began about eight. Hamilton (British) took the first shots for "some drunken frolic of the inhabitants, but going upon the parade heard the balls sing." This was where the bluff turned into a real fight, and the frontier riflemen won it fast. The stockade was unfinished and, in Hamilton's own word, so ill set up that its gunports barely shielded the gun crews. About 14 picked marksmen under Capt. Bayley, with the rest firing in relays from houses, fences, and a natural riverbank, poured fire through those ports. Hamilton's gunners were hit at their pieces; his 3- and 6-pounder cannon (light field guns, named for the weight of ball they threw) fired high and did nothing against covered men. By morning the fort's artillery was effectively silenced. The American loss in the entire siege was one man slightly wounded.

The next morning, February 24, Clark sent a demand under a flag of truce, and it was not gentle.

QUOTE: I expect you shall immediately surrender yourself with your Garrison prisoners at discretion... you may expect no mercy, for by Heavens you shall be treated as a murtherer.

Hamilton's written reply was that he and his garrison were not "to be prevailed on by threats to act in a manner unbecoming the character of British Subjects," and the firing resumed. Inside the fort, the arithmetic was already against him. He assembled the garrison: the regulars stood firm, but the Detroit French militia, in his own account, "hung their heads," and their sergeants muttered that it was hard they should fight against their own friends and relations in the town outside. Hamilton privately resolved to get honorable terms if he could.

And then, around midday, came the act that gave the bluff its teeth, and it is the part of this story that does not get softened. A British-allied war party, about 15 or 16 men, mostly Odawa and other Lakes warriors with a few French partisans, was returning to Vincennes from a raid toward the Falls of the Ohio, reportedly carrying scalps, and walked into the town not knowing it had changed hands. Clark's men ambushed them. In Bowman's flat tally: "killed two on the spot, wounded three, took six prisoners." Of the six captives, a French partisan was handed to the townspeople, and one young warrior was spared when a French volunteer in Clark's force, Lt. St. Croix, recognized his own son under the war paint and begged for his life.

The remaining four, five by some counts, were taken to the main street in front of the fort gate, in full view of the garrison, made to kneel in a circle, and tomahawked to death, and their bodies were thrown into the Wabash. Bowman wrote it without a flinch: the men "brought the Indians to the main street before the Fort gate, there tomahawked them, and throw them into the river." Hamilton's journal is the fullest account, and it is a hostile witness's account, so it is worth marking as his: the victims, he wrote, "began to sing their Death song, and were butcherd in succession." One of them, a young Odawa war chief named Macutté Mong, "having received the fatal stroke of a Tomahawk in the head, took it out and gave it again into the hands of his executioner who repeated the Stroke a second and third time," and was then dragged to the river and thrown in, with a rope around his neck, still alive. The blood, Hamilton added, "was still visible for days afterwards, a testimony of the courage and Humanity of Colonel Clarke."

Clark did it in full view of the walls on purpose, and he later said why. He believed he had "a fair opportunity of making an impression on the Indians... that of convincing them that Governor Hamilton could not give them that protection that he had made them to believe he could," and so he "Ordered the Prisoners to be Tomahawked in the face of the Garrison." It was aimed at two audiences at once: the Native nations weighing whether British protection was worth anything, and the garrison watching from the walls. The men who died were Lakes warriors far from home, not from the Wabash towns next door, and that was part of the calculation: the killing was staged to teach the watching nations a lesson at the expense of men who were not their own. It worked on both audiences. This is the cost of the frontier war, made visible, and it belongs in the middle of the story, not in a footnote.

One question the records do not answer is whether Clark swung a tomahawk himself. Hamilton wrote that one prisoner "was tomahawked either by Clarke or one of his Officers," and that Clark came to the parley "all bloody and sweating," and told him, with great satisfaction, how he had been employed. A British officer present, Lt. Jacob Schieffelin, stated flatly that Clark wielded the weapon. Clark and Bowman recorded no such detail. Both accusers were enemies with every reason to blacken Clark, and the silence on the American side proves nothing in either direction. Historians have never resolved it, and the honest answer is that we do not know.

By the afternoon of February 24 Hamilton was out of moves. Seven of his regulars were wounded, his guns were useless, his militia he privately called "treacherous cowards," and no relief could reach him. He proposed a three-day truce; Clark refused it. The two men met, by Clark's and Bowman's accounts at the church of St. Francis Xavier, by Hamilton's telling before the fort gate, and Clark, still washing off blood by Hamilton's account, made plain that if he had to storm the fort, not a single man inside would be spared. Hamilton at first refused to surrender at discretion. But the arithmetic did not change, and Helm, the prisoner inside, and Maj. Hay shuttled a compromise. On the morning of February 25, by terms that let the garrison march out with their arms and the officers keep their baggage, the 79 men of Fort Sackville grounded their weapons, and the American flag went up over what Clark renamed Fort Patrick Henry.

MEANWHILE: The far bank | The riflemen and the gunports | The whole fight turned on a piece of bad carpentry. Fort Sackville's stockade was unfinished, its gunports so poorly set that they barely covered the crews working the cannon. Clark's frontier riflemen, the best shots on the continent, lay behind houses and fences and a riverbank and fired through those gaps, and they killed the gunners faster than the gunners could load. The fort had artillery and the Americans had none, and by morning the artillery was silent and the Americans had lost one man wounded. A siege the textbooks would have called impossible was decided by marksmanship and an enemy's unfinished wall.

## SECTION: the-hair-buyer-in-irons
EYEBROW: Vincennes · What it meant
TITLE: The Hair-Buyer in irons

The first man the victory killed was on the American side, and it killed him by accident. During the 13-gun salute on February 25, about 26 captured 6-pounder cartridges exploded in the battery, terribly burning Maj. Joseph Bowman (American), Capt. Worthington, and four privates. Bowman, who had kept the journal that carried the whole march, died of the burns on August 14, 1779: the campaign's only American officer death, and he was killed by the celebration, not the fight. Clark's reports do not mention the accident.

Two days after the surrender the armed row-galley Willing came up the Wabash with about 40 more men, two days too late to fire a shot. There was still a prize to take, though. Helm led about 50 men in boats upriver and captured the British supply convoy coming down from Detroit, seven boats and about 40 prisoners, including Detroit's notorious magistrate Philip Dejean, and a fortune in goods. The fort, the governor, and the supply train were all in American hands within a week.

Then there is the matter of the governor himself, and the name the frontier had given him. Clark sent Henry Hamilton (British), Maj. Hay, and more than twenty others under escort about 1,200 miles east to Williamsburg, Virginia, where they arrived in June. The governor of Virginia now was Thomas Jefferson, and Jefferson and his council treated Hamilton not as a prisoner of war but as a war criminal, the Hair-Buyer, and jailed him in irons. He refused a parole whose terms would have gagged him. Eventually George Washington, and British threats to retaliate against American prisoners, pressed Virginia to relent; the scalp-buying charge was quietly dropped; and Hamilton was paroled in October 1780 and exchanged the following spring. He ended his career a colonial governor and died in office in 1796.

So what was the Hair-Buyer? The epithet was real propaganda doing real work, and the honest version is more uncomfortable than the legend. What the record supports is that Hamilton, on orders from London, organized and supplied Native war parties against the frontier and rewarded the returning parties, which presented scalps as proof, with goods. What the record does not support is that he paid a price per scalp. No such payment is documented; he denied it; Jefferson's own government, which had jailed him for it, eventually dropped the charge; and the standard scholarly treatment reads the per-scalp bounty as Clark's propaganda theme, not a fact. Meanwhile, American governments paid literal, written scalp bounties in the very same war: Pennsylvania, in April 1780, offered $2,500 in Continental currency for a Native scalp and $3,000 for a prisoner. Both of those things are true at once, and the page holds them together. Hamilton organized a brutal frontier war, and the specific crime his jailers named was one they could not prove and one their own side committed on paper.

PILL: /war-revolution/off-the-battlefield/native-nations | The nations caught between two empires, told whole

For the Native nations, the fall of Fort Sackville meant less than the legend says. Hamilton's capture removed one organizer and dried up one supply point, but Detroit, the real engine of the frontier war, never fell; Clark could never get the men or the money for the Detroit expedition, and it never came. The Odawa war chief Egushawa, Pontiac's political heir, who had advised Hamilton and run his scouts, escaped Vincennes with his war band intact and fought on into the 1790s. And the frontier war got worse after 1779, not better: Bird's invasion of Kentucky in 1780, La Balme's destruction the same year, the slaughter at Blue Licks in 1782, the massacre of peaceable Christian Delaware at Gnadenhutten in 1782, all of it still lay ahead. The Wabash towns that had watched the fort fall, the Piankeshaw and their neighbors, made or kept peace with the Americans, but American victory carried, for the Wabash and Lakes nations alike, the longer catastrophe of unrestrained settlement. The threat to Kentucky was blunted; it was not eliminated.

And what did the campaign win the United States? Less than the story it later told, and the two are worth keeping separate. It gave the infant American government real administration-in-fact at Kaskaskia, Cahokia, and Vincennes, a legal fig leaf in Illinois County, Virginia, and the founding of Louisville behind it. What it did not do is win the Old Northwest at the peace table. The traditional claim, that Clark's conquest delivered the territory northwest of the Ohio to the United States in the 1783 Treaty of Paris, is on the wrong side of modern scholarship. Britain did cede that territory, for reasons negotiated in Paris that most historians agree had little to do with Clark's 1779 march. Clark himself never wrote that he had won the Northwest; others wrote it for him, and the writing outlasted the man. The hero of Vincennes at 26 was a debt-ruined, hard-drinking, half-forgotten man by his fifties; a stroke and an amputated leg came in 1809, and he died in 1818, his own memoir, the source of the march's most cinematic details, written partly to salvage a reputation that the West had let slide.

The last word the country gave the campaign is a building. The George Rogers Clark Memorial, a sixteen-column granite and marble rotunda, was built from 1931 on the presumed site of Fort Sackville and dedicated in June 1936 by President Franklin Roosevelt; inside stands a bronze Clark beneath seven murals of the campaign. The fort's exact footprint has never been found, and lies somewhere within the park. It is worth seeing the 1936 building for what it is: high-imperial commemoration of exactly the conquest narrative the record will not quite support. The march was real, and as hard and as daring as any in the war. The legend that grew on top of it, the empire won by 170 men, is the part the historians keep trimming back. Downes put the trimmed-back version best: Clark "assisted the French and Indian inhabitants of that region to remove themselves from the very shadowy political rule of the British." The march made the legend. The negotiators made the map.

MEANWHILE: Detroit | The objective that never came | Vincennes was never the real prize. Detroit was, and Clark knew it: the post that armed and aimed the war parties, the place that, taken, might actually have ended the frontier war. He spent the rest of his command trying to get there and never could; Virginia could never resource the expedition. "My chain appears to have run out," he wrote. The governor of the western frontier sat in a Virginia cell, and the engine of the western war ran on at Detroit, untouched, for years.
