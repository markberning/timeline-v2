## INDEPENDENTLY BUILT MUST-COVER CHECKLIST

### A. Frame: what irregular war was (definitions + legal architecture)
1. Three-tier taxonomy: bushwhacking / partisan / regular raiding (Forrest belongs to raiding)
2. Partisan Ranger Act (1862 passage, 1864 repeal, Mosby/McNeill exemptions)
3. Lieber Code (General Orders No. 100, April 1863) — denies POW status to guerrillas
4. Bushwhacker vs. jayhawker vs. partisan ranger — inline definitions
5. Why border states bred it (mixed loyalty at the neighborhood level; no front line)

### B. The Confederate official stance on Black soldiers as enemy combatants
6. Confederate May 1, 1863 Retaliatory Act (Davis/Congress) — captured Black soldiers
   re-enslaved or returned to states; white officers of USCT regiments liable to execution
   for "inciting insurrection"
7. Lincoln's July 30, 1863 Order of Retaliation — Union's formal counter-threat
8. The de facto no-quarter practice: Edmund Kirby Smith's June 1863 letter urging
   subordinates to give "no quarter to armed negroes and their officers"; the pattern of
   condoning-never-punishing that made it effectively policy

### C. Fort Pillow (April 12, 1864)
9. Setting and garrison: ~600 men, ~half USCT (6th US Colored Heavy Artillery + 2nd US
   Colored Light Artillery section), half white 13th (West) Tennessee Cavalry Unionists
10. Commanders: Booth (killed), then Bradford
11. Confederate force: ~1,500–2,500 cavalry under Forrest (regular general, not a bushwhacker)
12. Surrender demand ~3:30 PM; Bradford refused; Confederate assault
13. The massacre: surrendering men shot/bayoneted, primarily Black soldiers; "No quarter!"
14. The color-line casualty split: ~70% white survival vs ~35% Black survival; ~1-in-5 Black
    taken prisoner vs ~3-in-5 white; ~14 Confederate dead vs ~300 Union dead
15. Congressional investigation: Joint Committee, Wade + Gooch, May 1864 report
16. Achilles V. Clark letter (Confederate sergeant, own side's eyewitness)
17. Forrest's official report quotes ("river was dyed," "negro soldiers cannot cope")
18. Grant's memoirs commentary on Forrest's expurgated later report
19. Forrest responsibility kept as an honest open question — massacre as fact, premeditation
    genuinely disputed

### D. Forrest → KKK (the postwar throughline)
20. KKK founding (Pulaski, TN, 1866); Forrest joins fall 1866, elected first Grand Wizard
    spring 1867 (Maxwell House, Nashville)
21. Klan's function: suppress Black political participation, especially 1868 elections
22. Forrest's 1869 dissolution letter; late conciliatory gestures not overstated

### E. Missouri–Kansas border war
23. Bleeding Kansas roots (1854–1859) as seedbed only
24. Quantrill: Ohio origins, schoolteacher, drifted west, formed Raiders; Cole Younger,
    Anderson, James brothers as members
25. Kansas City women's prison collapse (August 13, 1863) as Lawrence trigger;
    Josephine Anderson among dead
26. Lawrence Massacre (August 21, 1863): ~300–400 raiders, ~150–190 men and boys
    murdered, nearly all unarmed civilians; one raider killed; ~$1–1.5M damage; Lane's
    nightshirt escape
27. "Bloody Bill" Anderson: split from Quantrill, scalping/mutilation, knotted cord (~54
    knots), killed October 26, 1864
28. Centralia (September 27, 1864): 24 unarmed furloughed Union soldiers murdered on
    train, mutilated; Johnston's 147 men overwhelmed (123 killed); Frank and Jesse James
    present (Jesse-fired-the-shot attributed to Frank, not asserted)
29. James brothers as teenage bushwhackers → legacy hook to outlaw career

### F. General Order No. 11
30. Ewing's Order (August 25, 1863), four days after Lawrence
31. Forced depopulation: four Missouri counties, ~20,000 displaced; Cass County hollowed;
    "Burnt District"; ~one-third ever returned
32. Bingham's protest painting (*Martial Law / Order No. 11*)
33. The matched-pair moral point: Lawrence (guerrilla murder of civilians) ↔ Order No. 11
    (Union expulsion of civilians) — erasing the same line from different sides

### G. Mosby (contrast case)
34. 43rd Battalion Virginia Cavalry; "the Gray Ghost"; "Mosby's Confederacy"
35. The distinction: uniformed, commissioned, took prisoners — the line between partisan
    and bushwhacker drawn in the clearest terms
36. Front Royal executions (September 1864, Union under Custer/Sheridan; six or seven of
    Mosby's men killed)
37. Mosby's lottery retaliation (November 6, 1864; seven chosen, ~three executed, others
    escaped or spared)
38. Mosby's letter to Sheridan (November 11, 1864) stopping the tit-for-tat — the rare
    deliberate halt to the no-quarter spiral
39. Mosby's postwar: Republican, befriended Grant, consul to Hong Kong — inverse of Forrest

### H. Reckoning / war-crimes executions
40. Champ Ferguson: Cumberland Plateau guerrilla, claimed 100+ killed, personal-vendetta
    killings; Saltville murder of wounded USCT (5th US Colored Cavalry)
41. Ferguson's trial, unrepentant quote, hanged October 20, 1865
42. Ferguson + Wirz as the only two usually named executed for war crimes (Wirz cross-link
    only, not narrated); soft hedge for Magruder as possible third
43. The gap: atrocity was vast, reckoning was thin, late, and partial — that gap is itself
    a historical fact

### I. No-quarter pattern beyond Fort Pillow
44. Poison Spring (April 18, 1864): 1st Kansas Colored Infantry; "Remember Poison Springs!"
45. Saltville (October 2–3, 1864): wounded Black cavalrymen murdered; disputed toll
46. The Crater (July 1864): reference only → USCT section

### J. Legacy and closing throughline
47. The war's edges previewing Reconstruction-era racial terror (Forrest/Klan)
48. The border's hatreds not demobilizing: Quantrill dying after Appomattox; James brothers
    carrying bushwhacker ethos into 17-year outlaw career
49. Mosby ↔ Quantrill/Anderson/Ferguson as the section's moral spectrum

## COVERAGE ASSESSMENT

### A. Frame — COVERED (fully, all 5 items)
Definitions of bushwhacker, jayhawker, partisan ranger, raiding-cavalry all inline-defined.
Partisan Ranger Act passage/repeal/exemptions: COVERED. Lieber Code's denial of POW status
to guerrillas: COVERED with substance. Border-state causation: COVERED.

### B. Confederate official stance on USCT as enemy — THIN / PARTIALLY MISSING
**Item 6 (Confederate May 1863 Retaliatory Act):** NOT present in draft or fact pack.
The Confederate Congress's May 1, 1863, joint resolution — which formally declared captured
Black Union soldiers subject to re-enslavement and white officers of USCT liable to death
for "inciting insurrection" — is the legal/political architecture that sits directly behind
Fort Pillow and the pattern section. Its absence means the reader is told about a de facto
no-quarter practice without the formal governmental authorization that underpinned it.

**Item 7 (Lincoln's July 30, 1863 Order of Retaliation):** NOT present in draft or fact
pack. Lincoln's formal counter-threat — that the Union would kill one Confederate POW for
every Black Union soldier murdered — is essential context for why the no-quarter policy
became a political/diplomatic crisis and why prisoner exchanges broke down in 1864.

**Item 8 (Edmund Kirby Smith's June 1863 "no quarter" letter):** NOT present in draft or
fact pack. Smith's explicit written instruction urging subordinates to give "no quarter to
armed negroes and their officers" is the single clearest documented piece of Confederate
command-level authorization. Its absence is a notable fact-pack gap: the draft presents
the no-quarter killings as a pattern and frames the Forrest-responsibility question, but
never establishes that Confederate command was not merely permitting but explicitly
encouraging this practice at the theater-command level.

## FACT-PACK BLIND SPOTS

The fact pack is strong and well-organized. Three gaps matter:

**Gap 1 — Confederate May 1, 1863, Retaliatory Act (formal government policy):**
The pack covers the Lieber Code (Union's legal architecture) but does not include the
Confederate government's reciprocal formal act. The Act is directly load-bearing for the
"no quarter as pattern" section (Beat 7) and for the Fort Pillow context: Forrest's troops
operated in a political and legal environment where the Confederate Congress had officially
declared Black Union soldiers not entitled to prisoner-of-war treatment. The pack should
add this as a Beat 2 or Beat 7 item, with source (EJI calendar entry; Confederate Retaliatory
Act text at House Divided / Dickinson; May 1863 congressional journal).

**Gap 2 — Lincoln's July 30, 1863 Order of Retaliation:**
The Union's formal response to the Confederate retaliatory act — which threatened to
execute one Confederate POW for every Black Union soldier murdered — is absent. It is
relevant both as context for why this became a national political flashpoint and as the
reason prisoner exchanges (the Dix-Hill Cartel) effectively broke down in 1863–1864,
adding another dimension to why Fort Pillow was not an isolated event but a crisis at the
intersection of race, military law, and POW diplomacy.

**Gap 3 — Edmund Kirby Smith's "no quarter" letter (June 13, 1863):**
This is the documentary evidence most directly connecting Confederate command to an
explicit (not merely tacit) no-quarter instruction. Smith's Shreveport letter to Taylor,
urging "the propriety of giving no quarter to armed negroes and their officers," is
well-sourced (George Burkhardt's *Confederate Rage, Yankee Wrath* and multiple primary
collections) and belongs in Beat 2 or Beat 7. Its absence means the fact pack cannot
support a key scholarly claim (the "de facto Confederate policy" argument) with the
command-level documentation it needs.

**Non-blocking observation — Great Hanging at Gainesville (October 1862):**
The draft correctly stays within its scope. The Great Hanging (41 Unionist Texans
executed by a Confederate vigilante court) is a real Civil War atrocity but belongs to
the home-front/political-repression theme — it is not irregular/guerrilla war in the
section's sense, and its omission is not a gap for this section's scope.

**Non-blocking observation — John Hunt Morgan's raids:**
Morgan appears in the taxonomy section only (as an example of regular raiding-cavalry),
which is the appropriate level of treatment. No gap.

## VERDICT

**FAIL**

The draft is otherwise strong — structure is sound, Fort Pillow is handled with precision
and honesty, the Missouri border war and Mosby are fully covered, and the closing
throughline works. But three related items are MISSING that together constitute a material
comprehensiveness gap in the "no quarter as Confederate policy" throughline:

### MUST-ADD (blocking)

1. **Confederate May 1, 1863 Retaliatory Act** — the formal Confederate congressional
   resolution declaring captured Black Union soldiers subject to re-enslavement and white
   USCT officers liable to execution for "inciting insurrection." This is the legal/
   governmental foundation of the no-quarter pattern; its absence leaves the pattern section
   floating. Should be added briefly to the framing of the "pattern" beat (current section
   "A pattern, not a single day") — 2–3 sentences, not a full section. Needs to be added
   to the fact pack.

2. **Lincoln's July 30, 1863 Order of Retaliation** — Union's formal counter-threat (one
   Confederate POW executed for every USCT soldier killed in violation of the laws of war).
   Directly caused the collapse of prisoner exchanges in 1863–1864, which made the no-
   quarter practice politically load-bearing in ways the draft does not show. 1–2 sentences
   adjacent to the Retaliatory Act addition. Needs to be added to the fact pack.

3. **Edmund Kirby Smith's June 1863 "no quarter" letter** — the documented command-level
   Confederate authorization. Without it, the draft correctly states a massacre occurred and
   a pattern existed, but cannot support the "de facto Confederate policy" claim that the
   historiography (Burkhardt et al.) establishes. Should be cited in the fact pack as part
   of Beat 2 or Beat 7, and can be referenced in the draft's "pattern" section in one
   sentence with the Retaliatory Act.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER (non-blocking)

- The Centralia toll in the draft ("24 unarmed Union soldiers") matches one source but
  some sources say 22 — worth a quick cross-check against the *Official Records* before
  ship (the fact pack says 24, Western Border says 22; both are within the
  contested range, but the draft does not hedge this figure the way it hedges others).
  Current treatment is defensible; a range or soft hedge would be cleaner.

- The draft correctly attributes "Jesse fired the shot that killed Major Johnston" to Frank
  James's account, not as established fact. The Centralia massacre Wikipedia article and
  other sources confirm this attribution framing is correct. No change needed; noted as
  confirmation.

- The draft notes that "about one in five" Black soldiers was taken prisoner and "about
  three in five" white soldiers. This matches the fact pack's ~20% / ~60% figures. Solid.
