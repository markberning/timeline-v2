# STORYTELLING CRITIC · Bonhomme Richard vs Serapis (rev-bonhomme-richard)

*Critic pass 2026-06-12, against `rev-bonhomme-richard-draft.md`. Fact pack:
`rev-bonhomme-richard-factpack.md`. Bar: `rev-saratoga-final.md`. Format reference:
`rev-saratoga-critic-story.md`. Storytelling lane only; factual accuracy belongs to the
fact critic. No edits made to the draft.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

The draft is competent across most of its surface, and in one place it is genuinely excellent:
the Section 3 evidence chain on the famous line reads exactly as the fact pack asked for — a
detective story, not a footnote, each source arriving in sequence with its provenance built into
the prose. The naval mechanics are legible (the burst-gun logic, the lashing logic, the grenade
moment, the Alliance betrayal) and paced well. The Pearson honesty beat lands in Section 3 with
the right quiet emphasis. The voice is largely clean: no em-dashes in original prose, no
meta-narration, no drama-bold.

What keeps it from a clean PASS: Jones himself never becomes a difficult, vain, brilliant man.
He is DESCRIBED as prickly and vain, twice, but the draft never lets that cost him anything or
show in a decision or a texture. The cliffs-crowd atmosphere arrives and disappears in a single
clause in Section 2 without doing narrative work. Section 1 sets Jones up as "not an easy man"
and then delivers a biography in which every act confirms his heroism, which is exactly the
plaster-saint shape the brief warned against. And the battle's core wrongness — losing by every
measure, winning by refusal — is stated accurately in the dossier note but lands softer in the
prose than it should; the draft keeps returning Jones to competence rather than sitting in the
wrongness with him.

These are fixable. The bones are here and the structure is sound.

---

## BLOCKER

### B1. Jones is described as difficult but shown as heroic — the plaster-saint problem

The brief requires Jones to be "a difficult vain brilliant man, not a plaster saint." The draft
makes this claim about him in Section 1:

> "He was vain, prickly, chivalrous, and ferociously brave, all at once. None of those traits
> cancels the others."

And in the dossier bio:

> "Prickly, vain, and relentless, he was equally capable of writing florid apology letters and
> of fighting his own ship while she sank under him."

Those are good sentences. The problem is that no scene in the prose earns them. The Selkirk
silver is mentioned but compressed ("which ended with his crew making off with the family
silver") without naming the thing that makes it character-revealing: Jones ordered the raid,
the crew took the silver against his wishes or as the only prize they could get, and Jones's
response was a florid apology that was simultaneously a piece of self-promotion. The Tobago
killing is handled cleanly but neutrally. By the time the battle begins Jones is admirable
throughout: he makes the right tactical call (close and grapple when his guns fail), he fights
heroically, he refuses to surrender, he mourns his ship. There is nothing that costs him
anything or shows the vanity in action.

The one place vanity and difficulty are dramatizable in the battle itself is the concordat, the
agreement that left Landais nearly independent. The draft introduces this as a structural
background fact ("Hold onto that. It explains the worst thing that happened in the battle.") —
which is correct — but never revisits Jones's role in it. The concordat was a condition Jones
accepted (or failed to resist) to get the cruise funded, a piece of political maneuvering that
left him commanding a squadron in name only. His anguished report about the Alliance is
presented as pure victimhood. One sentence acknowledging that the structural failure of his
command was partly of his own making — that a man vain enough to take the cruise under bad
terms rather than delay it paid for it in those broadsides — would put the difficulty in the
story rather than in the adjectives.

**Fix:** In Section 1, expand the concordat sentence from structural background to a character
beat. The current draft says: "He could give orders. Whether his captains obeyed them was, by
the paperwork, up to the captains. Hold onto that. It explains the worst thing that happened in
the battle." This is setup — good setup — but it frames Jones as a man who was wronged by a
bad structure. Add one clause or sentence that puts him in the structure: something like the
fact that he accepted those terms to get the French money and the cruise he wanted, and that
the decision was his. E.g.: "He had accepted those terms to get the cruise. He got both." That
single move turns the concordat from a structural footnote into a character choice with
consequences, and the Alliance broadsides later hit differently.

### B2. The cliffs-crowd atmosphere is named but not used

The brief asks whether "the cliffs-crowd night-battle atmosphere lands." The draft introduces
this setting once, cleanly:

> "The moon rose about half past eight into a clear night, and the two ships hung there as
> one, bow to stern, the gun muzzles of each touching the other's planking. And the fight could
> be seen from the land. Crowds gathered on the high Yorkshire cliffs, from Scarborough down to
> Flamborough Head, to watch two ships burn and hammer each other by moonlight a few miles off
> their own coast: England watching an American raider fight under her own cliffs."

The last clause — "England watching an American raider fight under her own cliffs" — is the
right frame. But after this sentence the cliffs vanish. The crowds never see anything, never
react to anything, never give the battle a witness's perspective. The moonlit battle atmosphere
is named and not inhabited. The setting is one of the most unusual facts about this engagement:
a sea fight with a live civilian audience on the shore, the crowd as a mirror for what the
battle means to Britain. The crowd is also the locator map's design principle: the pack's map
caption frames this as Pearson "buying their escape with his ship" while crowds gathered to
watch. In the prose, it is one sentence of atmospheric setup and then gone.

**Fix:** Give the cliffs one more beat of real work, not just scene-setting. It does not require
much — a single return to the audience would do. The natural place is at the crisis around
10 p.m., when the Richard appears lost: something brief that reminds the reader what those
people watching on the cliffs were seeing — what it looked like from shore as a ship appeared
to be sinking in the dark and its flag kept flying. The point is not a set-piece but a return:
the frame was established; the frame should close.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. The battle's wrongness — "losing by every measure, winning by refusal" — is stated but not felt

The dossier note nails it: "Jones lost by every measure on the way to it... He refused to
surrender anyway, and it was the Englishman who struck." That is the battle's essential
dramatic shape. Section 2 executes the individual beats correctly but organizes them in a way
that keeps returning Jones to agency and competence, which softens the wrongness.

The most compressed version of the problem is this passage near the crisis:

> "Because the thing the dying Richard still had was her three remaining quarterdeck guns and
> Jones's refusal to stop."

That sentence is the right sentence — "the dying Richard" is good, and "refusal to stop" is
good — but the paragraph immediately runs into the tactical logic of the mainmast shot, which
lands Jones back in competence: he had concentrated his guns, he had a plan, the plan worked.
The wrongness should have been heavier just before this turn. The gunner shouting for quarter,
the flagstaff shot away, the master-at-arms releasing the prisoners — the draft renders these
correctly but moves through them quickly on the way to Jones's refusal. What gets underweighted
is the interval in which Jones is genuinely losing by every measure simultaneously. The gun
deck is wrecked. His own consort just hulled him. The gunner is shouting surrender. His ship is
flooding. And he is still fighting. The gap between the objective situation and his response is
where the battle's character lives, and the draft crosses it in one sentence.

**Fix:** At the crisis passage beginning "By about ten o'clock the Bonhomme Richard was, by any
reasonable judgment, lost," slow down before the refusal. The current draft lists the damage
efficiently and then pivots to the gunner. Let the piling wrongness land first — not melodrama,
just the accumulation held for a beat longer. The line "He refused, drove his own men back to
their guns, and the battle went on" is correct, but after the equivalent of everything going
wrong at once, it arrives too quickly and sounds too easy. One more sentence of the gap before
the refusal would do it.

---

### S2. Section 1 ends on logistics, not on the story's pull

Section 1's final lines:

> "Jones signaled a general chase. The most famous sea fight of the American Revolution was
> about to begin in the dark."

"The most famous sea fight of the American Revolution was about to begin in the dark" is
exactly the kind of meta-narrator announcement the voice rules prohibit. The writer is pointing
at the story and labeling it rather than inhabiting it. Compare the Saratoga bar's section
endings, which use structural pull ("One army on that river was shrinking; the other was
growing") — a fact that creates forward tension, not an announcement of what is coming.

The actual tension available here is better than the announcement: a convoy of forty ships
carrying the raw material of British sea power, Pearson standing the Serapis out to sea between
it and the raider, the light failing, and Jones in an old converted merchantman closing on a
brand-new purpose-built frigate with more than twice his heavy-gun weight. That is the story's
pull. The section can end on the tactical asymmetry rather than announcing that a famous fight
is starting.

**Fix:** Replace the closing sentence with the material of the setup: something that lands the
mismatch between the two ships — the old converted merchant hull versus the brand-new warship —
as the cliffhanger. The reader already knows Jones wins; the pull is how. E.g.: "Jones signaled
a general chase, and the old merchantman went toward the most powerful warship he had ever
faced." Or restructure so that the final beat is Pearson interposing — the one act that settled
the strategic question before a gun fired — and the cliffhanger is what comes next.

---

### S3. The surrender ceremony / "make a lord of him" paragraph loses the Pearson beat it is supposed to close

Section 3 handles the legend-kit items correctly (all properly badged). The Pearson paragraph
that follows is the page's honesty beat and it is well-executed. But the current ordering
places the "make a lord of him" apocrypha immediately before the Pearson paragraph, and the
apocrypha lands flat as a closer:

> "Whether he did, nobody can show."

That is the right thing to say and the right level of confidence. The problem is that it leaves
the reader on absence — there is no there there — right before the Pearson paragraph that
should be the section's emotional payoff. The transition from "nobody can show" to "And it is
the right place to be fair to Richard Pearson" is too explicit. The phrase "And it is the right
place" is the narrator managing the reader's expectations rather than just telling the story.

**Fix:** The "make a lord of him" line should kicker-close the apocrypha sequence, but the
transition into the Pearson paragraph should drop the "And it is the right place to be fair"
frame — just begin with Pearson's action, directly. The reader will feel the shift without
being told it is happening. Something like: "Richard Pearson lost his ship, and he had done
the one thing his orders required." Direct entry into Pearson cuts the narrator's hand.

## POLISH

### P1. "None of those traits cancels the others" — slight overreach

> "He was vain, prickly, chivalrous, and ferociously brave, all at once. None of those traits
> cancels the others."

"None of those traits cancels the others" is a thesis statement by the narrator, a closing
instruction on how to read the character. The Saratoga bar handles this kind of character
summary more obliquely, letting the reader hold the contradiction rather than resolving it. The
first sentence stands on its own. The second sentence can go.

---

### P2. The grenade anonymity beat almost works but missteps at the end

> "Almost nothing else is known about the man who did it, not even, for certain, his name.
> That blank is part of the story: the most important single act of the battle was performed by
> a sailor history cannot fully identify."

The first sentence is the better sentence. The second sentence explains the point of the first,
which the first sentence has already made. "That blank is part of the story" is the narrator
glossing what the reader already understood. Cut the second sentence.

---

### P3. The Alliance-betrayal passage over-glosses "rake"

> "To rake a ship is to fire down its length, bow to stern, the deadliest possible angle, and
> the Alliance did it to her own flagship."

This is the first naval page and the gloss is appropriate — the brief requires it. But the
parenthetical structure makes the sentence halt before the emotional payload (the Alliance
firing on its own flagship). The definition can be placed in a lighter construction so the
clause "and the Alliance did it to her own flagship" lands with more force. E.g.: "She raked
her own flagship — raking being to fire down a ship's length, bow to stern, the deadliest
angle in a sea fight — again and again." The verb-first construction keeps the drama moving
through the definition.

---

### P4. Section 2 closes on an anticlimactic detail

The section ends:

> "The wreck of the Bonhomme Richard, somewhere off Flamborough Head, has never been found."

This is a strong closer for the ship itself — and the pack endorses it as a fact worth landing.
But it follows immediately after the emotional close of the sinking (Jones's verbatim report
quote), and in context it reads like a footnote appended to the story rather than the story's
close. The sinking quote ("a little after ten, I Saw With inexpressible grief the last glimpse
of the B. H. R.") is the section's real close. The "never been found" fact could be folded into
the sinking sentence itself rather than standing as a standalone kicker: e.g., "…and the
Bonhomme Richard, somewhere on the bottom off Flamborough Head, has not been found since."
This integrates the wreck-detail into the moment of loss rather than appending it as a
subsequent fact.

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES (for the record)

- **Voice ship-blockers: clean.** No em-dashes in original prose; no drama-bold; no "as we'll
  see" or "our story." The Jones-report quotes carry their period capitals and original phrasing
  without interruption. Register is correct throughout.
- **Naval mechanics are legible.** The burst-gun logic (two guns fail → a gunnery duel is
  suicide → the only play is to close and hold) is a tight three-step that any reader can
  follow, and the setup pays off when Jones grapples. The lashing-together sequence, the
  vertical battle (tops vs. lower deck), the grenade mechanics, and the Alliance geometry are
  all clear.
- **The evidence chain on the famous line is the draft's best passage.** Section 3 runs
  Jones's report → Edinburgh Advertiser → London version → memoir → Dale 1825 as a clean
  sequence, each source arriving with its provenance and confidence level, no source treated as
  more authoritative than it is. The QUOTE block uses the right paraphrase. The chain reads as
  the detective story the brief asked for.
- **Pearson's honesty beat lands.** The passage on his court-martial, acquittal, knighthood,
  and the insurers' plate is clear, fair, and structurally placed. "The most honest reward of
  all" is the draft's best authorial phrase.
- **All apocrypha are correctly badged.** The famous line (Dale-1825, with alternatives), the
  sword-ceremony dialogue (not in either report, later invention), "make a lord of him" (is said
  to have, never contemporaneously recorded), the nailed-colors tradition (by the accounts) —
  all properly labeled.
- **The battle's true result is told with both columns.** The draft holds both truths throughout:
  Jones won, Pearson's convoy escaped. Neither is suppressed for the other's sake.
- **Landais's intent is correctly handled.** The broadsides are stated as fact; deliberate
  treachery is alleged and labeled ALLEGED; the court-martial is correctly scoped to the later
  voyage, matching the shipped theme's precision.
- **The concordat setup pays off.** "Hold onto that" plants the structural explanation for the
  Alliance's behavior, and Section 2 delivers on it. The structural setup is sound; B1 asks only
  that it also carry character weight.
- **MEANWHILEs are doing real work.** The Channel invasion-scare MEANWHILE (S1) makes the
  coastal panic concrete without inflating it. The forgotten second fight MEANWHILE (S2) is the
  right place for Cottineau's clean little action. The Texel MEANWHILE (S3) correctly handles
  the Dutch legal comedy without overclaiming it caused the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War.
- **Side-tags and pill routing are correct.** Jones/Pearson tagged on first mention per section;
  the pills route to built pages; no side-tags on non-combatants.

## SUMMARY FOR THE RECONCILER

**VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES**

2 BLOCKERS, 3 SHOULD-FIX, 4 POLISH.

The biggest storytelling problem: **Jones is described as difficult and vain but the prose never shows it costing him anything.** B1 (the concordat as a character choice, not a background structure) is the key fix: a single sentence makes the Alliance broadsides hit Jones as partly self-inflicted rather than purely victimhood, which is what the brief requires. B2 (the cliffs-crowd needs a return visit) is the atmosphere fix: the frame is planted but never closed, and the audience on the cliffs is one of the most unusual facts about this battle. S1 (the wrongness of losing-by-every-measure needs to be inhabited a beat longer before the refusal), S2 (section 1's closing sentence is a meta-narrator announcement, replace with tactical pull), and S3 (drop "it is the right place to be fair to Richard Pearson" and enter Pearson directly) are the shape fixes. The Polish items are compression and rhythm only. Nothing here touches a fact; every suggested rewrite recombines material already in the draft or its ledger. The fact critic should eyeball any new sentences before integration.
