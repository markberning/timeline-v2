## 3. What Came Before

### Sibley's Grand Design

- **Brig. Gen. Henry Hopkins Sibley** (CS) conceived the plan and won approval from Confederate President Jefferson Davis in 1861. | NPS Pecos; NPS Shattered Dream
- The objective: raise a force of Texans, march up the Rio Grande, conquer New Mexico Territory, then push west — securing the gold fields of Colorado, the silver mines of Nevada, and ultimately the Pacific ports of California, giving the Confederacy blockade-free international trade and trans-continental reach. | NPS Pecos; Essential Civil War Curriculum
- A Confederate victory here would have denied the Union crucial gold and silver financing and complicated Pacific naval operations. | ABT; Essential Civil War Curriculum
- The Confederate war aim was to extend a slaveholding republic to the Pacific; New Mexico Territory itself was thinly populated and the Confederacy hoped to recruit New Mexicans, Utah Mormons, and Colorado miners. | NPS Pecos
- Sibley raised roughly 2,500 mounted Texan troops (his "Army of New Mexico") and 15 artillery pieces. | ABT articles; Essential Civil War Curriculum

### The Road Up the Rio Grande

- **January 18, 1862**: Sibley proclaimed the Confederate Territory of Arizona (modern-day southern New Mexico and Arizona). | NPS Shattered Dream
- **Battle of Valverde, February 21, 1862**: Sibley's forces engaged Col. Edward Canby's Union garrison north of Fort Craig at a ford of the Rio Grande. Confederate tactical victory — Canby was driven back to Fort Craig — but the strategic prize (Fort Craig's supplies) was NOT captured. Confederate casualties: 36 killed, 150 wounded, 1 missing (out of ~2,590 engaged); Union: approximately 68 killed, 160 wounded, 25 missing/captured. [VERIFY: casualty figures vary across sources — Wikipedia gives Union 222 k+w, Confederate 183 k+w as alternate count.] | Wikipedia (Valverde); Battlefields.org Valverde; BLM
- Despite Valverde, Canby's garrison refused surrender, retreated to Fort Craig, and essentially bottled up the fort — denying Sibley those supplies and leaving a Union force at his rear. | Essential Civil War Curriculum; ABT
- **March 2, 1862**: Confederate advance occupied Albuquerque. | ABT articles
- **March 13, 1862**: Confederates occupied Santa Fe, the territorial capital. Sibley's forces discovered some provisions but failed to capture the main Union supply stockpiles before Union forces evacuated them. | ABT; Essential Civil War Curriculum; New Mexico Campaign Wikipedia
- With Santa Fe taken, the next objective was **Fort Union**, 90 miles northeast — the main Union supply depot for the entire Southwest. Control Fort Union = control the territory. | NPS Pecos; ABT

### The Union Response: Pike's Peakers

- Col. **Edward R.S. Canby** (US), commanding the Department of New Mexico, was bottled up at Fort Craig behind Sibley's line of march; he could not easily reinforce Fort Union directly. | Wikipedia; ABT
- The Union's answer came from Colorado: the **1st Colorado Volunteers** ("Pike's Peakers," recruited from the mining camps around Denver and Pike's Peak). | ABT articles; NPS Shattered Dream
- **February 22, 1862**: Seven initial companies departed Denver on a force march south. They collected three more companies at Fort Wise. The final leg: **67 miles covered in approximately 24 hours** — arriving at Fort Union around March 10–11, 1862 (sources give March 10 and March 11). | Essential Civil War Curriculum; ABT; NPS Shattered Dream
- Total march: roughly 300–400 miles from Denver to Fort Union. [VERIFY: distance estimate varies — "300 miles" per Essential Civil War Curriculum; some sources say ~400 miles for the full journey.] | Essential Civil War Curriculum
- The regiment was commanded overall by **Col. John P. Slough**, with **Maj. John M. Chivington** commanding the advance. | ABT; Wikipedia
- **March 22, 1862**: Slough's Union column departed Fort Union southward toward the Santa Fe Trail and Glorieta Pass. | ABT articles
- **March 25, 1862**: Chivington's advance guard reached Kozlowski's Ranch (east of the pass); Maj. Pyron's Confederate vanguard camped at Johnson's Ranch at the western mouth of Apache Canyon. | ABT articles

### The Strategic Chokepoint

- Glorieta Pass, at the southern tip of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains, was the only practical route for the Confederate army (and its supply wagons) along the Santa Fe Trail between Santa Fe and Fort Union. Control the pass = control the road. | NPS Pecos; Legends of America
- The pass had three key positions along its length, west to east: **Johnson's Ranch** (western mouth of Apache Canyon, Confederate camp and supply depot) → **Pigeon's Ranch** (hostelry/stage stop ~midway, the main March 28 battlefield, on Glorieta Creek) → the pass summit, with **Fort Union** ~90 miles further northeast. | Legends of America; Wikipedia; NPS hiking trail description

## 4. The Battle

### March 26: Apache Canyon

- Chivington commanded approximately 404–430 "picked men" (advance guard of Colorado Volunteers and regulars). | Wikipedia; NPS Shattered Dream
- Around 2:30 p.m., Chivington's force encountered Pyron's ~300–420 Confederates in Apache Canyon. | Wikipedia; ABT
- Chivington split his force to both sides of the narrow canyon walls (infantry climbed the hillsides above the trail) and caught the Confederates in enfilade/crossfire, forcing Pyron to retreat. | Wikipedia; NPS Shattered Dream; Legends of America
- After initial Confederate artillery threw back the first Union advance, a mounted Colorado company charged through the center, scattering the Confederate line. | Wikipedia
- Pyron regrouped roughly one mile back but was flanked again. | ABT articles
- Union cavalry charged the retreating Confederates down the Santa Fe Trail. Fighting ended around 4:00 p.m. | ABT articles
- Result: 70–75 Confederates captured; both sides broke off, agreed a brief truce to tend wounded. Chivington fell back east to Pigeon's Ranch; Pyron fell back west to Johnson's Ranch to await Scurry's reinforcements. | Wikipedia; NPS Shattered Dream; ABT

### March 27

- No combat. Both sides received reinforcements. Scurry arrived with the main Confederate column; Slough came up with the main Union force. Both sides planned to attack the following day. | NPS; Wikipedia

### March 28: Pigeon's Ranch

- **Slough's plan**: Attack with two columns simultaneously — Chivington with ~450–490 men to cross southward over Glorieta Mesa and swing west behind Confederate lines; Slough with ~850–900 men to advance along the Santa Fe Trail. | NPS Shattered Dream; ABT; Wikipedia
- **Scurry's plan** (unknown to Slough): Scurry was also advancing east along the trail with ~1,100–1,200 men. The two columns essentially stumbled into each other around 11:00 a.m. near Pigeon's Ranch. | ABT articles; civilwarmonths.com
- **The fighting**: Scurry launched a series of attacks (sources describe 3–5 assaults). The Confederate advantage was numerical — Slough had only ~850 men on the trail vs. Scurry's ~1,100. | Wikipedia; NPS; civilwarmonths.com
- The Union artillery on Windmill Hill (initially) performed well, silencing Confederate guns and repelling charges, but Confederate sharpshooters on Sharpshooter's Ridge kept Union infantry pinned. | civilwarmonths.com
- Confederate Maj. John Shropshire was killed assaulting the Union left. Confederate Lt. Col. Harold Raguet was mortally wounded around 3:00 p.m. when Scurry outflanked the Union right. | Wikipedia; ABT
- Around 3:00–4:00 p.m., the Confederate flanking move on the Union right succeeded. Slough ordered retreat east to Kozlowski's Ranch. Skirmishing continued until dusk. | Wikipedia; ABT
- **Scurry's report (direct quote):** "The loss of my supplies so crippled me that after burying my dead I was unable to follow up the victory." | NPS Shattered Dream (Teaching with Historic Places) — from Scurry's official after-action report
- **Slough's dispatch (direct quote, re: wagon train destruction):** "we took and destroyed their train of about 60 wagons, with their contents." | NPS Shattered Dream
- The Confederates had won the field. Scurry held Pigeon's Ranch. But the battle had already been decided.

### THE DECISIVE STROKE: Chivington at Johnson's Ranch

- While the main armies fought at Pigeon's Ranch, **Maj. Chivington's ~450–490-man flanking force** had crossed south of the trail, climbed the steep slopes of **Glorieta Mesa** (approximately 2,000 feet above the valley floor), traversed the mesa, and descended into the Confederate rear. | NPS Shattered Dream; Essential Civil War Curriculum; Wikipedia
- **Guide**: Lt. Col. **Manuel Chaves** of the 2nd New Mexico Mounted Volunteers was chosen by Canby and Chivington to guide the flanking force. Chaves, a skilled frontiersman and experienced soldier, led Chivington to a vantage point from which they could see the Confederate camp at Johnson's Ranch below. | Wikipedia (Manuel Antonio Chaves article); NPS Shattered Dream; search results citing ABT
- The mesa route covered approximately 16 miles through San Cristobal Canyon and across the mesa top. | NPS Shattered Dream; Essential Civil War Curriculum
- Scurry had left his supply train at Johnson's Ranch (also referred to as Cañoncito) under guard — variously described as ~200 guards. | civilwarmonths.com
- Chivington's men descended "the sheer cliffs in a half-hour's time" and overwhelmed the Confederate guard. | Essential Civil War Curriculum
- They spiked the one cannon left in camp, captured and paroled Confederate soldiers on guard, then destroyed the supply train:
  - **Wagons burned**: Approximately **60–80 wagons** across sources [CONTESTED — see Section 10]. Wikipedia: 80 wagons; Slough dispatch (NPS): "about 60 wagons"; NPS Shattered Dream: "60–80"; TSHAonline: "entire eighty-wagon supply train"; civilwarmonths.com: 73 wagons with 200 guards.
  - **Contents**: food, ammunition, medicine, forage, clothing, officers' baggage — essentially the army's entire forward supply reserve. | NPS Shattered Dream; multiple sources
  - **Animals**: approximately **500–600 horses and mules** killed or driven off [CONTESTED — see Section 10]. Wikipedia: ~500; civilwarmonths.com: "as many as 600." | Wikipedia; civilwarmonths.com
  - 1 artillery piece spiked; 17 prisoners taken. | NPS Shattered Dream; civilwarmonths.com
- The Texans had won the field. They had lost everything else.

## 5. The Retreat

- Learning that his supply train was destroyed, Scurry had no choice but to pull back toward Santa Fe. The Confederate field army could not be re-supplied in New Mexico — Fort Union was still in Union hands, Santa Fe's provisions were limited, and the Union was closing the ring. | ABT articles; NPS; Essential Civil War Curriculum
- **April 12, 1862**: Sibley ordered his army to evacuate Albuquerque and begin the retreat south to Texas. | New Mexico Campaign Wikipedia; Essential Civil War Curriculum
- The retreat route was brutal. Blocked on the east bank of the Rio Grande by Canby's forces, Sibley was forced to retreat down the **west bank or through the mountains**, crossing the **Jornada del Muerto Desert** — more than 100 miles with no reliable water. | Essential Civil War Curriculum; Legends of America; New Mexico Campaign Wikipedia
- Desperate Confederate soldiers looted and confiscated food from New Mexican civilians along the march, driving some residents to active resistance. | New Mexico Campaign Wikipedia
- Of Sibley's original ~2,500-man brigade, only about **1,500 reached San Antonio by summer 1862** (some sources say 1,700 reached El Paso by May 4, then continued to San Antonio). Roughly **500 were killed or died of starvation/disease; ~500 were captured** — approximately 40% total losses for the campaign. | Essential Civil War Curriculum; Legends of America; New Mexico Campaign Wikipedia
- The Confederacy never again mounted a serious military campaign aimed at the Southwest or the Pacific. | NPS; ABT; Wikipedia

## 6. Key People

**Union Side:**

- **Col. John P. Slough (US)** — Commander of the 1st Colorado Volunteers; led the main Union force at Pigeon's Ranch on March 28. Ordered the two-pronged attack plan that sent Chivington over the mesa while Slough engaged Scurry on the trail. Tactically defeated on the field but strategic victor. Sources: Wikipedia; ABT.

- **Maj. John M. Chivington (US)** — Led the advance Union force at Apache Canyon on March 26, then commanded the decisive ~450-490-man flanking detachment on March 28 that destroyed the Confederate supply train. His action at Glorieta Pass is widely credited with deciding the campaign. **Caution — forward reference:** Just over two years later, on November 29, 1864, Chivington — by then Colonel commanding the Colorado Military District — led the Sand Creek Massacre, in which his forces attacked a Cheyenne and Arapaho encampment in southeastern Colorado, killing an estimated 70+ to over 150 people (sources vary widely), mostly women and children. Congress's Joint Committee on the Conduct of War called it "a foul and dastardly massacre." He resigned from the Army under pressure. The same officer who saved the Union's hold on the Southwest became one of the worst perpetrators of anti-Native violence in the West. Sources: Wikipedia; NPS (Chivington biography); Warfare History Network.

- **Lt. Col. Manuel Chaves (US)** — Lieutenant Colonel, 2nd New Mexico Mounted Volunteers. A Hispanic New Mexican frontiersman and experienced soldier, chosen by Canby and Chivington to guide the flanking force over Glorieta Mesa to the Confederate rear. His local knowledge of the terrain was essential to finding the route and locating Johnson's Ranch from above. Contemporary sources say official military records "barely mentioned Chaves" despite his pivotal role. Sources: Wikipedia (Manuel Antonio Chaves article); NPS Shattered Dream.

- **Col. Edward R.S. Canby (US)** — Commander, Department of New Mexico. Canby had been bottled up at Fort Craig after Valverde but coordinated the overall Union response — directing the 1st Colorado Volunteers north and choosing Chaves as Chivington's guide. He later converged on Sibley's retreating army near Albuquerque, hastening the Confederate departure from the territory. Sources: ABT articles; Wikipedia.

**Confederate Side:**

- **Brig. Gen. Henry Hopkins Sibley (CS)** — Commander, Army of New Mexico. Sibley conceived and planned the entire southwestern campaign. He was NOT present on the battlefield at Glorieta Pass — he remained to the rear, reportedly ill (some accounts suggest intoxication, though this is disputed [VERIFY]). The failure of his campaign ended his military credibility; he was later criticized for mismanaging the retreat. Sources: NPS Pecos; Wikipedia; ABT; New Mexico Campaign Wikipedia.

- **Lt. Col. William R. Scurry (CS)** — Field commander at Pigeon's Ranch, March 28. Led Scurry's column (the main Confederate force) in what was a genuine tactical field victory over Slough. His own after-action report acknowledged the disaster: "The loss of my supplies so crippled me that after burying my dead I was unable to follow up the victory." Also noted: "I do not know if I write intelligently. I have not slept for three nights." Sources: NPS Shattered Dream (direct quote); Wikipedia; TSHAonline.

- **Maj. Charles L. Pyron (CS)** — Commander of the Confederate vanguard at Apache Canyon on March 26. Led roughly 300–420 Texans in the first clash; was outflanked and driven back by Chivington's force with ~70 men captured. Remained with the army through the retreat. Sources: Wikipedia; ABT; TSHAonline.

## 7. What It Meant

- **"The Gettysburg of the West"** — the phrase is widely used by historians and the NPS. The CWSAC assigned Glorieta Pass its highest possible rating, Priority I (Class A), placing it on the same strategic tier as Gettysburg and Antietam. Only 11 battles nationally received this designation. | NPS Pecos (battlefield designation); NPS CWSAC; ABT
- The battle was the **only serious Confederate attempt to conquer and occupy Union territory west of the Mississippi River** — the westernmost military operation of the entire war. | Wikipedia; NPS
- The Confederate objective was explicitly to extend a slaveholding republic to the Pacific, with access to gold, silver, and blockade-free ports — a prize that, if achieved, could have fundamentally altered the war's financing and international dimension. | ABT; Essential Civil War Curriculum; NPS Pecos
- **What actually decided it**: Not the fighting at Pigeon's Ranch, but the destruction of the supply train at Johnson's Ranch. Scurry had won the field; it made no difference. A Civil War army in 1862 could not fight — or even exist — without its supply wagons. The Texans had nowhere to go but home. | Scurry's own report (NPS); ABT; Wikipedia
- **The role of New Mexico Hispanic and Colorado volunteers**: The decisive flanking maneuver depended on Manuel Chaves's intimate knowledge of the Sangre de Cristo terrain. Without that local knowledge, Chivington could not have reached the Confederate rear undetected. Colorado's "Pike's Peakers" provided the manpower; New Mexico's volunteers provided the geography. | Wikipedia (Chaves article); NPS Shattered Dream
- The Confederacy never again seriously threatened the Far West. Union control of New Mexico, Colorado, and access to California gold was secure for the rest of the war. | Wikipedia; NPS; ABT

## 10. Contested / Uncertain Facts

### Wagon count destroyed at Johnson's Ranch
- **Slough dispatch**: "about 60 wagons" (NPS Shattered Dream)
- **Scurry (implied)**: not a precise number in available excerpts
- **TSHAonline**: "entire eighty-wagon supply train"
- **Wikipedia**: 80 wagons
- **NPS Shattered Dream teaching guide**: "60–80 wagons"
- **civilwarmonths.com**: 73 wagons
- **Consensus range**: 60–80. The 80-wagon figure appears most often in secondary sources; Slough's own dispatch gives 60. Use "approximately 73–80 wagons" as a defensible range, or simply "some 60–80 wagons." Do NOT write a single precise number without flagging uncertainty.

### Animals killed or driven off
- **Wikipedia**: ~500 horses and mules
- **civilwarmonths.com**: "as many as 600 horses and mules"
- **NPS Shattered Dream**: 30 horses/mules slaughtered (likely just those killed in place; the rest driven off) — this lower figure may refer only to animals killed on site vs. total
- **Consensus**: Use "approximately 500–600 horses and mules killed or driven off."

### Casualties — overall spread
- The combined totals of 147 Union / 222 Confederate (Wikipedia/ABT) are the most commonly cited and include the Apache Canyon prisoners (92 Confederate captured); these figures are plausible.
- Legends of America gives 142 Union / 189 Confederate — close but not identical.
- NPS Shattered Dream gives narrative lower estimates for Pigeon's Ranch alone ("probably 20 killed" Union; "over 75 Union killed" per Scurry's inflated claim).
- **Flag for author**: The Confederate capture total of 92 is driven almost entirely by the ~70 prisoners at Apache Canyon on March 26. This is well-attested across sources. The killed/wounded for the Pigeon's Ranch fighting are more variable.
- The 1987 archaeological find of a mass grave containing 31 Confederate soldiers at Pigeon's Ranch confirms significant Confederate dead (TSHAonline).

### Chivington vs. Lewis — who led the supply train attack
- Mainstream accounts (Wikipedia, NPS, ABT) credit Chivington with commanding the flanking force and the supply train attack.
- The *Rio Abajo Press* (1864) alleged Chivington had to be persuaded and that Capt. Lewis led the actual attack. Wikipedia notes this dispute.
- **Flag**: The 1864 newspaper account may be politically motivated (Chivington was becoming controversial by then). No corroborating source has been found. Mainstream/NPS position: Chivington led. Author should note the dispute exists without treating it as established.

### Manuel Chaves's exact route and how he identified Johnson's Ranch
- Sources agree Chaves guided Chivington over Glorieta Mesa to a "high ground above Johnson's Ranch" from which they could see the Confederate camp below.
- The exact trail across the mesa and the descent route are not precisely documented in available secondary sources.
- Wikipedia (Chaves article) notes official military records "barely mentioned Chaves" — his role is attested in contemporary accounts but not extensively in formal records.
- **Flag for author**: Write Chaves as the guide who led Chivington to the vantage point; do not assert specific topographic detail of the descent route beyond "down the steep western face of the mesa."

### Sibley's absence from the field
- All sources agree Sibley was NOT on the Glorieta Pass battlefield. The reason is variously given as illness; some accounts allege intoxication, but this is not established in the primary sources available here.
- **Flag**: Do not assert intoxication without a primary source citation.

### 1st Colorado Volunteers march distance
- Essential Civil War Curriculum: "300 miles from Denver"
- Some sources say ~400 miles for the full Denver-to-Glorieta journey
- The 67-mile-in-24-hours forced march (final leg to Fort Union) is well-attested across multiple sources. Use that specific figure; be approximate on the total distance.
