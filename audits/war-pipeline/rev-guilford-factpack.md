# FACT PACK — Battle of Guilford Courthouse, March 15, 1781

*American Revolution (southern campaign). ONLY source for the battle page. The "ruinous victory": Cornwallis wins the field, loses a quarter of his army, and ends the war for himself in the Carolinas — marching to Virginia, marching to Yorktown. The page's meaning-beat is the Camden/Cowpens/Guilford arc: the same general who destroyed the second southern army now faces a third southern army that learned both lessons — and Greene built that army on Daniel Morgan's blueprint, with militia on the front two lines and Continentals waiting behind. Confidence tags: HIGH / MED / DISPUTED; HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags inline.*

**CROSS-LEVEL COHERENCE NOTE (read first):** the Revolution spine (`audits/war-pipeline/rev-world-war-final.md`, "the war goes south" section) and the Cowpens fact pack (`rev-cowpens-factpack.md`) both lock framings that this page must agree with: Cowpens used the three-line Camden-inversion plan; the Race to the Dan ran Jan 18–Feb 14, 1781; Cornwallis burned his baggage at **Ramsour's Mill** (departed Jan 28); the Fox "another such victory" quote is attributed to **Charles James Fox** in Parliament (see §4 and §7 for the evidence level). The Tarleton hand-wound at Guilford is documented here — the Cowpens fact pack correctly notes Tarleton's first verifiable wound was at Guilford, not Cowpens. The **spine may call Cornwallis's losses "25%"** — that is consistent with ~532 casualties from ~2,100 engaged (≈25.3%); confirm against the spine before ship. **ONE CONSISTENCY NOTE:** the spine and Cowpens factpack refer to the "war of posts" / Greene's "posts" strategy; the battle page should echo that framing without introducing a competing narrative.

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING

- **Battle of Guilford Courthouse, March 15, 1781** — fought at Guilford Court House, North Carolina, today the Guilford Courthouse National Military Park in Greensboro, NC (coordinates ~36.118, -79.871). The New Garden Road ran through the field; the courthouse itself stood at the north end. Southern campaign, American Revolutionary War. [HIGH]
- **Result: British tactical victory, strategic catastrophe** — Cornwallis held the field; Greene retreated in good order. But the British lost ~532 killed and wounded (~25% of the force engaged), could not pursue Greene, withdrew to Wilmington, and within six weeks marched out of the Carolinas altogether toward Virginia — and Yorktown. [HIGH]
- **The signature premise [HIGH]:** three months after Cowpens cost Cornwallis the fast quarter of his army, he forced his redcoats on a bruising 240-mile chase — burning his own baggage wagons to keep up — crossed the Dan into Virginia and back, and finally caught Greene at the courthouse. He won. And he won himself into a corner from which the only exit ran through a French fleet and a tobacco-field surrender. The battle's meaning is not in the afternoon's fighting; it is in the morning after.
- **Signature elements [HIGH]:** (1) **Greene's three-line plan** — a conscious copy of Morgan's Cowpens blueprint, militia on lines 1 and 2, Continentals in the third; (2) **the grapeshot controversy** — Cornwallis ordering his guns to fire into his own troops to break a melee (the incident is real as event; the framing as deliberate decision is Light Horse Harry Lee, writing 30 years later — see §4 and §8); (3) **Webster's death** — Cornwallis's finest subordinate, mortally wounded leading the assault on the third line, dead two weeks later; Cornwallis's grief the war's most documented British epitaph; (4) **Fox in Parliament** — "Another such victory would ruin the British army," the Pyrrhic-victory verdict from the Whig opposition benches (source chain: Annual Register 1781 — see §7).

## 2. BACKGROUND — THE ROAD TO GUILFORD

### After Cowpens [HIGH]
- **Cowpens, January 17, 1781:** Tarleton's ~1,100-man command — the fast quarter of Cornwallis's army — was destroyed in under an hour by Daniel Morgan's three-line ambush. British losses: ~80–90% of the force, ~600–830 men (see the Cowpens fact pack for the full locked ranges). Cornwallis, at Ramsour's Mill, learned of the disaster on January 18. [HIGH; ranges locked to the Cowpens factpack]
- **The baggage burning [HIGH]:** resolved that the pace of the march was the only hope of catching Morgan and Greene, Cornwallis **burned his baggage train at Ramsour's Mill** — excepting wagons carrying medical supplies, salt, ammunition, and the sick — and departed January 28, 1781. His entire army now moved as light infantry; every comfort went up in smoke. O'Hara's recorded summary: "In this situation, without Baggage, necessaries, or Provisions of any sort, for Officer or Soldier, in the most barren inhospitable unhealthy part of North America, opposed to the most savage, inveterate perfidious cruel enemy... it was resolved to follow Greene's army to the end of the World." [HIGH as O'Hara's letter; chain via John Buchanan, *The Road to Guilford Courthouse* (1997) and standard secondary literature — attribute as O'Hara's letter]
- **The Race to the Dan, January 18 – February 14, 1781 [HIGH]:** a 240-mile footrace across the Carolina piedmont. Greene, after reuniting with Morgan's column at Trading Ford on February 3, fell back steadily northeast — Lighthorse Harry Lee's Legion screening the rear in daily skirmishes with Tarleton. Greene's council of war, meeting on the road, voted to continue the retreat rather than fight at a disadvantage. Cornwallis pushed hard. On February 14, Greene's last troops crossed the Dan River into Virginia, ferrying the boats with them; the flooded river stopped Cornwallis cold. He fell back to Hillsborough, issued a proclamation of royal restoration, and waited for Loyalist reinforcements that never came in useful numbers. [HIGH on the sequence; the council-vote detail MED-HIGH via ABT/standard histories]
- **Greene recrosses [HIGH]:** on February 22 Greene's army — now reinforced with ~1,400 Virginia militia and other fresh Continental troops, totaling ~4,400 men — crossed back over the Dan and began shadowing Cornwallis south through the piedmont. Light skirmishes continued; on March 6, Lee's Legion surprised a column of Loyalist militia (Pyle's "Massacre" — ~90 Tory cavalry cut down; outside the scope of this page but the surrounding context). By March 14 Cornwallis, down to ~1,900 men fit for duty, was camped within 8 miles of Greene's position at Guilford Courthouse. [HIGH on numbers and dates; Pyle's Massacre mention LINK ONLY]
- **The "war of posts" pivot [HIGH framing]:** Greene's strategy since taking command in December was never to risk the army in a showdown he couldn't afford to lose; he wrote to Washington that he intended to "fight, get beaten, rise, and fight again." His general approach — harassing, dividing, exhausting — was consistent with this; the Guilford fight was a moment when conditions finally favored a stand. [HIGH as Greene's strategic logic; the quote attribution MED — widely attributed to Greene, exact source not pinned this session; use "as Greene put it to Washington" with a hedge or paraphrase]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS

### Strengths (dossier stats — RANGES MANDATORY)
- **American [HIGH on the overall figure; component ranges MED-HIGH]:** ~**4,400–4,500** total engaged. Broken down:
  - **First line:** ~1,000 North Carolina militia (Cols. John Butler and Thomas Eaton, ~500 each), flanked on the right by Col. Charles Lynch's ~200 Virginia riflemen + William Washington's ~90 dragoons + Robert Kirkwood's ~80 Delaware Continentals; on the left by Col. William Campbell's ~200 riflemen + Lee's Legion (~75 horse, ~82 foot). Artillery: Anthony Singleton's two 6-pounders in the center. [HIGH on the composition via Wikipedia order-of-battle]
  - **Second line:** ~1,200 Virginia militia led by Brig. Gens. Robert Lawson and Edward Stevens; positioned in thick woods ~350 yards behind the first line.
  - **Third line:** ~1,400 Continentals — Isaac Huger's Virginia Brigade + Otho Holland Williams's Maryland Brigade (1st and 5th Maryland regiments) + Peter Jacquett's Delaware company; Samuel Finley's two 6-pounders. In a clearing ~550 yards beyond the second line.
- **British [HIGH]:** ~**1,900–2,100** effectives (Wikipedia and ABT state 2,100; Cornwallis was down from a larger total by sick and detachments; some sources write "~1,900 fit"). Components: 23rd Foot (Royal Welch Fusiliers), 33rd Foot, 71st Foot (Fraser's Highlanders), Hessian Regiment von Bose, 1st and 2nd Battalions of Foot Guards, Jägers, light infantry, British Legion cavalry (Tarleton, in reserve). Four guns (Royal Artillery, under Lt. John Macleod). [HIGH on composition; gun count confirmed at 3 operational, some accounts say 4 — use "three or four guns"]
- **Duration:** roughly 90 minutes of close fighting (~1:30–3:00 p.m., roughly); the whole action including the approach was about two hours. [MED on exact clock; "about an hour and a half of close fighting" is safe]

### American commander bios

- **Maj. Gen. Nathanael Greene (1742–1786) [HIGH]:** Rhode Island Quaker blacksmith who became Washington's most trusted general. Quartermaster General 1778–1780 (hated the desk work, never stopped lobbying for a field command); Congress gave him the Southern Department on October 14, 1780, over Cornwallis's first invasion — Washington's choice all along (the same preference denied at Camden). Inherited ~2,300 on paper, barely 1,500 fit. Arrived Charlotte December 3, divided the army December 16, watched Morgan win Cowpens, raced the Dan, and returned to fight at Guilford behind a plan built on what Morgan had proved. He lost this battle. He won the war in the South. In-repo portraits: `rev-camden-greene.jpg` / `rev-monmouth-greene.jpg` / `rev-trenton-greene.jpg` — REUSE. [HIGH]
- **Brig. Gen. Otho Holland Williams (1749–1794) [HIGH]:** Maryland-born; commanded the 2nd Maryland Brigade at Camden (the man whose return handed Gates his "3,052" moment) and now commanded the Maryland Brigade in the third line. A steady, literate officer whose battlefield presence at every southern fight made him one of Greene's anchors. His 1822 Narrative is the critical primary source for the Camden debacle; at Guilford he commands the veteran Marylanders who deliver the sharpest Continental blow of the afternoon. One-line role: "the Maryland Brigade's hammer, the best infantry on the field."
- **Lt. Col. William Washington (1752–1810) [HIGH]:** Returned from Cowpens commanding the dragoon reserve. His cavalry charge against the British Guards in the third-line fight is one of the day's decisive moments — and is the melee into which Cornwallis (allegedly) ordered grapeshot. See §4. In-repo: no dedicated Guilford portrait; REUSE from Cowpens if a face is needed. Portrait options weak (see §9).
- **Lt. Col. Henry "Light Horse Harry" Lee (1756–1818) [HIGH]:** commanded Lee's Legion on the American left — cavalry + infantry. His memoir (*Memoirs of the War in the Southern Department*, 1812/1869) is the primary source chain for the grapeshot anecdote (written ~30 years after the battle). At Guilford his troops held the left wing, cut off a Hessian regiment's retreat, and kept fighting long after the battle was decided. Father of Robert E. Lee.

### British commander bios

- **Lt. Gen. Charles, Earl Cornwallis (1738–1805) [HIGH]:** the best British field commander in America; lost Long Island, Trenton, and Brandywine (all instructive in different ways), then taken off the rolls when Saratoga's Convention Army surrendered. Charleston (May 1780) redeemed him; Camden confirmed it; Cowpens broke one arm. At 42, commanding the core of what remained, he marched his men across 240 miles of piedmont in winter, burned his baggage, and attacked a force twice his size. He won. Then he marched them to Yorktown. In-repo portraits: `rev-charleston-cornwallis.jpg` / `rev-monmouth-cornwallis.jpg` / `rev-camden-cornwallis.jpg` — REUSE.
- **Brig. Gen. Charles O'Hara (~1740–1802) [HIGH]:** Born in Lisbon; illegitimate son of James O'Hara, 2nd Baron Tyrawley. Commissioned in the Coldstream Guards; commanded the **Brigade of Guards** (1st and 2nd Battalions, Grenadiers, light infantry, Jägers) — the elite of Cornwallis's force. **Severely wounded at Guilford** (the wound is confirmed in multiple sources; specific detail of where he was hit not found this session — "severely wounded" is the safe form). O'Hara's later career: prisoner at Yorktown (where he surrendered Cornwallis's sword to Washington), governor of Gibraltar, full general 1798. His nephew, a lieutenant of the artillery, was killed in the battle. The "end of the World" quote above is O'Hara's, not Cornwallis's. [HIGH on bio; wound severity HIGH; exact wound location MED-NOT-FOUND this session]
- **Lt. Col. James Webster (1743–1781) [HIGH]:** Son of a Church of Scotland minister; joined the army as a young officer and rose to command the **23rd and 33rd Regiments of Foot** — two of the hardest-fighting battalions in the southern campaign. Cornwallis's most trusted subordinate: he led the right wing at Camden, broke Webster's charge shattered the Virginia militia; at Guilford he commanded the right wing again and delivered the assault on the Continental third line. **Mortally wounded** during the attack on the third line — right knee and thigh badly damaged. He died at Elizabethtown, NC, approximately **two weeks later** (around April 1, 1781). Cornwallis, on hearing of his death, reportedly looked at his sword and said "I have lost my scabbard" — the quote is recorded via a British sergeant's account (MED — secondhand through one post-battle account; widely reproduced; use with attribution). Cornwallis's written tribute to Webster was the strongest endorsement he put to paper for any officer in the campaign. [HIGH on career/wounds/death; the sword-quote MED]
- **Lt. Col. Banastre Tarleton (1754–1833) [HIGH]:** commanded the British Legion cavalry (in reserve through most of the battle). Early on March 15 his advance patrols skirmished with Lee's Legion at New Garden Meeting House at ~4 a.m. — Tarleton **took a musket ball to his right hand, losing two or three fingers** (the Wikipedia article says "losing two fingers"; other accounts say mangled hand; in-repo Cowpens fact pack correctly flags that "Tarleton's first documented wound came at Guilford"). His Legion cavalry were held in reserve; late in the battle he charged to rout Virginia riflemen under Col. William Campbell. His effective fighting role was curtailed by the hand wound. [HIGH on the wound at Guilford; finger count MED — "mangled his right hand, costing him at least two fingers" is the safe form]

## 4. THE CAMPAIGN AND THE BATTLE

### The approach, March 14–15 [HIGH]
- On March 14 Cornwallis was camped at Deep River Meeting House, 8 miles from Guilford. His patrols confirmed Greene's position. Early on March 15 the British column moved out on the New Garden Road toward the courthouse, ~12 miles in the dark. At ~4 a.m. Tarleton's van cavalry encountered Lee's Legion vedettes at New Garden Meeting House — the skirmish in which Tarleton took his hand wound. Lee's force fell back on the American first line.
- Greene deployed in the three-line arrangement he had worked out with Morgan's Cowpens lesson. The first-line militia were posted behind a **split-rail fence** at the edge of an open field — a shooting position, not a final stand. Greene reportedly rode the first line with the instruction (by tradition): **"Three rounds, my boys, and then you may fall back"** [MED — participant recollection via the amrevnc.com account; the exact wording is tradition-layer, attribute as "men remembered Greene riding the line saying…"]. The difference from Camden: the militia had a scripted plan and permission to leave; the Continentals stood behind them.

### First line (the rail fence) [HIGH on sequence; MED on detail]
- The British column deployed into line ~550 yards from the fence. Webster's right wing (23rd, 33rd, jägers, light infantry) and the Guards' left wing, with O'Hara, came on. The NC militia held for two volleys, then broke and ran — more or less as planned, or at least as Greene had accepted would happen. The riflemen and Lee's Legion on the flanks stayed longer, inflicting officer-heavy casualties on the Jägers and Guards. Lee's troops on the left cut off the Hessian Regiment von Bose's retreat, turning it into a separate small battle on the American left. [HIGH on the overall pattern; the Lee-vs-von-Bose subplot MED]

### Second line (Virginia militia in the woods) [HIGH on sequence; MED on detail]
- The Virginia militia in the second line — veterans of Cowpens in Edward Stevens's brigade, raw levies in Lawson's — were in thick timber, which broke up the British advance. Webster's right-flank troops hit Stevens's Virginians hard; they held longer than the North Carolinians had and inflicted heavier casualties but eventually gave way, part retreating through the Continentals. Greene later reported the fight here was "long, obstinate, and bloody." [HIGH as Greene's characterization — see §7 for source]

### Third line (the Continental fight) [HIGH on sequence]
- The 1st Maryland — Howard's veterans of Camden and Cowpens — met the advancing 2nd Guards Battalion and stopped it, then threw it back with a bayonet charge. William Washington's dragoons rode through the Guards' rear twice. The Guards, who had suffered ~56% casualties by battle's end, were very nearly broken. [HIGH on the Guards losses]
- **The grapeshot episode [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — see §8]:** at the crisis point, with the 1st Maryland and Washington's cavalry and the Guards all intermingled, Cornwallis ordered Lt. John Macleod to load his guns with **grapeshot and fire into the mass**. The grape killed Americans and British indiscriminately but broke up the American assault. The **primary source** for this incident is **Henry Lee's memoir** (*Memoirs of the War in the Southern Department*, published 1812, revised 1869) — written ~30 years after the battle. The incident is presented as fact in Wikipedia/ABT and multiple secondary sources; but the amrevnc.com account (citing Babits) flags it as "embellished from a claim by Light Horse Harry Lee, writing 30 years later." The **safe framing:** the guns did fire at the melee (the cannonade is not disputed), and it did kill some of Cornwallis's own men; whether Cornwallis personally ordered it knowing it would hit his own (or whether the guns simply fired on the mass as the British infantry withdrew) is the debated element. O'Hara, lying wounded near the guns, reportedly protested; the protest-and-defiance version of events ("Cornwallis told him he had no choice") is from the secondary chain (Buchanan 1997), which itself goes back to Lee. [DISPUTED on the deliberate-decision framing; CONFIRMED on the grapeshot being fired]
- The 5th Maryland — raw troops — broke under a Guards assault; Washington's cavalry rescued them. By about 3:00–3:30 p.m. Greene ordered a retreat to Reedy Fork Creek, and the Continentals fell back in good order. [HIGH on Greene ordering the retreat; MED on exact time]

### Tarleton's pursuit (limited) [HIGH]
- Tarleton charged the fleeing American right flank late in the battle to accelerate the withdrawal. His cavalry was not at full strength (his hand wound incapacitated him earlier; his Legion had not been heavily engaged). No devastating pursuit on the Cowpens model occurred; Greene's army retreated intact to Speedwell Iron Works, then regrouped. [HIGH]

## 5. CASUALTIES (dossier ranges — RANGES MANDATORY)

### British [HIGH on the totals; unit-level figures as cited]
- Killed: **93** · Wounded: **413** · Missing/captured: **26** — **total ~532** of ~2,100 engaged (~25.3%). [HIGH as the standard return; widely cited; Cornwallis's official casualty return]
- Hardest hit unit: **Foot Guards battalions — ~56% casualties**, including nearly all their officers. [HIGH via multiple sources including Wikipedia/ABT]
- Other notable losses: Webster (mortally wounded); O'Hara (severely wounded); Tarleton (hand wound). The 33rd Foot and 23rd Foot also took heavy losses leading the assault on the third line.
- **IMPORTANT NOTE:** The pension-record analysis (cited by Wikipedia) suggests these figures may undercount by 15–20%; the "93 killed / 413 wounded" set is the official return, which historians treat as a floor. Use "officially ~532, likely higher" or give the official return with the footnote. [MED on the pension revision — leave to the author to decide whether to carry the footnote]

### American [HIGH on the range; most missing were militia]
- Killed: **79** · Wounded: **184–185** · Missing: **971–1,046** — **total ~1,310** [official return; the missing figure = mostly NC militia who dispersed after the first line; they were not casualties in the usual sense, just gone. Explain the dispersal.]
- Effective casualty rate was ~**6%** of Greene's force — against the British ~25%. [HIGH framing — the disproportion IS the strategic point]
- The pension-analysis finding: "probably 15–20% higher than shown" for killed and wounded (i.e., likely ~90–94 killed, ~211–220 wounded) [MED — note as scholarly revision if used]
- **THE DISPERSAL EXPLAINED:** the ~1,000 "missing" North Carolina militia were not killed or captured; they melted away from the first line after (or during) their two volleys, many heading for home rather than the rally point. This was predictable and not entirely a failure — the plan was for them to fire and fall back; many just fell back further than intended. The militia account should explain this without either whitewashing or sneering. [HIGH framing from multiple sources]

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES

### Cornwallis at Ramsey's Mill [HIGH]
- After holding the field, Cornwallis could not pursue Greene — his army was too reduced. He marched his wounded to **Ramsey's Mill** on the Deep River, where he rested for nearly two weeks. He issued a victory proclamation. His effective strength was now ~1,400–1,500 fit for duty. [HIGH]

### The march to Wilmington and then Virginia [HIGH]
- Unable to subsist off the country and unwilling to risk another engagement against Greene, Cornwallis **marched to Wilmington, NC** (arriving mid-April 1781) to refit and resupply by sea. He spent approximately a week there.
- In **late April 1781** — against the advice of Henry Clinton in New York, who wanted Cornwallis to return to South Carolina — Cornwallis **marched north from Wilmington into Virginia**, to operate with Benedict Arnold's and later William Phillips's forces there. His reasoning: the war in the Carolinas could not be won while Greene could retreat to Virginia; hit Virginia, and you secure the whole South. Clinton never fully agreed. [HIGH on the Wilmington-to-Virginia march; MED on the Clinton dispute detail]
- The Virginia campaign: raids, the destruction of supplies, the pursuit of Lafayette's small Continental force — and then the order to find a defensive port position, and **Yorktown**. October 19, 1781: Cornwallis surrenders. (Set this up; do NOT tell it — Yorktown is a separate page or chapter.)

### Greene retakes the South [HIGH framing]
- While Cornwallis was in Virginia, Greene executed the "war of posts" — his army moved to isolate and reduce the British interior garrisons of South Carolina and Georgia one by one (Hobkirk's Hill, Ninety Six, Eutaw Springs — all tactical draws or defeats for Greene, all strategic gains). By the end of 1781 the British held only Savannah and Charleston; they evacuated Savannah December 1782, Charleston December 1782. Guilford planted the chain that bore this fruit.

### Webster's death — the war's best British epitaph [HIGH on the event; MED on the exact quote]
- Webster died at Elizabethtown, NC, ~two weeks after the battle (around April 1, 1781). Cornwallis was said to have looked at his sword and said "I have lost my scabbard." [MED — the anecdote traces to a British sergeant's account and is widely reproduced — one secondhand transmission; use as "Cornwallis is said to have…" or credit the sergeant.] Cornwallis's written dispatch to Germain on Webster is well-documented as unusually warm praise.

## 8. APOCRYPHA BLACKLIST / HANDLE-WITH-CARE LEDGER

1. **The grapeshot-as-deliberate-order framing** — Henry Lee's memoir (1812, revised 1869) is the source chain for the scene in which Cornwallis KNOWINGLY ordered his guns to fire into a mass of mixed combatants, with O'Hara protesting and Cornwallis saying he had no choice. The **amrevnc.com account (citing battlefield historians) explicitly calls this "embellished from a claim by Light Horse Harry Lee, writing 30 years later."** The honest frame: guns were fired into the melee at the crisis of the battle and some British soldiers were killed by their own side; the conscious-order narrative is Lee's construction, not a primary chain. Write the incident with the gun firing as fact, the deliberate-order framing as Lee's account, and the debate named plainly. Do NOT write: "Cornwallis cold-bloodedly ordered his guns to fire on his own men." [DISPUTED on the deliberate-decision framing; CONFIRMED on the cannonade]

2. **The Fox quote as an ORIGINAL Fox coinage** — Fox was consciously echoing Plutarch's Pyrrhus of Epirus (Fox says as much in the context the Annual Register records). This is not a misattribution (Fox DID say it), but the quote is a CLASSICAL ALLUSION, not spontaneous wit. The honest attribution is "Charles James Fox, echoing Plutarch…" — do not present it as Fox inventing a fresh observation. [HIGH on this distinction]

3. **Casualty percentages as an American "win"** — the field was a British victory; Greene retreated; Cornwallis held the courthouse. The strategic framing (Pyrrhic victory → Yorktown) is correct and important but should be layered on top of honest acknowledgment that the battle was a British tactical win. Do not write "Guilford was really an American victory." [Framing rule, HIGH]

4. **"Cornwallis abandoned the Carolinas" as a direct consequence of one day's fight** — accurate as arc, but the Guilford losses were the cumulative straw; the Race to the Dan, the baggage burning, the failed Loyalist recruitment, and months of attrition all fed the decision. Write as: "Guilford was the battle that drained the last resilience from Cornwallis's southern army and made the Carolina-to-Virginia pivot inevitable." [Nuance, HIGH]

5. **The "lost my scabbard" quote as definitely Cornwallis's words** — the chain is: British sergeant → secondhand account → reproduction in secondary sources. Plausible; not primary. "Said to have…" or "as a sergeant recorded…" mandatory. [MED — use with attribution, not as direct quote without hedge]

6. **Webster was "killed" at Guilford** — he was MORTALLY WOUNDED; he died ~two weeks later at Elizabethtown, NC. The distinction matters because the page's dossier stats box should show wounded (mortally) not killed. [HIGH on the precise form]

7. **Tarleton's wound as "losing two fingers" — exact finger count MED** — the documented fact is a musket ball damaged his right hand severely; the most precise secondary accounts say two or three fingers. "Costing him the use of part of his right hand" or "mangling his right hand, costing him at least two fingers" is the safe form. [MED on the exact count]

8. **Greene's "fight, get beaten, rise, and fight again" as a letter-exact quote** — widely attributed; letter reference not verified this session. Paraphrase as "as he had described his strategy to Washington" rather than quoting bare. [MED on the exact text]

9. **Claiming the Fox quote is "misattributed to Pyrrhus"** — the quote IS correctly attributed to Fox in Parliament (Annual Register 1781); the ADDITIONAL point is that Fox was consciously echoing Pyrrhus. These are both true simultaneously. Do NOT write the attribution as disputed or wrong; write it as a deliberate allusion. [Framing — not a blacklist item, a precision note]

## 11. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)

- **Date:** March 15, 1781 · **Where:** Guilford Court House, present-day Greensboro, North Carolina
- **Forces:** American ~4,400–4,500 (three lines: NC militia → VA militia → Continentals); British ~1,900–2,100
- **Duration:** ~90 minutes of close fighting
- **Casualties:** British 93 killed, 413 wounded, 26 missing (~532, ~25% of the force); American 79 killed, ~185 wounded, ~1,000 militia dispersed (~1,310 by the return, but effective losses far lower — ~6% of force)
- **Winner line material:** Tactical British victory — Cornwallis held the field. Strategic American victory — he bled so deeply doing it that he abandoned the Carolinas six weeks later, marched north from Wilmington to Virginia, and surrendered at Yorktown in October. Charles James Fox told the House of Commons, echoing Plutarch's Pyrrhus: "Another such victory would ruin the British army." It had.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES

1. **British strength:** 2,100 appears in Wikipedia and ABT as the standard figure; some sources write "~1,900 fit" after Cowpens losses. "About 2,100 effectives" is defensible; give as "roughly 1,900–2,100."
2. **American strengths by line:** the component figures (500 + 500 NC militia; 1,200 VA militia; 1,400 Continentals) are well-sourced via Wikipedia's order-of-battle; the flanking units (Lee's Legion, Washington's dragoons, Kirkwood, riflemen) bring the total to ~4,400. NEVER print a single flat number; print "about four thousand four hundred or so."
3. **Casualty pension-record revision:** the "15–20% higher" figure for killed/wounded is scholarly but secondary (cited by Wikipedia without naming the specific study). Use the official return as the baseline and note the likelihood of undercounting, without presenting the revision as proven.
4. **The grapeshot episode:** the cannonade happened; the deliberate-order narrative is Lee's construction. Frame carefully (§8, item 1). The role of O'Hara at the moment of the order — whether he was near the guns, whether he protested directly to Cornwallis — is from Buchanan 1997 (who cites Lee as the chain); treat O'Hara's presence as one version of the story, not documented fact.
5. **The Fox quote / Annual Register:** the Annual Register for 1781 (London, 1782, pp. 71–72) is the citation chain. The specific Hansard debate reference was not confirmed this session. The Wikiquote Fox page does not list this quote — a yellow flag, but the Annual Register chain is robust. Author should verify the exact wording against the Annual Register before byte-quoting inside quotation marks.
6. **The "lost my scabbard" quote:** the British-sergeant chain makes this MED at best. Frame as "said to have told an officer" or "a sergeant recorded that Cornwallis…" Never bare-quote as fact.
7. **Webster's death date:** "approximately two weeks after March 15" is confirmed (multiple sources converge on "two weeks later"; Wikipedia says "died two weeks later, aged 41, at Elizabethtown, NC"). An exact date of ~April 1, 1781 follows from this; if precision is needed, verify against a regimental record.
8. **Tarleton's wound finger count:** "at least two fingers" from the Wikipedia article; other accounts say "mangled his right hand." Keep the safer form.
9. **Greene's "fight, get beaten, rise, and fight again" paraphrase:** widely attributed but the exact letter not retrieved this session. Do not byte-quote; use "as Greene described his strategy to Washington."
10. **The three line-commanders for the 2nd line (VA militia):** Robert Lawson and Edward Stevens confirmed; Brig. Gen. Edward Stevens commanded at Camden (Virginia Continentals) and some sources give Stevens the larger share of the second-line command here — confirm the Stevens/Lawson split from the order-of-battle before printing confidently.
11. **Cornwallis's losses at Cowpens:** this page calls them "the fast quarter of his army" (matching the Cowpens factpack's locked framing); the Guilford force at ~1,900–2,100 is Cornwallis's post-Cowpens complement plus Leslie's reinforcements, now significantly ground down. Don't let the Guilford troop total imply Cowpens losses were recovered — they weren't; Guilford is a SMALLER force than Cornwallis had before Cowpens.
