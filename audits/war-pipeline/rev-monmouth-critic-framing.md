# FRAMING CRITIC — Battle of Monmouth draft (rev-monmouth)

*Critic pass 2026-06-12 against `rev-monmouth-draft.md` (all 374 lines, incl. FACT LEDGER
and compliance lists) and `rev-monmouth-factpack.md` (sole source). Scope: fairness,
emphasis, interpretive honesty. Cross-checked all handle-with-care surfaces against the
pack's DISPUTED register (§9) and apocrypha blacklist, with particular attention to: patriotic
myth / inevitability framing; Lee scapegoated vs. the pack's split modern verdict; Washington
hagiography in the confrontation scene; British competence (Clinton's strategic success);
Molly Pitcher confidence calibration across ALL surfaces (prose, captions, dossier); women of
the army treated with dignity; heat deaths acknowledged on both sides; no paid-quote-as-fact.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

No blocker. The hardest framing duties are met: Molly Pitcher is told as legend at the
evidence level; the swearing stories are refuted by the seven-witness record; Washington
does not cashier Lee on the field; the draw result is named honestly; Clinton's mission is
acknowledged as accomplished; Lee is given a fairness beat and his verdict is held open.
Three should-fixes and two polishes. None rises to a blocker; together, two tip the same way
(Washington elevated at Lee's expense beyond what the split verdict supports), and one
introduces a subtle inevitability note the pack does not endorse.

## SHOULD-FIX

**1. The outcome verdict — "the day Washington's command stopped being questionable" —
overstates the pack.**

The dossier `outcome: verdict` line reads: "A tactical draw · the day Washington's command
stopped being questionable."

The pack supports the political-morale effect: Congress voted thanks, the press celebrated,
"Father of his Country" is current by year's end, and the army's internal grumbling about
replacing Washington ended. But "stopped being questionable" is a narrator-asserted absolute
that does more than the pack licenses. The pack's own framing is "Washington's command stopped
being questionable" only inside the army (§8: "After Monmouth no one inside the army seriously
questioned Washington's command again") — and even that is scoped to internal dissent, not
public political challenge. Monmouth did not end civilian congressional criticism of Washington
in 1778; that would persist through the war. The outcome prose (line 56) gets it right, placing
the claim inside the army with "the talk of replacing Washington stopped." The dossier verdict
line — the reader's first interpretive frame — overstates the same claim as a general truth.

FIX: Tighten the verdict line to match the pack's actual scope, e.g.: "A tactical draw · the
day Washington's command inside the army stopped being questioned." Or simply cut the second
clause and let the outcome prose carry it, which it does cleanly.

---

**2. The outcome prose closes with a "the winter cashed out" flourish that tips into
triumphalism at the expense of the strategic wash.**

End of Section 3's Lee-aftermath / close (line 271): "But the army Steuben had drilled all
winter had stood for a full day in open field against the best regiments Britain had, finished
the day advancing, and slept on the ground it held. That was the thing the winter had been for,
and at Monmouth, in front of the whole country, it cashed out."

This is earned — the pack explicitly supports it (§1: "proof premise"; §8: "the Valley Forge
winter cashed out"). The framing problem is structural: the closing beat of the section comes
after two paragraphs on Clinton's successful mission ("Clinton had saved his army and his wagons
and gotten them to New York, exactly as he set out to do"), but the section's LAST word is the
American proof-of-concept rhapsody, and it erases the preceding strategic wash rather than
resting beside it. The pack's own "winner line material" (§12) places both truths in parallel:
"A draw that read like a victory and worked like one. Clinton saved his army and his wagons...
But the army Steuben drilled... stood all day..." The draft's section close uses the same
structure in its middle but then lets the American beat be the last word without a restraining
clause.

This is a framing problem not a fact problem: the draft does not suppress Clinton's success —
it names it clearly — but the rhetorical arc ends on triumphalism, which gives the draw a
winner. The pack's lesson from the Saratoga pass is that the closing beat is where
inevitability structures lodge.

FIX: Append a single restraining clause, or move the strategic-wash acknowledgment to the
actual last position: e.g., "That was the thing the winter had been for, and at Monmouth it
cashed out — but the man who ordered the retreat that started it walked away ruined, and the
general who slipped away at midnight had his whole army safe in New York." Or, less
elaborately: "...and slept on the ground it held. Whether it settled anything about who would
win the war, no one on either side in July 1778 yet knew." The goal is to hold both the
American proof and the strategic stalemate in the same breath, not let proof close the book.

---

**3. The Lee bio's "whether he deserved it is still argued" ending undersells the pack's
split verdict and arrives too abruptly.**

Commander bio for Lee (dossier, line 45, closing sentence): "Whether he deserved it is still
argued."

The pack's instruction on the modern historiography is explicit and strong: John Shy reads the
conviction as political necessity ("an acquittal on the first two charges would have been a
vote of no-confidence in Washington"); Ecelbarger (2024) argues the disobedience charge had
substance; the trial record shows the retreat was mostly orderly and may have saved the
vanguard; "present it as the army choosing Washington, with the merits still argued" (§5). The
bio's closing sentence compresses all of this to a one-clause hedge that, following "convicted,"
"suspended," "dismissed," and "dead in a Philadelphia tavern in 1782, broke and bitter," reads
as a perfunctory afterthought — a gentlemen's-agreement "well, who knows" that does not carry
the pack's real point: that historians with access to the trial record have actively contested
whether the conviction was just. The bio raises the fairness question (correctly) in the body
("He argued against attacking Clinton at all...He genuinely doubted the new army...His caution
at Hopewell was a professional's caution") but the commander bio does not.

The prose in Section 3 (line 268) carries the full Shy quote and the historians' argument
correctly. But the dossier bio is a freestanding reader surface — cast readers, people browsing
the commander panel — who may never reach Section 3. The bio should carry at least the
direction of the debate, not just its existence.

FIX: Extend the bio's close to name the split, e.g.: "Whether he deserved the verdict is still
argued — historians who read the trial record closely have called the conviction political
necessity as much as justice, the army choosing Washington over the merits." One sentence; it
names the interpretive stakes without asserting a verdict, consistent with the pack's framing.

## POLISH

**4. "The best regular infantry in the world" / "the best regiments Britain had" characterize
British quality without naming British losses — a minor dignity gap.**

Section 3 prose (line 251): "Against this came the best infantry in the British Army, and they
came hard." Section 3 close (line 271): "the best regiments Britain had." Both are
pack-consistent as house-voice intensifiers (the FACT LEDGER itself flags them: "the 'best
regular infantry' characterization is consistent with the pack's 'British regulars in open
field' framing"). The framing concern is narrow: the British troops are identified as elite on
the advance, and their defeat is recorded, but the passage does not dwell on what that cost
them in the heat. The pack is explicit (§4, §6): the British heat dead (59 "of fatigue" in
Clinton's return), the third of the German troops overcome, the wool-coated grenadiers "worst
of all" — these are already in the section prose (line 255), correctly. The framing is not
violated; this is a minor balance check. No change required, but the reviser should confirm
the heat-deaths-on-both-sides beat (line 255) is preserved in any edit, since it is the
British-dignity anchor for the elite-infantry framing.

---

**5. The "Today" MEANWHILE close — "with, somewhere in the crowd, a woman carrying a
pitcher" — is the right instinct but just barely maintains irony register.**

The section close (line 273): "Every year the battle is fought again in reenactment on the
park's open ground, with, somewhere in the crowd, a woman carrying a pitcher."

This is a knowing close: it names the legend's folk persistence without endorsing it, and it
returns the reader to the real camp women the section spent a full paragraph rehabilitating.
The risk is that after 600 words of carefully calibrated legend-debunking, the close reads as
the narrator winking at the myth and leaving the reader with the image of Molly Pitcher anyway
— the very image the section worked to displace. The cap women documented by Waldo and Martin
do not carry pitchers; the pitcher-carrier is the legend's shorthand.

FIX (optional): A one-word tweak restores the ironic distance: "with, somewhere in the crowd,
a woman carrying a pitcher and playing the part the country invented." Or: "with, somewhere in
the crowd, someone carrying a pitcher — the legend outliving the scholarship." Neither is
required; the current line is defensible. Flag for reviser judgment.

## CHECKED AND PASSING

- **Molly Pitcher confidence across ALL surfaces:** Prose (Section 3) correctly handles the
  legend at the evidence level: no contemporary naming, the 1876 identification noted, the
  1832 obituary's silence named, scholars' "composite" reading given. The Waldo (musket,
  unnamed) and Martin (cannon, unnamed) accounts are presented as real and distinct. The 1822
  Pennsylvania pension is on the record with the rationale-change noted. Washington's
  commendation is explicitly denied. The section-4 IMAGES caption for the Currier & Ives
  print carries the mandatory LEGEND-IMAGE caveat verbatim. The MEANWHILE close (see Finding
  5) is the only surface that requires reviser attention, and it is a polish not a blocker.

- **Washington hagiography in the confrontation scene:** The confrontation is told through
  Tilghman's sworn testimony, not Washington-venerating retrospection. The seven-witness
  record is presented as the evidentiary basis. "Considerable warmth, real anger" is named.
  The swearing legends are refuted on their merits (Scott half a mile away, printed 1860;
  Lafayette's table talk vs. his 1779 silence; Lee's own failure to claim he was sworn at).
  Washington is not made a saint: his awkward handling of the vanguard-command farce is named
  plainly ("Washington, awkwardly, let him"), and the Section 2 MEANWHILE credits Lee's
  rearguard delay without which the main line would not have been ready.

- **Lee scapegoating / caricature:** The fairness beat in Section 1 is genuine and specific:
  Lee's professional background named ("more formal military experience than anyone in it,
  Washington included"), his argument given its strongest form ("a real strand of modern
  scholarship thinks he was at least half right"), the council compromise reported without
  mockery. The retreat is defended as "not a rout" in Section 2 with the exact evidence
  (three miles in good order, Steuben visible even in the failure). Lee's real sins are
  distinguished from cowardice or treason. The court-martial outcome is reported with Shy's
  "political necessity" framing and the court's own softenings (struck "shameful," "in some
  few instances") as load-bearing nuance. The Section 1 preview line — "this is the place to
  be fair to him, because the page that follows will not be kind" — is an honest narrator
  signal, not a fig leaf. Finding 3 above asks the dossier bio to carry more of this weight.

- **British competence — Clinton's withdrawal as a strategic success:** Clinton's mission is
  named as accomplished in the dossier Winner line ("Clinton got his army and his 1,500-wagon
  train safely to New York, exactly as planned"), the outcome prose ("Clinton accomplished his
  mission"), and the Clinton bio ("his mission, narrowly, accomplished"). The near-miss with
  d'Estaing is presented as what it was: a genuine close call that Clinton won by days. The
  battle framing (§1 identity: "tactically inconclusive / drawn battle — an enormous American
  political and morale victory") is reflected correctly — the page does not pretend the
  British lost a battle they tactically did not lose. The British assaults are described as
  coming "hard" against elite troops, not as bumbling attacks.

- **Lee treason — not asserted:** "Not cowardice and not treason" is stated explicitly in
  Section 3. The captivity document is not invoked (the FACT LEDGER notes the decision to
  omit it to avoid the treason read). This is the correct call.

- **"Washington ordered Lee off the field" — blacklist compliant:** Section 2 explicitly
  names the legend and corrects it: "the cashiering everyone imagines, the general publicly
  stripped of his command in the middle of the battlefield, did not happen." Washington gave
  Lee the rearguard at his own request; Lee fought ~30 minutes; Steuben relieved him at
  Englishtown around 3 p.m. The sequence is stated clearly.

- **Heat deaths on BOTH sides:** Named correctly and in both armies. Clinton's return "59
  dead 'of fatigue' (heatstroke)" is in the dossier casualties. The prose (Section 3) adds
  "about a third of the German troops were overcome" and "American heat deaths ran to dozens"
  and "on both sides" in the footnote-grade explanation. The British wool-coated grenadiers
  are named as "worst of all." The "more died of heat than fighting" claim is attributed to
  "many accounts," not asserted as the narrator's own fact.

- **Women of the army — treated with dignity, not mascot-ized:** The draft's treatment of
  camp followers is the right call. The Molly Pitcher legend beat explicitly names the
  scholars' "composite" reading and the "G.I. Joe" parallel (the Emily Teipe framing from
  the pack — not quoted verbatim but the substance is there). Waldo's woman is introduced as
  "one of the camp women," not as a curiosity or an exception. Martin's woman is introduced
  as an "unnamed woman serving at a cannon" — not "a brave heroine." The 1822 pension is
  presented as the one solid documented thing about Mary Hays McCauley, and it is presented
  as an acknowledgment of real service, not as confirmation of the legend.

- **"Last major battle in the north" phrasing:** Correctly stated throughout with the
  required hedge ("last major battle," never "last battle in the north"). Smaller northern
  actions are acknowledged in the aftermath beat ("the northern war settled into watching New
  York"). Blacklist compliant.

- **Superlatives hedged:** "By tradition the largest field-artillery exchange of the war,
  though that is the kind of superlative that gets attached to a lot of battles, so call it
  one of the largest." Compliant with §9.6.

- **Washington-Lafayette cloak scene:** Excluded. Compliant with §9.5.

- **The Hamilton "midwives" quote:** Used byte-exact (§7.9), framed as Hamilton's contempt
  not as the narrator's verdict, and attributed correctly (his letter to Boudinot). The
  framing — "wrote afterward, with the contempt of a young man for his elders" — is the
  right distance.

- **No paid quote used as fact:** The Congress resolution "for the important victory of
  Monmouth over the British grand army" is attributed as a Congress resolution and correctly
  labeled MED-HIGH in the FACT LEDGER. It is introduced to show how the battle was read, not
  as a factual characterization of the outcome.

- **Monckton's theatrical death:** Excluded. Only the verified core (led the grenadiers,
  killed near the parsonage farm, buried at Old Tennent Church). Compliant with §9.7.

- **The American comeback narrative:** Section 3 correctly names this as "the first time this
  army ended a battle advancing" (pack-supported as the proof-of-training beat) without
  extending it into a destiny claim. The evening counterattacks are described in measured
  terms: halted by darkness, the armies "about a mile apart," Washington's morning-renewal
  plan foiled by Clinton's midnight exit.
