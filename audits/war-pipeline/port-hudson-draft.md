## the-last-lock · Port Hudson · The river's last lock · The Last Lock on the Mississippi

By the spring of 1863 the Confederacy held exactly one stretch of the Mississippi River, and it was holding it with two clenched fists. The river runs north to south down the middle of the continent, and for two years it had been the great prize of the war in the West — whoever held it could move armies and grain and gunboats up and down a thousand miles of water, and whoever lost it was cut in half. By that spring the Union had taken almost all of it. What was left to the South was a single ribbon of about 150 miles, and at each end of that ribbon sat a fortress on a bluff: **Vicksburg** upstream in Mississippi, and **Port Hudson** downstream in Louisiana, about 15 to 25 miles up the river from Baton Rouge. As long as both forts stood, that middle stretch stayed Confederate — and that mattered for one specific reason.

Halfway between the two forts, the **Red River** empties into the Mississippi from the west. The Red was the supply artery of the western Confederacy: cattle, food, and the resources of Texas, Arkansas, and western Louisiana floated down it and crossed the Mississippi to feed the armies fighting in the east. Hold Vicksburg and Port Hudson and you held that crossing open. Take them and the artery is severed — the western Confederacy is sawn off from the eastern, and the river runs Union from its source to the Gulf. The whole campaign in this corner of the war was a fight to close that last lock.

Why was Port Hudson so hard to take? Geography. The fort sat on an **80-foot bluff** on the east bank, above a vicious **hairpin bend** in the river where the channel doubles back on itself in nearly a U-turn. The bend forced any steamboat coming through to slow almost to a stop — directly under the Confederate guns. Roughly **thirteen heavy cannon** were dug into the bluff facing the water, and a boat creeping around that bend was a target that could barely move. [VERIFY gun count: factpack §2 gives "~13 heavy guns."]

[FIG: strategic map — the Mississippi N→S, Vicksburg upstream/N, Red River mouth between, Port Hudson on its bluff at the bend, Baton Rouge downstream/S. Caption: "The last Confederate-held stretch of the Mississippi, locked at both ends — Vicksburg upstream, Port Hudson downstream, the Red River feeding in between." Credit: Stuff Happened map]

Holding the bluff was Major General **Franklin Gardner (South)**, a professional soldier with a complicated story. He was a New York native by birth who had married into a Louisiana family and thrown in with the Confederacy. His garrison numbered somewhere around **6,800 to 7,500 men** — sources disagree on the exact figure — dug into an arc of earthworks on the land side: a semicircle of trenches, redoubts, and parapets, anchored on the river at both ends, three to four and a half miles long, with strongpoints the defenders named **Fort Desperate**, the **Priest Cap**, and the **Citadel**. In front of that line lay the deadliest part of all: deep forested ravines, some 60 feet down, choked with swamp and cane, a natural maze that would break up an attacking line into a few stumbling men and then sweep them with crossfire. It was, in short, a position built to kill anyone who came at it head-on. Which is exactly what the Union army was about to do.

*Meanwhile, in Mississippi:* The reason Port Hudson was worth besieging at all was happening 150 river-miles north, where Major General **Ulysses S. Grant (North)** was closing his own ring around Vicksburg that same spring. The two sieges were never really separate operations — they were two ends of one strategic act. Vicksburg was the bigger prize and got the bigger army; Port Hudson was the southern bookend, the cork in the bottom of the bottle. Neither fort meant much without the other. Take only one and the river was still blocked. The men dying in the ravines below Port Hudson were, in a sense, fighting to make Grant's victory upstream worth something — and, though none of them could know it yet, their fort's fate had already been chained to his.

## the-night-run · Port Hudson · Farragut runs the guns · Running the Batteries by Night

Before the army ever marched on Port Hudson, the navy tried to handle it the navy's way — by simply steaming past. The man who tried it was Rear Admiral **David G. Farragut (North)**, the most aggressive sailor in the Union fleet, the officer who had already run his ships past the forts below New Orleans and captured the South's largest city the year before. His problem in March 1863 was the Red River crossing: Confederate supplies were still flowing across the Mississippi above Port Hudson, and Farragut wanted his warships upstream to cut that traffic. To get them upstream, they had to pass under the bluff guns. So on the night of **March 14, 1863**, two full months before the land siege began, he tried to run them.

He had seven vessels: the big sloops-of-war *Hartford* (his flagship), *Richmond*, and *Monongahela*; the side-wheel frigate *Mississippi*; and a cluster of smaller gunboats, several of them lashed to the larger ships' sides so a crippled boat could be dragged along by a healthy one. The plan was to slip past in the dark. It did not stay dark. The Confederates lit bonfires on the west bank and set the whole river aglow, and the bluff batteries opened on the fleet as it crawled around the bend.

What followed was about three hours of close-range slaughter on the water. The guns on the 80-foot bluff fired down onto the decks; the ships, fighting the current and the hairpin turn, could barely make headway. The frigate **USS *Mississippi* ran aground** under the guns, was set ablaze to keep her out of Confederate hands, and burned through the night until her fire reached her magazine. *Richmond* was disabled and drifted back downriver. One by one the ships fell back — all but two. Only the *Hartford*, with the gunboat *Albatross* lashed to her side, fought all the way through and continued upstream toward the Red River. Union losses that night ran to roughly 78 killed or missing and 35 wounded; the Confederates on the bluff lost almost no one. [VERIFY casualty figures: factpack §5 gives "~78 killed/missing, ~35 wounded."]

[FIG: night-run map — the hairpin bend, the bluff batteries firing down, the fleet strung out below, *Mississippi* aground and burning, *Hartford* + *Albatross* breaking through upstream. Caption: "Farragut's night run, March 14, 1863 — only the *Hartford* and *Albatross* got past; the *Mississippi* burned under the bluff." Credit: Stuff Happened map]

The run made one thing brutally clear. Farragut had gotten a single ship past, but he had lost a frigate and proved the larger point in the worst way: the bluff guns could not simply be steamed past. A fleet could not pry Port Hudson off the river. If the fort was going to fall, it would have to be **taken from the land** — surrounded, dug out, and starved or stormed by an army. That job belonged to someone else.

*Meanwhile, upstream:* The *Hartford*'s lonely run was not wasted, even if six of seven ships failed. With Farragut's flagship and the *Albatross* loose above Port Hudson, the admiral could finally do what the whole operation was for — patrol the reach where the Red River met the Mississippi and choke the Confederate supply traffic feeding across it. One sloop and one gunboat were not a blockade, but they were a knife held against the South's western artery, and they were proof of concept for the strategy that the armies would now have to finish by siege. The river battle of March pointed straight at the land battle of May.

## the-doomed-charges · Port Hudson · The futile assaults · The Charges Into the Ravines

Major General **Nathaniel P. Banks (North)** marched his **Army of the Gulf** up from Baton Rouge in the south and in from Bayou Sara in the north — a pincer — and by **May 22, 1863** he had surrounded Port Hudson's landward earthworks and sealed **Gardner (South)** and his garrison inside. Banks held something like a four-to-one advantage in numbers, perhaps 30,000 men against fewer than 7,500, and he expected the math to settle the matter quickly. He would assault the works, carry them by weight of numbers, and be done. He did not understand the ground. Five days into the siege he ordered the first attack, and the ground taught him.

On **May 27, 1863**, Banks sent his divisions forward against the earthworks. It was supposed to be a coordinated blow — Brigadier Generals Godfrey Weitzel, Cuvier Grover, Christopher Augur, and Thomas Sherman striking together so the defenders couldn't shift men to meet one threat at a time. It came apart instantly. The attacks went in **piecemeal**, one group at a time, funneled by those deep forested ravines into narrow approaches where a broad assault line dissolved into a thin file of men climbing and stumbling through swamp and cane. The Confederates on the parapets simply waited and shot them down. By the end of the day Banks had lost on the order of **1,995 men** — roughly 293 killed, 1,545 wounded, 157 missing — to take ground he did not take. Gardner lost about **235**. The works were untouched.

### The Native Guard's charge — and Captain Cailloux

And it was on that day, on the Confederate right, that the larger meaning of the war walked onto the field. Among the regiments Banks committed were the **1st and 3rd Louisiana Native Guard** — **United States Colored Troops**, Black soldiers raised in Union-occupied New Orleans, **many of them men who had been enslaved** until the war reached them. They were ordered into the assault by Brigadier General **William Dwight (North)**. This was one of the **first major combat actions by Black soldiers in the Civil War**. It came before the more famous fights of that summer — Milliken's Bend, upriver, followed about two weeks later in June, and the 54th Massachusetts charged Fort Wagner in July. Port Hudson came first. [VERIFY the "first" framing: factpack §6 flags NPS-TWHP's unqualified "first" and instructs "one of the first major combat actions," which all sources support.]

Here is the thing to understand about why these men were fighting, because it is the whole point of the war and it was never clearer than here. The Confederacy existed to keep human beings enslaved — that was the institution it had seceded to protect, and the thing its armies were in the field to defend. The men of the Native Guard were charging the works of a slaveholders' republic, and a great many of them were charging to destroy the specific system that had owned them. The South was fighting to keep slavery; these soldiers were fighting to end it, with their own bodies, in the open, against the guns. No one watching that day could mistake what the war was about.

The Native Guard's orders were close to hopeless. They were sent across open ground, including a pontoon-bridge approach along Telegraph Road, straight into what the records describe as heavy crossfire from rifles, field artillery, and the heavy coast guns on the bluff. They crossed it, were torn apart, fell back, reformed, and went forward again — repeatedly — taking heavy losses each time, until staying any longer would have meant annihilation and they were forced to retreat. They did not break and run. They went in, and went in again, into fire that could not be survived in the open, and that is what people remembered.

Leading Company E of the 1st Native Guard was Captain **André Cailloux (North)** — pronounced "ka-YOO." He was a free Black man of New Orleans, and his story carried the whole weight of the cause. He had been **born enslaved** in Plaquemines Parish in 1825 and freed by manumission in 1846, at twenty-one. By the war he was a free man of color, a cigar maker, a property owner, and a community leader, one of the roughly 11,000 free Black people in antebellum New Orleans. As a captain he was among the **first African American officers** in any North American army, and — a fact worth pausing on — he drew **equal pay** with white officers of his rank, $60 a month. He gave his commands in both English and French so that all his men could follow them.

On May 27 he led his company into that crossfire and did not come back. A shot **shattered his left arm**, and he kept going, waving his men forward with the sword in his right hand, until an artillery shell struck him and killed him. [VERIFY Cailloux death details: factpack §6 sources the shattered arm, sword, and shell to ABT-CAIL / WIKI-CAIL; flags the "looting" detail separately.]

And then came the indignity that ties the cause to its cost more sharply than any speech could. After the failed assault, a truce allowed the Union army to recover its **white** dead and wounded from the field — but the Native Guard were not permitted to retrieve their own fallen. Cailloux's body lay where it fell, in front of the Confederate works, **for about 47 days** — through the whole rest of the siege, until the fort surrendered. When his men finally recovered him, they identified him by a ring they recognized. A captain of the United States, killed leading a charge, left to lie on the ground he died taking, because of the color of his skin.

[FIG: assault map — the earthworks ring with Fort Desperate / Priest Cap / Citadel, the forested ravines, the Native Guard's axis on the Confederate right along Telegraph Road and the pontoon-bridge approach, the May 27 attack arrows breaking up in the ravines. Caption: "May 27 — Banks's assault funnels into the ravines and dies; on the Confederate right, the Louisiana Native Guard charge the bluff along Telegraph Road." Credit: Stuff Happened map]

Banks tried once more. After an opening bombardment on June 13, he demanded Gardner surrender; Gardner refused, replying in substance that his duty was to defend the position and so he declined to give it up. [VERIFY Gardner's refusal quote: factpack §7 flags exact wording — render indirectly unless confirmed.] Before dawn on **June 14, 1863**, around 3:30 in the morning, Banks threw a second assault at the Priest Cap and the Citadel. It was the same story written in more blood: another bloody repulse, roughly **1,792 Union casualties** against about **47 Confederate**. Brigadier General **Halbert Paine (North)** was hit and lost a leg. One Union officer who lived through it described the open space before the works strewn with men in blue, dead and dying, and the ditch at the foot of the parapet filled with the living and the dead, the survivors clawing at the face of the earthwork within a few yards of the muzzles. [VERIFY Fitts quote: factpack §7 flags wording — paraphrase as rendered here unless confirmed.]

Two assaults, two repulses: nearly **3,800 men down** in two doomed charges, to take nothing, against a few hundred defenders standing behind dirt. The works had won. Banks finally accepted what the ravines had been telling him since May 22 — Port Hudson would not be stormed. It would have to be starved.

*Meanwhile, in the North:* The Native Guard's charge did not stay on the battlefield; it traveled north as news, and it changed minds. Northern opinion on Black soldiers had been openly skeptical — a wide assumption that freedmen and former slaves would not stand under fire. Port Hudson, with Milliken's Bend two weeks later, began to dismantle that assumption in public. A New York newspaper editorial that June told its readers it was no longer possible to doubt the bravery of the colored race when rightly led, and Banks himself reported that the determination of his Black troops left him no doubt of their fitness for the work. [VERIFY both quotes — NYT editorial date/wording and Banks's report wording flagged in factpack §7; render as paraphrase.] The practical consequence was enormous. The proof these men gave in the ravines helped open the floodgates of Black enlistment in the Union army, which by the war's end numbered close to **200,000 men**. The charge that failed to take a single yard of earthwork helped win the larger war.

## the-starving-siege · Port Hudson · Forty days in the wilderness of death · The Siege and the Surrender

After June 14, Banks stopped trying to take Port Hudson with men's bodies and started taking it with engineering and time. This was the part the manuals were written for. Under his chief engineer, Captain John C. Palfrey, the Union army dug **approach trenches** — saps, zigzag ditches that crept the attackers' line closer and closer to the works without exposing the diggers — and started three **mines**, tunnels driven under the earth toward Fort Desperate, the Priest Cap, and the Citadel, meant to be packed with powder and blown. The guns bombarded the fort day and night. And around all of it pressed the Louisiana summer, which turned out to be a weapon as deadly as any cannon.

Inside the ring, the garrison began to starve. They had been sealed in since May 22, with no way to resupply and a four-to-one army squeezing the perimeter tighter every week. They ate the beef first. Then, when the beef was gone, they ate the **mules**. Then the **dogs and the rats**. They brewed a kind of beer from sugar and molasses for want of anything else. A Confederate soldier's diary recorded the descent plainly, that they had eaten the meat and the bread, then the beef, then the mules, then the dogs and the rats around them. [VERIFY starvation quote: factpack §7 flags attribution/wording — render indirectly as done here.] Scurvy spread for want of fresh food; dysentery and malaria spread in the heat and the trenches; men began to desert. The National Park Service later borrowed a survivor's phrase to name the ordeal: **forty days and nights in the wilderness of death**.

The siege was murderous to the besiegers too, in a quieter way. While the assaults killed in great visible bursts, disease and sunstroke killed steadily for weeks — felling somewhere on the order of **4,000 to 5,000** Union men over the campaign, a toll roughly comparable to all the battle casualties combined. The Mississippi summer did not care which uniform a man wore.

[FIG: siege map — the Union outer semicircle of siege lines and saps facing the Confederate inner earthworks, the three mine approaches toward Fort Desperate / Priest Cap / Citadel, the river at the back. Caption: "The siege, June 15 – July 9 — Union saps and mines crawl toward the works while the garrison eats its mules." Credit: Stuff Happened map]

What finally ended it came not from the trenches but from upstream. On **July 4, 1863, Vicksburg surrendered to Grant.** With Vicksburg gone, the entire logic of Port Hudson collapsed in a single stroke. The fort had never been an end in itself — it existed to help hold the middle stretch of the river that Vicksburg's fall had now lost completely. The Confederacy no longer controlled the Mississippi above Port Hudson, so there was nothing left for Port Hudson to be the southern lock *of*. Gardner was guarding a door whose wall had been torn down. (For the fall of Vicksburg itself, and the river it opened, see the Vicksburg battle.)

When word reached Gardner that Vicksburg was gone, he recognized his position for what it was — hopeless. There was no army coming to relieve him, no point left to defend, and a starving garrison that had already eaten its animals. On **July 9, 1863** — five days after Vicksburg — he surrendered Port Hudson. Roughly **6,340 men** laid down their arms; most, around **5,935**, were paroled, while **405 officers** were kept as prisoners and sent off to Memphis and New Orleans. The siege had lasted 48 continuous days, frequently cited as the **longest true siege in American military history**. [VERIFY surrender figures and "longest siege" claim — factpack §1 and §5.] And it had been decided, in the end, not by the bayonet that failed twice in the ravines but by the river and by hunger.

*Meanwhile, upstream and east:* Port Hudson did not fall on its own merits — it fell because of victories elsewhere, and that is the truest thing about it. Vicksburg's surrender on July 4 was half of a stunning week for the Union; the other half was the repulse of Lee's invasion at Gettysburg in Pennsylvania the same week, the pairing conventionally counted as the war's military turning point. Port Hudson's surrender five days later was the quiet coda — the last lock clicking open after the key had already turned. The men who had held the bluff for 48 days, eaten their mules, and buried their dead in the heat had been defending a position that the rest of the war had already made pointless. They simply hadn't known it until the news came down the river.

## the-meaning · Port Hudson · What it opened · The River Whole, and a Question Answered

With Port Hudson's surrender on July 9, 1863, the Union held the **entire Mississippi River**, end to end, from its headwaters down to New Orleans and the Gulf. The last lock was open. For two years the river had been the spine of the war in the West, and now it ran unbroken in Union hands.

That did two enormous things. First, it **split the Confederacy in two.** The Trans-Mississippi — Texas, Arkansas, and western Louisiana, with their cattle, their grain, and their men — was now cut off from the eastern Confederacy by a river the South could no longer cross. The Red River artery that Port Hudson and Vicksburg had existed to protect was severed. The supplies that had been feeding the eastern armies stopped coming. (Lincoln's famous line about the Father of Waters going unvexed to the sea belongs to that larger opening of the river — see the Vicksburg battle, which carries it.)

Second — and this is the part of Port Hudson that outlived its dirt and its dead — it helped **answer a question the country had been arguing about for two years: would Black men fight?** On May 27, in the ravines below the bluff, the Louisiana Native Guard answered it. Many of them had been enslaved; they charged a slaveholders' fortress into hopeless fire, and they kept charging. Together with Milliken's Bend and, that summer, Fort Wagner, Port Hudson broke the claim that the formerly enslaved would not stand and fight. It moved Northern opinion, accelerated the recruitment of the United States Colored Troops toward the nearly 200,000 who would ultimately serve, and gave the cause a martyr in Captain André Cailloux.

Cailloux's story did not end on the field. After the surrender, when his body was finally recovered from the ground where it had lain for those 47 days, New Orleans held him a **public funeral on July 29, 1863** that drew thousands of Black New Orleanians into the streets — a vast procession through the city for a man who had been born enslaved and died a captain of the United States, leading free men against the system that had owned him. A priest, Father Claude Paschal Maistre, defied a Confederate-sympathizing archbishop to perform the rites. Cailloux became a national martyr-hero of Black military service and a recruiting symbol across the North. [VERIFY funeral details — date, crowd, Maistre, archbishop — factpack §6.] The funeral is remembered as a forerunner of the New Orleans tradition that would later become the "second line."

That is the double meaning of Port Hudson. As a battle it was a near-failure redeemed by an event 150 miles away: two slaughtering assaults that took nothing, a siege won by hunger and by Grant's victory at Vicksburg rather than by Banks's army. But as a moment in the war it was a hinge — the southern half of the act that opened the Mississippi and split the South, and one of the first proofs, paid for in the bodies of formerly enslaved men, that the people the war was being fought over would fight, and die, to free themselves.

**Outcome: Union victory.** Port Hudson surrendered on July 9, 1863, five days after Vicksburg fell — and it surrendered *because* Vicksburg fell, the fort having lost the only thing it existed to defend. Its capture, paired with Vicksburg's, gave the Union the entire Mississippi River, severed the Trans-Mississippi from the rest of the Confederacy, and ended the longest true siege in American military history at 48 days. Its deepest mark, though, was made on May 27, when the Louisiana Native Guard's doomed charge — and Captain André Cailloux's death leading it — became one of the war's first public proofs that Black soldiers, many of them formerly enslaved, would stand and fight for their own freedom.

*Meanwhile, across the war:* The summer of 1863 is where the Civil War tilted and did not tilt back. Gettysburg turned Lee's invasion around in the East on July 3; Vicksburg surrendered in the West on July 4; Port Hudson gave up the last of the river on July 9. In nine days the Confederacy lost the initiative it would never recover and was cut physically in two. Port Hudson was the smallest of those three events and the most dependent on the others — but it closed the river, and it carried into the rest of the war a truth the country could no longer deny: that the nearly 200,000 Black men who would serve the Union, many of them lately enslaved, had earned their place in its armies in fire, at places like the bluff above the bend.
