## The one real gap (SHOULD-FIX, not ship-blocking)

**Beat 14 — the Mi'kmaq war's actual ending is told incorrectly/incompletely.**

The draft closes the Mi'kmaq strand with: *"The war wound down only when the French fort that anchored the whole resistance fell in the summer of 1755."* Web verification contradicts this for the Mi'kmaq specifically:

- After Beauséjour fell, Acadian and Mi'kmaq partisans (under the French officer Charles Deschamps de Boishébert, and Acadian leader Joseph Broussard "Beausoleil") fought a guerrilla war that continued until Broussard's band was starved into surrender in **1761**.
- The Anglo-Mi'kmaq conflict was formally ended not by Beauséjour but by the **Peace and Friendship Treaties of 1760–61** — a series signed at Halifax/Fort Frederick (Maliseet/Passamaquoddy 22 Feb 1760; first Mi'kmaq treaty 10 March 1760; the **"Burying the Hatchet" ceremony 25 June 1761**, with Chignecto signing 8 July and Pictou 12 October 1761). These ceded **no land** and preserved hunting/fishing/trading rights; the Mi'kmaq understood them as peace between nations, not submission.

Why this matters for THIS section's stated thesis: the draft makes a point of elevating the Mi'kmaq as "a nation defending its land" with "their own aims," and ends on "the people who lost the most were the ones who had been there longest: the Mi'kmaq." Having promised that weighting, the draft then drops the Mi'kmaq the moment the Acadian story takes over (summer 1755) and never says how *their* war and *their* aims resolved. A reader is left thinking the Mi'kmaq story ended in 1755. It did not — and the resolution (treaties that ceded no land and are still legally live today) is precisely the Native-perspective payoff the brief asked to weight correctly. Right now the Mi'kmaq get a strong middle and no ending.

This is a SHOULD-FIX rather than a MUST-FIX because: (a) the section's *primary* subject is the Acadian removal, which is fully and well covered; (b) the Mi'kmaq are present and correctly weighted everywhere they appear; (c) a theme section is allowed to be selective. But it is the strongest single improvement available, and it fixes a small factual overstatement ("wound down... summer 1755") at the same time. One or two sentences in the closing "Naming it" section (or a short tail to the Mi'kmaq section) would close it.

**This needs new facts → FACT PACK FIRST.** The pack does not contain the 1760–61 treaties or the post-1755 partisan war; the author cannot write this beat from the current pack without inventing. Add to the pack: the Boishébert/Broussard guerrilla phase ending ~1761, and the 1760–61 Peace and Friendship Treaties (no land ceded; Mi'kmaq vs British interpretations; "Burying the Hatchet" 25 June 1761). Sources: Wikipedia *Peace and Friendship Treaties*; Crown-Indigenous Relations Canada fact sheet (rcaanc-cirnac.gc.ca).

## Native perspective — assessment (the brief's special focus)

**Present and, where present, correctly weighted — with one structural shortfall.** The draft does well above the bar for a war vertical: it gives the Mi'kmaq their own section, names their land (Mi'kma'ki), states their aims in their own terms ("to keep the British off their land"), credits their actions as their own initiative ("not French operations; they were Mi'kmaq operations"), names their chiefs, explicitly refuses to let Le Loutre stand in for their agency, names the British scalp bounty as "a price on the heads of a people for living where they had always lived," and gives them the last word in the closing. That is the right instinct and well executed.

The shortfall is purely the missing ending (beat 14): the draft amplifies the Mi'kmaq as co-protagonists, then lets their thread go dark at 1755 while the Acadian thread runs to 1847. Giving them a resolution (the 1760–61 treaties, no land ceded, still live) would make the weighting consistent end-to-end. Nothing in the draft *misrepresents* the Mi'kmaq; it just stops their story early.

## Fact-pack blind spots

1. **Missing: the Mi'kmaq war's resolution.** The pack ends the Father Le Loutre's War section at "the fall of Fort Beauséjour (June 1755)... broke organized French–Mi'kmaq–Acadian resistance" and never covers the post-1755 partisan war or the 1760–61 Peace and Friendship Treaties. Given the pack's own instruction to "keep the Mi'kmaq present as actors with their own stake," this is an internal inconsistency: it provides a Mi'kmaq beginning and middle but no end. The draft inherited exactly that shape. (See beat 14 above for the facts/sources to add.)

2. **Minor: Faragher's own death estimate (~10,000) is not in the pack.** The pack's death-toll section gives the ~5,000 summary tally only. Faragher (whom the pack cites by name in the framing section) estimates the expulsion cost roughly 10,000 lives, and some summaries cite ~55% mortality among the deported. This does not need to change the draft (its "thousands / as many as ~5,000, exact total unknown" phrasing is defensible and honestly hedged), but the pack should note the wider spread so the author/critic knows the ~5,000 figure sits at the low end of the cited range, not the consensus center.

3. **Minor (no action needed): the genocide/ethnic-cleansing distinction.** The pack's framing section is sound. Web check confirms Faragher's nuance is specifically *ethnic cleansing yes, genocide no* (he rests the distinction on intent — relocate, not exterminate). The draft captures this fine via Basque's genocide rejection; no change required.
