# FACT PACK — "The Treaty of Paris & What It Cost" (1782–1783)

*American Revolution vertical, OFF-THE-BATTLEFIELD arc, RECKONING/finale chapter (route slug `treaty-of-paris`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against the cited sources (Avalon Project full treaty text, founders.archives.gov, LOC, Mount Vernon, Maryland State Archives, NARA, UK National Archives–adjacent scholarship); confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. This chapter is the arc's finale: it GATHERS the threads the sibling chapters built — each "who paid" beat is ONE beat with a cross-ref, never a retelling. Cross-ref facts below are checked against the shipped sibling finals (`rev-slavery-and-the-revolution-final.md`, `rev-civil-war-among-themselves-final.md`, `rev-army-from-nothing-final.md`) and the `rev-native-nations-factpack.md` so the finale never contradicts what the app already says. The chapter's register is the BILL, not the victory lap.*

**Orientation.** Yorktown (October 1781) did not end the war; it ended Britain's appetite for it. For two more years the fighting sputtered on — British garrisons sat in New York, Charleston, and Savannah; the frontier war produced some of the conflict's ugliest killing (Gnadenhutten, Blue Licks, both 1782); and the global war Britain was *also* fighting actually turned in its favor (the Saintes, April 1782; Gibraltar, September 1782). Britain could have fought on. Instead the Commons, counting the cost, pulled the plug: North fell in March 1782, and the ministries that followed decided the colonies were worth more as customers than as possessions. In Paris, three (then four) American commissioners — instructed by Congress to do nothing without France — negotiated with the British separately anyway, and got astonishing terms: independence outright, the Mississippi, the fisheries. The treaty's ten articles also carried the bill, payable by people who never sat at the table: the Loyalists (a toothless "earnest recommendation"), the Native nations (not mentioned at all), the enslaved (eleven words in Article 7 with a fifty-year afterlife), the unpaid soldiers, and some 25,000 American dead. The endings — Evacuation Day, the farewell at Fraunces Tavern, Washington handing back his commission at Annapolis — close the arc on the republican precedent the army chapter set up. The country the treaty made was an argument, not an answer; the open items (the West, the debts, the contradictions) are exactly what the rest of the app carries forward.

## 1. YORKTOWN WAS NOT THE END (1781–1783)

- **What Britain still held after Yorktown.** Cornwallis surrendered one field army (~7,000–8,000 men). Britain still garrisoned **New York City, Charleston, and Savannah**, still held Canada, Nova Scotia, and East Florida, and still had the largest navy in the world — with substantial forces (tens of thousands of troops in North America in total) untouched by Yorktown. The war was lost politically, not militarily annihilated. (HIGH on the spine; keep troop totals qualitative — see uncertainties.)
- **The frontier kept bleeding — two beats, both cross-refs to the native-nations chapter; do NOT retell here:**
  - **Gnadenhutten, 7–8 March 1782** — Pennsylvania militia under Col. David Williamson murdered **96** unarmed, pacifist Moravian Christian Delaware (men, women, and children) at their mission town in Ohio; no one was ever punished. ONE sentence here, framed as proof the war's ugliest fighting continued AFTER Yorktown; the full story belongs to the native-nations chapter. (HIGH; matches `rev-native-nations-factpack.md` exactly — 96 total, the 28/29/39 breakdown is MED and should not be used here.)
  - **Blue Licks, 19 August 1782** — a British/Loyalist-and-Native force ambushed pursuing Kentucky militia at the Licking River; about **70 Kentuckians killed** in minutes, among them **Israel Boone, Daniel Boone's son**, with Boone present (he had warned against the attack). Ten months after Yorktown. (HIGH) — Often called "the last battle of the Revolution"; it wasn't quite (Clark's retaliatory raid into the Shawnee towns came in November 1782), so use "among the last," not "the last." (HIGH)
- **The global war actually turned Britain's way in 1782 — two one-beat verifications (war-at-sea owns naval narrative; keep each to a beat):**
  - **The Battle of the Saintes, 12 April 1782** (Caribbean, near Guadeloupe) — Admiral **Sir George Rodney** (36 ships of the line) defeated the French fleet under the **Comte de Grasse** — the same de Grasse whose fleet had sealed Yorktown. De Grasse's flagship *Ville de Paris* surrendered with **de Grasse captured aboard**; about five French ships were taken or destroyed; the planned Franco-Spanish invasion of Jamaica was dead. (HIGH)
  - **The Great Siege of Gibraltar's climax, 13 September 1782** — Spain and France launched the "Grand Assault" with **ten purpose-built floating batteries** (engineered to be fireproof and unsinkable, ~5,260 men aboard) against the British garrison; the defenders' **red-hot shot** set them ablaze and **all ten were destroyed**, with allied losses around 1,400–1,500 for the assault. Howe's fleet resupplied the Rock in October. Gibraltar — besieged since 1779 — never fell, which gutted Spain's central war aim and shaped the final deal (Spain took the Floridas and Minorca instead; Gibraltar stayed British). (HIGH)
- **KEY FRAMING — Britain chose to stop.** By 1782 Britain was winning its world war (the Saintes, Gibraltar) while losing only the American piece of it. The decision to let the thirteen colonies go was a **political calculation made in the House of Commons** — cut the unwinnable land war in America, keep the sugar islands, India, and Gibraltar — not a military collapse. State this plainly; it is the chapter's anti-inevitability beat. (HIGH)
- The war had been ruinously expensive: the British national debt roughly doubled over the war (from something like £127 million in 1775 toward ~£240–250 million by 1783–84). (MED — standard scholarly figures but not pinned to a primary source this pass; phrase softly, "roughly doubled," or omit the numbers. Note the echo: the *French & Indian* vertical's "Bill Comes Due" chapter opens with the debt from the LAST Treaty of Paris, 1763 — the 1763/1783 symmetry is a legitimate, verified framing beat.)

## 2. LONDON TURNS (November 1781 – July 1782)

- **The news arrives, 25 November 1781.** Official word of Yorktown reached **Lord George Germain** (the American Secretary) in Pall Mall around noon on Sunday, 25 November 1781 — about five weeks after the surrender. Germain carried it to **Lord North** at Downing Street between one and two o'clock. (HIGH — Wraxall's account)
- **"O God! it is all over!" — BADGE THE CHAIN.** The diarist **Sir Nathaniel Wraxall** asked Germain how North had taken it. Germain's answer, as Wraxall records it: *"As he would have taken a ball in his breast"* — North threw open his arms and paced the room exclaiming wildly, *"O God! it is all over!"*, "repeating the words many times under emotions of the deepest consternation and distress." **This is secondhand-once-removed: North → Germain (eyewitness) → Wraxall (memoirist), published in Wraxall's *Historical Memoirs of My Own Time* decades later (1815).** Use it — it is the standard account — but attribute it as Germain's account via Wraxall ("as the story was told..."), never as a stenographic record. (MED as verbatim; HIGH that this is the canonical chain.) (HANDLE-WITH-CARE #1)
- **George III's first reaction** was the opposite of North's: his note to Germain acknowledged "the deepest concern" at "the unfortunate result of the operations in Virginia" but showed no intention of quitting. (HIGH)
- **The Commons votes the war down (February–March 1782):**
  - **22 February 1782** — General **Henry Seymour Conway**'s motion against continuing offensive war in America **failed by one vote**. (HIGH)
  - **27 February 1782** — Conway's renewed motion **passed, 234–215**: the Commons formally resolved against "the further prosecution of offensive warfare on the continent of North America." (HIGH)
  - Early–mid March 1782 — no-confidence motions came within single digits (one censure motion failed 236–227 on 15 March). (HIGH)
  - **20 March 1782** — North announced his resignation in the Commons, pre-empting a no-confidence vote he would have lost. He had been prime minister for twelve years. **The first British ministry ever brought down by what was in effect a parliamentary vote of no confidence.** (HIGH on 20 March as the resignation announcement; some accounts date the formal end of the ministry 27 March, when Rockingham kissed hands — don't build a scene on the gap, see uncertainties.)
- **George III drafted an abdication message** (the undelivered draft survives, c. 27 March 1782): finding himself unable to conduct the war "with effect" or obtain any acceptable peace, "His Majesty therefore with much sorrow finds he can be of no further utility to his native country which drives him to the painful step of quitting it for ever." He never sent it. One beat — the king took losing America that hard. (HIGH — LOC "Two Georges" exhibition / Georgian Papers)
- **Rockingham, then Shelburne.** The **Marquess of Rockingham** (the man who had repealed the Stamp Act in 1766 — terse cross-chapter callback) formed a ministry in late March 1782 committed to peace — then **died on 1 July 1782**. The **Earl of Shelburne** succeeded him and owned the peace. (HIGH)
- **Shelburne's bet — let them go, then trade with them.** Shelburne saw American independence as an opportunity: Britain could keep the *profits* of America (as a trading partner) without the *costs* (garrisons, administration, wars). Influenced by free-trade thinking (his circle included the abbé Morellet; the intellectual climate was Adam Smith's — *Wealth of Nations*, 1776), he favored **generous boundaries** — the whole trans-Appalachian West — precisely to bind the new republic to British commerce and pry it away from France. Historians (Harlow, Ritcheson, et al.) treat the generosity as strategy, not sentiment. Keep the sugar islands, India, Gibraltar; concede the continent's interior to a future customer. (HIGH on the framing; MED on naming Morellet/Smith — keep light, "the new free-trade thinking," rather than implying Smith advised him.)

## 3. THE NEGOTIATORS & THE VERGENNES PROBLEM

- **The American commission:** Congress named five commissioners; **four** signed: **Benjamin Franklin** (in Paris throughout), **John Jay** (arrived from Madrid, June 1782), **John Adams** (arrived from the Netherlands, late October 1782), and **Henry Laurens** (arrived two days before the preliminaries were signed). **Thomas Jefferson was appointed but never arrived** (his 1781 appointment lapsed amid his wife's illness and death; renamed late 1782, his sailing was delayed until word came that the preliminaries were signed). (HIGH on the four + Jefferson never arriving; MED on the fine detail of Jefferson's delays — one clause at most.)
- **Henry Laurens, fresh from the Tower.** Captured at sea in 1780, Laurens spent over a year imprisoned in the **Tower of London** — the only American ever held there — and was released **31 December 1781**, ultimately **exchanged for Cornwallis**. He reached Paris just **two days before** the preliminary signing and made one famous contribution (see Article 7, §4). (HIGH)
- **The British side:** **Richard Oswald** — an elderly Scottish merchant who knew America well, chosen by Shelburne as a congenial channel to Franklin — negotiated the preliminaries; **David Hartley**, MP (a longtime friend of Franklin and opponent of the war), signed the definitive treaty in 1783. (HIGH) Oswald had grown rich partly in the Atlantic slave trade, and Laurens — formerly one of America's biggest slave merchants — had been his Charleston business associate; Oswald had even helped stand bail for Laurens's release from the Tower. (MED — widely documented but not primary-verified this pass; if used, one dry clause, no elaboration. It is an honest-register beat: both sides' peacemakers had slave-trade money on their hands.)
- **Congress's instructions — the leash (15 June 1781).** Congress, desperate for French support after the disasters of 1780, instructed its commissioners (verbatim): *"to undertake nothing in the negotiations for peace or truce without their [France's] knowledge and concurrence; and ultimately to govern yourselves by their advice and opinion."* The only non-negotiables were independence and the alliance. The commissioners were, on paper, France's juniors at their own peace. (HIGH — Founders Online, the 15 June 1781 instructions)
- **Jay's suspicion — the Rayneval mission (September 1782).** Vergennes's under-secretary **Joseph-Matthias Gérard de Rayneval** left secretly for England (traveling under the name "Castel") to sound out Shelburne. Jay learned of it, suspected a Franco-Spanish arrangement at America's expense (Spain wanted the trans-Appalachian West hemmed in; Rayneval had floated boundary ideas unfavorable to the US), and — without Franklin's concurrence — sent **Benjamin Vaughan** to London (departed 11 September 1782) to tell Shelburne that Britain's true interest was a fast, generous, direct deal with the Americans. (HIGH on the events; **DISPUTED on Rayneval's actual purpose** — later historians note his mission was chiefly about Franco-Spanish terms, esp. Gibraltar, not carving up America. Present it as Jay's *suspicion*, real and consequential, not as proven French treachery.)
- **They negotiated separately — state it precisely.** From fall 1782 the Americans negotiated terms with Oswald **without informing Vergennes of the substance and without seeking his advice — a direct violation of the June 1781 instructions**. BUT: the result was **preliminary articles explicitly conditional on a general peace** — the preamble states the treaty "is not to be concluded untill terms of a peace shall be agreed upon between Great Britain and France." So it was **not a separate peace in the treaty-law sense** (the 1778 alliance's no-separate-peace pledge was arguably skirted in spirit, not broken in letter). Both halves are true; hold them together. (HIGH — Avalon preliminary articles text + the instructions) (HANDLE-WITH-CARE #4)
- **Vergennes's actual reaction — annoyance, admiration, and a loan.** Vergennes was struck less by betrayal than by what the Americans got — his remark (to Rayneval): **"The English buy peace rather than make it."** (HIGH that he said it; MED on pinning the recipient/date — attribute simply "Vergennes marveled.") His formal protest to Franklin (15 December 1782) reproached the commissioners for sending the articles to America without even informing the French court. (HIGH)
- **Franklin's smoothing letter, 17 December 1782** — shown to Adams, Jay, and Laurens, who unanimously told him to send it. Verbatim (Founders Online): *"Nothing has been agreed in the Preliminaries contrary to the Interests of France; and no Peace is to take Place between us and England till you have concluded yours."* He conceded the commissioners had been guilty of neglecting a point of *bienséance* (propriety) — and then the famous turn: *"The English, I just now learn, flatter themselves they have already divided us. I hope this little Misunderstanding will therefore be kept a perfect Secret, and that they will find themselves totally mistaken."* (HIGH — verified verbatim)
- **The sequence that proves it worked:** Vergennes accepted the apology — and France extended a **new loan of six million livres** (1783); the first installment crossed the Atlantic on the same ship that carried the preliminary articles to Congress (the *General Washington*). (HIGH on the loan; MED on the same-ship detail — a fine beat if kept loose, "the ship that carried the articles home also carried French money.") The man who had just been cut out of the deal financed its delivery.

## 4. THE TERMS, ARTICLE BY ARTICLE (the definitive treaty's 10 articles — verified against the Avalon Project full text)

*All quotations below are verbatim from the Avalon Project transcription of the Definitive Treaty of Peace, 3 September 1783. The preliminary articles of 30 November 1782 contained essentially the same terms (verified against Avalon's preliminary-articles text); the definitive treaty carried them over nearly unchanged.* (HIGH)

- **Article 1 — independence, by name, state by state.** *"His Brittanic Majesty acknowledges the said United States, viz., New Hampshire, Massachusetts Bay, Rhode Island and Providence Plantations, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina and Georgia, to be free sovereign and independent states, that he treats with them as such, and for himself, his heirs, and successors, relinquishes all claims to the government, propriety, and territorial rights of the same and every part thereof."* Note the plural — thirteen states named one by one, not one nation. (HIGH — verbatim; note Avalon prints "Brittanic," its transcription's spelling)
- **Article 2 — the boundaries.** A line from the St. Croix River and the highlands, down the 45th parallel, **through the middle of the Great Lakes** (Ontario, Erie, Huron, Superior) to the Lake of the Woods, then "on a due west course to the river Mississippi" — and **down the middle of the Mississippi** to the 31st parallel, then east along it to the Atlantic via the St. Marys River. In plain terms: the new republic got everything from the Atlantic to the Mississippi, the Great Lakes to (roughly) Florida — **roughly doubling the territory the thirteen states actually occupied**, almost all of it Native homeland (see §6). (HIGH on the line; the "doubling" framing is fair but keep it qualitative)
- **Article 3 — the fisheries: Adams's crusade, and one loaded word.** Americans *"shall continue to enjoy unmolested the **right** to take fish"* on the Grand Bank and the high-seas banks of Newfoundland — but shall have *"**liberty** to take fish"* on the coasts of Newfoundland and the other British dominions, and "liberty" to dry and cure fish on unsettled shores of Nova Scotia, the Magdalens, and Labrador. **"Right" on the open sea; "liberty" (a revocable permission) inshore.** Adams — New England's man — fought the fisheries fight to the final session and counted it among his life's services; the right/liberty distinction would feed Anglo-American disputes for decades. (HIGH — verbatim wording verified; Adams's central role HIGH; keep the "decades of disputes" to one clause)
- **Article 4 — the debts.** *"Creditors on either side shall meet with no lawful impediment to the recovery of the full value in sterling money of all bona fide debts heretofore contracted."* In practice: British merchants could sue Americans (mostly southern planters) for prewar debts. The states obstructed it for years — one of the two great broken promises (see §8). (HIGH)
- **Article 5 — the Loyalists: toothless by design.** *"Congress shall **earnestly recommend** it to the legislatures of the respective states to provide for the restitution of all estates, rights, and properties, which have been confiscated…"* Congress could only *recommend* because it had no power to compel the states — **and every negotiator on both sides knew the recommendation would be ignored**. The British needed the fig leaf for Parliament; the Americans gave them words. Contemporary acknowledgment is explicit in the structure itself (recommendation, not obligation) and was said plainly at the time — British critics of the treaty savaged the article as the abandonment of the Loyalists, and the parliamentary debates over the peace (which nearly brought Shelburne down in February 1783 — his ministry fell partly over the peace terms) dwelt on exactly this. (HIGH on the verbatim + the toothlessness-was-understood framing; MED on any specific contemporary quotation — none verified this pass, so characterize the debate, don't quote it. Shelburne's fall: HIGH that the Commons censured the peace terms in February 1783 and Shelburne resigned; keep to a clause.)
- **Article 6 — no future persecution.** *"There shall be no future confiscations made nor any prosecutions commenced against any person or persons for, or by reason of, the part which he or they may have taken in the present war."* The forward-looking half of the Loyalist settlement — also widely flouted in the first postwar years. (HIGH)
- **Article 7 — peace, withdrawal, and eleven words about human beings.** *"All prisoners on both sides shall be set at liberty, and his Brittanic Majesty shall with all convenient speed, and **without causing any destruction, or carrying away any Negroes or other property of the American inhabitants**, withdraw all his armies, garrisons, and fleets from the said United States…"* — **Henry Laurens, on the day of the preliminary signing (30 November 1782), procured the insertion of the "Negroes" clause** — it was interlined into the document, and the witnesses' notation records the interlineation. A slaveholder's clause, classing people with property. **Sir Guy Carleton declined to apply it retroactively** — at the Orangetown meeting with Washington (6 May 1783) he held that people freed by British proclamation *before* the treaty were not "property of the American inhabitants" to be returned; the slavery chapter tells that whole story. Here: the TREATY text, Laurens's insertion, and the diplomatic fight in ONE beat with the cross-ref. (HIGH — verbatim verified, interlineation verified, Laurens's role verified; Carleton matches the shipped slavery chapter)
- **Article 8** — Mississippi navigation "shall forever remain free and open" to both British subjects and US citizens. (HIGH)
- **Article 9** — conquests made before the articles arrived in America to be restored without compensation. (HIGH)
- **Article 10** — ratifications to be exchanged within **six months** — the clock that nearly embarrassed Congress (see §5). (HIGH)
- **The secret separate article (preliminaries only).** The 30 November 1782 preliminaries carried a **separate article, kept from France**: if Britain ended the war holding **West Florida**, its northern boundary with the US would run further north (a line due east from the mouth of the Yazoo) than the 31st parallel offered to Spain. It never operated — Spain kept the Floridas — but when the packet reached Congress it caused real discomfort about what the commissioners had signed in secret. (HIGH on the article's existence and content; MED on the "discomfort in Congress" — one clause, no scene.)
- **The rest of the Peace of Paris (one beat — the American treaty was one of several signed 3 September 1783):** Britain's separate treaties with France and Spain were signed at **Versailles** the same day. **Spain got East Florida and kept West Florida and Minorca; Britain kept Gibraltar** (the siege's survival made that non-negotiable) and recovered the Bahamas; **France got Tobago and Senegal** — and a mountain of debt that historians point toward 1789. The Floridas' boundary ambiguity (31° vs. 32°28′) was left to poison US–Spanish relations. (HIGH; the France-debt-to-Revolution line is standard but keep it to a dry clause, MED as a causal claim)

## 5. SIGNED, SEALED, RATIFIED (November 1782 – May 1784)

- **Preliminary articles signed: 30 November 1782**, Paris — Oswald for Britain; **Adams, Franklin, Jay, Laurens** for the United States. Conditional by their own terms on a Franco-British peace. (HIGH)
- **The general peace caught up:** Britain, France, and Spain signed *their* preliminaries on **20 January 1783**; hostilities were then formally suspended (Congress proclaimed the cessation of hostilities **11 April 1783**). (HIGH on 20 Jan; MED on 11 April — standard date, not primary-verified this pass.)
- **Definitive treaty signed: Wednesday, 3 September 1783**, at the **Hôtel d'York — David Hartley's Paris lodgings, today 56 rue Jacob** — by Hartley, Adams, Franklin, and Jay (Laurens was absent). NOT at Versailles with the other treaties — the Americans and Hartley signed in a hotel room on the Left Bank, a deliberately ordinary setting for an extraordinary document. (HIGH; note pitfall — Avalon's page header misprints "September 30"; the treaty is 3 September. Also note Laurens did not sign the definitive treaty though he appears in West's painting of the *preliminary* negotiators.)
- **THE IMAGE WITH THE STORY — Benjamin West's unfinished painting.** West (the Pennsylvania-born painter who was simultaneously history painter to George III) began a group portrait of the peacemakers: ***American Commissioners of the Preliminary Peace Agreement with Great Britain*** (1783–84, Winterthur Museum). The American side sits finished — **Jay, Adams, Franklin, Laurens, and William Temple Franklin** (Franklin's grandson, the commission's secretary) — and the right half of the canvas, where the British negotiators belonged, is **blank**: **Richard Oswald and his secretary Caleb Whitefoord declined to sit**. Britain had no wish to pose for its own defeat (and Oswald, elderly and blind in one eye, reportedly disliked being depicted at all — MED on that detail). West never finished it. Half a peace, painted. (HIGH — Winterthur/State Department Diplomatic Reception Rooms; this is candidate image #1, and the caption IS the story)
- **Ratification: Annapolis, 14 January 1784.** Congress — sitting in the **Old Senate Chamber of the Maryland State House** — needed **nine states** for a treaty and for weeks could not assemble them (a brutal winter; absentee delegates; Jefferson insisted a nine-state ratification was constitutionally required and beat back a seven-state shortcut). The arrival of **Richard Beresford of South Carolina** completed the ninth delegation, and Congress ratified unanimously on **14 January 1784** — uncomfortably near the treaty's six-month deadline; the ratification crossed the Atlantic late, and **ratifications were exchanged in Paris on 12 May 1784**. (HIGH — Maryland State Archives / LOC; the deadline was technically blown without consequence — keep as a wry clause, MED on framing it "blown")

## 6. WHO PAID — the arc's ledger (every beat ONE beat + cross-ref; the sibling chapters own the stories)

- **The Loyalists.** Around **60,000 Loyalists** left the new United States (Maya Jasanoff's careful modern count; older estimates ran 80,000–100,000), taking with them about **15,000 enslaved people**. Article 5's "earnest recommendation" produced almost nothing from the states; instead **Parliament created the Loyalist Claims Commission (July 1783)**, which examined **3,225 claims** and paid out, over years (final payments 1789, with parliamentary compensation acts in 1785 and 1788), on the order of **£3 million** — Britain compensating its own refugees for the treaty's dead letter. (HIGH on 60,000/15,000 — matches the shipped civil-war chapter; HIGH on the commission + 3,225; MED on the ~£3 million total — phrase "on the order of.") **Cross-ref:** the civil-war chapter owns the exodus (Shelburne the boomtown, New Brunswick, the diaspora). One beat here: the treaty article that failed, and who actually paid.
- **The Native nations — not present, not mentioned.** **The treaty contains no Native signatories, no Native provisions, and no mention of Native nations at all** — verified against the full text of all ten articles. Britain transferred the entire trans-Appalachian West — the country of its own allies (Haudenosaunee, Shawnee, Delaware, Cherokee, and dozens more) — without telling them, consulting them, or naming them. The US then treated the ceded lands as conquered: at **Fort Stanwix (October 1784)** American commissioners dictated a punitive cession to the Haudenosaunee — one forward line ONLY; the native-nations chapter owns the story. (HIGH; matches `rev-native-nations-factpack.md`)
- **The enslaved.** Article 7's eleven words. At New York the British registered some **3,000 Black refugees** in the **Book of Negroes** and sailed them to freedom over American protest (Carleton's stand); from **Savannah and Charleston (1782)** they had already carried off far larger numbers — roughly 3,500 and more than 5,000 — **still enslaved**, the property of departing Loyalists. (HIGH; numbers match the shipped slavery chapter — do not restate beyond one beat; the slavery chapter owns the evacuation.) **Article 7's long afterlife:** American slaveholders' compensation claims poisoned Anglo-American relations for decades — the Jay Treaty (1794) settled debts and the occupied forts but got nothing on the carried-off slaves; the same clause-fight recurred after the War of 1812 (Treaty of Ghent), went to the Tsar of Russia as arbitrator, and ended only in **1826, when Britain paid the United States $1,204,960** for people freed in the *second* war. One line. (HIGH — verified)
- **The soldiers.** The army that won the war was furloughed home from June 1783 with **three months' pay in "Morris notes" and final-settlement certificates** — paper promises most men sold to speculators for a fraction of face value. ONE beat + cross-ref; the army chapter owns Newburgh, the furloughs, and the Cincinnati. (HIGH; matches the shipped army chapter)
- **The dead.** The standard scholarly floor is **Howard Peckham's ~25,000 American military deaths** (battle, camp disease, and prison ships combined) — about **1 percent of a population of roughly 2.5 million**; estimates running higher (to ~35,000) exist, and Patriot civilian deaths, Loyalist deaths, British/German deaths, and Native deaths sit on top of that, mostly uncounted. **The per-capita claim, in its defensible form:** *as a share of the American population, the Revolution killed more than any other war in American history except the Civil War.* Do NOT say "bloodiest until the Civil War" without the per-capita qualifier, and do not give a single hard total — "some 25,000 American soldiers and sailors, probably more" is the honest register. (HIGH on Peckham ~25,000 as the standard figure; MED on upper range; the per-capita formulation as stated is the standard one — keep its exact form.) (HANDLE-WITH-CARE #6)

## 7. THE ENDINGS (July 1782 – December 1783)

- **The evacuations, in order:** **Savannah, 11 July 1782** (garrison and officials out; Loyalists and their enslaved people to St. Augustine, the West Indies, New York). **Charleston, 14 December 1782** (about 4,200 Loyalist civilians and over 5,000 people of African descent carried out — the latter matching the slavery chapter's beat). **New York, 25 November 1783** — the last and largest, almost two years after Yorktown. (HIGH)
- **Evacuation Day, 25 November 1783.** The British timetable set the handover for noon; as the last redcoats embarked, **Washington and Governor George Clinton rode into Manhattan at the head of the American column** — a city held by the British for seven years. New York celebrated November 25 as "Evacuation Day" for over a century. (HIGH)
  - **The greased flagpole — DOCUMENTATION LEVEL, handle honestly.** The famous story: departing soldiers nailed the Union Jack to the flagpole at Fort George, cut away the halyards, and greased the pole; a young sailor-veteran, **John Van Arsdale**, nailed on cleats, climbed it, tore down the British colors, and ran up the Stars and Stripes before the fleet cleared the harbor. **The tale appears only in later accounts** — its fullest canonical telling is *Evacuation Day, 1783* (1883), written for the centennial by **James Riker — Van Arsdale's own grandson** — and details (including the climber's age) shift across versions. A greased pole and SOME flag incident at Fort George is plausibly grounded in early recollections, but the polished Van Arsdale heroics are family-transmitted legend. Tell it AS the story New York told, badge it ("the story, as New Yorkers told it for a century..."), don't assert it as documented fact. (MED on any flag incident; the specific Van Arsdale narrative is DISPUTED-grade for documentation) (HANDLE-WITH-CARE #2)
- **Fraunces Tavern, 4 December 1783.** Nine days after Evacuation Day, Washington took leave of his officers in the Long Room of Fraunces Tavern, Pearl Street. **The ONLY firsthand account is Colonel Benjamin Tallmadge's memoir — written in 1830, forty-seven years later** (badge it as a memoir, not a transcript). Tallmadge's account: Washington, filling a glass of wine, said *"With a heart full of love and gratitude I now take leave of you. I most devoutly wish that your latter days may be as prosperous and happy as your former ones have been glorious and honorable."* Knox, nearest, stepped forward; Washington, "suffused in tears, was incapable of utterance," and embraced him in silence — then every officer in the room, one by one. Tallmadge: *"Such a scene of sorrow and weeping I had never before witnessed… Not a word was uttered to break the solemn silence."* (HIGH that this is Tallmadge's 1830 text; the quotes are verbatim from the memoir — present them as Tallmadge's recollection.)
- **Annapolis, 23 December 1783 — the resignation.** Around noon in the **Old Senate Chamber of the Maryland State House**, before a Congress presided over by **Thomas Mifflin** — note the verified irony, which the army chapter set up: Mifflin was the failed Quartermaster General of the Valley Forge winter, and now, as President of Congress, he received the victorious commander's commission. The delegates sat with hats on (the doffed hat, not the bow, marking civil power's precedence — MED on the etiquette gloss; the seated-and-covered fact is standard). **James McHenry**, an eyewitness, wrote the same day: "It was a solemn and affecting spectacle; such an one as history does not present… The spectators all wept, and there was hardly a member of Congress who did not drop tears." Washington's hand **shook** as he read; at the passage commending his officers he "was obliged to support the paper with both hands." (HIGH — McHenry's letter, Maryland State Archives / Mount Vernon)
  - **The speech's close, verbatim** (Washington's own text, Founders Online / Maryland State Archives): *"Having now finished the work assigned me, I retire from the great theatre of Action — and bidding an Affectionate farewell to this August body under whose orders I have so long acted, I here offer my Commission, and take my leave of all the employments of public life."* (HIGH — verified verbatim)
  - He left Annapolis at once and **reached Mount Vernon on Christmas Eve** — a private citizen for the first time in eight and a half years. (HIGH)
- **"The greatest man in the world" — BADGE THE CHAIN HARD.** The line attributed to George III exists in **two secondhand versions, both told by the painter Benjamin West, recorded years after the fact, and they disagree**: (1) **Joseph Farington's diary** (entry of 28 December 1799, recounting a wartime conversation): the King asked West what Washington would do if America won independence; West: retire to his farm; the King: *"if he did, he would be the greatest man in the world."* (2) **Rufus King's memorandum** (3 May 1797, after Washington declined a third term): the King told West that Washington's resignations placed him "in a light the most distinguished of any man living" — *"the greatest character of the age."* **Neither is a verbatim record of George III; both are West retelling royal conversation from memory.** Usable ONLY with the badge ("a story the painter Benjamin West told…"); never as a clean royal quote. (HIGH on the two-source chain as described — LOC Manuscript Division analysis; the quote itself is MED-at-best as the king's actual words) (HANDLE-WITH-CARE #3)
- **Why the resignation mattered — the republican precedent.** A victorious general, holding the only real army on the continent, handed his power back to a civilian Congress that could not even pay his men — the **Cincinnatus** act the army chapter set up (the Society of the Cincinnati had been founded that spring; the farmer-general who went back to his plow). This is the close's load-bearing beat: state it concretely (the trembling hands, the commission on the table, the road to Mount Vernon), not as editorial. (HIGH on facts; the meaning-beat is the chapter's own and must avoid meta-narration)

## 8. THE LEDGER LEFT OPEN (the close — what the treaty did not settle)

- **Britain kept the northwestern posts.** Despite Article 7's "all convenient speed," British garrisons held **Detroit, Niagara, Michilimackinac, Oswego, and other posts on US soil until 1796**, citing American violations of Articles 4 and 5 (states blocking British creditors; Loyalist property never restored). Both sides broke the treaty first, depending on who was counting. Resolved only by the Jay Treaty (Britain out of the posts by June 1796). One forward beat. (HIGH)
- **The debts and the Loyalist articles stayed broken** for over a decade (state courts vs. Article 4; the states' near-total refusal on Article 5) — feeding the case that the Confederation Congress couldn't enforce its own treaty, which is part of the road to the Constitution. One dry clause; no constitutional narration. (HIGH on the facts; keep the Constitution link to a single forward-pointing clause)
- **The West the treaty gave away** was full of nations that hadn't surrendered anything (Fort Stanwix and everything after — native-nations chapter), and full of contradictions (slavery's expansion or restriction in the territories) the next decades would fight over. (HIGH; one beat each)
- **The closing image is Annapolis**: the commission handed over, the hat doffed, the road home. The country the treaty made — a republic twice the size of its settled self, indebted, victorious, internally contradictory — is an argument the rest of the app's chapters carry forward; END on the concrete scene, not on a thesis statement. (Framing instruction, not a fact)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **The North "Oh God! It is all over" chain.** North → Germain (the eyewitness) → Wraxall (memoirist, published 1815, decades later). The canonical account, but secondhand-once-removed and from a memoirist with a flair for scenes. Attribute it ("as Germain told the story…" / "the story that came down…"); never present as a transcript.
2. **The greased flagpole.** A post-hoc story whose fullest telling (1883) was written by the hero's own grandson for the centennial; versions disagree on details. Some flag incident at Fort George is plausible; the polished Van Arsdale heroics are legend-grade. Tell it as the story New York told for a century, explicitly badged.
3. **George III / "the greatest man in the world."** Two versions, both via Benjamin West, recorded 1797 (Rufus King memo: "the greatest character of the age") and 1799 (Farington diary: "the greatest man in the world," of a conversation years earlier); they disagree on timing and wording. Usable only as "a story the King's own painter told." Never as a clean royal quote.
4. **The "separate peace" framing — be precise.** The commissioners DID violate their June 1781 instructions (negotiated and signed without French knowledge of the terms, plus a secret article kept from France). They did NOT sign a separate peace in the legal sense — the preliminaries were expressly conditional on a Franco-British settlement, and the definitive treaties were all signed the same day. Vergennes's reaction was a measured protest plus continued aid (the 6-million-livre loan), not a rupture. Both the violation and the technical fidelity are true; the chapter must carry both. Equally: present Jay's Rayneval suspicion as suspicion — consequential, partially vindicated in spirit (France was not fighting for American maximalism), but Rayneval's mission was chiefly about Franco-Spanish terms (Gibraltar), not carving up America.
5. **No triumphalist register.** The chapter's job is the BILL. The terms were astonishing — say so once, through Vergennes's line ("The English buy peace rather than make it") — and then count who paid: Loyalists, Native nations, the enslaved, the unpaid army, the dead. "America wins" is not the thesis; "this is what the ending cost, and who it cost" is.
6. **The "bloodiest per capita" claim — exact form only.** Defensible: *as a share of the American population, the deadliest war in US history except the Civil War* (Peckham's ~25,000 military deaths ≈ ~1% of ~2.5 million). NOT defensible: "bloodiest war until the Civil War" unqualified, or any hard single death total. Civilian, Loyalist, British/German, and Native deaths are largely uncounted — say so.
7. **The Loyalist articles' toothlessness was understood at the time** — that's the honest framing (a fig leaf both sides knowingly traded) — but no specific contemporary quotation was verified this pass; characterize the parliamentary anger over the abandoned Loyalists (it helped bring Shelburne down, Feb 1783), don't invent a quote.
8. **Article 7 and the slavery chapter's boundary.** This chapter gets the treaty TEXT, Laurens's interlineation, and the Carleton-declined-retroactivity point in one beat; the Orangetown scene, Boston King's terror, the Book of Negroes mechanism, and the evacuation stories belong to the slavery chapter (already shipped — keep numbers consistent: ~3,000 Book of Negroes; ~3,500 Savannah / 5,000+ Charleston carried off enslaved).
9. **The Native silence must be stated as fact, not flourish.** "The treaty does not mention them" is verified against the full text and is the strongest sentence available — don't decorate it. Fort Stanwix 1784 is ONE forward line (matches the native-nations factpack: October 1784, dictated terms, later repudiated and enforced anyway).
10. **Britain as reasoning actor.** North was not a fool, George III not a cartoon tyrant (he drafted an abdication rather than accept the loss), and Shelburne's generosity was strategy (trade over empire). The British cut their losses while WINNING the global war — that's the interesting, true version.
11. **Dates with source-wobble:** North's fall (announced in the Commons 20 March 1782; ministry formally over with Rockingham's appointment 27 March — use 20 March, don't build on the gap). Avalon's page header misprints the definitive treaty as "September 30"; the date is 3 September 1783. Congress's cessation-of-hostilities proclamation (11 April 1783) is standard but was not primary-verified — keep soft if used.
