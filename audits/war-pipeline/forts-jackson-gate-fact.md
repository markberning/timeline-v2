## Methodology

Every factual claim in the draft was independently verified against authoritative external sources: Wikipedia "Battle of Forts Jackson and St. Philip" and "Capture of New Orleans" (the pack's own primary sources), the Naval History & Heritage Command, NPS Battle Detail LA001, American Battlefield Trust, Emerging Civil War, Historic New Orleans Collection, Smithsonian Magazine, and specialized scholarly sources. ## CLAIM-BY-CLAIM VERIFICATION

### Section 1 — The Great Port

**New Orleans ~168,000 people, 6th-largest US city, bigger than Charleston/Richmond/Mobile/Memphis/Savannah combined**
✅ Confirmed. NPS-10FACTS, WP-NEWORLEANS, EMERGINGCW all give 168,000–170,000. The named comparison cities match the 1860 census figures in the pack.

**More than half of all American cotton through the port; outlet for Mississippi valley goods; 2nd to Richmond in manufactured goods**
✅ Confirmed via NPS, EMERGINGCW, WP-NEWORLEANS.

**"New Orleans was also the South's great slave market — the largest slave-trading center in a country that had gone to war to keep human beings as property"**
✅ VERIFIED — this was the author-flagged claim not independently sourced in the pack body. Multiple authoritative sources confirm it:
- Smithsonian Magazine: "Before the Civil War, New Orleans Was the *Center* of the U.S. Slave Trade"
- Historic New Orleans Collection (HNOC): documents 135,000 enslaved people bought and sold in New Orleans, describing it as the nation's largest slave market
- Academic comparative data (Project MUSE / Mapping the Slave Trade, Richmond and New Orleans): New Orleans's slave trade exceeded $8 million annually vs. Richmond's $4 million; both were the two largest markets, with New Orleans clearly the larger of the two
- Emerging Civil War: describes New Orleans as "the epicenter of the slave trade"
- Wikipedia "New Orleans slave market": calls it "the chief center of the slave trade in the lower South" and "one of the most important American slave markets" (somewhat more hedged, but does not assert a different city was largest)

The draft's phrasing — "the South's great slave market — the largest slave-trading center" — is well-supported. The claim is accurate as written. Note: some sources hedge ("one of the most important") while others are more direct ("the nation's largest"). The draft's phrasing is defensible and consistent with the Smithsonian, HNOC, and academic scholarship.

**Anaconda Plan — Winfield Scott's strangle-by-blockade-and-river strategy**
✅ Confirmed. ABT, ECCW.

**Defenses stripped: Confederate focus upstream (Island No. 10, Fort Pillow, Vicksburg); Henry/Donelson Feb 1862; Shiloh April 6–7 drained resources; Hollins's fleet sent upstream**
✅ Confirmed. ECCW, EMERGINGCW.

## MUST-FIX

### MUST-FIX #1 — Casualty language ("remarkably light" / "a few dozen killed and a hundred-odd wounded") needs a specific clarification or caveat

**Draft states (Section 2, passage battle):** "The casualties of that night were, for a battle this decisive, remarkably light — on the order of a few dozen Union sailors killed and a hundred-odd wounded."

**Draft states (Section 3, closing):** "it had cost the Union, on the night that decided it, only a few dozen lives."

**Verification findings:**
- The NPS total for the FULL battle (April 18–28) is 229 Union / 782 Confederate — total 1,011. These are the figures that appear in any stat block.
- The passage-night-only figures (April 24) are: 36–39 Union killed, 135–171 Union wounded (ECCW, 64PARISHES, HISTORYCENTRAL). These are the narrower figures.
- "A few dozen Union sailors killed" is consistent with the passage-night figures (36–39 killed). But "a hundred-odd wounded" is the lower end of the passage-night wounded range (135–171); calling it "a hundred-odd" slightly understates it.
- More importantly, calling casualties "remarkably light" while the full-battle stat block will show 229/782 (1,011 total) could confuse readers if not scoped. The draft's fact ledger (§10A) says it used the "narrower passage-night figures" intentionally, distinct from the NPS full-battle totals reserved for the stat block. This is a legitimate narrative choice (the passage was 90 minutes of chaos; the broader total includes five days of bombardment and fort surrender).
- The phrase "only a few dozen lives" at the section's close is the bigger issue — "a few dozen" typically means around 24–36; the actual passage-night dead was 36–39. This is marginally accurate but risks misleading the reader.

**Required fix:** Tighten the casualty language in the closing paragraph to be more specific. Change "only a few dozen lives" to "fewer than forty Union sailors' lives" or similar. The "few dozen killed and a hundred-odd wounded" in Section 2 should be revised to "fewer than forty killed and roughly 135–170 wounded" to avoid both understating and the ambiguity of "a hundred-odd." Alternatively, add the passage-scope qualifier explicitly: "(passage-night losses only; the full nine-day battle cost the Union 229 total casualties [NPS])."

**Why this is MUST-FIX and not just a SHOULD-FIX:** The stat block will show 229/782 and a reader who reads the narrative first ("a few dozen lives") and then sees 229 Union casualties will experience a jarring discrepancy. The draft must either scope the casualty language explicitly to the passage night, or use the full-battle figures, or provide a brief bridge.

## SHOULD-FIX

### SHOULD-FIX #1 — Lovell troop count: 3,000 vs. 4,000

**Draft:** "about 3,000 militia" evacuated 78 miles north to Camp Moore.
**Verification:** The pack gives "~3,000" (ECCW, EMERGINGCW, CAPTURE-WIKI), but one web source (Civil War Monitor / Fall of New Orleans) gives "4,000 Confederate troops." The pack's 3,000 figure is from ECCW and EMERGINGCW and aligns with the same figure Lovell had in the city (not all troops evacuated from the city — he pulled ~3,000 militia). The 4,000 figure may reflect a broader departmental count or include forces from outside the city. The pack's 3,000 is defensible. **Low priority** — the draft's figure is within the range of sources. No change strictly required, but a hedge ("roughly 3,000") is already present and appropriate.

### SHOULD-FIX #2 — Mumford flagstaff description

**Draft:** "hanged...from a flagstaff jutting out of the very Mint where he had pulled the flag down."
**Verification:** WP-MUMFORD gives the execution location as "the courtyard of the mint." Some sources say "from a window of the Mint." The "flagstaff jutting out" phrasing is a common popular account, though the precise location within the Mint (courtyard vs. window vs. flagstaff) varies by source. The ABT and ExecutedToday both confirm the Mint as the location; the specific "flagstaff" detail is in the popular narrative and not directly contradicted. Low-confidence but not a hard error — consider softening to "at the Mint" without specifying the flagstaff if seeking maximum precision.

### SHOULD-FIX #3 — Farragut described as "sixty years old"

**Draft:** "a sixty-year-old Tennessee-born sailor."
**Verification:** Born July 5, 1801. In April 1862 he was 60 years old (turning 61 in July). ✅ Strictly accurate for the battle date. No fix needed — noted only to confirm.

### SHOULD-FIX #4 — "Meanwhile in Europe" section: "British and France had been weighing whether to recognize the Confederacy"

**Draft:** "Britain and France had been weighing whether to recognize the Confederacy as a real nation — a step that could have brought money, trade, even intervention."
**Verification:** ✅ Confirmed broadly by WP-BATTLE citing Nevins and all sources on the diplomatic impact. The Nevins quote is confirmed as cited. However, the draft says "the historian Allan Nevins judged" — the pack flags this as a citation from WP-BATTLE citing Nevins, not a confirmed primary Nevins text. The attribution chain (Wikipedia → Nevins) is appropriate for a narrative; the quote is within the normal secondary-citation tolerance.
