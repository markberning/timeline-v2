# STORYTELLING CRITIC · Siege of Savannah draft (rev-savannah)

*Critic pass 2026-06-12, against `rev-savannah-draft.md`. Fact pack: `rev-savannah-factpack.md`.
Bar: `rev-saratoga-final.md`. Storytelling lane only; factual accuracy belongs to the fact critic.
No edits made to the draft.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This draft arrives in good shape for its page's job. The hurricane-clock is planted in section 1
and holds as the through-line; the 24-hour stall reads as the masterstroke it was (competence, not
trickery); the diversity of the allied army is woven as texture, not as a lecture; and the Chasseurs
safe-framing is correctly maintained throughout. The prose is clean: no em-dashes, no meta-narrator,
no drama-bold. The section 3 close — the Haitian legacy, Pulaski's unsettled bones, and the
Jasper-legend deconstruction — lands with the right register, and the "gap between the documented
reserve and the honored legend" sentence at the monument is the best line in the piece.

What keeps it from a clean PASS: Maitland, the man who actually won this battle, is vivid in the
MEANWHILE box but nearly absent from the main prose where his feat is announced. The assault hour
at Spring Hill runs accurately but is paced as a report — the human beats (Jasper, Pulaski) are
given in the right order but neither one earns its weight because neither man has been established
before the hour they appear. And section 3's opening, which needs to carry the bruised-alliance
consequence and the McPherson braid (the Caribbean theater, the alliance politics, the southern
strategy's logic), instead opens on a straight sequence of outcomes and defers the international
weight to the Haitian beat much later. The bones are there; the draft needs to put faces on its
two human beats and give section 3 a better entry.

## BLOCKER

### B1. Maitland is the masterstroke but the main prose never makes him a presence — the feat is named, not landed

The brief asks for the 24-hour stall as a heist-in-reverse (Maitland wading the creeks while the
negotiators talk). The MEANWHILE box in section 2 does this superbly:

> "Maitland carried a fever through every mile of it and would be dead inside six weeks because
> of it. He arrived in time, which is the whole of it: without those 800 men in the Spring Hill
> sector, the garrison that broke the assault on October 9 would not have been there to break it."

But in the main prose of section 2, where the stall is told, the operative passage is:

> "Lieutenant Colonel John Maitland (British) had been at Beaufort, on Port Royal Island up the
> coast, with about 800 men of the 71st Highlanders and other troops. Sick with fever himself,
> he brought them out of Port Royal and threaded them through the half-charted tidal creeks of
> the inside passage, behind Daufuskie Island, by Wall's Cut and Skull Creek, with local fishermen
> as guides, dodging the French ships in the sounds and Lincoln's army on land."

The geography is vivid and correctly follows the pack. The problem: Maitland is not yet a person
when this passage arrives. We have learned nothing about him before he appears in section 2.
The dossier bio is tight and correct ("beaten Lincoln at Stono Ferry in June"), but dossier
material only lands for the reader who drills in; the commander card is not the prose. The result
is that the heist-in-reverse is told as logistics, not as a character's feat. Compare the Saratoga
final, where Fraser's death has no weight in the draft BECAUSE Fraser wasn't established in
section 1 (Saratoga critic B2) — that is exactly the defect here, for the man who is the whole
reason the British won.

The section 1 closing passage gestures at what should have been Maitland's setup:

> "It should have been enough. It was not, and the reason was a single day."

"A single day" is the right hook. But the draft moves immediately to section 2 without naming
the person who would make the single day count. A one-sentence bridge in section 1 — not a
bio-dump, just a name and a fact that makes him a presence before he acts — would give section 2's
heist its human driver.

**Fix:** At the section 1 close, after "the reason was a single day," add a sentence that
plants Maitland's name and his one prior credential: something like "At Beaufort, 40 miles up
the coast, a Scottish colonel named John Maitland had 800 men, a fever, and a knowledge of
the inside water passage that the French and the Americans had both failed to watch." That
sentence costs almost nothing and pays off the entire heist passage in section 2.

### B2. Pulaski and Jasper are neither established before the assault hour — the human beats die on the page they first appear

The brief asks for Pulaski and Jasper as human beats. The draft gives each man one passage in
section 3, and both passages are accurate and appropriately hedged. But this is the defect:
both men appear for the first and last time in the assault sequence, in the middle of a
tactical account, without any prior setup that makes the reader care.

Jasper's setup consists of this clause:

> "Sergeant William Jasper (American), famous since the defense of Fort Sullivan three years
> earlier..."

One clause of reputation is not a person. The reader who doesn't already know the Fort Sullivan
story — and this draft is for a reader with zero prior knowledge — has no reason to feel the
loss. The section 3 legendary-vs.-record close is the best Jasper writing on the page, but it
arrives after the fact of his death, when the reader needed something to care about before the
assault paragraph.

Pulaski is worse. His commander card is in the dossier, and it is the right material:

> "A Polish nobleman who had fought Russia in the Bar Confederation uprising... Benjamin Franklin
> sent him to Washington as 'an officer famous throughout Europe for his bravery.' At Brandywine
> his charge is credited with covering the army's retreat..."

None of that is in the sections. In section 3 the prose gives:

> "Into this rode Casimir Pulaski (American). He had been waiting with about 200 horse to charge
> through the breach the infantry was supposed to open..."

"Into this rode" is good cinematic prose. But we don't know who he is when he rides in. He is
not established at the army muster in section 1, where the draft describes Pulaski's legion as
"about 200 cavalry and infantry drawn from Poles, Frenchmen, Germans, Irishmen, and Americans"
and never names him in that sentence. The one naming in section 1 is in the dossier-level
aside — "the legion of Casimir Pulaski (American), the Polish general Congress had made
'Commander of the Horse'" — but it's a parenthetical in a list. Neither his Franklin credential
nor the Brandywine resonance (the man who covered the army's escape is now waiting for a breach
that will never come) appears in the prose.

**Fix:** Two edits, not a rewrite:

1. In section 1, at the army muster, name Pulaski in the prose (he is already named, but
expand by one clause): "...and the legion of Casimir Pulaski, the Polish nobleman Benjamin
Franklin had sent to Washington as 'an officer famous throughout Europe for his bravery,'
about 200 cavalry and infantry drawn from Poles, Frenchmen, Germans, Irishmen, and Americans."
That plants the Franklin credential so the Brandywine resonance can follow in the assault passage.

2. In section 3, at the Pulaski assault sentence, add a half-sentence of resonance: after
"He had been waiting with about 200 horse to charge through the breach the infantry was supposed
to open" add "— the same man whose charge at Brandywine had covered the army's retreat, now
waiting for a gap that never came." Then proceed as written. This costs one sentence and pays
off both the section 1 plant and the human beat the brief requires.

For Jasper: In section 3, at the assault passage, replace "famous since the defense of Fort
Sullivan three years earlier" with a brief clause that tells us WHY he was famous: "Sergeant
William Jasper, the man who had remounted the flag at Fort Sullivan in 1776 under British
fire..." That one gesture, replacing reputation with deed, is enough for the reader to feel
the loss in the legend-vs.-record close.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. Section 3's entry does not carry the McPherson braid — the alliance consequence arrives late and thin

The brief asks for the McPherson braid woven through the battle story: the Caribbean war, the
southern strategy, the alliance politics in the battle story itself. Section 1 does this well
for the Caribbean-clock strand. Section 3 is where the alliance consequence (the bruised
relationship after the second failed joint operation) should land, and it does, but it arrives
as one sentence of summary:

> "The second straight joint operation, after the wreck at Newport the year before, had ended
> in failure and recrimination, and Franco-American relations hit their wartime low. The bruised
> alliance held, but barely, and that mattered..."

"That mattered" is the kind of sentence that needs to be followed by WHY it mattered for the
war's whole arc. The section 1 setup (France entered the war, the Caribbean war competed for
ships and soldiers) and the section 3 close (the Haitian legacy, veterans of the Chasseurs in
the Haitian Revolution) are both present, but the middle of the braid — the strategic consequence
of two failed joint operations on the alliance that would eventually produce Yorktown — is not
woven in. The reader finishes section 3 knowing the alliance "held, but barely," but not what
that barely-holding unlocked.

The Saratoga critic (S3) flagged the same structural issue there: simultaneity strands woven
into the main prose carry more weight than material sealed in a MEANWHILE. Here the fix is
similar: one sentence in the aftermath paragraph that connects the bruised alliance forward
to its consequence. The alliance having held barely (1779) is what made d'Estaing's eventual
return (the Chesapeake, 1781) possible, and that chain is the section's genuine McPherson braid.

**Fix:** After "The bruised alliance held, but barely, and that mattered," add one clause that
names the stakes: "because the French fleet the alliance would need to seal the trap at Yorktown
was the same fleet that had just failed twice in a row." This names the forward consequence
without requiring the reader to have read any other page, and it gives "that mattered" the
weight the sentence is promising.

### S2. The assault hour pacing is accurate but runs as a report — the sequence of events needs one more beat of granularity before the retreat

Section 3's assault passage is correctly ordered and appropriately hedged. But it reads as
tactical summary rather than a scene. The Saratoga final's equivalent (the Bemis Heights
passage) earns its pace through physical detail: "grapeshot and rifle fire poured out of the
works into the white-coated French columns, and the dead and wounded of the first wave filled
the ditch." The Savannah draft has this too, but the pace accelerates too fast from the
ditch-filled-before-the-second-wave to d'Estaing's retreat, skipping the moment when the attack
was most nearly successful — Stedingk's column at the top of the entrenchment, flag up, before
the cross-fire shot them off.

The Stedingk beat is in the prose:

> "The Swedish count Curt von Stedingk (French) got an American flag onto the entrenchment for
> a moment before the cross-fire shot his men off it; he came back with about twenty men, every
> one of them wounded, and remembered the retreat... as the bitterest moment of his life."

This is good material, but it arrives inside a run of five separate actions in one paragraph
(Laurens' column, the color-bearers dying, Jasper, Stedingk, d'Estaing hit twice) and none
of them breathes. The "bitterest moment of his life" is the emotional anchor the assault needs,
and it's buried in the middle of the sequence. The assault paragraph needs a single breath
after the Stedingk beat — even a short sentence break — so the reader can register that the
nearest thing to success in the whole assault happened there, before the retreat.

**Fix:** After "he came back with about twenty men, every one of them wounded," add: "For a
moment it was the highest the allies got." Then proceed to d'Estaing shot twice and the retreat.
One sentence, no new facts, and the assault sequence has a hinge the pace can turn on before
the retreat.

### S3. The deadline-pressure thread established in section 1 is not picked up again in section 2 — it disappears after "the clock" and never drives the siege decisions explicitly

Section 1 plants the clock beautifully:

> "Every decision in this battle, the early summons, the impatience with a proper siege, the
> final gamble on a frontal assault, runs back to that clock."

This is the draft's best structural sentence. But section 2 never explicitly returns to it.
The clock is visible implicitly in the premature summons and in the decision to assault on
October 9, but the FEELING of deadline pressure — the thing that makes the siege a race, not
just a military operation — is absent from the middle of section 2. The pack is clear that
naval officers pressed d'Estaing daily about the exposed fleet, sick crews, and the season,
and that the decision to assault rather than lift was driven by the clock. The prose tells us
this ("his naval officers pressed him daily about the exposed fleet, the sick crews, and the
season") but it arrives in a subordinate clause attached to the decision paragraph, not as
the pressure that has been building since section 1.

The Saratoga final handles its own structural through-line (the growing/shrinking armies) by
returning to it explicitly at the section 2 opener. Savannah's clock through-line gets one
great plant in section 1 and then retreats to a dependent clause.

**Fix:** In section 2, at the decision-to-assault paragraph, bring the clock out of the
subordinate clause into the opening. Replace:

> "By October 8 d'Estaing had run out of patience and clock together."

with something like:

> "By October 8 the fleet had been on an exposed coast for five weeks, the crews were sicker,
> and the clock that had been running since section 1 was now running out of weather and ships.
> D'Estaing's engineers told him a proper siege needed perhaps ten more days; his naval officers
> gave him none."

(The exact wording is for the reviser, not this critic. The instruction is: make the clock
explicit and active at the assault decision, not buried in a subordinate clause.)

## TRIM

### T1. Section 2 — the "popular version makes the truce a simple blunder" paragraph can be halved

> "The popular version makes the truce a simple blunder by d'Estaing, and it was a blunder, but
> the failure should be spread honestly. The allies also failed to close a water route they knew
> about, and d'Estaing had summoned the town before he was ready to take it. The twenty-four
> hours did not lose the battle by themselves. They were the hinge on which a thin garrison
> became a held one."

This is correct and necessary framing. But it arrives immediately after the heist passage that
already made the point by showing it. The final two sentences — "The twenty-four hours did not
lose the battle by themselves. They were the hinge on which a thin garrison became a held one."
— are doing the work the whole paragraph already did. The first two sentences can be collapsed
into one: "That reading makes it d'Estaing's blunder alone, which undersells the allied failure
to guard a water route they knew about, and the fact that he had summoned the town before he was
ready to take it." Then close on the hinge sentence. Saves ~40 words, tightens the section.

### T2. Section 3 — the Pulaski bones/burial narrative can shed the 2019 study's methodology

> "a Georgia Southern-led team reported that the skeleton was consistent with Pulaski's known
> injuries and that its mitochondrial DNA matched a grandniece's, concluding the Monterey Square
> remains are most likely Pulaski's"

The methodology (mtDNA vs. nuclear DNA, the population prevalence figure) is in the fact pack
for the fact critic's benefit, not for the reader. The reader page needs: (a) the conclusion
reached, (b) the basis stated at the right level of abstraction, and (c) the scholarly dispute
flagged. "A 2019 Georgia Southern-led study found the skeleton consistent with Pulaski's injuries
and a DNA match to a grandniece — concluding the Monterey Square remains are most likely his"
is the reader-facing version. The distinction between mtDNA and nuclear DNA is a methodology
note that the fact critic should assess for inclusion; this critic recommends dropping it from
the reader prose.

## NIT

### N1. "It should have been enough. It was not, and the reason was a single day."

"The reason was a single day" is doing the right structural work as a section bridge, but "a
single day" will not read clearly to a first-time reader as a reference to the 24-hour truce.
Consider: "the reason was a day's truce" or "the reason was twenty-four hours a garrison asked
for and got." The ambiguity is small but it's the sentence the reader carries into section 2.

### N2. "the order said they 'were only to be used to dig trenches'"

This is the right quote to use, but the attribution in the prose ("the French order of battle
said they 'were only to be used to dig trenches'") requires the reader to trust the narrative
about an internal document they haven't seen. The pack attributes this to "the French order of
battle," which is precise enough for the fact critic's purposes. For the reader prose, either
attribute it as "the unit's official assignment" (dropping the internal-document precision) or
carry the attribution exactly as the pack gives it. As written it is slightly in the middle.
The fact critic should adjudicate; this is a nit, not a problem.

### N3. "the contradiction they had marched into, free and enslaved men of color fighting for a
liberty their own home denied them"

This is one of the best sentences in section 3. Its only weakness: "a liberty their own home
denied them" conflates two different situations — the free men of color, whose liberty was real
but hedged; and the enslaved men, promised freedom for service, who were not yet free. The pack
flags this distinction (gens de couleur libres vs. enslaved volunteers promised freedom). The
sentence as written smooths it. The revision is small: "fighting for a liberty their own home
would have to fight for a decade later" is accurate for all the men and more precisely sets up
the Haitian Revolution forward. The reviser can weigh whether the precision is worth the
rewrite.

### N4. "the most international hour of the war kept a long, strange shadow"

The MEANWHILE at section 3's close ends on this sentence, which is doing good work as a
section close. "Long, strange shadow" is the right register. Minor: "kept" is a slightly odd
verb choice (shadows usually "cast" or "throw"). "The most international hour of the war cast
a long, strange shadow" would remove the mild awkwardness without changing the effect.

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES (for the record)

- **Voice ship-blockers: clean.** No em-dashes in original prose; no drama-bold; no "as we'll
  see," no "our story," no meta-narrator addressing the reader. Parentheses for definitions
  throughout. Miles-first with km in parens.
- **The clock through-line is planted correctly.** Section 1's "every decision... runs back to
  that clock" is the right structural sentence, and the draft earns it by establishing the
  hurricane season, the scurvy, the short supplies, and the orders home before it arrives.
- **The 24-hour stall as competence, not trickery, is honored.** "This was the masterstroke of
  the siege, and it belongs to the British as competence, not trickery" — the framing directive
  in the brief is delivered explicitly and correctly.
- **The international cast is texture, not lecture.** The army muster in section 1 names the
  nationalities without stopping to discuss them; the column-leaders list (Swedish count, Irish
  colonel, Polish general) lands as one clause, not a paragraph.
- **All safe-framing requirements are met.** Chasseurs rear-guard stand as tradition/reserve
  (not documented fact), Christophe as "tradition holds he was here as a boy," the Haitian
  Monument placard quote carried as the monument's tribute not history's verdict, Jasper's
  deathbed speech blacklisted correctly.
- **The bruised-alliance close is earned.** The recrimination sentence, the Clinton quote, the
  southern-war catalogue (Camden/Kings Mountain/Cowpens/Guilford), and the Haitian legacy
  together give section 3 the weight the legacy brief requires. The section's final sentence
  about the gap between documented reserve and honored legend is the draft's best closer and
  should not be changed.
- **Apocrypha blacklist untouched.** Jasper deathbed speech: absent. Pulaski last words: absent.
  Christophe at Savannah as plain fact: absent. "Militia only" at Spring Hill: absent.
  Campbell "first stripe" boast: absent. Precise bloodiest-battle ranking: absent.
  d'Estaing buried at Savannah: absent. Intersex as settled: correctly a hypothesis on contested
  remains.
- **No em-dashes in original prose** (the Prevost "evening gun" reply uses none; the Stedingk
  paraphrase uses none). Confirmed clean.

moment of their feat or death, with no prior setup in the sections**. The heist-in-reverse
(B1) dies as logistics because Maitland is not a person before he acts; the assault's human
