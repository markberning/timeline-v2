**Gate:** NEWCOMER / CLARITY CRITIC
**Reviewer posture:** Sharp 15-year-old, zero prior knowledge of the Civil War era, American government, or 19th-century culture.

## Section-by-Section Findings

**"Confederate prisoners of war"** / "the Union had largely stopped exchanging prisoners" — the prisoner-exchange system is assumed to be known. ### The Vice President Who Was Never Touched

**"Andrew Johnson — the man whose accession would shape the entire postwar era"** — "accession" is a mildly formal word (= taking power/becoming president). A 15-year-old may not know it. NEEDS-GLOSS (minor) — but comprehensible from context.

**Booth's calling card to Johnson** — the implication that this "deepened the suspicions that swirled around Johnson himself" is left dangling. Suspicions of *what*? That Johnson was in on the plot? A reader who doesn't know the historical backdrop will find this confusing rather than intriguing. SHOULD-FIX: one clause stating what people suspected ("some in Washington briefly wondered whether Johnson himself had been part of the conspiracy").

## MUST-FIX (Comprehension Breakers)

**1. "The Supreme Court had cast doubt on military trials of civilians" (The Gallows section)**
A zero-knowledge reader has no way to follow this. The text says John Surratt "was tried in a civilian court" because the Supreme Court "had cast doubt on military trials of civilians" — but there's no explanation of what happened (a ruling in another case, not even named) or what it meant legally. The contrast between Mary Surratt (military commission, hanged) and John Surratt (civilian jury, walked free) is the sharpest irony in the section, and the legal hinge is opaque. Fix: name the Milligan decision briefly, or add a one-sentence plain explanation: "In the meantime the Supreme Court had ruled, in a separate case, that civilians couldn't be tried by military courts while the regular courts were open — a ruling that hadn't existed when Mary Surratt was hanged."

**2. Mary Todd Lincoln — never given a who-is-she tag**
She's mentioned in "The Last Speech" (Lamon told "a small group, including his wife Mary Todd Lincoln") and appears in the theater box. A zero-knowledge reader has no reason to know who Mary Todd Lincoln is. She needs a single-word gloss on first mention: "his wife, Mary Todd Lincoln." This is a first-mention comprehension gap; it's a figure-of-importance (first lady, in the box when her husband was shot) being treated as a known quantity.

**3. The Johnson-suspicion sentence is genuinely LOST**
"for a while it deepened the suspicions that swirled around Johnson himself" — suspicions of *what*? A zero-knowledge reader will be confused. The sentence is evocative but context-free. Fix: "for a while it deepened suspicions that Johnson himself might have been part of the plot — a theory that was investigated and eventually dismissed."

## Summary

The draft is strong and mostly self-defining. The voice is accessible. The fact-hedging on disputed claims is exemplary and actually *helps* a newcomer understand which things are solid and which are legend. Three items are genuine comprehension breakers that will leave a zero-knowledge reader confused or misinformed:

1. The Supreme Court / civilian-trial sentence in The Gallows is a legal non-sequitur without a line of context.
2. Mary Todd Lincoln is never introduced as Lincoln's wife.
3. The Johnson-suspicion sentence raises a question and immediately drops it with no resolution, leaving the reader hanging.

Fix those three and the piece clears the clarity bar.
