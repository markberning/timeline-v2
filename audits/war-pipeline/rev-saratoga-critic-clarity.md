# NEWCOMER-CLARITY CRITIC · The Battles of Saratoga draft

*Critic pass on `audits/war-pipeline/rev-saratoga-draft.md`, 2026-06-12. Reader model:
zero prior knowledge — does not know the Revolution's geography, cannot place Albany or
the Hudson on a mental map, has not read any other article in this vertical, cannot
picture a redoubt or a picket, does not know what "Continentals" are as distinct from
militia.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is a strong draft and clearly the flagship piece — the three-week siege structure,
the Sept 19 / Oct 7 gap, the convention narrative, and the Arnold-at-evidence-level
handling are all excellent. The strategic stake (cut New England off) IS explained early
and plainly. Geography, scale, and the key mechanism (Burgoyne could not force the defile,
so he had to swing west, which is why both battles happened in the same woods) are all
clear. The main clarity problems are: one BLOCKER undefined term woven through Sections 1
and 2 ("pickets"), and a cluster of lesser undefined terms — "Brunswickers/Brunswickers,"
"Continentals," "grenadiers," "light infantry," "bateaux," and "redoubt" — some of which
are glossed correctly but arrive one or two paragraphs late or miss key surfaces. No
structural BLOCKERS on geography or the strategic map.

---

## BLOCKER

**B1. "Pickets" / "picket" never defined anywhere.**
First use is late in Section 1: "Morgan's men sniped the pickets nearly every night."
The word appears again in Section 2: "militia and Morgan's men sniped the pickets nearly
every night" (the poisoned-month paragraph). A newcomer reads "pickets" as tent stakes or
fence posts, not as sentries / forward-guard posts. The word sits in a pivotal sentence
that explains WHY the wait was so costly to Burgoyne — an undefined key term drains that
paragraph of its weight.
- FIX (S1, at the first use): change "sniped the pickets nearly every night" to "sniped
  the pickets (the sentries and small guard posts out ahead of the main camp) nearly every
  night." The S2 reuse then inherits the gloss by proximity.

## SHOULD-FIX

**S1. "Continentals" never defined; "militia" defined only after Continentals have been
assumed.**
Section 1 uses "militia" correctly with implicit contrast to the regulars, but the draft
never explains the two-tier army distinction that runs through the entire battle narrative
("Morgan's ~500 riflemen... Morgan's rifle corps," "Continentals dug in on Bemis Heights"
in the dossier sides note, "Continentals" in the Section 2 counterpunch). The dossier
sides note reads: "Continentals dug in on Bemis Heights behind Kościuszko's entrenchments,
Morgan's ~500 riflemen owning the woods, and militia pouring in daily." That is the
newcomer's FIRST encounter with "Continentals" and it is cold.
- FIX (dossier sides note, first sentence): "Continentals (the full-time paid soldiers of
  the Continental Army) dug in on Bemis Heights..."  and/or (first prose use in S1, the
  Bennington paragraph): define militia at that natural moment — "John Stark's New
  Hampshire militia (part-time citizen soldiers, not the Continental regulars)" — which
  automatically implies the two-tier structure so "Continentals" lands cleanly later.

**S2. "Brunswickers" / "Brunswickers" defined in the dossier commander bio for Riedesel
("roughly 4,000 Brunswick troops Britain hired") but never defined in the PROSE sections
where newcomers need it.**
Riedesel's arrival in Section 2 prose — "Riedesel, on his own initiative, left 500 men
on the baggage and threw his Brunswickers and two guns against the American right" — uses
"Brunswickers" as a bare noun. The dossier is not prose-read by most phone readers before
they tap into a section. "Hessians" appears nowhere (the more common generic term), but
"Brunswickers" is equally opaque without the "hired German soldiers" frame.
- FIX (S2, first prose appearance — the Riedesel flank-attack sentence): "threw his
  Brunswickers (the hired German soldiers from Brunswick who made up roughly half of
  Burgoyne's army) and two guns."  The Section 1 prose already has "half hired Brunswickers
  under Baron Riedesel" in the dossier sides note, so a light back-ref "(see above)" is
  also acceptable if the reviser prefers to gloss there instead and trust the reader to
  carry it into S2.

**S3. "Grenadiers" never defined in any prose surface.**
Section 2 (Oct 7 counterpunch): "Poor's brigade smashed the grenadiers on the British
left, where Major Acland fell shot through both legs." Grenadiers are only explained in
context of their role on the Oct 7 battle line — there is nothing to tell the newcomer
these were the army's biggest, most experienced heavy infantry (historically the men who
threw grenades, retained as elite heavy shock troops even when grenades went out of
fashion). The word appears only this once in prose, making a one-word gloss easy.
- FIX: "Poor's brigade smashed the grenadiers (the British army's elite heavy infantry,
  the biggest and most experienced men in the line) on the British left..."

**S4. "Light infantry" never defined.**
Used in Section 2 ("Morgan's ~500 riflemen owning the woods" positions light infantry as
distinct, and in the Oct 7 counterpunch: "Morgan swung wide and crushed the light infantry
on the right"). The dossier sides note for the British army mentions nothing about their
organization. "Light infantry" is military jargon for fast, skirmishing troops who operate
in open order, distinct from the line infantry — but the newcomer gets no clue.
- FIX (S2, at the Oct 7 counterpunch or first S2 use — whichever comes first, which is
  the dossier note "Morgan's ~500 riflemen owning the woods, and militia pouring in daily"):
  when light infantry first appears in S2 prose, gloss: "light infantry (fast-moving
  skirmishing troops who fought in open formation, not in the shoulder-to-shoulder line)."

**S5. "Redoubt" is defined in Section 1 as "defile (a narrow pass)" — but "defile" is
what is glossed, not "redoubt." The word "redoubt" first appears in the dossier sides note
("Continentals dug in on Bemis Heights behind Kościuszko's entrenchments") and then
repeatedly in S2 (the Balcarres and Breymann redoubts). It is never glossed.**
The storming of the Breymann redoubt is the climax of the entire article; "Arnold led
the storming through the gap between the works" partially compensates, but "the works" is
itself jargon.
- FIX (S2, at first redoubt appearance — "the Balcarres redoubt, the fortified line at
  Freeman's Farm"): add "(a redoubt: a small earthwork fortification, usually a rectangle
  of dug earth walls with firing platforms on top)." Or gloss one occurrence in the dossier
  sides note: "Balcarres and Breymann redoubts (small earthwork forts) that anchored the
  line."

**S6. "Reconnaissance-in-force" is glossed ("a heavy armed probe") but "bateaux" or
"bridge of boats" is not the clarity problem — it's that the NEW sentence carrying the
Oct 7 reconnaissance logic says "probe the American left and cut forage in the wheat" and
a newcomer cannot picture WHY you cut forage. The sentence implies foraging is optional;
the narrative elsewhere makes clear the army is starving. The causal link is missing.**
Draft: "to test the American left and cut forage in the wheat."
- FIX: add a short clause — "to test the American left and cut forage in the wheat
  (the army was down to eight days' rations and needed the harvest in the fields)." The
  fact pack's nutrition-crisis detail belongs here to motivate the mission.

**S7. Philip Schuyler's replacement by Gates — a backstory whose omission creates a logic
gap in Section 2's feud passage.**
The draft says Arnold's aides were "former aides of the general Gates had replaced, which
did not help." "The general Gates had replaced" is a floating description; the newcomer
who read Section 1 was told Gates was "the new American commander" but was never told he
replaced anyone, let alone who. Philip Schuyler's name does not appear in the draft.
The Schuyler-vs.-Gates faction dynamics are what make the feud's bitterness legible
(Arnold was a Schuyler ally; his aides were Schuyler's former aides; Gates had just
shunted Schuyler aside, which put Arnold and Gates on opposite sides of a political fight
before the battle even started).
- FIX: at the first Gates introduction in Section 1 (the McCrea beat): identify him as
  "Horatio Gates, who had just replaced General Philip Schuyler as Northern commander on
  August 19, three weeks before Freeman's Farm." Then the Section 2 feud passage's "former
  aides of the general Gates had replaced" has a proper antecedent — or revise to "former
  aides of the Schuyler he had replaced."

## NIT

**N1. The strategic-map sentence is slightly back-loaded.**
Section 1 opens with Burgoyne's plan to "seize the Hudson River corridor and cut New
England, the furnace of the rebellion, off from the other colonies." This is exactly right
and exactly what the newcomer needs. The only polish: "off from the other colonies" is a
subordinate clause. The sentence could punch harder as: "...seize the Hudson River
corridor — the spine of the eastern colonies — and cut New England off from the rest,
starving the rebellion of its furnace." But the current version is fully clear; polish
only.

**N2. "Barry St. Leger" introduced with no side tag.**
First Section 1 use: "A second, smaller force under Barry St. Leger was to swing in from
the west." He is clearly British by context (part of Burgoyne's plan) but adding
"(British)" costs nothing: "a second British force under Colonel Barry St. Leger."

**N3. "Hamilton" in Section 2 is a bare surname — which Hamilton?**
"four regiments under Brigadier General Hamilton in the center with Burgoyne himself."
There were multiple Hamiltons in the war, including the famous one. This is Brigadier
General James Hamilton of the 21st Foot — "(British, commanding the four center
regiments)" would defuse the ambiguity.

**N4. "Major Dearborn" enters Section 2 with no role or side tag.**
First use in Section 2: "Gates grudgingly let Morgan's riflemen and Major Dearborn's light
infantry go forward." Dearborn appears again at the Oct 7 counterpunch: "Dearborn hit the
breaking line head-on." The dossier does not carry a Dearborn commander card. First use
needs "(Henry Dearborn, American, commanding a light infantry battalion)."

**N5. "Major Acland" enters the Oct 7 passage with no side tag or role.**
"Poor's brigade smashed the grenadiers on the British left, where Major Acland fell shot
through both legs and was captured." Acland has a MEANWHILE devoted to his wife Lady
Harriet — but the newcomer who reads the MEANWHILE without full context meets a bare "Major
Acland... in American hands." The prose sentence needs "(Major John Acland, commanding the
British grenadier battalion)." The MEANWHILE then self-selects its antecedent.

**N6. "Poor's brigade" — Colonel Enoch Poor is never introduced at all.**
The Oct 7 attack names "Poor's brigade" as if the reader knows whose it is; Poor has no
commander card and no introduction. Either gloss "(Colonel Enoch Poor's Continental
brigade, attacking the British left)" or replace with "the Continental brigade on the
American right" to avoid a cold surname.

**N7. The Gates-to-Philadelphia pass on Arnold is slightly confusing.**
"Gates issued him a pass to leave for Philadelphia. Arnold stayed in camp, with no clear
command." The newcomer may not know what "issued a pass" means in an 18th-century army
context — it is an official dismissal notice. A one-phrase gloss: "Gates issued him a
formal pass to leave for Philadelphia — effectively sidelining him."

**N8. "The battle the two-armies spine chapter owns" in the IMAGE block (the Trumbull note)
will be read by the editor/reviser but not the phone reader — this is internal production
language, not a content surface. No fix needed.**

**N9. "Calash" (Section 3) defined only implicitly by "(a light carriage)".**
The inline definition IS present — "Frederika Riedesel rode the retreat in a calash (a
light carriage)" — this passes. POLISH: "a calash (a light open carriage)" is slightly
richer.

**N10. "Honors of war" never defined.**
Section 3: "the army marched out of camp with the honors of war." This is military
ceremonial vocabulary — it meant the garrison was allowed to march out under arms with
flags flying and drums beating, as opposed to surrendering like prisoners. The newcomer
has no frame for it.
- FIX: "the army marched out of camp with the honors of war (meaning the men marched out
  with their flags, drums beating and weapons shouldered, not as captives)."

**N11. "Convention Army" used twice in Section 3 without being explained.**
"The army was marched to Cambridge" and "Most of the privates who grounded arms at
Saratoga did not see home before the peace of 1783" — this passage is very clear. But the
term "Convention Army" (the name this force acquired in captivity) appears in the fact
ledger and would be confusing if ever leaked into prose. It does not appear in the prose
sections — no fix needed, flagged only for the reviser's attention.

**N12. "Paroled home" — newcomer may not know what parole meant for an officer.**
Section 3: "Burgoyne himself was paroled home in May 1778." Parole in this context meant
he was released on his word of honor not to serve against the Americans until formally
exchanged — it was NOT a free release. Given that the draft spends a paragraph on Congress
breaking the convention, a brief gloss here prevents the newcomer from thinking Burgoyne
simply walked free: "paroled home (released on his word of honor not to serve against
America until formally exchanged for an American prisoner of equal rank)."

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES CLEANLY (for the reviser's confidence)

- **Strategic map / Hudson-cut logic: PASS.** The plan to "seize the Hudson River corridor
  and cut New England... off from the other colonies" is stated in S1 paragraph 1. The
  defile geometry (road squeezed between bluff and river; Burgoyne could not force it) is
  explained plainly. The 25–30 miles north of Albany is in the dossier and the fact ledger.

- **The Sept 19 / Oct 7 gap (check 5): PASS.** The wait between battles is a full
  narrative beat — Clinton's note, the poisoned month, the shrinking rations, the feud.
  The eighteen-day gap is named in the dossier note and stats and clearly structured in
  the prose.

- **Unit scale given context: PASS.** ~8,000 from Canada; ~8,500 American effectives Sept
  19 growing to 15,000+; ~5,000 British effectives by Oct 7; nearly 6,000 surrendered.
  Relative sizes repeatedly anchored.

- **Miles and dates: PASS.** 23 miles to Fort Edward, 25–30 miles north of Albany, 45 miles
  short of Albany for Clinton. Dates are unambiguous throughout.

- **Howe planning failure framing: PASS.** "It was not a betrayal. It was a planning
  failure" — exactly the right gloss for a newcomer who might assume sabotage.

- **The "why both battles were in the same woods" mechanism: PASS.** S1's closing paragraph
  makes the ground geometry and its tactical consequence completely explicit.

- **McCrea framing: PASS.** Handled at evidence level with appropriate uncertainty.

- **Arnold's ride at evidence level: PASS.** Three competing accounts named and evaluated;
  "what Arnold did on the field is not [in dispute]" is a clear structural separation.

- **The convention deal and Congress breaking it: PASS.** Told plainly and critically.

- **Fraser burial scene: PASS.** The finest prose beat in the draft; the Frederika journal
  excerpts are integrated cleanly, she is introduced with full context, and the round-shot
  detail lands.

- **Dossier commander bios standing alone: MOSTLY PASS.** All seven bios give role + side.
  One improvement needed: Kościuszko's bio says "a Polish volunteer who had sailed for
  America in 1776, laid out the line on Bemis Heights" — but his rank (colonel of
  engineers) and the fact that he was MILITARY TRAINED (Warsaw and Paris) are in the bio
  block but not in the prose. This is fine; the dossier bio covers it for readers who open
  the commander tab.

- **Glossing for "defile": PASS.** "a defile (a narrow pass)" appears at the natural
  location.

- **The silver-bullet / Clinton confusion: PASS.** The same-surname problem is explicitly
  defused ("New York's American governor, George Clinton... the British Clinton").

- **Blacklist compliance carried into the clarity assessment:** No em-dashes in prose;
  apocrypha correctly absent; tradition-badges in place. No clarity cost from the
  blacklist.
