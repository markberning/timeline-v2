## Checklist: Must-Cover Elements for a Strong Selective Telling

Rating key: **COVERED** = draft handles it well | **THIN** = present but underdeveloped | **MISSING** = absent or effectively absent

### 1. New Orleans's strategic and economic value

**New Orleans as the Confederacy's largest city and greatest port** — COVERED
Draft: population, rank, cotton share, Mississippi valley outlet, manufactured-goods rank (2nd to Richmond). Strong and specific.

**New Orleans as the South's great slave market** — THIN
Draft has one sentence: "It was also the South's great slave market — the largest slave-trading center in a country that had gone to war to keep human beings as property." That's accurate, but it's the thinnest element in the entire opening. The pack doesn't flesh this out in §3, and the draft doesn't either. The slave-market dimension isn't just biographical color — it's why New Orleans was the symbolic heart of the Confederate economy and one of the strongest arguments for the Anaconda's strategic logic. A paragraph on scale (135,000+ people sold there over the trade's life; ~50+ active markets in the city; the $110M cotton trade in 1860 flowing through the same port) would earn it. The connection — same city that shipped cotton also sold the people who grew it — is currently gestured at but not made.

Web confirmation: Smithsonian, ABT, and HNOC sources confirm New Orleans as the single largest domestic slave market; ~$110M cotton through the port in 1860. The draft's one-liner is accurate but slim for a gate criterion this important.

**The Anaconda Plan** — COVERED
Properly named, attributed to Winfield Scott, purpose explained.

### 2. Why the city's defenses were stripped

**Confederate misread of the threat direction (from south vs. north)** — COVERED
The upstream-focus misread is central to the second subsection. Henry/Donelson, Shiloh, Hollins's fleet all named.

**Lovell's thin garrison** — COVERED
~3,000 militia, ~1,200 armed, ~1,100 in the forts.

**Leeds Foundry and the South's industrial loss** — MISSING
The fact pack (§7) specifically notes that with New Orleans fell the Leeds Foundry — "the South's second-most important ironworks after the Tredegar in Richmond." The draft's closing section names the city's "second-greatest manufacturing base" in general terms but never names the Leeds Foundry. For a selective telling, the Tredegar comparison is exactly the kind of vivid anchor that earns the significance claim. SHOULD-CONSIDER (not MUST-ADD — the general claim is present; the specific name makes it land harder).

### 3. The forts themselves — construction, guns, placement

**Fort Jackson and Fort St. Philip — layout, guns, relative strength** — COVERED
Placement, gun counts, brick/earth construction all clear.

**Forts were designed for defense from above (sail era), not from below** — MISSING
This is a genuinely important irony the draft does not make: Fort Jackson was built 1822–1832 as Third System coastal defense, designed to slow ships rounding Plaquemines Bend — for the era of sail, when ships moved slowly. The forts faced upstream (from the design logic) and their armament was optimized for a slowing ship in the bend. Farragut's steam fleet was faster than the guns had been designed for. The fact that the Confederacy's inherited defenses were pointed the wrong way in more than one sense (wrong direction from south, wrong era of warfare) is a sharp structural point the draft misses. SHOULD-CONSIDER.

### 4. The chain, fire rafts, and River Defense Fleet

**Chain and hulks** — COVERED
Eight hulks, chain cable, function explained.

**Fire rafts** — COVERED
Present in the gauntlet description and the night battle.

**River Defense Fleet (cottonclad rams)** — COVERED
Six cottonclads named as a type; Governor Moore's ramming of Varuna is the dramatic centerpiece of the night battle. The draft correctly identifies them as "ordinary river steamers" with reinforced bows and cotton-bale armor.

**Command friction — River Defense Fleet vs. Confederate Navy** — MISSING
This is a genuine MUST-ADD omission. The draft uses the Winters quote about "self-destruction, lack of co-operation, cowardice of untrained officers" — but it never explains WHY there was no cooperation. The River Defense Fleet, under Captain John A. Stephenson, was not under Mitchell's naval command and explicitly refused to be. Stephenson told his captains they were "independent of the Navy" and that order "could not be punished" because of the peculiar legal relationship of the fleet to the army. This is the structural explanation for the Confederate naval collapse — it wasn't just chaos, it was three separate chains of command (Confederate Navy / Louisiana state navy / River Defense Fleet) that couldn't coordinate even during the battle. The Winters quote hangs in the air without it. The pack mentions Mitchell and Stephenson in the commanders list (§1) but doesn't develop the friction in §3 or §4. This belongs in "the gauntlet" or the night battle section.

Source: Emerging Civil War, "The Confederate Navy's Order of Battle at New Orleans"; River Defense Fleet Wikipedia.

### 5. CSS Louisiana, CSS Mississippi, CSS Manassas

**CSS Louisiana — ironclad, immobile, floating battery** — COVERED
Size, gun count, engine failure, moored-as-battery role, blown up at the end. All present.

**CSS Mississippi — unfinished** — COVERED
"Never operational," burned on fall of city.

**CSS Manassas — early ironclad ram** — COVERED
One gun, crew of 104, rammed Mississippi and Brooklyn, driven ashore, sank.

**Why the ironclads weren't ready — the construction failure story** — THIN
The draft calls them "the half-finished ones" and says "the iron ships weren't ready" without saying why. The pack (§3) doesn't dig into this either, but web research reveals a richer story: Louisiana and Mississippi were built in adjacent yards competing for the same scarce skilled labor pool; unseasoned timber made the gun deck knee-deep in water; the paddlewheel arrangement made Louisiana unsteerable even when her engines worked; Mitchell later documented that her gun ports were too small for proper traverse. The Confederate ironclad-building program's endemic failures (labor scarcity, inadequate industrial base, management failures) are exactly the kind of structural explanation this vertical is built for. SHOULD-CONSIDER — not necessary to the story at narrative length, but one sentence on "competing for the same pool of scarce skilled workers" sharpens the "the iron ships weren't ready" line considerably.

### 9. The night battle (April 24)

**Battle opening — forts firing, fire rafts, muzzle flash chaos** — COVERED
Scene-setting is strong. The pre-dawn timing, both forts firing, fire rafts, the near-blind conditions.

**USS Hartford aground and afire** — COVERED
Specific and dramatic. The flagship catching fire is the right human anchor for the night.

**USS Varuna sunk by Governor Moore** — COVERED
Only Union warship lost in passage. Named. Good.

**CSS Manassas's attack and fate** — COVERED
Rammed Mississippi and Brooklyn, driven ashore, sank. Correct.

**CSS Louisiana as floating battery** — COVERED
Her immobility restated in the battle context.

**Chalmette batteries — the second gauntlet between forts and city** — MISSING
After passing the forts, Farragut's fleet had to run the Chalmette-McGehee artillery line a few miles below New Orleans before reaching the city on April 25. The pack mentions Chalmette in the geography section (§9) but the draft omits it entirely from the narrative. Web research confirms the batteries were silenced relatively quickly (the big ships overwhelmed them), but their existence is part of the full picture of what "passing the defenses" meant. This is SHOULD-CONSIDER rather than MUST-ADD — the Chalmette line was militarily insignificant compared to the forts, and the selective telling doesn't need to enumerate every defensive tier. But one sentence in the morning-after section ("above the forts there was still the Chalmette artillery line, which the fleet silenced in short order on April 25 as it steamed for the city") would round out the campaign correctly.

**Three Union vessels turned back** — COVERED
Itasca, Pinola, Winona named in the fact ledger, implied in "three had been forced to turn back" in the narrative. Adequate for selective telling.

**Confederate casualties and vessels destroyed** — COVERED
12 Confederate vessels destroyed; Union casualty range ("a few dozen killed and a hundred-odd wounded") is correctly flagged as passage-only figures.

### 11. The fort garrison's mutiny

**Fort Jackson mutiny** — COVERED
~300 soldiers, spiked guns, walked out, night of April 27–28.

**Fort St. Philip did NOT mutiny** — COVERED
Explicitly noted. Important distinction and the draft catches it.

**Why the mutiny happened — isolation, no supplies, no hope** — COVERED (briefly)
"Cut off, with the city already lost behind them, their boats destroyed and their supplies gone." The draft gets the causal logic right.

**Duncan's background — a Pennsylvanian commanding Confederate forts** — MISSING (pack-blind-spot)
The fact pack (§6) carries a striking detail: Brigadier General Johnson K. Duncan was born in York County, Pennsylvania, a Northerner commanding Southern defenses — and died of malaria in Knoxville at age 36 shortly after the exchange. This irony (a Yankee engineer presiding over the fall of New Orleans) is the kind of biographical hook the vertical loves. The draft never names Duncan in the narrative sections at all (only the FACT LEDGER quotes him). SHOULD-CONSIDER — name him once in the surrender section: "General Duncan (South), a Pennsylvania-born engineer who had spent the war defending a city he'd been born far from..."

### 12. Butler's occupation and the "Woman Order"

**Butler's arrival and martial law** — COVERED
May 1, martial law, loyalty oaths, confiscated weapons.

**Woman Order (General Order No. 28)** — COVERED
Exact phrasing quoted, international uproar noted. Palmerston's condemnation is in the pack (§5) but not in the draft — SHOULD-CONSIDER adding it (one line; it concretizes "international uproar").

**Mumford's execution** — COVERED
Date, location (the Mint flagstaff), the deliberate symbolism. The Confederate response (Jefferson Davis called Butler deserving of execution) is in the pack but not the draft — SHOULD-CONSIDER.

**Butler's Black regiments — 3,122 soldiers, Louisiana Native Guards** — MISSING
The pack (§7) notes Butler raised three Black regiments totaling 3,122 soldiers during the occupation. This is a significant omission for this vertical's orientation ("why we went to war" / the occupation's deeper meaning). The Louisiana Native Guards — a pre-existing free Black unit in New Orleans that offered its services and was eventually mustered into Union service — is one of the most historically resonant facts about the occupation. Taking the city that was the South's largest slave market and then beginning to arm Black soldiers in it is exactly the kind of connective payoff the intro's slave-market paragraph sets up. MUST-ADD.

**Butler's food relief and infrastructure work** — not flagged
The pack (§7) mentions Order No. 25 (2,000 relief jobs), Order No. 55 (11,000 families on relief), and sewer improvements that reduced yellow fever deaths. These are contextually real but the selective telling correctly omits them. Not a gap.

### 13. Strategic impact — the Anaconda, the river, the Confederacy

**Anaconda tightening — river from Gulf to Vicksburg** — COVERED
Baton Rouge and Natchez named. Vicksburg as the remaining strongpoint named. The choking of Confederate commerce.

**Confederate ironclad program crippled** — THIN
The draft mentions CSS Mississippi burned and CSS Louisiana blown up, but the pack (§7) makes explicit that the loss dealt a "crippling blow" to the Confederate ironclad-building program as a whole. Both ships represented the Confederacy's best bet for a river navy that could contest Union control — gone in one night. SHOULD-CONSIDER — one sentence in the strategic summary: "With Louisiana and Mississippi gone, the Confederate dream of an ironclad river navy died here."

**European recognition** — COVERED
Britain and France named, Nevins quote on Confederate weakness on display. Clean.

**Mary Chesnut's line** — COVERED (correctly cautious)
Attributed as "the Confederate diarist," paraphrased per the pack's VERIFY flag on direct quote provenance. Good call.

**Lovell's court of inquiry and exoneration** — MISSING (pack-blind-spot)
The pack (§6) notes Lovell demanded a court of inquiry, which met in April 1863 and declared him innocent of incompetence — and that historian Charles Dufour argued Jefferson Davis and Naval Secretary Mallory bore primary responsibility. This is a richer accountability story than the draft touches: the man who evacuated the city was exonerated; the blame went to Richmond. SHOULD-CONSIDER for the strategic fallout section — it puts the systemic failure where it belongs.

## VERDICT SUMMARY

### MUST-ADD (2)

**M1 — Confederate command fragmentation (River Defense Fleet refusal to take Navy orders)**
The Winters quote about "self-destruction, lack of co-operation" needs its structural explanation: three separate commands, Stephenson's explicit refusal to accept Mitchell's authority, a legal no-man's-land that made the refusal unpunishable. Without it the Confederate collapse reads as individual cowardice/incompetence rather than institutional failure. This is the "why" behind the quote the draft is already using.

**M2 — Butler's Black regiments (Louisiana Native Guards, 3,122 soldiers)**
Taking the South's largest slave market and beginning to arm Black men in it is the sharpest possible closing of the loop the draft's intro opens. The slave-market mention in paragraph 1 sets up a payoff that never arrives. One paragraph in the Butler section connects the whole arc. The pack has the facts; they just weren't pulled into the narrative.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER (6)

**S1 — Slave-market scale (bulk up from one sentence)**
Add scale: ~135,000 people sold there over the trade's life, ~50+ active markets in the city, $110M in cotton (1860) through the same port. The connection (same city exported cotton AND sold the people who grew it) is the thesis behind the opening section; it deserves two sentences, not one.

**S2 — Leeds Foundry by name**
Pack has it; draft has the category ("second-greatest manufacturing base") but not the name. One line earns the Tredegar comparison that makes the industrial loss concrete.

**S3 — Forts designed for sail-era upstream defense, not steam-era downstream attack**
The inherited wrong-era, wrong-direction problem of the forts sharpens the Confederate misread story beyond just "they thought the threat was from the north."

**S4 — Chalmette batteries (one sentence)**
"Above the forts there was still the Chalmette artillery line, silenced quickly on April 25 as the fleet steamed to the city" — completes the picture of the campaign without padding.

**S5 — Duncan's Pennsylvania birth / biographical hook**
The Northerner commanding the Confederate forts, never named in the narrative sections, dead of malaria at 36 six months later. One sentence in the surrender subsection.

**S6 — Lovell exonerated; blame at Richmond (Davis/Mallory)**
Closes the accountability arc. The man who abandoned the city was cleared; the failure was systemic. One sentence or two in the closing.
