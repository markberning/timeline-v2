## Independent Must-Cover Checklist (built before reading draft)

1. Self-emancipation as mass agency — the enslaved fleeing to Union lines in hundreds of thousands, forcing the issue before any law existed
2. "Contraband" — Butler at Fort Monroe (May 1861), the three men, the legal maneuver, the word's double-edged meaning
3. Confiscation Acts (1861, 1862) and Militia Act — the law chasing the people
4. Contraband camps — conditions (disease, mortality), but also the positive (schools, wages, churches, family reunion)
5. The Emancipation Proclamation as the ENABLING act for Black enlistment (not its full political story)
6. The three "firsts" of Black regiments — combat (1st Kansas), muster (1st Louisiana), organized-from-slaves (1st SC Volunteers)
7. Bureau of Colored Troops / General Order No. 143 — standardizing the USCT
8. Numbers: ~180,000 soldiers, ~10% of the Union army, 175 regiments; ~18–20k Black sailors
9. Origins: majority Southern, formerly enslaved men
10. Deaths: ~40,000 total, mostly disease; mortality rate ~1/3 higher than white troops, cause = disproportionate fatigue/labor duty and worse medical care
11. Medal of Honor: 16–18 USCT soldiers; 14 from a single battle
12. The pay fight: $13+clothing vs. $7 net; 54th Massachusetts refusing all pay for ~18 months; refusal of Massachusetts state make-up money on principle; Gooding's letter to Lincoln; Walker's execution
13. Equal-pay legislation (1864) and its galling free-before-war vs. formerly-enslaved distinction
14. Proving battles: Port Hudson, Milliken's Bend, Fort Wagner/54th Mass., Battle of the Crater, New Market Heights
15. Fort Pillow — Confederate no-quarter policy; the massacre's racial math; "Remember Fort Pillow" as a battle cry; Lincoln's Order of Retaliation
16. Black voices: Douglass recruiting, "Men of Color, To Arms!"; the citizenship-through-service argument; his sons enlisting
17. Black sailors in the Union Navy — always integrated; ~18–20k served; ~16% of the Navy by some counts; Robert Smalls
18. Disproportionate labor/fatigue duty before (and often during) combat
19. The ban on Black commissioned officers in the USCT (white officers commanded; ~100 exceptions, mostly in Louisiana Native Guards)
20. The Butler Medal — Butler's Tiffany-made supplement to the MoH after New Market Heights (notable because it named the fight and the men)

## Checklist Review — Draft Coverage Assessment

### 1. Self-emancipation as mass agency
**COVERED — strongly.** The draft opens with this as its structural thesis, calls it by name ("self-emancipation"), quotes Encyclopedia Virginia on the framing, gives the ~500,000 figure with appropriate hedge, and makes "they acted first, the law stumbled after" the section's spine. Excellent.

### 2. "Contraband" — Butler, the three men, the legal maneuver, the word's double meaning
**COVERED — fully.** The three men are named, dated, and contextualized (forced to build the battery aimed at the fort). Butler's political identity (Massachusetts Democrat/lawyer, not abolitionist) is called out as the reason his ruling had weight. The Fugitive Slave Act demand and Butler's foreign-enemy/war-property response are explained. The word's double-edged quality ("halfway status: out of slavery, not into freedom or rights") is directly addressed.

### 3. Confiscation Acts and Militia Act
**COVERED.** Both Confiscation Acts and the Militia Act are explained in sequence with dates; the Militia Act's importance as the first federal crack at Black enlistment is noted; the First Act's failure to declare freedom is flagged. The through-line is explicit.

### 4. Contraband camps — conditions and positive dimensions
**COVERED.** Disease list, Camp Barker death rate, Freedman's Village daily deaths, Hampton "Slabtown," Mary Peake/Emancipation Oak, Freedman's Village's near-utopian design vs. reality. The "graveyard and a cradle" formulation addresses both sides. Adequate.

### 5. Emancipation Proclamation as enabling act
**COVERED.** The draft names the specific enlistment sentence, explicitly defers the Proclamation's own political story to its sibling section, and uses it as the hinge from improvised regiments to national policy. Correctly scoped.

### 6. Three "firsts" of Black regiments
**COVERED.** The draft explicitly labels each as a distinct "first" and cautions against blurring them. 1st Kansas (combat), 1st Louisiana (muster), 1st South Carolina (former slaves) — all three are there with correct dates and context.

### 7. Bureau of Colored Troops / General Order No. 143
**COVERED.** Date, order number, and the standardization-to-USCT consequence are all present.

### 8. Numbers (soldiers, percentage, regiments, sailors)
**COVERED.** ~180,000 / ~10% / 175 regiments / ~18,000–19,000 sailors all present. Contested ranges noted appropriately.

### 9. Origins (majority Southern, formerly enslaved)
**COVERED.** The documented >90,000 Southern vs. ~79,000 Northern breakdown is included with appropriate hedging on the definition-dependence of the higher popular figure.

### 10. Deaths and mortality disparity
**COVERED.** ~40,000 total, majority disease; both denominators (40k vs. 68k broader count) flagged; ~1/3 higher rate with causes named (fatigue duty, worse camps, worse care).

### 11. Medal of Honor (numbers)
**COVERED.** 16 USCT (some say 18), 14 at New Market Heights — with the Carney distinction (earliest *action*, latest *award*) carefully handled.

### 12–13. Pay fight and equal-pay legislation
**COVERED — exceptionally well.** The $13+clothing vs. $7-net disparity; the Militia Act origin as laborer's wage; the 54th's 18-month refusal; rejection of Massachusetts's state money; Gooding's letter verbatim; Walker's execution; the retroactive-pay free-before-war vs. formerly-enslaved injustice; the 54th finally accepting back pay fall 1864. This is one of the draft's strongest sections.

### 14. Proving battles
**COVERED.** Port Hudson, Milliken's Bend, Fort Wagner/54th, the Crater, New Market Heights — all five with dates, outcomes, and thematic weight. Dana/Dennis quote for Milliken's Bend. Carney and Lewis Douglass at Fort Wagner. The Crater's double indictment (pulled from the lead, then murdered when surrendering). New Market Heights' 14 MoH and the Butler-"contraband"-circle.

### 15. Fort Pillow + Confederate no-quarter policy + Lincoln's retaliation order
**COVERED.** Confederate 1862–63 policy stated; Fort Pillow's racial math (survival rates ~70% white vs. ~35% Black); "Remember Fort Pillow" as a battle cry; Lincoln's Order of Retaliation quoted verbatim; the order's limits candidly acknowledged.

### 16. Douglass recruiting + citizenship argument + his sons
**COVERED.** "Men of Color, To Arms!" with date and quote. Citizenship-through-service argument from the correct Philadelphia speech (not misattributed). Both sons named with their correct roles and outcomes. The provenance correction is explicitly flagged in the draft — meticulous.

### 17. Black sailors — always integrated; ~18–20k
**THIN.** The draft gives the numbers (~18,000–19,000, Navy never barred Black sailors) in a single sentence in the "One in Ten" stats section. That is accurate and present. However, no individual Black naval story, no mention of the remarkable fact that Black sailors made up roughly 16–20% of the Union Navy (a proportionally higher share than they held in the Army), and no mention of **Robert Smalls** — the enslaved harbor pilot who commandeered the Confederate steamer *Planter*, delivered it to the Union, and became the first Black captain in the U.S. Navy. Web research confirms Smalls played a direct role in persuading Lincoln to accept Black soldiers into the army (he met with Lincoln; Secretary Stanton ordered 5,000 former slaves to fight shortly after). The Navy section is present but underpowered given that "Black sailors" is explicitly named in the checklist brief. **Smalls is entirely absent from both draft and fact pack.**

### 18. Disproportionate labor/fatigue duty
**COVERED — partially, but thin.** The draft mentions fatigue duty twice: once as a cause of higher mortality ("more of the exhausting fatigue and labor duty"), and once in the pay-fight section ("Militia Act imagined them as laborers"). But the systematic, ongoing assignment of USCT to fatigue/labor duty *instead of combat* — an injustice that was both a morale issue and a mortality driver, and that was formally protested — is not rendered as its own scene or beat. It's present as a cause in a causal chain, not as a lived experience. General Lorenzo Thomas's General Order 21 outlawing discriminatory fatigue labor exists but is not mentioned. This is borderline THIN — not absent, but not fully surfaced.

### 19. Ban on Black commissioned officers in the USCT
**MISSING — but in scope only as a footnote.** The draft notes in the Louisiana Native Guard passage that it "had Black officers, men of color commanding men of color, which the army would soon work hard to prevent." That one sentence is the only reference. The systematic policy — the Bureau of Colored Troops mandating white commissioned officers for all USCT regiments, with ~100 exceptions almost entirely in Louisiana — is not explained. This is a genuine structural discrimination that shaped the USCT's identity. However, it is arguably a "should-consider" rather than a "must-add" given that the draft's scope is Black agency, not the army's administrative structure.

### 20. The Butler Medal
**MISSING.** After New Market Heights, Butler had Tiffany manufacture special medals for the USCT regiments he commanded — supplementing the MoH awards. The draft mentions the 14 MoH recipients at New Market Heights but not the Butler Medal. This is genuinely obscure and not in the fact pack; it is a SHOULD-CONSIDER, not a MUST-ADD.

## Fact Pack Blind Spots

1. **Robert Smalls entirely absent from both draft and fact pack.** He is the most famous individual Black sailor of the Civil War — commandeered the Confederate steamer *Planter* from Charleston Harbor, May 13, 1862; delivered it to the Union; met Lincoln; arguably influenced the decision to enlist Black soldiers; became first Black captain in the U.S. Navy. If Black sailors are to be more than a one-sentence stat, Smalls is the human face of the Navy's story. The fact pack should have flagged him.

2. **Black sailor MoH recipients not tallied clearly.** The pack notes "~25 Black recipients total for the war counting sailors" but lists no naval MoH recipients by name. Web research finds 8 Black naval MoH recipients (John Lawson, Robert Blake, Aaron Anderson, William H. Brown, Thomas English, James Mifflin, and others). These are not required for the draft but represent a pack gap.

3. **Enrollment Act of March 3, 1865** — the law that *actually* granted full and equal back pay for ALL Black soldiers (including the formerly enslaved) regardless of pre-war free status. The draft presents the June/July 1864 law as the resolution, which is accurate as far as it goes, but the "later largely smoothed over" hedge at the end is vague about what smoothed it over. The Enrollment Act of 1865 is the mechanism. Not a critical gap but worth noting.

4. **Butler Medal at New Market Heights** — not in pack at all. Tiffany-made, Butler-funded supplement to the MoH. Small but vivid.

5. **General Lorenzo Thomas's General Order 21** (outlawing discriminatory fatigue labor for USCT) — mentioned in web sources, not in pack. Its existence and routine flouting is a crisp fact that would tighten the fatigue-duty beat.

## MUST-ADD

**1. Robert Smalls and Black sailors as actors, not just statistics.**
The draft's entire engine is "Black people as agents, not passive recipients." It applies this perfectly to the Army but drops it for the Navy. Black sailors made up ~16–20% of the Union Navy — a proportionally larger share than in the Army — and the most famous individual act of naval self-emancipation (Smalls commandeering the *Planter*, May 13, 1862) is a direct mirror of the self-emancipation theme on which the draft opens. Smalls also personally met Lincoln and helped make the case for Black Army enlistment. The Navy currently gets one sentence of raw numbers. It needs at minimum two to three sentences with Smalls as the named human anchor. **New facts needed: web-verified (above); not in fact pack — fact pack must be updated before revision.**

**2. The "labor not soldiers" reality — fatigue duty as lived experience, not just a mortality cause.**
The draft mentions fatigue duty as a cause of higher death rates and invokes it in the pay-fight context (the Militia Act imagined them as laborers). But the systematic assignment of USCT to digging, hauling, and camp labor — often *instead of* combat duty, a sustained humiliation for men who had enlisted to fight — is never rendered as a scene or beat. It is the missing middle between the pay fight (called laborers in the wage law) and the proving battles (had to prove they would fight). The disproportionate fatigue duty assignment is the lived institutional racism between those two sections. A short passage or even a strong sentence explicitly naming it as policy-driven discrimination — not just a mortality footnote — would close this gap. **No new web verification needed; facts are already in the draft's own mortality section and can be reframed.**

---

## SHOULD-CONSIDER

**A. The ban on Black commissioned officers in the USCT.**
One sentence in the Louisiana Native Guard passage ("the army would soon work hard to prevent [Black officers]") gestures at this but leaves it hanging. The Bureau of Colored Troops' explicit policy — white officers for all USCT regiments, with ~100 Black commissioned exceptions mostly in Louisiana — is a revealing structural fact. It shows that the army trusted Black men with their lives but not with authority over men. A sentence or two would sharpen the "two fights" theme (against the enemy AND against their own army's racism). Not essential given the draft's scope, but the Louisiana passage already opens the door.

**B. The Butler Medal.**
Butler had Tiffany design medals specifically for the USCT soldiers at New Market Heights — supplementing the federal MoH with a regimental honor he funded out of pocket. It is a vivid, specific, reportable detail that deepens the closing New Market Heights circle (the man who coined "contraband" now commissioning Tiffany-made medals for those same men). One sentence if space allows.

**C. The Enrollment Act of March 3, 1865** as the mechanism by which the formerly-enslaved finally received full retroactive back pay (currently glossed as "later largely smoothed over"). Pinning the mechanism would make the pay-fight resolution crisper — though the draft's current hedged phrasing is honest and defensible.

## MUST-ADD
*(Essential; revision required before ship)*

1. **Robert Smalls + Black sailors as agents, not just a number.** ~16–20% of the Union Navy was Black; Smalls commandeered the CSS *Planter* on May 13, 1862 — a direct act of self-emancipation on water — and helped persuade Lincoln to accept Black Army enlistment. The draft's central thesis demands he appear. Fact pack blind spot: new facts need web-verification (completed above; sources: Smithsonian Magazine, Wikipedia, American Battlefield Trust, NPR).

2. **Fatigue duty as lived institutional discrimination — not just a mortality footnote.** The systematic assignment of USCT to labor instead of combat was policy-driven, protested, and formally (if ineffectively) banned by Lorenzo Thomas's GO 21. The draft should render it as a named injustice in its own right — the bridge between the pay fight ("we are called laborers") and the proving battles. No new facts needed; reframing of existing material.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER
*(Enriching but not blocking)*

A. Ban on Black commissioned officers in the USCT — one sentence to close the "army would soon prevent this" hanging gesture in the Louisiana passage.  
B. The Butler Medal — Tiffany-made, Butler-funded, given to NMH USCT; deepens the closing circle.  
C. Enrollment Act of March 3, 1865 — pins the "later smoothed over" mechanism for the retroactive pay fix.
