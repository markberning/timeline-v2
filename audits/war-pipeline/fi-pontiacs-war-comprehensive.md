## How I judged this

Storytelling-first. A theme section is allowed to be SELECTIVE — I am not asking for an
encyclopedia, and I did not penalize the draft for omitting minor forts, the full
casualty arithmetic, or Pontiac's 1769 death (the fact pack flags that as optional and
the draft's choice to drop it is defensible). I flag an item as a MUST-ADD only when its
absence distorts the story the section sets out to tell — and this section explicitly
sets out to tell a **Native-nations-first** story of what the British victory cost them.
That framing raises the bar on one specific thing: the section must show what the war
actually meant *for Native people on the ground*, including the violence turned against
them. That is where the one essential gap sits.

## MUST-ADD (verdict-blocking)

### 1. The Paxton Boys and the Conestoga Massacre (December 1763) — MISSING; needs new facts → FACT PACK FIRST

**Why this is essential, not gold-plating.** This section's entire purpose is to tell the
war from the Native nations' side and to "name the dispossession plainly … do not
sanitize it." The draft does that for the *land* and for the *smallpox blankets* — but it
omits the war's most notorious act of violence against Native *people* on the colonial
side of the line. In December 1763, amid the frontier panic Pontiac's War created, a
group of Scots-Irish frontiersmen from Paxton, Pennsylvania (the "Paxton Boys") murdered
and scalped the **Conestoga** — a small, peaceful, neutral Native community that had lived
alongside colonists for decades and had nothing to do with the rising. Six were killed
and the village burned on **December 14, 1763**; the remaining fourteen, taken into
"protective" custody in a Lancaster workhouse, were broken in upon and butchered —
including women and children — on **December 27, 1763**. Roughly **600 armed
frontiersmen** then marched on Philadelphia (turned back by a delegation including
Benjamin Franklin). **No one was ever convicted.**

This is not a tangent. It is the direct domestic face of the same conflict and the same
racial logic the draft already names in Amherst ("extirpate this execrable race," "no
better than slaves or dogs"): the war legitimized killing Native people *as Native
people*, friendly or hostile. A Native-nations-first telling that includes Amherst's
genocidal intent and the blankets but omits colonists actually carrying out the massacre
of a neutral Native town is structurally incomplete — it shows the empire's intent but
not the settler violence that the empire's posture licensed, which is precisely the
"slow catastrophe" the closing section is reaching for. The Conestoga were destroyed *as
a people* in this episode.

A SELECTIVE theme does not need every detail (the tract war, electoral fallout, Will
Sock can stay out), but it needs the beat itself: one tight passage (a short section or a
strong paragraph) that the war turned indiscriminate anti-Native violence loose on the
frontier, and that its clearest instance was the extermination of the peaceful Conestoga
by colonists who were never punished.

**This needs the FACT PACK FIRST.** The fact pack has **no Paxton Boys / Conestoga
content at all** — no dates, no toll, no the-Conestoga-were-neutral framing, no
march-on-Philadelphia, no no-convictions line. Author cannot write this from the current
pack without inventing facts. The fact-checker should add a Paxton/Conestoga block:
neutral Conestoga; Dec 14 (six killed, town burned) and Dec 27 (fourteen killed in the
Lancaster workhouse, incl. women/children); ~600-strong march on Philadelphia turned
back by a Franklin-led delegation; no convictions; the charge that the Conestoga
harbored hostile spies was fabricated. (Sourcing: Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia,
"Pontiac's War and the Paxton Boys"; Britannica, "Paxton Boys uprising"; American
History Central, "Paxton Boys and the Conestoga Massacre (1763)".)

## SHOULD-FIX (not verdict-blocking)

- **The Illinois holdout / "the war did not end everywhere at once" strand is THIN.** The
  draft names Charlot Kaské and his crossing the Mississippi, which is good, but a reader
  could come away thinking peace in 1764–66 was a smooth, single arc. One clause noting
  that fighting and resistance persisted in the Illinois country into 1764–65 — that the
  coalition's nations made peace on their own timetables, not in unison — would reinforce
  the draft's own (correct) "coalition, not a king" thesis at the back end, where it
  currently flattens into a tidy peace sequence. Optional; the current text is not wrong,
  just smooth.

- **Choctaw / southern and trans-Appalachian breadth.** Several reputable overviews list
  Choctaw, Tunica, Piankashaw, and Peoria among nations touched by the rising's orbit.
  The draft's nation list is already generous and Great-Lakes/Ohio-focused, which is the
  right center of gravity — no change required — but if the author wants one line on the
  rising's geographic reach beyond the Lakes/Ohio core, the facts support it. Pure
  enrichment, not an omission.

## Blind spots in the FACT PACK itself

1. **No Paxton Boys / Conestoga Massacre entry (the load-bearing gap).** The pack is
   otherwise strong and careful, but it is silent on the single most significant act of
   anti-Native violence in the conflict. Because the pack drives what the author can
   write, this silence is *why* the draft is missing the beat. Add the block described in
   MUST-ADD #1. This is the fact pack's one essential omission.

2. **Peace-was-not-unanimous is underdeveloped.** Section 7 presents the 1764–66 peace as
   a clean three-step sequence (Niagara → Muskingum → Pontiac 1766). It does not capture
   that resistance continued in the Illinois country into 1765, that nations peeled off
   the coalition at different times, or that Kaské sought renewed French aid in New
   Orleans before giving up. The pack mentions Kaské's intransigence in §4 but never ties
   it to the *timeline* of the peace. Minor — supports SHOULD-FIX #1.

No other factual blind spots found. The contested-points handling (fort count, smallpox
effectiveness, quote-wording locks, Michilimackinac date) is exemplary and the draft
honored all of it.

## On the Native perspective and aims (the special-attention item)

**Present and correctly weighted — this is the draft's strongest dimension.** The section
opens by reframing the war's name as erasure, centers the two-empire leverage as a
*Native* survival strategy, names the nations and leaders in their own preferred names,
treats Neolin's vision as spiritual fuel on its own terms, insists on "coalition, not a
king," and closes on sovereignty: "not as rebels against a rightful master but as nations
defending their own land." The "won the battle to be treated as nations again … could
not win the longer war over the land" formulation is exactly the weighting the brief
asked for.

The **one** place the Native-perspective mandate is not fully honored is the Conestoga:
the draft documents what the empire *intended* to do to Native people (Amherst, the
blankets) but omits what colonists actually *did* to a peaceful Native nation in the same
months. Adding the Paxton Boys beat is therefore not just a comprehensiveness fix — it is
what makes the Native-side telling honest about settler violence, not only imperial
policy.

---

## Bottom line

Fifteen of sixteen core beats are COVERED, most of them strongly, and the contested-fact
discipline is excellent. The section FAILS only because the Paxton Boys / Conestoga
Massacre — the war's defining act of violence against neutral Native people, and a direct
test of the section's own "name the dispossession, don't sanitize it" mandate — is
MISSING from both draft and fact pack. Add the fact-pack block first, then a tight
passage. That single addition clears the gate.
