# FRAMING CRITIC — Fall of Fort Ticonderoga (1777) battle page

Draft: `audits/war-pipeline/rev-ticonderoga-1777-draft.md`
Fact pack: `audits/war-pipeline/rev-ticonderoga-1777-factpack.md`
Critic lane: fairness, emphasis, interpretive honesty. (Byte-level fact checks belong to the fact critic.)

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

No blockers. The page's framing heart (St. Clair's right-call/ruined-career antithesis), the
Howe debunk, the legend-tagging (George III / Phillips / Fermoy / Warner), and the Saratoga
inversion are all handled at evidence level and with the right temperature. Four SHOULD-FIX
items, all small: one sentence that quietly re-asserts the very Howe myth the page debunks,
one unsupported superlative, one caption over-claim, and one CC BY-SA credit left as a
placeholder.

## Findings

### 1. The St. Clair question — SOUND

The decision is honored without sainting, and the vilification is told as panic-politics
without smugness:

- "Nobody outside the northern army understood the arithmetic of the place (the hollow
  garrison, the sprawling works, the bare hill), so the explanations people reached for were
  darker" — this is the right move: it explains WHY the public reached for treason (they
  lacked the information) instead of sneering at them. The silver-balls story is told as
  "absurd even by the standards of wartime rumor," which the pack itself licenses ("one
  absurd story," §6).
- Both courts-martial at evidence level: St. Clair "with the highest honor" (1778), Schuyler
  "cleared of neglect of duty," "Congress confirmed both verdicts," "Neither man ever led a
  major field command again" — all pack §6 HIGH, none over-claimed.
- Anti-sainting ballast is present: the bio carries St. Clair's Defeat 1791 ("the worst rout
  Native forces ever inflicted on a US army") and "Died poor, 1818." The man is not polished.
- "He was right about both halves" and "It took years, and a lost British army, for that
  sentence to read as the achievement it was" are page-voice judgments, but both are the
  pack's own §1 HIGH verdict (evacuation preserved the army that trapped Burgoyne) rendered
  in prose. Within tolerance. (NIT-3 below offers an optional soften.)
- The character-vs-army reflection is correctly reported speech ("St. Clair said afterward
  that..."), per pack §8.5.

### 2. Both-sides — SOUND

- Defiance insight credited to the right people per pack §4: Twiss did the reconnaissance
  and the report; Fraser + Phillips drove the road. The draft renders exactly that ("The real
  action was an engineering survey... Lieutenant William Twiss... climbed Sugar Loaf and
  reported"; "Fraser and Major General William Phillips... put about 400 men and most of the
  army's draft cattle on the job"). British competence is the section's engine, not a foil.
- Fraser respected: Advanced Corps spearhead, dawn attack, his >20% loss at Hubbardton stated,
  the Bemis Heights death and dusk burial dignified; the disputed Murphy attribution correctly
  absent.
- Riedesel respected: the force-march credited, and the hymn-singing arrival told as the
  strange, vivid thing it was — "advancing into the battle singing a hymn to a military band,
  on Riedesel's order, to sound like an army" — with the motive kept from the German accounts
  per pack §4, not played for comedy. The Baroness MEANWHILE ("written from inside the army
  the Americans were running from") and the Schwalm quote give the German side interior life.
- Burgoyne's proclamation mockery ("magnificently pompous," "the whole continent would shortly
  be laughing") is period-grounded — the pack says it was "widely reprinted and mocked in
  1777" — so this is reporting the mockery, not inventing it.

### 3. The Howe question — ONE LEAK (SHOULD-FIX 1)

The MEANWHILE itself is exemplary: the pigeonhole version named AND debunked ("The record is
messier and worse"), no order, Burgoyne's foreknowledge, closing on the pack's exact safest
phrasing ("sailed on an assumption of cooperation that nobody had actually arranged"). It does
not overcorrect into a confident counter-theory — "messier and worse" describes the evidence
pattern, not a new villain. Section 1's "Nobody in London ever turned that assumption into an
order. Hold that thought." is equally clean.

But Section 3 then leaks the myth back in — see SHOULD-FIX 1.

### 4. Legend framing — SOUND

- George III: "That scene is a legend, a piece of gossip with no contemporary source behind
  it... not a fact about the King, but a perfect fossil of the mood" — exactly the required
  framing, and the legend-hedge is even carried into the section blurb ("a king (the story
  goes) crowing"). Nothing resolved by fiat.
- Phillips goat line: "is supposed to have said (the attribution is traditional; no document
  records it)" — correct attributed-undocumented framing per §8.6.
- Fermoy fire: burned against orders stated as fact (HIGH), betrayal-framing doubted in the
  draft's own voice with the pack's evidence ("British accounts credit deserters with the
  news, and dawn was an hour off") — and the closing clause ("it did the men marching under
  its light no favors") keeps the drama without re-asserting causation. Correct.
- Warner's "meet me at Manchester": "by the traditional account" — correct.
- Burgoyne bio's "the King was (reportedly) ecstatic" keeps the pack's own hedge.

### 5. Emphasis vs. pack confidence — TWO SUPERLATIVE SLIPS

- "Gibraltar of the North" consistently framed as period reputation ("People called it...
  and they meant it"), never the page's own engineering judgment — correct.
- The Saratoga inversion is proportionate: the draft never claims the fall caused Saratoga.
  The slowing is attributed to Burgoyne's own Skenesborough choice plus Schuyler's axemen;
  the saved garrison "became part of the army" (pack verbatim), and the vilified generals
  "between them, saved the soldiers and slowed the enemy" correctly splits the credit
  (St. Clair the men, Schuyler the roads). "The verdict... depends entirely on where you
  stop the clock" is honest two-sided framing, not triumphalism.
- The two-neglects distinction (§12.13) survives into prose: "The British had ignored it as
  attackers... The Americans were about to learn what ignoring it cost a defender."
- Two superlatives exceed the pack — see SHOULD-FIX 2 and NIT-2.

### 6. Captions and dossier surfaces — TWO FIXES

- "Possibly Fraser": intact in caption AND credit AND the commander img note, with the
  uncertainty itself made content ("the uncertainty is part of the record"). Correct.
- Warner no-image card: honest twice over — the gap stated as the fact it is, and the
  Bennington statue explicitly rejected as "a 20th-century invention, never a likeness."
- Mwanner photo (CC BY-SA 3.0): photographer named in the credit. Correct.
- Hubbardton photo (CC BY-SA 2.0): credit is a placeholder — see SHOULD-FIX 4.
- Jefferys 1758 plan: fresh caption explicitly dates it ("1758 plan... under its French name,
  Carillon, made for the battle the British lost here nineteen years earlier") — the
  other-war provenance is unmistakable. Correct.
- Winner line proportionate in both places: stats ("Winner: Britain... the garrison escaped
  to fight at Saratoga") and outcome ("British victory · the evacuation that saved an army")
  carry both halves per pack §1's explicit instruction.
- Caption 5 (Defiance-from-the-fort) over-claims what the garrison saw — see SHOULD-FIX 3.

## SHOULD-FIX

1. **Section 3 re-asserts the debunked Howe myth in passing.**
   Draft: "keep walking south into a country that was felling trees across every road, away
   from the one army that was supposed to meet you and never came."
   "Supposed to meet you" is precisely the lost-order framing the page's own MEANWHILE
   debunks (pack §12.1: assumed-but-never-ordered; "no positive order compelled Howe north").
   A reader who hits this sentence after the MEANWHILE gets two contradictory verdicts; a
   reader who skips the MEANWHILE gets only the myth.
   Fix: rephrase at assumption level, e.g. "...away from the army he had assumed would meet
   him at Albany, which was never coming" or "...the cooperation he had counted on and nobody
   had ever ordered."

2. **"It became the hardest small battle of the year" (Hubbardton) is a year-wide superlative
   the pack never makes.** Pack §1/§4 call it "the only pitched battle of the episode" / "the
   only real battle," and back its intensity only through the loss percentages (40% / >20%),
   which the draft already gives. Fix: cut the superlative and let the percentages carry it —
   e.g. "It became a savage stand-up fight:" or simply "Francis and Warner formed on Monument
   Hill..." The numbers two sentences later make the point harder than the adjective does.

3. **Caption for `Mount Defiance from Fort Ticonderoga.jpg` over-claims the sighting.**
   Draft: "This is what the garrison saw on the morning of July 5, 1777, with red coats and
   gun carriages visible on the summit."
   Pack §4 supports "visible movement/scarlet on the rock" — movement and a flash of scarlet,
   not gun carriages; "gun carriages visible" is invented detail on a gated surface (and the
   phrasing also awkwardly implies the 2009 photo contains red coats).
   Fix: "...what the garrison saw on the morning of July 5, 1777, when movement and a flash
   of scarlet on the bare summit gave the battery away."

4. **Hubbardton photo (CC BY-SA 2.0) credit is a placeholder, not an attribution.**
   Draft credit: "photograph · 2013 · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 2.0 (photographer
   attribution REQUIRED; credit the named photographer from the Commons file page)."
   The instruction is right but the surface as drafted does not carry the photographer's
   name, and CC BY-SA attribution is a license condition, not a style preference. The Mwanner
   credit shows the correct pattern. Fix: resolve the name at image-fetch time and put it in
   the credit string before integration; the integrator must not ship the bracketed TODO.

## NIT

1. **Dossier `note` line ambiguity:** "the British wrecked an army here in 1758" reads as if
   the British did the wrecking; it was a British army that wrecked itself against the French
   lines. The section blurb gets it right ("a British army wrecked itself against its lines").
   Fix: "a British army wrecked itself here in 1758."

2. **"One of the cheapest great victories of the century"** (Section 3 stop-the-clock passage)
   is the page's own century-scale superlative; the pack supports "in four days, almost for
   free" but not the league table. Internally consistent (on July 6 the cost WAS a handful)
   and clearly rhetorical, so NIT not SHOULD-FIX — but "Burgoyne has taken the most famous
   fort in America in four days, almost for free" says the same thing inside pack bounds.

3. **Optional temperature trim on the St. Clair verdict:** "He was right about both halves"
   plus "the achievement it was" both assert the page's verdict; each is pack-§1-backed, but
   two explicit vindications may read as advocacy stacked on advocacy. Keeping one and letting
   the other ride on the court-martial verdict ("The court-martial verdicts said as much")
   would be slightly cooler. Author's call.

4. **Meta-narration flags for the voice critic's lane:** "Remember the hill." and "Hold that
   thought." are direct-address narrator moves (see `memory/feedback_no_meta_narration`).
   Noted here only because they function as emphasis devices; ruling belongs to the voice
   critic.
