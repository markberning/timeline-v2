# FACT PACK — Battles of Saratoga (Freeman's Farm, Sept 19, 1777 + Bemis Heights, Oct 7, 1777 + the Convention, Oct 17, 1777)

*American Revolution vertical, XL battle page — the war's most consequential battle. TWO battles + a surrender, told as one three-week squeeze. Confidence tags: HIGH / MED / DISPUTED, with HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags. The world-consequence section owns only the BATTLEFIELD half of the French story; the french-alliance theme owns the diplomacy. The native-nations theme owns the deep Haudenosaunee telling; section 7 here is the campaign-local view only.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- TWO battles + a surrender, all within ~10 miles on the west bank of the Hudson, ~25-30 mi north of Albany, NY [HIGH]:
  - **Freeman's Farm** (First Saratoga), Sept 19, 1777 — at an abandoned farm clearing ~1 mi north of the American lines on Bemis Heights, near present-day Stillwater, NY (~43.01, -73.63).
  - **Bemis Heights** (Second Saratoga), Oct 7, 1777 — same ground plus the British fortified camp (Balcarres + Breymann redoubts) just north/west of Freeman's Farm.
  - **The Convention (surrender)**, Oct 17, 1777 — at Saratoga, present-day Schuylerville, NY (~43.10, -73.58), after the retreat and encirclement.
- Campaign-endpoint duration for the dossier: Sept 19 – Oct 17, 1777 (the two battles bracket 18 days; surrender 10 days after Bemis Heights) [HIGH].
- Significance: the turning point of the war — a whole British field army surrendered intact, and the news brought France openly into the war (treaties signed Feb 6, 1778) [HIGH]. Routinely listed among the most decisive battles in world history (Creasy's *Fifteen Decisive Battles* includes it) [HIGH for the convention of calling it that; phrase as historians' verdict, not breathless].
- Winner line material: **Decisive American victory — the surrender that brought France into the war.** Tactically Freeman's Farm was a British field-holding draw bought at unaffordable cost; Bemis Heights was an outright American victory; the campaign ended in capitulation [HIGH].

## 2. BACKGROUND — THE SQUEEZE
- **The plan**: Burgoyne's 1777 scheme (submitted to Lord Germain Feb 1777, approved) — his ~8,000-man army south from Canada via Lake Champlain and the upper Hudson to Albany; St. Leger's ~2,000 down the Mohawk Valley; the expectation of cooperation from Howe's army at New York. Object: control the Hudson corridor and cut New England off from the other colonies [HIGH].
- **Howe went to Philadelphia instead** — by sea via the Chesapeake, leaving no main-army push up the Hudson. Whether Burgoyne fully understood Howe wasn't coming is murky (Germain approved both plans; the orders left "too much latitude or no clearly defined overall strategy" — standard historiography) [MED on who-knew-what; HANDLE WITH CARE — don't assert Burgoyne was "abandoned" by a coordinated betrayal; it was a planning failure in London].
- **Early success**: Ticonderoga taken without a fight July 5-6, 1777 (the guns on Mount Defiance); rearguard mauled at Hubbardton July 7 [HIGH]. Then the crawl: Burgoyne took the overland Skenesborough route while the Americans felled trees and burned bridges — roughly three weeks to cover ~23 miles to Fort Edward [HIGH on the slow advance; MED on exact day-counts].
- **Bennington, Aug 16, 1777**: Baum's German foraging expedition (sent for horses, draft animals, supplies) destroyed by John Stark's New Hampshire militia + Seth Warner; with Breymann's mauled relief column, Burgoyne lost ~900-1,000 men and got no supplies [HIGH]. After garrisoning Ticonderoga/Crown Point and the summer's losses, his field force was down to just over 7,000 by early September, on a wilderness supply line short of carts and draft animals; the army went onto reduced rations in early October [HIGH on the squeeze; ration-cut timing MED — half/third rations are documented for early Oct, "half-rations by Sept" appears loosely in secondary sources].
- **St. Leger's prong collapsed**: bloodied at Oriskany (Aug 6), spooked by Arnold's faked-numbers ruse (the Hon Yost Schuyler story — tradition-flavored but standard), abandoned the Fort Stanwix siege Aug 22 and fell back to Canada. No Mohawk Valley pincer [HIGH on the retreat; the ruse's details MED].
- **Jane McCrea, July 27, 1777** — THE propaganda explosion; this page owns the story. What's documented: McCrea (~25, from a NJ family, engaged to David Jones, a Loyalist officer WITH Burgoyne) was seized near Fort Edward and killed and scalped; her scalp was recognized in the British camp [HIGH that she was killed + scalped by members of Burgoyne's Native contingent's party; everything past that contested]. The competing accounts: (a) killed by her escorts in a quarrel over the reward (the Anburey/camp version, blame fixed on a warrior called the Wyandot Panther / Le Loup); (b) hit by American bullets fired at the retreating party from Fort Edward, then scalped after death (Le Loup's own statement; an 1887 exhumation was claimed to show bullet wounds and no tomahawk wounds — but her skull was missing at the 2003 exhumation, so forensics settle nothing) [DISPUTED — present as genuinely unknown]. Burgoyne demanded the culprit for execution; Simon Fraser (per most accounts, La Corne also) warned that punishing him would send every Native ally home or into revenge; Burgoyne backed down [HIGH]. The propaganda: Gates's open letter to Burgoyne ("a young lady lovely to the sight, of virtuous character... scalped and mangled") ran through the newspapers; the story — a Loyalist's own fiancée, killed by the King's allies — became a militia-raising gift [HIGH that it was used and spread; its measurable effect on militia turnout is asserted everywhere but hard to quantify — MED, phrase as "helped fill Gates's camp," not a counted cause]. Wilkinson (who knew of her) undercut the legend: "a country girl... without either beauty or accomplishments" [HIGH as a quote, from his memoirs — HANDLE WITH CARE, Wilkinson is a self-serving memoirist]. The Vanderlyn painting (1804) is the icon of the LEGEND, not a document of the event — caption it that way [HIGH].
- **Native desertions**: Burgoyne started with ~1,000 Native allies (two waves of ~500); after Bennington (where ~80 became casualties and got "no gratitude") and the McCrea affair (proof Burgoyne couldn't protect them from punishment politics, and the campaign was stalling), La Corne, Langlade, and most of the contingent went home — by mid-September he had fewer than 100 scouts. Consequence: the army that crossed the Hudson was nearly blind in the woods, while Morgan's riflemen owned the screen [HIGH on the collapse + the blindness consequence; precise head-counts MED].
- **The Hudson crossing, Sept 13-15, 1777** — the no-going-back decision. Burgoyne crossed on a bridge of boats just north of Saratoga (Schuylerville) to the west bank, and deliberately cut his communications to Ticonderoga rather than garrison a chain of posts. From that moment the choice was Albany or capitulation; he said as much in his own defense later [HIGH on date/place/meaning; his exact retrospective wording varies — quote only what's verified in §8].

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
**The American army GREW between the battles — capture this in the dossier.**
- **American strength**: ~8,500-10,000 effectives around Sept 19 (Ketchum ~9,000; Wikipedia/Luzader ~10,000 total, ~8,500 effective) → ~12,000-13,000 by Oct 7 (Ketchum "12,000+") → on the order of 17,000-20,000+ ringing Burgoyne at Saratoga by mid-October as militia poured in (use "well over 15,000" if cautious) [HIGH on the growth; exact end-figure MED — sources run 17,000-20,000+].
- **British/German strength**: ~7,200 crossed the Hudson (Luzader) → ~5,000 battle-ready effectives by Oct 7 (Nickerson; nominal strength higher) → ~5,800-6,000 surrendered Oct 17 (commonly cited 5,791-5,895; see §6) [HIGH on the arc; tag the precise figures MED].
- **American command**: Maj. Gen. **Horatio Gates** (Northern Department, took over from Schuyler Aug 19, 1777), Maj. Gen. **Benedict Arnold** (left wing), Maj. Gen. **Benjamin Lincoln** (right wing + the militia on Burgoyne's rear), Col. **Daniel Morgan** (Provisional Rifle Corps ~400-500 + Maj. Henry Dearborn's ~300 light infantry), Brig. Gens. Enoch Poor and Ebenezer Learned, Brig. Gen. Abraham Ten Broeck (Albany militia), Col. **Tadeusz Kościuszko** (chief engineer) [HIGH].
- **British command**: Lt. Gen. **John Burgoyne**; Brig. Gen. **Simon Fraser** (Advanced Corps, the right), Brig. Gen. James Hamilton (center column Sept 19), Maj. Gen. **Friedrich Adolf Riedesel** (the ~4,000-strong Brunswick/German contingent, the river-road left), Maj. Gen. William Phillips (artillery), Maj. John Dyke **Acland** (grenadiers), Maj. Alexander Lindsay, Earl of **Balcarres** (light infantry), Lt. Col. Heinrich **Breymann** (German grenadiers/reserve, killed Oct 7) [HIGH].

### Commander bio material (60-90 words each, verified)
- **Horatio Gates** (1727-1806) [HIGH]: English-born professional soldier — a former British Army major who'd been wounded carrying Braddock's doomed column to the Monongahela in 1755, then retired to Virginia and threw in with the Revolution. A superb organizer and congressional favorite, cautious by doctrine: dig in, let the enemy bleed. Took the Northern Department from Philip Schuyler on Aug 19, 1777, three weeks before Freeman's Farm — inheriting an army that Bennington, Stanwix, and the militia surge were already swelling. Saratoga made him "the hero of Saratoga"; Camden (1780) unmade him. The "Granny Gates" epithet: see §8 — thin evidence, tag as later/derisive tradition [epithet DISPUTED].
- **Benedict Arnold** (1741-1801) [HIGH]: Connecticut apothecary-merchant turned the most aggressive battlefield general the Revolution had — co-captor of Ticonderoga (1775), leader of the starving march on Quebec where his left leg took a ball, builder of the Valcour Island fleet that bought 1776's survival. At Saratoga he was the fighting half of a feuding command: stripped of his role after Freeman's Farm, he charged onto the field Oct 7 anyway and took a second ball in the same left leg storming the Breymann redoubt. Three years later he sold West Point. (In-repo portrait `rev-quebec1775-arnold.jpg` is born-verified — reuse with a Saratoga caption.)
- **Daniel Morgan** (c. 1735-1802) [HIGH on career; the 499-lashes story is tradition — HANDLE WITH CARE]: Virginia frontier teamster ("the Old Wagoner") who carried a grudge against the British Army said to date from a flogging in the French and Indian War. Led riflemen to Quebec (captured, exchanged), then got command of the ~500-man Provisional Rifle Corps Washington sent north in August 1777. His riflemen — long rifles, no bayonets, officer-killing accuracy — opened both Saratoga battles and blinded Burgoyne all campaign. His masterpiece came later: Cowpens, 1781. (In-repo `rev-quebec1775-morgan.jpg` born-verified — reuse.)
- **Tadeusz Kościuszko** (1746-1817) [HIGH]: Polish military engineer, trained in Warsaw and Paris, who sailed for America in 1776 and was commissioned colonel of engineers that October. In September 1777 he picked and fortified the ground that decided the campaign: Bemis Heights, a bluff where the Albany road squeezed between hills and the Hudson, laid out with batteries and entrenchments Burgoyne couldn't go through and died trying to go around. Gates told Benjamin Rush the campaign's "great tacticians" were "hills and forests, which a young Polish engineer was skillful enough to select" [quote chain in §8]. Later fortified West Point; led Poland's 1794 uprising.
- **Benjamin Lincoln** (1733-1810) [HIGH]: Massachusetts farmer-militiaman risen to Continental major general, the unglamorous second-ranking American at Saratoga. His job was Burgoyne's rear: the militia columns he directed raided the Lake George supply line in mid-September (Brown's raid freed American prisoners and netted hundreds of British), helping convince Burgoyne the road home was closing. Took a bad ankle wound Oct 8. Later surrendered Charleston (1780), then stood at Yorktown to receive the British surrender — and became the first Secretary at War.
- **John Burgoyne** (1722-1792) [HIGH]: "Gentleman Johnny" — playwright, MP, gambler, and a genuinely humane officer by the standards of the age (his men liked him; he disliked the lash). Made his name leading cavalry in Portugal in 1762, then sold London on his own plan: an army from Canada down the Champlain-Hudson corridor to Albany, splitting New England from the rest. He executed it with style, a 30-wagon personal baggage train (the champagne is documented complaint-level, not invented), and fatal optimism. After the surrender he went home on parole to defend himself in Parliament; he never commanded again, and went back to writing plays.
- **Simon Fraser** (1729-1777) [HIGH]: Scottish professional, the best soldier in Burgoyne's army — veteran of Louisbourg and the Plains of Abraham with the 78th Highlanders, commander of the elite Advanced Corps in 1777. He won Hubbardton, talked Burgoyne out of punishing Jane McCrea's killer (to keep the Native alliance), and anchored every advance. On Oct 7 he was rallying the collapsing reconnaissance when a rifle ball went through his stomach; he died the next morning and was buried at sunset inside the Great Redoubt while American guns fired on the procession [burial under fire HIGH; the Murphy attribution DISPUTED — §4/§8].
- **Friedrich Adolf Riedesel** (1738-1800) [HIGH]: Baron and major general commanding the ~4,000 Brunswick troops Britain hired for the campaign — roughly half of Burgoyne's army. A solid, unlucky professional: his flank attack saved Burgoyne's center from collapse at Freeman's Farm, and his advice (retreat while you can) was overruled twice. Captured with the army, he spent years as a Convention Army prisoner-celebrity. His wife **Frederika**, who crossed the Atlantic with three small daughters and kept a journal through the cannonade and the surrender, left the campaign's best eyewitness account [her journal = primary source, quotes in §8].
- **John Dyke Acland** (c. 1746-1778) [MED — use only if space allows]: Tory MP and major commanding Burgoyne's grenadiers; shot through both legs leading the bayonet charge at Bemis Heights and captured. His pregnant wife, Lady Harriet Acland, rowed down the Hudson under a flag of truce in the night rain to nurse him in American captivity — a story Burgoyne himself made famous. Died in England in 1778 (the duel-and-chill story attached to his death is shaky — HANDLE WITH CARE, omit or flag).

## 4. THE BATTLE(S)
### The ground (Kościuszko's choice) [HIGH]
- Gates moved the army north from Stillwater starting Sept 8 (some accounts Sept 12 for occupying the heights); Kościuszko laid out the line on **Bemis Heights**, a plateau ~100-200 ft above the Hudson where the river road to Albany passed through a defile between the bluff and the water. Entrenchments + batteries commanded the road; a fortified line ran inland (west). Burgoyne could not force the defile; his only play was to swing west through the woods around the American left — which is why both battles happened in the forest clearings (Freeman's Farm, Barber's wheat field) NW of the lines.

### FREEMAN'S FARM — Sept 19, 1777 [HIGH unless tagged]
- Burgoyne advanced in three columns: Fraser's Advanced Corps swinging wide right (west), Hamilton's four regiments (9th, 20th, 21st, 62nd) in the center with Burgoyne, Riedesel's Germans + the guns and supplies on the river road.
- Gates wanted to wait behind the works; Arnold (and others) pressed him to fight in the woods where British formations and bayonets were worthless. Gates "grudgingly" let Morgan's corps + Dearborn's light infantry go forward — the only major decision he had to make, and he made it right [framing per standard accounts].
- ~12:30-1 pm: Morgan's riflemen met the center column's advance company at the Freeman farm clearing and **picked off virtually every officer in the advance party** in the opening fire. The riflemen then over-pursued, ran into Fraser's main body, and scattered — Morgan reportedly rallying them with his turkey-call [turkey call = memoir/tradition (Wilkinson), MED — fine with "the story goes"].
- The seesaw afternoon: 3-4 hours of attack and counterattack across the ~350-yard clearing; the field and the British guns changed hands repeatedly (Americans several times took the field pieces but could neither hold nor drag them off — they'd shot the artillery horses and crews; ~36 of 48 British artillerymen in the center fell). The 62nd Foot was wrecked — reduced to about company strength; the 20th lost 116. Phillips personally led the 20th in to save the center.
- ~3 pm order / late afternoon execution: Riedesel, on his own initiative within discretionary orders, left ~500 men on the baggage and threw his Brunswickers + two guns against the American right flank, "on the double" — the shock stabilized the line. Darkness ended it. The Americans withdrew to their works; **Burgoyne held the field and claimed victory, but had lost ~600 men — roughly twice the American ~300-320 — and his best regiments were gutted** [casualty detail HIGH; see §5].
- **Was Arnold on the field?** [DISPUTED — handle at evidence level.] Tradition (and most popular histories) put Arnold galloping at the front. Luzader (the NPS Saratoga historian): no foundation in contemporary materials; Arnold was at headquarters feeding in regiments. Martin and others: he directed troops forward and may have led movements before Gates ordered him back. Contemporary scraps cut both ways (Van Cortlandt: colonels ran the front; the Wakefield account has Arnold instructing Morgan). SAFE TELLING: Arnold's DIVISION fought the battle, Arnold pushed the troops in and lobbied Gates for more; do not stage a horseback Arnold in the smoke on Sept 19.
- Why no second day: Burgoyne planned to renew the attack Sept 20-21, then on **Sept 21 received Henry Clinton's ciphered note from New York** (written Sept 12 — "You know my good will & are not ignorant of my poverty"; an offer to "make a push at [Fort] Montgomery in about ten days" with ~2,000 men). Burgoyne chose to dig in and wait [HIGH]. The wait cost him everything: rations shrank, horses starved, ~every night militia and Morgan's men sniped the pickets, and the American army grew daily.

### THE GATES-ARNOLD FEUD (between the battles) [evidence-tagged]
- DOCUMENTED [HIGH]: Gates's report to Congress on Freeman's Farm did not mention Arnold (the fighting was credited to "a detachment of the army"); Gates stripped Morgan's corps out of Arnold's division and made it answerable to headquarters only; Arnold protested in writing; there was a furious confrontation (the camp's letter-writers — Arnold's aides Varick and Livingston, both ex-Schuyler men Gates despised — recorded the breach in contemporary letters); Gates gave Arnold a pass to leave for Philadelphia; Arnold stayed in camp. Lincoln took effective command of the right; Arnold was left with no clear command.
- MEMOIR/TRADITION [MED-DISPUTED]: the cinematic "shouting match in the command tent" details and most direct quotes come from later memoir (especially Wilkinson, 1816 — a witness with an agenda) and from the aides' partisan letters. The generals-petition-Arnold-to-stay story is standard but secondhand.
- HANDLE WITH CARE: the feud is real and documented; its dialogue is not. Also real: the politics underneath (Gates vs Schuyler factions; Arnold staffed with Schuyler's ex-aides).

### CLINTON FROM NEW YORK — the hope that came too late and too small [HIGH]
- Sir Henry Clinton sailed up the Hudson Oct 3 with ~3,000 men, stormed **Forts Montgomery and Clinton** in the Highlands Oct 6, broke the chain across the Hudson, and sent gunboats upriver. Vanguard raids reached Kingston (then New York's capital), **burned Oct 16**, and the Livingston estate at Clermont — still ~45 miles short of Albany, with no orders or intention to fight through to Burgoyne. It was a raid, not a rescue.
- The cipher chain [HIGH, great detail]: Clinton's Oct 8 note from Fort Montgomery — "*Nous y voici*, and nothing now between us but Gates" — was carried by Lt. Daniel Taylor, who was caught at Little Britain ~Oct 10, swallowed the message in its hollow **silver bullet**, was dosed with emetic by order of (American) Gov. George Clinton, produced the bullet, and was hanged as a spy at Hurley on Oct 18 — the day after Burgoyne surrendered. The note never arrived; nothing Clinton did could have arrived in time.

### BEMIS HEIGHTS — Oct 7, 1777 [HIGH unless tagged]
- By early October Burgoyne was on short rations (cut ~Oct 3), down to ~5,000 effectives, and out of time. Over Riedesel's and Fraser's advice to fall back across the Hudson, he ordered a **reconnaissance-in-force** — ~1,500-1,700 picked men + 10 guns, led by himself, Fraser, Riedesel, and Phillips — to probe/turn the American left and cut forage in the wheat.
- ~2-2:30 pm: the column halted in **Barber's wheat field**, an exposed rise, while officers glassed the woods (foragers cutting wheat; the famous officers'-dinner-on-the-field detail appears in participant accounts — fine as color [MED]). Gates, told the British were out, reportedly answered "Order on Morgan to begin the game" [Wilkinson memoir — tag as "the story goes"].
- The American counterpunch hit both ends at once: **Poor's brigade** smashed the grenadiers on the British left (Acland down, shot through both legs, captured; his guns taken), while **Morgan swung wide and crushed the light infantry on the right flank**; Dearborn hit the breaking line frontally. Within an hour the reconnaissance lost ~400 men and 6 of 10 guns and was streaming back to its fortifications [HIGH].
- **Fraser's mortal wounding**: rallying the rearguard on horseback, Fraser was shot through the abdomen by one of Morgan's riflemen. The attribution to **Timothy Murphy** (the famous double-barreled-rifle sniper story, with Morgan's "that gallant officer... it is necessary that he should die" order) first solidifies in 19th-century accounts; Luzader calls it a possible 19th-century fabrication. SAFE TELLING: "one of Morgan's riflemen — tradition says Timothy Murphy" [DISPUTED attribution; the wounding itself HIGH].
- **ARNOLD'S RIDE** [evidence-tagged — this is the page's set piece, get it right]: Arnold, without a command, rode to the firing. The TRADITIONAL story (Wilkinson again): "betraying great agitation and wrath," galloping off **without orders**, Gates sending Maj. Armstrong to recall him — Armstrong never catching him until it was over. The COUNTER-EVIDENCE (JAR/Lynch): Burgoyne himself wrote Arnold "commanded on the left"; Benjamin Lincoln wrote (1799) that "General Gates... ordered General Arnold to advance"; Gates's own Oct 12 report praised "the Gallant General Arnold," odd if Arnold were mutinous. SAFE TELLING: Arnold had no formal command and rode out anyway — whether Gates blessed it, tolerated it, or chased him with a messenger is genuinely disputed; what nobody disputes is what he DID on the field [frame exactly this way].
- What he did [HIGH]: led Poor's/Learned's men against the **Balcarres redoubt** (the fortified Freeman's Farm line) — repulsed, fierce and futile; then spurred ACROSS the field (through the crossfire, unhurt) to Learned's attack on the weaker **Breymann redoubt** anchoring the British far right; led the storming through the gap between the works while Morgan's men circled behind. The redoubt fell at dusk; **Breymann was killed** (the "killed by his own men after sabering four of them" detail is tradition — [DISPUTED], use "reportedly"); in the final volleys Arnold's horse was shot and a musket ball shattered his left leg — **the same leg wounded at Quebec** — pinned under the falling horse [HIGH].
- The Breymann redoubt's fall turned the whole British fortified line: Burgoyne's camp was open on its right. That night he abandoned the position and began the retreat. (The **Boot Monument** at the spot — erected 1887, a sculpted boot honoring "the most brilliant soldier of the Continental Army... winner of the decisive battle of the American Revolution" — famously never names Arnold [verify inscription text in §9 images; the no-name fact HIGH].)

### RETREAT, ENCIRCLEMENT, AND THE CONVENTION [HIGH unless tagged]
- Night of Oct 7-8: withdrawal to the Great Redoubt by the river. **Oct 8, sunset: Fraser buried in the Great Redoubt** per his dying wish, chaplain Brudenell reading the service while American round shot threw dirt over the party (Burgoyne and the generals attending bareheaded) — until, the story goes, Gates's gunners realized it was a funeral [the cannonade during the burial HIGH — multiple eyewitnesses incl. Burgoyne and the Riedesels; the "Americans stopped/fired a minute-gun salute" coda is tradition, MED-DISPUTED].
- Oct 8-9: retreat north in cold rain to Saratoga (Schuylerville), abandoning the hospital (~300 sick/wounded left to Gates's mercy) and most baggage. Frederika Riedesel and her three small daughters rode the retreat in a calash and spent the next six days in the cellar of a house (the Marshall House) under American artillery fire — her journal's most famous passages [HIGH; quotes §8].
- By Oct 12-13 the army was surrounded at Saratoga: Gates across the south and west, Stark (back with fresh militia) sealing the north at Fort Edward, militia and Morgan everywhere, American guns commanding the camp, ~20,000 vs ~5,800 effective. Council of war voted to open talks Oct 13; cessation Oct 14-16.
- **THE CONVENTION — Burgoyne's negotiating coup** [HIGH]: Burgoyne refused the word "capitulation"; the document signed Oct 16 is the **"Convention of Saratoga."** Terms: the army marched out of camp "with the Honors of War" and grounded arms at the riverside; it would march to Boston and embark for Britain on condition of **not serving again in North America during the war**; officers kept sidearms and baggage. Gates accepted astonishingly generous terms because Clinton was loose in the Highlands and he wanted the army in hand NOW — when he briefly demanded grounding arms in camp (surrender-style), Burgoyne threatened to fight on and Gates folded within hours [the rush-because-of-Clinton motive HIGH in scholarship].
- **Oct 17, 1777**: ~5,800-6,000 troops grounded arms on the meadow by Fort Hardy (Schuylerville); Burgoyne in full dress offered his sword to Gates — Wilkinson's memoir supplies the famous exchange ("The fortune of war, General Gates, has made me your prisoner" / "I shall always be ready to bear testimony that it has not been through any fault of your Excellency") — Gates returned the sword and the armies dined together; American bands reportedly played "Yankee Doodle" [sword exchange + dialogue = Wilkinson/memoir, MED — use "by the famous account"; the dinner Burgoyne-Gates HIGH (multiple accounts incl. Frederika's)].
- **The Convention Army's actual fate — tell it honestly** [HIGH]: Congress never ratified the convention and never let the army sail. Pretexts accumulated (cartouche boxes not surrendered, Burgoyne's "the public faith is broke" line seized as evidence Britain would void the no-re-service pledge, fear London would simply free up garrison troops elsewhere). The ~5,800 marched to Cambridge, Mass. (1777-78), then ~700 miles to Charlottesville, Virginia (winter 1778-79), then were shuttled onward as the war moved; men deserted or melted into the population by the thousands. Only a fraction (officers exchanged, like Riedesel and Phillips in 1780; Burgoyne paroled home May 1778) ever went home before the peace. A genuinely shabby episode on the American side — the page should say so plainly.

## 5. CASUALTIES (per battle + the surrender — ranges)
- **Freeman's Farm, Sept 19** [HIGH]: British ~600 (often given ~160 killed / ~360 wounded / ~40-100 missing; Ketchum's aggregate "440 killed, 695 wounded" is the TWO-battle total — don't misassign it). The 62nd Foot reduced to ~company strength; ~36 of 48 artillerymen in the center column down; the 20th Foot lost 116. American ~300-320 (commonly ~65-90 killed, ~210-240 wounded/missing) [tag exact splits MED — returns vary; safest: "about 600 British to about 300 American"].
- **Bemis Heights, Oct 7** [HIGH]: British ~400 in the wheat-field phase alone, ~600+ total for the day including the redoubt fighting (killed/wounded/captured; 6 of 10 guns lost; Breymann dead; Acland and ~200 captured). American ~130-150 (Ketchum's ~330 figure = killed+wounded BOTH battles for the Americans in some renderings — treat per-battle splits as MED and use "about 150 against four times that").
- **Whole campaign, both sides** [MED — use ranges]: British/German battle casualties ~1,000-1,200 across the two battles + the retreat; American ~450-500 total. 
- **THE NUMBER THAT MATTERS — the surrender** [HIGH]: ~**5,800-6,000 men marched into captivity Oct 17** (period returns 5,791-5,895; NPS "about 5,900 British, German, and provincial troops"; "nearly 6,000" is the safe phrase) — all that remained of the ~9,000-10,000 (with auxiliaries) who left Canada in June. Surrendered with them: 27-42 pieces of artillery (sources vary — tag MED, "more than two dozen guns"), ~5,000 stand of small arms, the colors having been smuggled home (Frederika Riedesel sewed the Brunswick flags into a pillow per her journal — charming, MED-tradition from her own account [actually her journal/family tradition; flag-burning vs smuggling versions vary — HANDLE WITH CARE]).
- Fraser dead (Oct 8), Breymann dead (Oct 7); Arnold's left leg shattered; Lincoln shot in the ankle (Oct 8); Acland double-leg-wounded + captured [all HIGH].

## 6. AFTERMATH / WORLD CONSEQUENCE
**Boundary note: this battle page owns the BATTLEFIELD half — the surrender, the news, and the fact of the French treaties. The french-alliance theme owns the diplomacy's inside story (Franklin's Paris, the Beaumarchais pipeline, treaty mechanics). Keep the Versailles material here to a tight paragraph and link out.**
- **The fact chain, verified** [HIGH]: the news crossed in ~6 weeks — Jonathan Loring Austin sailed from Boston and reached Franklin at Passy on **Dec 4, 1777** ("Sir, is Philadelphia taken?" — "Yes sir." — "But, sir, I bring you greater news: General Burgoyne and his whole army are prisoners of war!" — the exchange is from later retellings of Austin's arrival; use as "the story goes" [MED]). **Dec 6**: Vergennes's office summoned the commissioners; negotiations opened within days. **Dec 17, 1777**: France communicated its decision to recognize and treat with the United States. **Feb 6, 1778**: the **Treaty of Amity and Commerce** and the **Treaty of Alliance** signed in Paris (Franklin, Deane, Lee for the US; Gérard for France). Britain and France were shooting at each other by June 1778; Spain followed in 1779. The war went global — which is what made Yorktown possible.
- Why Saratoga moved Vergennes: it was PROOF the Americans could destroy a British field army — the demonstration France had waited for since 1776 (and fear that Britain would now offer terms the Americans might take: London's Carlisle Commission / North's conciliation offer of early 1778 — everything short of independence — confirmed the fear and arrived too late) [HIGH as standard scholarship].
- **Burgoyne**: paroled, sailed home May 1778; demanded a court-martial he never got; defended himself in Parliament ("A State of the Expedition from Canada," 1780); never commanded in the field again [HIGH].
- **Gates**: the hero of Saratoga — Congress struck a gold medal; his name immediately entangled in the winter's "Conway Cabal" murmurs about replacing Washington [HIGH on medal + entanglement; the "cabal's" coherence is itself a historiographical dispute — don't overclaim].
- **Arnold**: crippled months in Albany hospital; promotion seniority restored, but Saratoga's credit fight festered — the road to West Point starts in this hospital bed (standard framing; keep one line, the ARW vertical will tell the treason elsewhere) [HIGH].
- **Ticonderoga**: the British abandoned it in November 1777 — the whole corridor gained in 1777 was given back [HIGH].
- **The Convention Army**: see §4 — Congress repudiated the convention (Jan 8, 1778 suspension); the army was marched Cambridge → Charlottesville, VA (Nov 1778 - Jan 1779, ~700 miles in winter) → dispersed onward (Frederick MD, Lancaster PA...) as the war moved; thousands deserted/settled; most privates never went home until 1783. Burgoyne was exchanged (formally 1781); Riedesel and Phillips exchanged 1780 [HIGH].

## 7. NATIVE PERSPECTIVE
**Boundary note: the native-nations theme owns the deep telling (the Haudenosaunee civil war, Oriskany as kin-against-kin, Sullivan 1779). This page tells the campaign-local story.**
- Burgoyne opened the campaign with ~1,000 Native allies in two waves: ~500 mostly Haudenosaunee-allied and mission communities from the St. Lawrence, then ~500 western nations (Ottawa, Ojibwe, Fox, Mississauga and others) under the French-Canadian partisan leaders La Corne and Langlade [HIGH on scale; tribal composition MED].
- Burgoyne tried to have it both ways: a bombastic proclamation threatening to loose his warriors on rebels, and rules forbidding scalping of non-combatants. The McCrea affair (§2) exposed the contradiction — he couldn't punish the accused without losing the alliance, and by not punishing he handed Gates a propaganda weapon while STILL losing the alliance [HIGH as framing; grounded in documented events].
- After Bennington (~80 Native casualties, "no gratitude" from Burgoyne) and the McCrea fallout, La Corne, Langlade, and most of the contingent went home — under 100 scouts remained by the Hudson crossing. The practical result decided battles: the army that fought at Freeman's Farm had no woods screen, while Morgan's riflemen and the militia owned the forest. British pickets and foraging parties were picked off nightly; Burgoyne marched blind [HIGH].
- For the Haudenosaunee, 1777 was the year the Confederacy's neutrality broke into civil war — Oriskany (Aug 6, the Mohawk Valley prong of this same campaign) put Mohawk/Seneca against Oneida/Tuscarora. Saratoga's outcome — the British army gone, the corridor American — left Britain's Native allies to carry the frontier war themselves (1778-79), and brought Sullivan's answer. One paragraph here, then hand off to the theme [HIGH as framing].

## 11. DOSSIER STATS (assembled)
- **Dates**: Sept 19 + Oct 7, 1777; surrender Oct 17, 1777 (campaign endpoint pair for the dossier: Sept 19 - Oct 17).
- **Forces, Freeman's Farm**: ~8,500-9,000 Americans effective (of ~10,000) vs ~7,200 British/German (engaged subsets smaller on both sides).
- **Forces, Bemis Heights**: ~12,000-13,000 Americans (and climbing) vs ~5,000 British/German effectives (the reconnaissance itself ~1,500-1,700).
- **Casualties**: Freeman's Farm ~600 British / ~300 American; Bemis Heights ~600+ British (incl. captured) / ~150 American; **surrendered Oct 17: ~5,800-6,000** ("nearly 6,000" safe; period returns 5,791-5,895).
- **Winner**: Decisive American victory — the surrender of an entire British field army; the victory that brought France into the war.
