# Chancellorsville — NEWCOMER / CLARITY critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

**Reader persona:** sharp 15-year-old, ZERO prior Civil War / military knowledge.
**Read cold against:** `chancellorsville-draft.md`.
**Verdict legend:** CLEAR (a newcomer follows it) · NEEDS-GLOSS (term/leap is graspable but should be defined inline on first use) · LOST (a newcomer is stranded — blocks comprehension).
**Fix legend:** MUST-FIX = comprehension-blocking, ship-blocking · SHOULD-FIX = friction, not fatal.

The good news up front: this draft is unusually friendly to a newcomer. The author already inline-defines a lot — "in the air," "beaten in detail," "the finest army," the Wilderness, the Chancellorsville house — and does it in-voice. The problems below are concentrated in a handful of military nouns that are used as if already known, plus a couple of orientation gaps (who's where, what a "corps" is) and two or three passages that pile up too much at once.

---

## 1. JARGON / UNDEFINED TERMS

### MUST-FIX (LOST or comprehension-blocking on first use)

**`corps`** — §the-plan ("sent three entire corps"), and then everywhere (Second Corps, VI Corps, XI Corps, Second Corps again). **LOST.** This is the single biggest gap. "Corps" is used 15+ times as the basic unit of the whole battle, and it is never defined. A newcomer has no idea whether a corps is 500 men or 50,000, and the entire story is about how big each chunk of army is (Lee splitting his into three, Jackson taking "his entire Second Corps, around 28,000 to 30,000 men"). The numbers eventually let you back-infer it, but the reader is guessing for a full section first. **Define on first use in §the-plan**: a corps = a large self-contained chunk of an army, here roughly 10,000–30,000 men, several of which make up the whole army.

**`flank` / `right flank` / "around the side" / "behind Lee's left flank"** — §the-plan. **NEEDS-GLOSS, borderline LOST.** "Flank" appears first in §the-plan ("come down behind Lee's left flank") with no definition, and the entire battle is a flank attack. The draft *does* eventually explain the concept beautifully in §lee-divides ("in the air"... "getting around the end of it") and §the-flank-march ("a flanked line cannot fight back"), but that's two sections too late. The first use in §the-plan needs a one-clause gloss: a flank = the end/side of an army's line, as opposed to its front. Right now a newcomer hits "left flank" cold.

**`battery` / "thirty-odd guns" vs "battery"** — the word **`battery`** does not actually appear in the prose (good — the ledger lists it as a watch-term but the author avoided it). However **`artillery` / `guns` / "artillery duel"** (§jackson-falls) are used heavily. "Guns" is fine and plain. "Artillery duel" (§jackson-falls FIG + "dominated the duel," "overpowered") is graspable in context. **No fix needed for "battery"; SHOULD-FIX**: the very first artillery mention ("country that swallows artillery," §the-plan) assumes the reader pictures cannons being hard to move through forest — a 3-word gloss ("artillery — the cannons") on first use would lock it in.

**`demonstration` / "make a loud demonstration"** — §the-plan ("Sedgwick would stay below Fredericksburg... and make a loud demonstration, pinning Lee in place"). **NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX.** "Demonstration" here is a military term of art meaning *a fake/noisy attack to hold the enemy's attention*, NOT its everyday meaning (a protest). The next clause ("pinning Lee in place and convincing him the blow was still coming") does explain the *purpose*, so a sharp reader recovers — but the word itself reads wrong on first contact. Either swap to plain language ("make a loud, faked threat") or gloss inline.

### SHOULD-FIX (NEEDS-GLOSS — recoverable but adds friction)

**`turning march` / `turning movement`** — appears in §the-plan FIG ("Hooker's turning march") and the OUTCOME ("Hooker's turning march was one of the best-conceived operations"). **NEEDS-GLOSS.** It's only in a figure caption and a summary line, and the body prose explains the maneuver ("slip around the side") well enough that a reader maps "turning march" onto it. Low risk, but if a caption is the *first* place a reader sees the term, gloss it once.

**`flanking column` / "the flanking column had concentrated"** — §the-plan ("By April 30 the flanking column had concentrated at a place called Chancellorsville"). **NEEDS-GLOSS.** "Concentrated" = gathered/massed in one place (military sense). Graspable, but "concentrated at a place" reads oddly to a newcomer. Minor.

**`holding force` / "small holding force"** — §lee-divides ("left a small holding force of... men under Jubal Early to watch Fredericksburg and hold off Sedgwick"). **CLEAR** — the same sentence says what it does ("to watch... and hold off"). No fix.

**`rear guard`** — §the-flank-march ("the 23rd Georgia, thrown out as a rear guard, fought a delaying action"). **CLEAR** — "fought a delaying action that bought Jackson the time he needed" defines it in place. No fix.

**`pickets`** — §the-flank-march/Meanwhile ("denied reinforcement after their own pickets reported the enemy massing"). **NEEDS-GLOSS.** First and only use of "pickets" (= forward lookout/sentry troops). A newcomer can guess from "reported the enemy massing," but it's an undefined term. One-word gloss or swap to "lookouts/scouts."

**`refuse the line` / "refuse the order"** — the watch-term `refuse the line` (a real maneuver: bending the end of your line back to protect a flank) does **NOT** appear. §the-flank-march has "no one with the authority to **refuse the order** had taken their reports seriously" — that's the ordinary English "refuse," not the maneuver, so **no jargon problem**. Flagging only because it was on the watch-list: CLEAR, no fix.

**`echelon` / `in detail`** — "echelon" does **not** appear (good). **`in detail`** appears once, §lee-divides OUTCOME ("the textbook recipe for getting beaten in detail, destroyed one piece at a time"). **CLEAR** — it's defined in the same breath ("destroyed one piece at a time"). Model behavior; no fix.

**`pontoon bridges`** — §hooker-quits ("pulled his corps back north across the Rappahannock over pontoon bridges at Banks' Ford"). **NEEDS-GLOSS (minor).** A newcomer may not know a pontoon bridge is a temporary floating bridge. Recoverable, low stakes.

**`pickets / green troops / "the Dutch"`** — "green troops" (§the-flank-march, "the nervousness of green troops") = inexperienced/new soldiers. **NEEDS-GLOSS (minor).** Common idiom; a sharp 15-year-old probably gets it, but it's worth a 2-word gloss. **"the Dutch" / "Flying Dutchmen"** IS explained well ("German-American regiments... half-dismissed as the 'Dutch'") — CLEAR, the slur and its unfairness are both spelled out.

**`artillerist`** — §jackson-falls ("Lee's gifted young artillerist"). **CLEAR** in context (the next clause has him rushing guns up a hill). No fix.

## 2. NAMED ACTORS — side-tag / scene-set

Side-tagging is **excellent and consistent** — every named officer carries (North)/(South) on first mention per section, per the war rule. The clarity gaps are about *who these people are*, not which side.

### MUST-FIX

None blocking — but see Sickles below.

### SHOULD-FIX

**Daniel Sickles (North)** — §the-flank-march ("Maj. Gen. Daniel Sickles (North), holding high ground at a place called Hazel Grove, spotted the column"). **NEEDS scene-set.** Sickles is introduced mid-action with no sense of who he commands (a corps? which?) — he just "holds high ground." Then in §jackson-falls he's central ("held by Sickles's men," "Hooker ordered Sickles off it"). Fine for plot, but the reader never learns he commands the III Corps or how he fits the army. Minor — his *role* (the guy on the key hill) is always clear even if his *command* isn't.

**Darius Couch (North)** — §jackson-falls ("he refused to hand command... over to his senior subordinate, Maj. Gen. Darius Couch"). **CLEAR** — "his senior subordinate" tells you exactly who he is and why he matters. Model intro.

**Carl Schurz (North)** — §the-flank-march ("Maj. Gen. Carl Schurz's division among them, managed a stand"). **CLEAR** — appears once, role obvious (a division inside the routed corps). No fix.

**Jedediah Hotchkiss & Charles C. Wellford (South)** — §lee-divides. **CLEAR and well-set** — both are given their jobs inline (Hotchkiss "the topographer whose careful maps were one of the Confederacy's quiet advantages"; Wellford "the proprietor of an iron furnace... who knew the local roads"). Strong.

**Jubal Early, Lafayette McLaws, Robert Rodes (South)** — each tagged and given a role in place. **CLEAR.**

**J.E.B. "Jeb" Stuart (South)** — §jackson-falls. **CLEAR and excellent** — "Lee's flamboyant cavalry chief... who had never in his life commanded infantry in a pitched battle" sets the stakes perfectly for a newcomer.

**E. Porter Alexander (South)** — §jackson-falls, "Lee's gifted young artillerist." **CLEAR.**

**Observation (no fix):** the draft tags people on every first mention but rarely re-cues a returning minor actor across sections. A newcomer reading straight through is fine because tags reset per section. CLEAR.

## 3. UNEXPLAINED LEAPS / ORIENTATION GAPS

### MUST-FIX

**Geography orientation — "behind," "in the rear," "two fires," east/west.** Throughout. **NEEDS-GLOSS → MUST-FIX as a set.** The whole narrative runs on spatial relationships — Hooker swings "upriver," comes "down behind Lee's left flank," Sedgwick is "below Fredericksburg," Jackson loops "south and then west," the XI Corps faces "south" with its "right end open to the west." A newcomer with no mental map is asked to track a lot of compass directions and "behind/in front/in the rear." The draft *leans hard* on the FIG maps to carry this (and the captions are detailed), so **this is only a MUST-FIX if the maps ship alongside the prose.** If maps are present, downgrade to CLEAR. Flagging because the prose alone, without the map, would leave a newcomer spatially lost by §the-flank-march. **Recommendation: confirm every spatial section has its map, and that the prose names match the map labels.**

**"caught between two fires" / "two fires"** — §the-plan ("Lee was caught between two fires") and §lee-divides OUTCOME. **NEEDS-GLOSS.** Idiom for "enemies on two sides." The §the-plan sentence does surround it with context ("a powerful army planted squarely behind Lee's position, with Sedgwick's force still threatening the front"), so it's recoverable — SHOULD-FIX, not blocking.

**The May 1 "flinch" — why is pulling back into the woods bad?** §lee-divides ("Then Hooker ordered his men to stop and pull back into the Wilderness, surrendering the open ground"). **CLEAR, but only because §the-plan set it up** ("the open ground where their numbers and their guns would finally count" / "two-to-one advantage was only an advantage in the open"). This is a genuine strength — the draft pre-loaded the "open ground = Hooker's advantage, woods = Lee's advantage" idea so the flinch lands. No fix; noting it as the model the other gaps should follow.

### SHOULD-FIX

**The "three pieces" math.** §the-flank-march ("Lee had cut his army into three pieces"). **NEEDS-GLOSS.** A newcomer has to have tracked: (1) Early's force left at Fredericksburg, (2) Lee's holding sliver in front of Hooker, (3) Jackson's flanking corps. The [I] arithmetic beat in §lee-divides lays this out well, but the "three pieces" payoff in the *next* section assumes the reader did that bookkeeping. A one-clause reminder ("Early at Fredericksburg, Lee's sliver in front, Jackson on the march — three pieces") would seal it. Minor.

**Two Fredericksburgs / "the previous December."** §the-plan references the December slaughter at Fredericksburg; §jackson-falls/Meanwhile has Sedgwick storming "the same stone wall and sunken road that had butchered the Union attackers the previous December... in the Second Battle of Fredericksburg." **CLEAR** — the callback is explicit and the "Second" is explained by the "same... December" cue. Good.

**"the new emancipation policy" / "the teeth of the new emancipation policy"** — §hooker-quits closing. **CLEAR** — the §the-plan Meanwhile sidebar established the Emancipation Proclamation, and the closing line references it. A straight-through reader is fine.

## 4. OVERWHELMING PASSAGES (density / too-much-at-once)

### SHOULD-FIX

**§the-plan, paragraph 2 (the plan itself)** — the single sentence "He sent three entire corps on a wide swing upriver: cross the Rappahannock far to the west at Kelly's Ford, then cross the Rapidan River... at Germanna and Ely's Fords, and come down behind Lee's left flank." **NEEDS-FIX (density).** This is FOUR place names (Kelly's Ford, Rapidan, Germanna, Ely's Fords) plus two rivers plus "left flank" plus "three corps" in one breath, all undefined, in the reader's first real exposure. It's the densest sentence in the draft and it lands before the reader knows what a corps or a flank is. **Recommend:** define corps/flank earlier, and/or let the map carry the four fords so the prose can say "crossed both rivers far upstream and came down behind Lee" without the gazetteer.

**§jackson-falls, the May 3 Hazel Grove / Fairview passage** — "Hazel Grove was commanding high open ground southwest of the crossroads... held by Sickles's men. On the morning of the third, Hooker ordered Sickles off it... Alexander immediately rushed thirty-odd guns up onto Hazel Grove and opened on the Union artillery posted at Fairview, the height just to the northeast." **SHOULD-FIX (density + 2 new place names).** Hazel Grove AND Fairview, plus the artillery-duel logic, plus the "Hooker gave it away" blunder, plus directions (southwest, northeast), arrive fast. It's *well-written* and each piece is defined ("commanding high open ground... one of the few places... where artillery could see and be massed") — but it's a lot of new geography in one paragraph. A newcomer gets the gist (Confederates took the good hill and won the gun fight) but may lose the Hazel-Grove-vs-Fairview distinction. Lean on the FIG map.

**§hooker-quits, the casualty/proportion passage** — "around 17,300 to the South's roughly 13,000. But Lee's 13,000 came out of an army half the size... something like a fifth of his entire force, against roughly an eighth of Hooker's." **CLEAR but dense.** The proportional argument (raw numbers favor calling it even, but as a *share* Lee bled far worse) is the most conceptually demanding moment in the draft. The author scaffolds it ("a far heavier share of a much smaller body of men") and it works — but it asks a newcomer to hold fractions of two different totals at once. Keep, but consider it the one spot where a sharp 15-year-old has to reread. SHOULD-FIX only if you want it bulletproof.

### CLEAR (notably well-paced)

- The §lee-divides cracker-box / flank-march reasoning is paced ideally — one idea per sentence, each term defined as it lands ("in the air," "roll the whole line up like a carpet").
- The §the-flank-march rout (deer and rabbits → trees come apart → rebel yell → flanked line can't fight back) is vivid and fully self-explaining.
- The Jackson wounding (§jackson-falls) is clean cause-and-effect a newcomer follows with zero background.
