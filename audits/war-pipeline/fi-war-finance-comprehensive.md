## How I judged this

This is a theme section whose declared spine is "one war debt, two fuses": the cost of winning fell west onto the Native nations (army + Proclamation Line) and east onto the colonists (Sugar/Currency/Stamp). The must-cover list is therefore the load-bearing beats of THAT argument — the causal chain from debt to revolution, with the Native nations as first-class actors, not the entire 1754–1776 textbook. I built my checklist independently from the historiography (debt → army → Proclamation/Pontiac → why-not-tax-England → Grenville's logic → the three acts → no-taxation-without-representation → colonial response → repeal + Declaratory Act → the union throughline) and then checked the draft against it.

## CANDIDATE ADDS (assessed, not all warranted)

### SHOULD-FIX — Quartering Act of 1765 (THIN / arguably MISSING but not essential)
The **Quartering Act (royal assent 24 May 1765)** is the one beat genuinely absent from the draft that has a real claim to belong. It is the direct fiscal bridge between the draft's two halves: the standing army of §"An army that wouldn't go home" landed its upkeep bill **directly on the colonists** by requiring colonial assemblies to house and supply the troops. It passed in the same session as the Stamp Act, it fused the "standing army = tyranny" fear (§24-line) with the "taxation without consent" grievance, and it became Grievance 13 of the Declaration of Independence ("For quartering large bodies of armed troops among us"). In a section whose thesis is "the bill for that same army, handed to people who had no vote," the army's *other* bill is a conspicuous gap.

I am NOT calling this MISSING-essential (and therefore not a FAIL) because: (a) the section is explicitly SELECTIVE and storytelling-first; (b) the army-cost fuse is already fully established; and (c) the Quartering Act's real teeth (the New York Restraining Act) come in 1767, just outside this window. But it is the single highest-value add and would cost one short paragraph in §"An army that wouldn't go home." **It needs new facts → fact pack first** (the fact pack does not cover it at all — see blind spots below).

### CONSIDERED AND DECLINED (do NOT add — would gold-plate)
- **Writs of assistance / Otis's 1761 writs case** — tempting as the seed of the search-and-seizure grievance, but the draft already carries warrantless-search outrage via the Cyder Act and vice-admiralty courts; adding Otis's earlier case would dilute, not sharpen. Decline.
- **Paxton Boys / frontier vigilantism (1764)** — a real consequence of the Proclamation-line tension but a different story; out of scope for a finance theme. Decline.
- **Full Seven Years' War global cost / Pitt's subsidies to Prussia** — the draft rightly keeps the lens on Britain's debt as the colonists experienced it. Decline.
- **Specific colonial assemblies' resolves (Virginia Resolves, Patrick Henry)** — would be a worthy storytelling beat, but the Stamp Act Congress + Sons of Liberty already carry the "colonies answer" load; adding state-level resolves risks list-creep. Decline (optional flavor only).

## BLIND SPOTS IN THE FACT PACK ITSELF
1. **Quartering Act of 1765 is entirely absent from the fact pack.** Given the section's army-cost thesis, this is a real gap in the source material, not just the draft. If the writer adds the beat above, the fact pack must first be extended (royal assent date, what it required of assemblies, that it was perceived as a backdoor tax / standing-army threat, and that it became a Declaration grievance). Born-verified, no asserting from memory.
2. **The fact pack does not connect the standing-army upkeep to the colonists' purse at all** — it treats the army purely as a Crown expense and a tyranny-symbol. The Quartering Act is the missing causal link; the pack's "two fuses" logic (§9) is slightly incomplete without it.

These are the only fact-pack gaps that bear on comprehensiveness. Everything else the draft needed was in the pack and used faithfully.
