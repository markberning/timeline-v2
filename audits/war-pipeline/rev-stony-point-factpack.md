# FACT PACK — Battle of Stony Point, July 16, 1779

*American Revolution (Hudson Highlands, 1779). ONLY source for the battle page. The midnight bayonet battle: Wayne's picked Corps of Light Infantry stormed a fortified rock the British called impregnable, with unloaded muskets, in 25 minutes — and then, with every Paoli grievance in their hands, took ~500 prisoners without a massacre. The restraint is the meaning-beat. Confidence tags: HIGH / MED / DISPUTED; HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags inline. Apocrypha blacklist at the end — Stony Point is one of the most myth-encrusted small battles of the war (Sheehan, "The Mythology of Stony Point," Journal of the American Revolution, 2016, is the standing debunk source).*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **Battle of Stony Point, night of July 15-16, 1779** (assault began around midnight; the fort was taken by about 1 a.m. on the 16th) — Stony Point, New York, a rocky peninsula on the west bank of the Hudson about 30 miles north of Manhattan, opposite Verplanck's Point (the two together commanded King's Ferry). Today: Stony Point Battlefield State Historic Site, ~41.242,-73.972. [HIGH]
- **Result: American victory.** The fort, its garrison, and its guns were all taken. [HIGH]
- **The premise [HIGH]:** After Monmouth (June 1778) the war in the north had gone static — Clinton in New York City, Washington watching him from the Highlands, neither able to force a decisive battle. In late May/June 1779 Clinton lunged 40 miles up the Hudson and seized both ends of King's Ferry (Stony Point + Verplanck's Point), the river crossing that tied New England to the mid-Atlantic states, hoping to threaten West Point and lure Washington into the open. Washington refused the bait — and instead answered with a scalpel: a hand-picked elite corps, a single midnight bayonet assault, no artillery preparation, no loaded muskets.
- **Signature elements [HIGH]:** (1) **bayonets only** — Washington's and Wayne's orders sent the two assault columns in with muskets UNLOADED, so no nervous shot could give the surprise away; only Murfree's two-company diversion in the center fired; (2) the **forlorn hope** — 20-man volunteer parties with axemen hacking lanes through two abatis lines ahead of each column; (3) the **watchword "The fort's our own!"**, to be shouted only once inside the works; (4) **Wayne's head wound** at the inner abatis and the night-march drama; (5) the **restraint**: the same army that had been bayoneted at Paoli and Old Tappan took the fort at bayonet point and then gave quarter — contemporaries on BOTH sides remarked on it.
- **The meaning-beat [HIGH, well documented]:** Wayne's own report singled out "the humanity of our brave Soldiery who scorned to take the lives of a vanquished foe, calling for mercy"; British Commodore George Collier's journal conceded the attackers "showed at this moment a generosity and clemency which during the course of the Rebellion had no parallel." Write the discipline, not melodrama: roughly 470-540 prisoners taken by men with bayonets in their hands and Paoli in their memory, and the official British dead numbered about 20.
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — strategic weight:** Stony Point did not change the strategic balance (Washington abandoned the point within days; the British reoccupied it on the 19th). Its real effects were morale, prestige (celebrated across the states and in Congress: a gold medal and two silver medals), the wreck of Clinton's hope of luring Washington into a mistake, and proof that Steuben-drilled Continentals could execute the war's most demanding infantry task. Do NOT inflate it into a turning point.

## 2. BACKGROUND
- **The stalemate [HIGH]:** After Monmouth (June 28, 1778) the main armies fought no general battle in the north for the rest of the war. Clinton held New York City; Washington's army ringed it from New Jersey and the Hudson Highlands. British strategy was shifting south (Savannah taken December 1778); in the north Clinton probed for a cheap way to hurt Washington or force him to fight.
- **Why the Hudson mattered [HIGH]:** the Hudson was the seam of the Revolution — if the British controlled it they could split New England from the rest of the states. The American answer was the fortress complex at **West Point** (begun 1778), with the **great iron chain** stretched across the river to stop ships. **King's Ferry** — the crossing between Stony Point (west bank) and Verplanck's Point (east bank) — was the southernmost safe crossing, the shortcut for men, mail, and supplies between New England and the mid-Atlantic; losing it forced a detour of dozens of miles upriver through the mountains. [HIGH on all; "about 60 miles of detour" appears in some accounts — use "a long detour through the Highlands" rather than a precise figure]
- **Clinton takes King's Ferry, May 30 - June 1, 1779 [HIGH]:** Clinton came up the Hudson with about 6,000 men and a naval squadron (Commodore George Collier). The unfinished American blockhouse on Stony Point was burned and abandoned without a fight (May 31 - June 1); the small garrison of Fort Lafayette on Verplanck's Point (~70 men) surrendered June 1. Clinton fortified both points, garrisoned them, and waited — West Point was 12 miles upriver, and his real hope was that Washington would come down and fight for the ferry. Washington instead shifted his army to cover West Point (headquarters at New Windsor; the army at Smith's Clove and the Highlands forts) and started planning a counterstroke. [HIGH; the ~70-man Fort Lafayette garrison figure MED-HIGH]
- **Tryon's Connecticut raids [HIGH on the raids; MED on the causal link]:** in early July Clinton sent Maj. Gen. William Tryon with ~2,600 men to burn and plunder the Connecticut coast (New Haven July 5, Fairfield July 8, Norwalk July 11) — partly punitive, partly more bait to pull Washington east. Washington again refused to move the army; instead Stony Point fell while the raiders were still at work. The standard account: news of Stony Point led Clinton to recall the raiders and abandon plans for further coastal strikes (New London was widely believed next). Frame the recall as "Clinton broke off the raiding campaign" rather than a precise causal mechanism. [Causal link MED — common in the literature but Clinton had several reasons]
- **The fort ("little Gibraltar") [HIGH on the ground; the nickname MED-HIGH]:** Stony Point is a rocky promontory rising about 150 feet from the river, surrounded by water on three sides; on the landward (west) side a tidal marsh nearly cut it off, crossed by a causeway. At high tide the flats flooded. The British works were field fortifications, not a masonry fort: gun batteries (about 15 pieces — cannon, mortars, howitzers), earthen fleches, and **two lines of abatis** (felled trees, sharpened branches outward) belting the slope. A Royal Navy armed sloop (the *Vulture*) and a gunboat covered the river approaches. British engineers and officers considered it close to impregnable; "little Gibraltar" is the nickname attached to it in the literature (attested in secondary sources including the NY State Parks site history — do not put the phrase in a specific officer's mouth). [HIGH on terrain/abatis/guns; gun count "about 15" HIGH per official returns]
- **The garrison [HIGH]:** Lt. Col. **Henry Johnson** of the 17th Regiment of Foot commanded roughly **600-750 men**: the 17th Foot (the core), the grenadier company of the 71st (Fraser's Highlanders), a company-strength detachment of the Loyal American Regiment (Loyalists), and Royal Artillery detachments. [HIGH on composition; strength is given as ~600 (American Battlefield Trust) to ~750 (Wikipedia/Boatner-derived) — use "about 600-700" or "roughly 700"]
- **Washington's preparation [HIGH]:** Washington reconnoitered the point himself (from Buckberg Mountain, by standard accounts), gathered intelligence through July (deserters, local informants, and Capt. Allan McLane, who got inside the works under a flag of truce escorting a local woman — McLane's visit is well attested [MED-HIGH]), and gave Wayne a written plan that fixed the essentials: a night assault, bayonets, secrecy, columns on the flanks where the water covered the approaches. Wayne reconnoitered with his officers on July 15. The plan of attack enclosure is in the Washington Papers (Founders Online, "Enclosure: Plan of Attack, c.15 July 1779"). [HIGH]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
### Strengths (dossier stats)
- **American: ~1,200-1,350 engaged** from the Corps of Light Infantry (~1,500 total strength; figures vary by source — Wikipedia gives ~1,350 in the assault, American Battlefield Trust ~1,200). [HIGH on the range]
- **British: ~600-750** in garrison. [HIGH on the range]
- **Duration:** the assault itself took about **25-30 minutes** from first contact to the watchword in the works; columns stepped off from Springsteel's farm ~11:30 p.m., attack ~midnight-12:30 a.m., fort secured by ~1 a.m. Wayne's famous dispatch is time-dated 2 o'clock A.M. [HIGH]

### The Corps of Light Infantry [HIGH]
- Formed in (re-formed for) the 1779 campaign season from the **picked light companies of the Continental line** — by design the army's elite, young, agile, vetted men, drilled to Baron von Steuben's standard (the 1779 *Regulations* — Steuben's drill is exactly what a silent night bayonet assault tests). Wayne took command of the corps July 1, 1779. Four regiments:
  1. **1st Regiment — Col. Christian Febiger** (Virginia + Pennsylvania companies)
  2. **2nd Regiment — Col. Richard Butler** (Pennsylvania + Maryland companies)
  3. **3rd Regiment — Col. Return Jonathan Meigs** (Connecticut companies)
  4. **4th Regiment — Maj. William Hull** (Massachusetts + North Carolina detachment)
- [HIGH on the four-regiment structure and commanders; "formed July 15" appears in one source vs. Wayne assuming command July 1 — say the corps was assembled in early-mid July under Wayne]

### The plan [HIGH]
- **Two bayonet columns + one firing diversion:**
  - **South column (main attack), Wayne with it:** Febiger's 1st Regt + Meigs's 3rd + Hull's detachment — around the southern flank, across the flooded sand flats (men waded water reported 2-4 feet deep).
  - **North column: Col. Richard Butler** with the 2nd Regt (~300) — around the northern flank along the river.
  - **Center diversion: Maj. Hardy Murfree's two North Carolina companies** — the ONLY men with loaded muskets, to open a noisy fire at the causeway/center and fix the garrison's attention. It worked: Johnson led a large part of the 17th Foot (accounts say roughly half the garrison's infantry) down toward the noise and was cut off when the flanks broke in. [HIGH on the diversion and that Johnson marched toward it; "half the garrison" MED]
  - Each assault column was led by a **van** (Fleury led the south column's advance party of ~150; Maj. John Stewart led the north column's van) preceded by a **20-man forlorn hope** with axemen to cut through the two abatis lines (Lt. George Knox led the south forlorn hope, Lt. James Gibbon the north — Gibbon's party is the one famously shredded, 17 of 20 hit by most accounts). [HIGH on vans/forlorn hope structure and Fleury/Stewart; names of the two lieutenants and the 17-of-20 figure MED — standard in the literature, verify wording at draft time]
  - **Unloaded muskets, fixed bayonets** for both assault columns — explicit in the orders, so that no accidental shot could trigger the garrison; men who fired or "hallooed" were threatened with death (one officer was court-martialed after the battle for hallooing — the only documented shouting). [HIGH]
  - **White paper in hats** as the night recognition mark; watchword **"The fort's our own!"** to be raised once inside the works. [HIGH]
  - **Rewards posted in advance:** $500 to the first man into the works, then $400/$300/$200/$100 for the next four. [HIGH — in Wayne's orders; the order of entry was later officially adjudicated]
  - **Security on the march [HIGH, and a myth-correction]:** the corps marched ~13-14 miles from Sandy Beach (near Fort Montgomery) starting ~noon July 15, by inland tracks, to Springsteel's farm ~1.5 miles west of the point; Wayne ordered Capt. James Chrystie to **detain the male inhabitants** along the route so no one could carry word to the garrison. The famous "they bayoneted every dog in the neighborhood" detail is a Lossing-era embellishment — see APOCRYPHA. No soldier was told the objective until the halt. [HIGH on march/detention/secrecy; distances MED]

### American commander bios (60-90w material, verified)
- **Anthony Wayne (1745-1796) [HIGH]:** Pennsylvania tanner's son and surveyor turned brigadier; his division was bayoneted in the dark at Paoli (September 1777) — a court of inquiry and then a court-martial at his own demand cleared him "with the highest honor." Given the new Corps of Light Infantry on July 1, 1779. At Stony Point he marched with the south column, was grazed in the scalp by a musket ball at the inner abatis, and entered the fort with his men. His 2 a.m. dispatch to Washington is the battle in two sentences. ("Mad Anthony" — the nickname dates from later in the war; use sparingly.)
- **François-Louis Teissèdre de Fleury (1749-c. 1799) [HIGH on Stony Point role]:** French career engineer-officer volunteering with the Continentals since 1777 (already cited for gallantry at Fort Mifflin). At Stony Point he led the south column's 150-man van and was officially adjudged **first man into the works**, personally striking the British colors. Congress voted him a silver medal — the first Congressional medal actually struck (in Paris, by Duvivier), and the only one of the war's Comitia Americana medals given to a foreign junior officer. He returned to French service and was at Yorktown with Rochambeau's army [MED on Yorktown detail].
- **Christian Febiger (1746-1796) [HIGH]:** Danish-born ("Old Denmark"), a Bunker Hill and Quebec veteran, colonel of the 1st light regiment, in the thick of the south column's assault. (One standard account has Febiger briefly steadying the column when Wayne went down [MED].)
- **Richard Butler (1743-1791) [HIGH]:** Irish-born Pennsylvania frontier officer; led the north column, which cut through the abatis with light losses and met the south column at the summit. Later killed at St. Clair's Defeat (1791).
- **Maj. Hardy Murfree (1752-1809) [HIGH]:** North Carolina major whose two companies fired the only American shots of the night — the diversion that pulled Johnson and much of the 17th Foot out of position. Murfreesboro, North Carolina (and via his family, Murfreesboro, Tennessee) carries his name.
- **Maj. John Stewart (Maryland) [HIGH on role]:** led the north column's van; voted the war's other Stony Point silver medal alongside Fleury. [Biographical details beyond this MED — keep to the role + medal]
- **Maj. William Hull (1753-1825) [HIGH]:** Massachusetts major commanding the 4th regiment's detachment in the south column; thirty-three years later, as a general, he would surrender Detroit in 1812 — usable as an aside only if the page wants the irony; otherwise skip.
- **(Context) George Washington [HIGH]:** author of the operation's concept and its restraints; watched from New Windsor; rode down to inspect the captured works July 17.

### British commander bios
- **Lt. Col. Henry Johnson (1748-1835) [HIGH]:** Irish-born career officer of the 17th Foot, ~30 years old, commanding the Stony Point garrison. Murfree's firing drew him with a large part of the 17th toward the causeway; by the time he turned back the flag was down and the watchword was being shouted behind him. Taken prisoner with the garrison. After exchange he demanded a court-martial to clear his conduct (he testified February 19, 1781) and his career survived — he died a full general and baronet. [HIGH on capture + later career; the court-martial verdict itself: clearing/acquittal is the standard account but tag MED — say "a court-martial examined the loss and his career went on," don't assert verdict wording]
- **(Context) Sir Henry Clinton (1730-1795) [HIGH]:** British commander-in-chief; the King's Ferry seizure and the Connecticut raids were his attempt to pry Washington out of the Highlands. Stony Point cost him the campaign's only trophy and any momentum; he reoccupied the point on July 19 but evacuated both points that October. (In-repo portrait `rev-longisland-clinton.jpg`.)
- **(Naval context) Commodore Sir George Collier [HIGH]:** commanded the naval side of the May-June operation; his journal supplies the British tribute to American clemency (see Quotes).

## 4. THE BATTLE
### The approach [HIGH]
- The corps left Sandy Beach (near Fort Montgomery, ~13-14 miles north) about noon on July 15, marched single file by rough inland tracks behind the Dunderberg, and assembled at Springsteel's farm ~1.5 miles west of the point by ~8 p.m. Officers learned the objective there; the men later. Detachments detained civilians along the route. About 11:30 p.m. the columns moved out. The night was dark and windy; the weather had forced the *Vulture* and the gunboat off their close stations [MED-HIGH on the ships' absence aiding surprise — in standard accounts].
### The assault (~midnight - 1 a.m.) [HIGH]
- The south column hit water deeper than expected (2-4 feet over the flooded flats) and lost time; British pickets fired the alarm; the works opened with cannon and musketry on both flanks while Murfree's companies blazed away noisily at the center. Johnson personally led several companies of the 17th down toward Murfree's fire — the diversion swallowed the garrison's reserve.
- The forlorn hopes and axemen cut through both abatis under fire (Gibbon's 20-man party on the north losing 17 men by the standard count [MED]). The columns never stopped to fire — they couldn't — and went over the works with the bayonet. **Fleury was first in and struck the colors**; Lt. George Knox, then Sgts. Baker, Spencer, and Dunlop followed (the order was officially settled for the cash rewards) [HIGH on Fleury; MED on the exact list spelling — sources give Baker/Spencer/Dunlop with variant spellings].
- **Wayne's wound [HIGH on the wound]:** near the inner abatis a musket ball grazed Wayne's scalp and knocked him down, briefly stunning and bloodying him; he was helped up and went on into the fort with the column. The theatrical line — "Carry me into the fort; if I am to die, I want to die at the head of the column" — is a 19th-century-transmitted tradition, NOT in Wayne's report or any contemporary account found this session; see DISPUTED. The wound itself is in Wayne's own July 17 report.
- The two columns met inside; the watchword went up; resistance collapsed. The fighting lasted about 25-30 minutes. The garrison asked quarter and got it — "Mercy!" cried and granted is in multiple participant-derived accounts. [HIGH]
### The restraint (the meaning-beat) [HIGH — document it exactly this way]
- The Continentals had every license contemporaries recognized for storming a defended work at night, and two festering grievances besides: Paoli (Wayne's own men bayoneted in the dark, September 1777) and the Baylor/Old Tappan affair (September 1778). They killed about 20 of the garrison and took ~470-540 prisoners, including the wounded, who were collected and treated. Wayne's report to Washington made the clemency the headline (Quote 2). Collier's journal — the enemy's own pen — called it "a generosity and clemency which during the course of the Rebellion had no parallel" (Quote 3). Frame as discipline + deliberate moral contrast, not softness: the column had been ordered to use the bayonet and did; it stopped when resistance stopped.
- HANDLE-WITH-CARE: do NOT write that "not one British soldier was bayoneted after surrendering" or similar absolutes — the sources support "no massacre, quarter given, remarked on by both sides," not a forensic zero. Also do not stage anyone shouting "Remember Paoli!" — no witness reports it (see APOCRYPHA).
### Verplanck's Point fails [HIGH]
- The plan's second half — Maj. Gen. Robert Howe moving on Verplanck's Point across the river — miscarried: orders and siege guns went astray, the British garrison (Lt. Col. Webster) held, reinforcements came up from New York, and Howe withdrew by July 18. King's Ferry's east bank stayed British. [HIGH on the failure; detail level MED — keep brief]
- Captured guns were turned on Verplanck's and on the *Vulture* during the 16th [MED-HIGH].

## 5. CASUALTIES (dossier ranges)
- **American [HIGH]:** **15 killed, 83 wounded** (~98 total) of ~1,200-1,350 engaged. A large share fell in the forlorn hopes.
- **British [HIGH on the official figures]:** official return ~**20 killed**, 74 wounded (taken prisoner), **472 unwounded prisoners**, ~58 listed missing (some likely drowned swimming to the *Vulture*) — total prisoners ~472-546 depending on whether the wounded are counted. American/patriot claims of 63 British killed circulated; Boatner and modern accounts accept the official ~20. USE: "about 20 killed (American claims ran as high as 63) and roughly 500 prisoners" or the range 470-540.
- ~1 British sergeant killed and ~20 wounded in a failed prisoner break on July 17 [MED — Wikipedia-sourced; omit unless wanted].
- Prisoners were marched to Easton, Pennsylvania. [HIGH]

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **Washington abandons the point (July 18) [HIGH]:** Washington came down on the 17th, looked at the ground, and decided what he had suspected: holding Stony Point would tie down 1,500+ men under the guns of the Royal Navy, exposed exactly as Johnson had been. The captured artillery (~15 pieces) and stores were hauled off, the works razed, and the army withdrew on July 18. The British (Brig. Gen. Thomas Stirling's brigade) reoccupied the point July 19 and refortified it (Capt. Patrick Ferguson designing new works) — then **Clinton evacuated both Stony Point and Verplanck's Point on October 21-22, 1779**, pulling in his lines as the war's center of gravity moved south (the Charleston expedition sailed that December). King's Ferry came back into American use for good. So the raid's logic held: take the trophy, keep nothing the enemy could besiege. [HIGH; evacuation date HIGH per Sheehan, JAR "The Second Occupation"]
- **The honors [HIGH]:** Congress (resolutions of July 26, 1779) voted thanks to Wayne and the corps, a **gold medal to Wayne**, and **silver medals to Fleury and Stewart** — three of only about a dozen Congressional medals of the entire war, and the only battle that produced three. Fleury's was the first struck (Paris, by Pierre-Simon Duvivier) and the only one to a foreign officer; Wayne's gold medal was not actually fabricated and delivered until 1790. Congress also ordered the **value of the captured stores divided among the troops as prize money** — the stores and ordnance were appraised at roughly $150,000-180,000 (Continental dollars; sources vary — use "more than $150,000" hedged), shared out by rank. [HIGH on medals + division of stores; appraisal figure MED — varies by source]
- **Morale + propaganda [HIGH]:** celebrated throughout the states; Washington's covering letter to Congress praised the operation; for the Continental Army it was proof-of-arm that the Steuben-drilled line could execute the hardest mission in the eighteenth-century manual — a silent night escalade — better than the British had at Paoli, and more humanely. British officers were stung (London and New York commentary treated the loss as an embarrassment; Johnson sought a court-martial to clear himself).
- **The copycat [HIGH]:** one month later (August 19, 1779) Maj. Henry Lee's similar midnight bayonet raid took the British post at **Paulus Hook**, New Jersey — explicitly in the Stony Point pattern. Good closing beat for "what it meant."
- **Effect on Clinton [HIGH at this framing level]:** the Hudson thrust ended with nothing — Washington never offered battle, West Point was untouched and stronger every month, the Connecticut raids were broken off, and in October Clinton gave up King's Ferry itself. 1779 in the north closed as it opened: stalemate, with the initiative draining south.
- **The ground today [HIGH]:** Stony Point Battlefield State Historic Site (NY State Parks), with the **Stony Point Lighthouse (1826) — the oldest lighthouse on the Hudson** — standing on the battlefield. Good "still there" beat; modern photos on Commons are CC BY-SA.

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE
1. **"Carry me into the fort! …die at the head of the column." [DISPUTED — tradition, not record]:** Wayne's grazing head wound is documented (his own report; HIGH). The dramatic order is not in the report, not in any participant account located this session, and Wikiquote carries it citation-needed; it surfaces in 19th-century biography/tradition (often via aides'-family transmission). SAFE FRAMING: "Tradition has him gasping to be carried into the fort at the head of his column; what the record shows is that minutes later he was inside it, writing to Washington at 2 a.m. with a bandaged head." Never quote it as verbatim speech.
2. **Garrison & prisoner totals:** garrison ~600 (ABT) to ~750 (Boatner/Wikipedia); prisoners 472 unwounded + 74 wounded ≈ 546, vs "over 500" / "~470-540" in various accounts. USE ranges; never one bare precise number.
3. **British killed:** official ~20 vs patriot-claimed 63. Boatner accepts ~20. Give the official figure, optionally noting the inflated American claim — the LOW number is what makes the restraint story true; do not use 63.
4. **The dogs [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — mostly myth]:** the oft-repeated order that every dog near the line of march be bayoneted/killed traces to Lossing (1850, unsourced); Sheehan (JAR 2016) finds no order or correspondence supporting it — only a vague hearsay line in Collier's diary ("it was said…"). What IS documented: Wayne had the male inhabitants along the route detained (Capt. Chrystie's task). Tell the detention, drop the dog-slaughter, or at most: "the story that they killed every dog on the road is probably embellishment."
5. **"Little Gibraltar":** standard nickname in the literature and at the state historic site, but don't attribute it to a named contemporary.
6. **Who took command when Wayne fell:** one tertiary account has Febiger assuming command; mostly Wayne stays effectively in command (he entered the fort and wrote the 2 a.m. dispatch). Keep Wayne's agency; at most "his officers carried the moment."
7. **Johnson's court-martial outcome:** that he was exchanged, demanded a court-martial (testified Feb. 19, 1781), and that his career flourished afterward (later full general, baronet) is solid; the precise verdict language was not verified this session — say the court examined the loss and his career went on.
8. **Stores appraisal:** "$150,000" (Congress' assessment per some accounts) vs ">$160,000" vs "$180,654" (Johnston 1900-derived) — all in Continental currency. Use "more than $150,000 in captured guns and stores, divided among the corps as prize money."
9. **Numbers in Murfree's diversion's success:** "Johnson led half the garrison toward the firing" is standard but imprecise — say "several companies of the 17th Foot, a large part of his infantry."

## APOCRYPHA BLACKLIST (never use; all per Sheehan, "The Mythology of Stony Point," JAR 2016, unless noted)
1. **Pompey Lamb, the strawberry-selling enslaved spy** who supposedly learned the British countersign and got the columns past the sentries. 19th-century origin (popularized by Lossing, 1850). The evidence against: no British or American source mentions him before, during, or after the battle; the only "Pomp Lamb" record is a militia list compiled in the early 1900s; the British took the point May 31 (not strawberry season); and Ensign Henry Hamilton testified at Johnson's court-martial that **"Lt. Col. Johnson would not allow of a Countersign, either the Night of the Attack or any other."** The Stony Point site's own senior historian (Sheehan) calls the story a myth, while allowing the man may have existed. If the page addresses it at all, address it AS a legend with this evidence — it is a teachable case of how Revolution memory got written; never present it as fact.
2. **"General, if you only plan it, I'll storm Hell!" / "Better try Stony Point first."** No documentation in the Washington-Wayne correspondence; circulates from Washington Irving-era tradition and Mexican-War-era newspapers. Blacklisted as quotation.
3. **"Remember Paoli!" shouted during the assault.** No witness on either side reports it; the orders demanded strict silence, and the only documented shout was the prescribed watchword (one officer was court-martialed for "hallooing"). The Paoli CONTRAST is real and documented (use Wayne's humanity line + Collier); the battle-cry is not.
4. **Peter Francisco second-man-in stories.** His 1820 pension petition claims it; the adjudicated first-five list (Fleury, Knox, Baker, Spencer, Dunlop) — fixed at the time for the cash awards — does not include him.
5. **The Haverstraw "pay tree"** (Washington personally paying the troops under a walnut tree, marker erected 2010). Logistically false; the corps returned north and pay/prize money moved through channels.
6. **"No quarter asked, none given" / heaps of bayoneted dead** — any framing that inflates British dead or makes the assault a slaughter inverts the battle's documented meaning. ~20 killed, ~500 prisoners, quarter given.
7. **Dog-slaughter order** as fact — see HANDLE-WITH-CARE #4.
8. **"Not a shot was fired by the Americans"** absolute — Murfree's two companies fired plenty (that was the plan); the ASSAULT columns went in unloaded. Phrase it precisely.

## 10. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)
- **Date:** night of July 15-16, 1779 (assault ~midnight; fort taken by ~1 a.m.) · **Where:** Stony Point, New York — west bank of the Hudson at King's Ferry, ~12 miles below West Point
- **Forces:** ~1,200-1,350 American light infantry vs ~600-700 British garrison
- **Duration:** ~25-30 minutes of fighting after a 13-mile approach march
- **Casualties:** American 15 killed, 83 wounded; British ~20 killed (American claims ran higher), ~470-540 captured including wounded
- **Winner line material:** American victory — the army's picked light infantry took "little Gibraltar" at midnight with unloaded muskets and the bayonet, then gave the surrendering garrison the quarter the British had denied at Paoli. Washington kept the prisoners and the guns, razed the works, and walked away three days later: the point was never the prize. The proof was. (Three Congressional medals — Wayne's gold, silver for Fleury and Stewart — out of only about a dozen voted in the entire war.)
