## Scope / dedup note

This is the **operational / strategic spine** of 1864 — the connective middle that
ties the year's ~12 major battles into one war with a plan. It is **NOT** a tactical
retelling (the battle dossiers do that) and it is **NOT** the social/home-front story
(the "Off the Battlefield" theme sections do that). The angle here is: **the logic** —
how Grant changed the war's underlying strategy, how each win/loss reshaped the plan,
and how supply / manpower / casualties became a *system* the North could win and the
South could not. It LINKS DOWN to dossiers and themes rather than re-narrating them.

**The chapter thesis (the spine to hold every section against):** When Grant became
general-in-chief in March 1864 he stopped fighting the theatres as separate wars. He
ordered all the Union armies to advance *at the same time*, so the South — with its
smaller army and shrinking supply — could no longer shuttle troops from a quiet front
to a hot one. The plan was to grind the Confederate armies down until they could not
replace their losses. And in 1864 the battlefield became a *political* objective: the
war's outcome, and Lincoln's re-election, rode on it.

## §1 — "One war, not five": Grant takes command and rewrites the logic

**The promotion.**
- Congress revived the rank of **lieutenant general** (held in full grade before only by
  **George Washington**; Winfield Scott held it by brevet). Lincoln (North) signed Grant's
  promotion **March 2, 1864**; **Ulysses S. Grant (North)** arrived in Washington **March 8**
  and was formally commissioned by Lincoln at a Cabinet meeting **March 9, 1864**, becoming
  general-in-chief — **command of all the Union armies**. [Wikipedia: Ulysses S. Grant]
- **William T. Sherman (North)** succeeded Grant in command of all the western armies.
  [Wikipedia: Ulysses S. Grant]

**The new idea — simultaneous advance.** The earlier war had been fought piecemeal: one
army moved while the others sat, so the Confederacy could use its **interior lines** (shorter
internal routes) to rush troops from a quiet front to a threatened one. Grant's plan was to
make every Union army advance **at the same time**, so the South could never again concentrate
against one threat at a time. [historynet/NIU; Wikipedia: Overland Campaign]

**The five coordinated advances of spring 1864:**
1. **Grant + Meade (North) vs. Lee (South) in Virginia.** Grant traveled with **George G.
   Meade's (North)** Army of the Potomac for a direct overland drive against **Robert E. Lee's
   (South)** Army of Northern Virginia. [Wikipedia: Overland Campaign]
2. **Sherman (North) vs. Johnston (South) → Atlanta.** Sherman to destroy **Joseph E.
   Johnston's (South)** Army of Tennessee and take Atlanta. [Wikipedia: Atlanta campaign]
3. **Sigel (North) → the Shenandoah Valley** (the South's breadbasket and a rail corridor).
   [Wikipedia: Valley campaigns of 1864]
4. **Butler (North) → up the James River** against Richmond from the southeast (the Army of
   the James). [Wikipedia: Bermuda Hundred campaign]
5. **Banks (North) → Mobile / the Gulf** (in practice diverted into the Red River expedition).
   [Wikipedia: Red River campaign]

**The defining quote — and a correction.** The famous homespun line — *"Those not skinning
can hold a leg"* — is **Lincoln's (North)**, his approval of Grant's all-fronts plan (a western
butchering proverb: even the men not doing the skinning are useful if they hold the animal
down). It is NOT Grant's. Lincoln used it to bless the idea that armies which advance hold
territory as well as armies that sit still. [erenow/*The Presidents' War*; Lincoln Wonk]
> **[VERIFY exact wording in prose] — "Those not skinning can hold a leg." — Lincoln (North)**
> (well attested as Lincoln's characterization of Grant's coordinated-advance plan; confirm
> phrasing against a primary/standard source before quoting).

**The Lincoln–Grant relationship as a command system.** Lincoln "allowed Grant to devise his
own strategy" — the first time the President had a general-in-chief he could trust to run the
whole war, ending the meddle-and-relieve cycle of 1861–63. [Wikipedia: Ulysses S. Grant]

**NAME THE CAUSE seed.** By 1864 the Union's ability to keep advancing on all fronts rests on
a manpower base the Confederacy can't match — and a large and growing share of that base is
Black: ~179,000+ men of the **U.S. Colored Troops**, most of them formerly enslaved, now in
Union uniform as white enlistment flags. The war Grant is grinding forward is, by now,
explicitly a war to destroy slavery, fought in growing part by the people slavery was built on.
[LOC: African-American Soldiers; National Archives] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/usct` and `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/emancipation`.

**People (with side):** Grant (North), Lincoln (North), Sherman (North), Meade (North),
Lee (South), Johnston (South), Sigel (North), Butler (North), Banks (North).

## §2 — The Overland Campaign: attrition as a decision

**The frame.** The Overland Campaign ran **May 4 – June 24, 1864**, in Virginia — a near-
continuous, six-week running battle, not a sequence of separate fights. Sequence: **the
Wilderness** (May 5–7) → **Spotsylvania Court House** (May 8–21) → **North Anna** (May 23–26)
→ **Cold Harbor** (May 31 – June 12). [Wikipedia: Overland Campaign]

**Forces.** Union ~103,875–124,232 present for duty; Confederate ~60,000–65,000. The Union
army was nearly twice Lee's — and *that* is the strategy: Grant meant to "hammer continuously
against the armed force of the enemy" knowing the North could replace losses and the South
could not. [Wikipedia: Overland Campaign]

**The decision that defines the chapter (the Wilderness, night of May 7).** After two days of
blind, burning slaughter in the Wilderness thicket — a tactical check, the kind that had sent
every previous Union commander retreating back across the Rapidan — **Grant did NOT turn back.**
He ordered the army *south*, around Lee's right, toward Spotsylvania. Veterans who expected the
old retreat realized they were still advancing. This is the hinge of the whole chapter: a bloody
repulse no longer meant retreat. [Wikipedia: Overland Campaign] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/eastern/wilderness`.

**Spotsylvania and the resolve telegram.** At Spotsylvania (the Bloody Angle / "Mule Shoe," some
of the war's most savage hand-to-hand fighting), Grant wrote to Army chief of staff **Henry
Halleck (North)** on **May 11, 1864**. The dispatch's closing line became the rallying cry of the
North:
> *"I propose to fight it out on this line if it takes all summer."* — Grant (North), to Halleck,
> May 11, 1864.
> Fuller context of the message: *"We have now ended the sixth day of very heavy fighting. The
> result to this time is much to our favor. But our losses have been heavy, as well as those of
> the enemy …"* followed by the famous line. Congressman **Elihu Washburne (North)** carried it
> back to Washington; it ran in Northern newspapers. [CSS/St. Scholastica Grant quotes; multiple]

**Cold Harbor — the cost of the method, in Grant's own voice.** On **June 3, 1864**, Grant ordered
a frontal assault on Lee's entrenched line at Cold Harbor. About **7,000 Union men** fell dead,
wounded, or missing in roughly an hour for no gain. Grant later wrote in his *Personal Memoirs*:
> *"I have always regretted that the last assault at Cold Harbor was ever made. … No advantage
> whatever was gained to compensate for the heavy loss we sustained."* — Grant (North), *Personal
> Memoirs*. [Grant, *Personal Memoirs*, ch. 55; RealClearHistory; ABT] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/eastern/cold-harbor`.

**Campaign casualties (CONTESTED — flag the range, see bottom).**
- Union: **~54,926 total** (commonly rounded to ~50,000+) — 7,621 killed, 38,339 wounded, 8,966
  captured/missing. [Wikipedia: Overland Campaign]
- Confederate: **~30,000–35,000** (figures less firm; ~4,200–4,350 killed, ~18,500–19,100 wounded,
  ~9,900–10,200 missing/captured). [Wikipedia: Overland Campaign]
- **The strategic point, not the body count:** in raw numbers Lee inflicted more than he lost, and
  the North called Grant a "butcher." But Grant lost a *smaller share* of a *much larger, replaceable*
  army, while Lee's irreplaceable veteran army bled toward a number it could not sustain. Attrition
  was working as designed even when each individual fight looked like a Union defeat. [Wikipedia:
  Overland Campaign; historynet]

**People (with side):** Grant (North), Meade (North), Lee (South), Halleck (North), Washburne (North),
Philip Sheridan (North, cavalry), J.E.B. Stuart (South — mortally wounded at Yellow Tavern, May 11,
1864). [Wikipedia: Overland Campaign]

## §3 — Pin Lee in place: the slide to Petersburg and the siege

**The maneuver.** After Cold Harbor, Grant abandoned the head-on approach. In mid-June 1864 he
slipped the whole army across the **James River** — a difficult, brilliantly screened crossing —
to strike **Petersburg**, the rail hub 20+ miles south of Richmond through which nearly all of Lee's
supplies flowed. Take Petersburg and Richmond starves; the war of movement becomes a war of supply.
[Wikipedia: Overland Campaign / Siege of Petersburg]

**The open door that closed (Second Petersburg, June 15–18, 1864).** The first Union assaults — with
USCT troops among the leading attackers — broke into Petersburg's outer works against a thin garrison,
but the attack stalled and Lee's army arrived to man the lines. The chance to take the city by storm
was lost, and a **siege of nearly ten months** (June 1864 – April 1865) began. [roster hook;
Wikipedia: Siege of Petersburg] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/eastern/second-petersburg`.

**Why the siege IS the strategy.** Grant's object was no longer to destroy Lee's army in a day's
battle but to **fix it in place** — pin it to a fortified line it could not leave — and slowly cut
the railroads feeding it while the rest of the Union armies wrecked the Confederacy elsewhere. A
besieged army cannot maneuver, cannot reinforce other fronts, and cannot replace what it loses. The
trench lines (a preview of 1914–18) and the slow rail-cutting were attrition made geographic.
[Wikipedia: Siege of Petersburg]

**The Crater (July 30, 1864) — the strategy's ugliest failure, and a slavery-war atrocity.**
- Coal miners of the **48th Pennsylvania (North)**, led by **Lt. Col. Henry Pleasants (North)**,
  tunneled under the Confederate line and packed it with **8,000 lbs of powder** (320 kegs). It blew
  at **4:44 a.m.**, killing ~278 Confederates and tearing a crater ~170 ft long, 100–120 ft wide,
  30+ ft deep. [Wikipedia: Battle of the Crater]
- A **USCT division had trained for weeks to lead the assault.** At the last minute **Meade (North)**
  ordered **Burnside (North)** to swap in untrained white troops, fearing the political backlash if
  Black soldiers were slaughtered leading a failed attack. The white divisions poured *into* the
  crater instead of around it and were trapped in a killing pit; the USCT, sent in later, took
  disproportionate losses, with many Black soldiers murdered after trying to surrender. [Wikipedia:
  Battle of the Crater]
- Casualties: **Union ~3,798; Confederate ~1,491.** Grant (North) called it
  > *"the saddest affair I have witnessed in this war."* — Grant (North).
  [Wikipedia: Battle of the Crater] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/eastern/crater`. (The
  decision to pull the Black troops, and the massacre, also tie to `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/usct`.)

**People (with side):** Grant (North), Lee (South), Meade (North), Ambrose Burnside (North),
Henry Pleasants (North).

## §4 — The subsidiary advances fail — and Grant's machine keeps moving anyway

**The point of this section:** simultaneity meant Grant could *afford to lose the side bets.* Three
of the five spring advances failed outright, yet the two main ones (Grant→Lee, Sherman→Atlanta) ground
on — which is exactly the resilience a coordinated, multi-front plan buys that the old one-army-at-a-time
war never had.

- **Butler "bottled up" at Bermuda Hundred (May 1864).** **Benjamin Butler's (North)** Army of the
  James landed between Richmond and Petersburg but was sealed off by **P.G.T. Beauregard (South)** with
  a far smaller force. Grant's engineer's metaphor stuck: Butler was in a bottle and Beauregard had
  "corked" it — a small Confederate force held the neck while Butler sat useless. [Wikipedia: Bermuda
  Hundred campaign]
- **Sigel routed at New Market (May 15, 1864).** **Franz Sigel's (North)** Valley advance was beaten by
  a scratch Confederate force under **John C. Breckinridge (South)** — famously including **247 Virginia
  Military Institute cadets**, ten of whom were killed. Sigel retreated; the Valley stayed Confederate
  for the moment. [Wikipedia: Battle of New Market]
- **Banks wrecked on the Red River (Mar 10 – May 22, 1864).** **Nathaniel Banks's (North)** cotton-and-
  conquest expedition up Louisiana's Red River was defeated by **Richard Taylor (South)** at Mansfield /
  Sabine Crossroads (Apr 8), barely extracted its fleet, and accomplished nothing — ending Banks's field
  career and tying up troops Grant wanted at Mobile. [Wikipedia: Red River campaign] (Mansfield is a
  built Trans-Miss dossier: `/war-civil-war/trans-mississippi/mansfield`.)

**Takeaway for the author:** three failures, no collapse. Because every front moved at once, the South
could not turn a local win into a decisive one — there was always another Union army still coming.

**People (with side):** Butler (North), Beauregard (South), Sigel (North), Breckinridge (South),
Banks (North), Taylor (South).

## §5 — Atlanta: from maneuver to a political objective

**The campaign.** Sherman's Atlanta Campaign ran **May 7 – September 2, 1864**: **Sherman (North)**,
~112,000 men, against **Johnston (South)**, ~50,000. Johnston's smaller army gave ground deliberately;
Sherman repeatedly **flanked** rather than charged — sliding around Johnston's left to force him back
toward Atlanta without a head-on bloodbath. (His men joked he could "flank the devil out of hell.")
[Wikipedia: Atlanta campaign]

**The one exception, and its lesson.** At **Kennesaw Mountain (June 27, 1864)** Sherman abandoned
flanking for a frontal assault and lost ~3,000 to ~1,000 Confederate — a costly reminder of why he
maneuvered. He went back to flanking. [Wikipedia: Atlanta campaign]

**Davis fires the Fabian and gets the gambler.** Frustrated by retreat, **Jefferson Davis (South)**
replaced Johnston with **John Bell Hood (South)** on **July 17–18, 1864**. Hood promptly attacked —
**Peachtree Creek (Jul 20)**, the **Battle of Atlanta (Jul 22**, where Union Maj. Gen. **James B.
McPherson (North)** was killed), and **Ezra Church (Jul 28)** — and was bloodily repulsed each time,
burning up the army he was trying to save. [Wikipedia: Atlanta campaign]

**The fall (Jonesborough → Atlanta).** Sherman cut the last railroad into Atlanta at **Jonesborough
(Aug 31 – Sep 1, 1864)**; Hood evacuated and **Atlanta fell September 2, 1864.** Sherman's telegram:
> *"Atlanta is ours, and fairly won."* — Sherman (North), Sep 2, 1864.
[Wikipedia: Atlanta campaign] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/western/jonesborough`.

**Campaign casualties:** Union ~31,687; Confederate ~34,979 — proportionally far worse for the smaller
Southern army (Hood's remnant ~30,000 vs. Sherman's ~81,000 by campaign's end). [Wikipedia: Atlanta
campaign]

**Why Atlanta is the pivot of the whole chapter:** it converted a *military* gain into the *political*
event that saved the Union war effort (see §6). A battlefield result decided an election — which is the
chapter's second thesis: in 1864 the battlefield *became* a political objective.

**People (with side):** Sherman (North), Johnston (South), Davis (South), Hood (South),
McPherson (North).

## §6 — The battlefield decides the election

**The despair (summer 1864).** Through the summer, after the Overland Campaign's casualty lists and the
Petersburg stalemate, Lincoln (North) himself expected to lose re-election. On **August 23, 1864** he
wrote, sealed, and had his Cabinet sign sight-unseen the "**Blind Memorandum**":
> *"This morning, as for some days past, it seems exceedingly probable that this Administration will not
> be re-elected. Then it will be my duty to so co-operate with the President elect, as to save the Union
> between the election and the inauguration …"* — Lincoln (North), Aug 23, 1864. [LOC; ABT; House Divided]

**The Democratic peace platform.** The Democrats nominated **George B. McClellan (North)** — the general
Lincoln had twice relieved — on a platform declaring the war a *failure* and calling for a negotiated
peace. (McClellan, a War Democrat, personally repudiated the peace plank, but the platform was the party's
position.) A McClellan win plausibly meant a negotiated end that left the Confederacy — and slavery —
intact. [Wikipedia: 1864 United States presidential election].
> **[VERIFY exact platform wording]** — confirm the "experiment of war … a failure" phrasing against the
> Democratic platform text before quoting it verbatim.

**The three battlefield rescues, in order:**
1. **Mobile Bay (Aug 5, 1864)** — **Farragut (North)** runs the forts (see §7).
2. **Atlanta falls (Sep 2, 1864)** — *"Atlanta is ours, and fairly won."* With Atlanta taken, "there was
   no longer any question that a Union military victory was inevitable." [Wikipedia: 1864 election]
3. **Sheridan in the Valley (Sep–Oct 1864)** — Opequon, Fisher's Hill, Cedar Creek (see §7) — a string of
   decisive wins that "helped Lincoln get re-elected." [Wikipedia: Valley campaigns of 1864]

**The result (Nov 8, 1864).** Lincoln **212 electoral votes to McClellan's 21**; popular vote **2,218,388
(55.0%) to 1,812,807 (45.0%)**. The **soldier vote** went overwhelmingly Lincoln — of ~40,247 military
ballots across seven reporting states, Lincoln took ~30,503 (≈76%). The men doing the fighting voted to
keep fighting. [Wikipedia: 1864 election] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/war-within-north` (the home-front politics, Copperheads, and the election in full).

**Author note (strategic synthesis):** this is where the chapter's two theses meet. The *military* attrition
plan (§§1–5) only had to last long enough to deliver a battlefield result before the *political* clock ran
out in November. Atlanta + Mobile + the Valley delivered it. The war's outcome rode on whether Grant's
machine could produce a visible victory before the electorate quit.

**People (with side):** Lincoln (North), McClellan (North), Farragut (North), Sheridan (North).

## §7 — Hard war: the Valley, the Sea, Tennessee — and the logistics that won it

This section gathers the **hard-war / logistics-as-strategy** thread: 1864 is when the Union stops trying
only to beat the South's *armies* and starts deliberately wrecking the South's *capacity and will* to
field them — while its own industrial supply machine runs at a scale the Confederacy can't approach.

**(a) Mobile Bay (Aug 5, 1864) — closing the last Gulf port.** **Rear Adm. David Farragut (North)** forced
the entrance to Mobile Bay, ran past the mines ("torpedoes"), and captured the ironclad **CSS Tennessee**
and **Adm. Franklin Buchanan (South)**, closing the Confederacy's last major Gulf port east of the
Mississippi (the *city* of Mobile held out until spring 1865). Union ~150 killed / ~170 wounded; the monitor
**USS Tecumseh** sank on a mine, 93 of 114 lost. The famous line —
> *"Damn the torpedoes! … Full speed ahead!"* — attributed to Farragut (North) —
is **likely apocryphal**: it did not appear in print until years later; the most credible version is "Damn
the torpedoes. Four bells, Captain Drayton." [Wikipedia: Battle of Mobile Bay] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/naval/mobile-bay`.

**(b) Early's raid and Sheridan's Valley (Jul–Oct 1864) — denying the breadbasket.**
- **Jubal Early (South)** drove down the Valley and across the Potomac, won at **Monocacy (Jul 9)** — a
  delay that saved the capital — and reached the **Fort Stevens** defenses on Washington's edge (Jul 11–12)
  with Lincoln (North) himself present under fire before withdrawing. [Wikipedia: Valley campaigns of 1864]
- Grant gave **Philip Sheridan (North)** the new Army of the Shenandoah and ordered the Valley — the South's
  breadbasket — stripped: a deliberate **scorched-earth** campaign that "ruthlessly burned crops, barns,
  mills, and factories" ("The Burning") so it could never again feed Lee's army. Hard war as *supply* strategy,
  presaging Sherman's march. [Wikipedia: Valley campaigns of 1864]
  > **[VERIFY quote] — Grant's order that the Valley be left so barren that "crows flying over it … will have
  > to carry their provender with them"** is widely attributed to Grant (North) but verify exact wording and
  > attribution before quoting.
- Sheridan then won three decisive fights: **Third Winchester / Opequon (Sep 19)** — the largest battle of the
  Valley campaigns, set up partly by intelligence from Unionist **Rebecca Wright** — **Fisher's Hill (Sep 22)**,
  and **Cedar Creek (Oct 19)**, where Early's surprise dawn attack broke two-thirds of the Union army before
  Sheridan's ride from Winchester rallied it into a rout that won the Valley for good. [Wikipedia: Valley
  campaigns of 1864] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/eastern/opequon` and `/war-civil-war/eastern/cedar-creek`.

**(c) Sherman's March to the Sea (Nov 15 – Dec 21, 1864) — wrecking the war machine.** Sherman (North) cut
loose from his supply line and marched **~62,000 men ~300 miles** from Atlanta to Savannah, living off the
land and systematically destroying railroads, mills, gins, and crops — the South's capacity *and* its will to
keep an army supplied. Sherman estimated **~$100 million** in damage. ~10,000 formerly enslaved people followed
the army; the **Ebenezer Creek** drowning (Union Gen. **Jefferson C. Davis (North)** pulling up the pontoon
bridge, leaving freedpeople to Confederate cavalry) is the march's darkest episode. His telegram to Lincoln:
> *"I beg to present you as a Christmas-gift the City of Savannah, with one hundred and fifty heavy guns and
> plenty of ammunition …"* — Sherman (North), Dec 22, 1864. [Wikipedia: Sherman's March to the Sea]
- **No dossier exists** — carry in narrative. **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/new-way-of-war` and `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/home-front` (the hard-war doctrine and civilian experience).
  > **[VERIFY quote] — "make Georgia howl"** (Sherman to Grant, well attested) — confirm exact wording/source if quoted.

**(d) Hood's doomed Tennessee counter-offensive (Nov–Dec 1864).** Rather than chase Sherman, **Hood (South)**
swung north into Tennessee, hoping to draw Sherman back. It destroyed his own army instead.
- **Franklin (Nov 30, 1864):** Hood ordered a frontal assault across ~2 miles of open ground at entrenched
  Union troops under **John Schofield (North)**. **Six Confederate generals killed or mortally wounded**
  (including **Patrick Cleburne (South)**), ~14 generals lost overall; ~6,252 Confederate casualties to
  ~2,300 Union. [Wikipedia: Franklin–Nashville campaign] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/western/franklin`.
- **Nashville (Dec 15–16, 1864):** **George H. Thomas (North)**, ~55,000, **destroyed the Army of Tennessee as
  a fighting force** — with **USCT regiments** prominent in the assault. ~3,061 Union casualties to ~6,000+
  Confederate; Hood resigned **Jan 13, 1865**. The Confederacy's second great army effectively ceased to exist.
  [Wikipedia: Franklin–Nashville campaign] — **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/western/nashville`.

**(e) The logistics machine (the system that made attrition possible).** Attrition only works if you can keep
replacing what you spend. The North could; the South could not.
- **U.S. Military Railroad (USMRR):** grew from 7 miles in 1862 to **2,000+ miles** operated by war's end, with
  **419 locomotives and 6,330 cars** of its own. Under **Daniel McCallum** and builder **Herman Haupt (North)**,
  it could move armies at unheard-of speed (≈25,000 men ~1,200 miles to relieve Chattanooga in 1863). [Wikipedia:
  United States Military Railroad]
- **City Point** (Grant's supply base at the Petersburg siege) became one of the busiest ports in the world in
  1864–65, supplying **100,000+ troops and 65,000+ horses and mules** from the James River docks; the USMRR ran
  21 miles of track partly encircling Petersburg. The Confederate rail net, by contrast, was decaying, gauge-
  mismatched, and could not be repaired. [Wikipedia: United States Military Railroad].
  > **[VERIFY if used as a hard stat] — "City Point: one of the busiest ports in the world (1864–65)"** is a
  > standard characterization; verify a citable source before stating it as a flat fact.

**(f) Fort Pillow & the manpower math (NAME-THE-CAUSE anchor).** The reason the attrition math broke the South's
way is also the reason 1864 is a slavery war made explicit:
- **Manpower:** ~179,000–200,000 Black men served in the **USCT**, most formerly enslaved, filling Union ranks as
  Northern white enlistment flagged. Lincoln (North) in 1864: "We can not spare the hundred and forty or fifty
  thousand now serving us as soldiers, seamen, and laborers." [LOC; National Archives] The Confederacy had no such
  reservoir — it was *fighting to keep* the very people the Union was now arming.

# CONTESTED / [VERIFY] FLAGS

1. **Overland Campaign casualties are genuinely contested.** Union ~54,926 is firm-ish (Wikipedia/Grant's returns);
   Confederate losses (~30,000–35,000) are an estimate and vary by source. Use a range and say so; do NOT present
   the Confederate number as exact. The *strategic* reading (Lee bled an irreplaceable army even while inflicting
   more) matters more than the precise totals. [Wikipedia: Overland Campaign]

2. **"Those not skinning can hold a leg" is LINCOLN's, not Grant's.** Common error to attribute it to Grant. It is
   Lincoln's homespun blessing of Grant's coordinated-advance plan. Verify exact phrasing against a primary/standard
   source before quoting. [erenow/*The Presidents' War*; Lincoln Wonk]

3. **"Damn the torpedoes, full speed ahead!" (Farragut) is likely apocryphal.** It did not appear in print until
   years later; the credible version is "Damn the torpedoes. Four bells, Captain Drayton." If used, frame it as the
   famous-but-probably-polished version. [Wikipedia: Battle of Mobile Bay]

4. **Grant's "crows flying over it … carry their provender" Valley order** — widely attributed to Grant; verify exact
   wording and that it's Grant (not paraphrase/conflation) before quoting verbatim.

5. **Sherman's "make Georgia howl"** — well attested (Sherman to Grant) but confirm exact wording/source if quoted
   directly.

6. **Democratic 1864 "war is a failure" platform** — the plank's exact wording ("four years of failure to restore
   the Union by the experiment of war …") should be pulled from the platform text before quoting verbatim. McClellan
   personally repudiated the peace plank — say so, don't imply he ran on it. [Wikipedia: 1864 election]

7. **City Point "busiest port in the world"** — standard popular characterization; verify a citable source before
   stating flatly. The throughput figures (100,000+ troops, 65,000+ animals) are sourced. [Wikipedia: USMRR]

8. **Fort Pillow exact casualty/survival counts and "massacre" framing** — the massacre is the scholarly consensus,
   but exact numbers (deaths ~277–297; prisoner ratios) vary; cite as approximate. The *disparity* (Black soldiers far
   less likely to be taken prisoner) is the robust, citable fact. [Wikipedia: Battle of Fort Pillow]

9. **Sherman's $100 million March damage** is *Sherman's own estimate* — attribute it to him, not as an audited figure.
   [Wikipedia: Sherman's March to the Sea]

10. **USCT total (~179,000 vs. ~200,000)** — ~179,000 is the standard Army figure; "more than 200,000" usually includes
    Navy and later enlistments. Use the figure that matches the claim and note the basis. [LOC; National Archives]

11. **Atlanta Campaign casualties (Union ~31,687 / CS ~34,979)** — round/approximate; sources vary. [Wikipedia: Atlanta
    campaign]

12. **Two "Jefferson Davis" people — DO NOT CONFLATE.** Confederate **President Jefferson Davis (South)** and Union
    Brig./Maj. Gen. **Jefferson C. Davis (North)** (Ebenezer Creek) are different men. Tag and distinguish explicitly.
