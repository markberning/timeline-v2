# Fact Pack — Battle / Raid of Fort Frontenac (August 25–27, 1758)

French and Indian War (North American theater of the Seven Years' War). Neutral
evidence base assembled from live web research. Numbered facts; sources cited;
DISPUTED figures flagged with ranges. Invent nothing downstream of this file.

## A. PLACE AND STRATEGIC ROLE

1. Fort Frontenac stood at Cataraqui, at the eastern end of Lake Ontario where
the lake drains into the St. Lawrence River. The site is present-day Kingston,
Ontario. [S1, S3]

2. It was a French fort and trading post and the hinge of French communications
and supply on Lake Ontario. It was one of two major communication/supply routes
between the eastern centers (Montreal, Quebec City) and France's western
territories; the other was the Ottawa River route, which remained open after the
fort fell. [S1]

3. As a depot, Frontenac fed the western posts. Supplies and trade goods flowed
through it to the *pays d'en haut* (the upper country / western Great Lakes) and
to the Ohio Country posts including Fort Duquesne. [S1, S2]

4. By 1758 the fort was in poor physical condition: variously described as a
"crumbling limestone construction" (S1) and a fort the garrison "could man fewer
than a dozen of sixty artillery pieces" (S2). DCB describes the installation as
in poor condition (S4).

5. French control of Lake Ontario had been secured when the British base at Fort
Oswego (across the lake on the south shore) fell to the French under Montcalm in
1756 and was destroyed. After Oswego's fall the French held a naval presence on
the lake and assumed the British "would not dare to enter Lake Ontario on which
[the French] had vessels" (attributed to Governor Vaudreuil's belief). [S1]

## B. COMMANDERS

6. British: Lieutenant Colonel John Bradstreet. Born Jean-Baptiste Bradstreet in
Nova Scotia, 1714; British army father, Acadian mother. Distinguished himself at
the 1745 Louisbourg siege; by 1758 was deputy quartermaster general. [S2]

7. Bradstreet's interest in raiding Frontenac dated to 1755, when he supervised
supplies at Fort Oswego and recognized Frontenac's role in Canada's western
Indian trade. He lobbied persistently, reportedly offering to fund the raid
personally if reimbursed only on success; he secured authorization in 1758 and
pressed the new commander Abercromby after the British defeat at Ticonderoga
(Carillon). [S2]

8. French: Major / commander Pierre-Jacques Payen de Noyan et de Chavoy. Born
November 3, 1695, in Montreal; an aging veteran (early 60s in 1758) of earlier
colonial wars. Was king's lieutenant at Trois-Rivières when appointed to command
Fort Frontenac. [S1, S4]

9. Noyan's fate: taken prisoner; Bradstreet arranged his exchange for Colonel
Peter Schuyler, and he returned to Montreal in declining health. Criticized by
Governor Vaudreuil; requested and received retirement with a pension in January
1759. Later imprisoned in the Bastille (March 1762 – December 1763) during the
*affaire du Canada*; ultimately fined a nominal six livres (minimal culpability).
Died in Paris, December 30, 1771. [S4]

## C. FORCES (sizes — note ranges)

10. British force size — DISPUTED / range:
- ~3,000 / "over 3,000" total. [S1]
- ~3,100 reaching Lake Ontario. [S2]
- 2,635 total (one breakdown: 2,500 militia and Indians + 135 regulars), after
  ~600 lost to desertion from a starting ~3,500 by Aug 21. [S1]
- "3,600 colonial troops." [S5]
- Consensus framing: roughly 3,000 men, overwhelmingly provincials (the part-time
  troops raised in the colonies) and bateaumen, with a small core of regulars
  (the king's full-time professional soldiers). Regular count given as ~135 (S1)
  / ~157 (S2). Small number of Indigenous warriors (~70, S2).

11. The force was overwhelmingly provincial troops from New York, New Jersey, and
New England (New York, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Rhode Island named). [S1, S2]

12. French garrison size — DISPUTED / range:
- 110 (most commonly cited; "garrison of 110 people"; "110 strong garrison").
  [S1, S5]
- ~110 regulars plus militia and Indigenous allies. [S1]
- 110 soldiers, supplemented by women and children. [S2]
- "approximately 60 soldiers." [S4]
- Consensus framing: a small garrison of about 110, plus women and children; the
  ~60 figure (S4) may count effective soldiers only.

## D. THE CROSSING, SIEGE, AND SURRENDER

13. Route: Bradstreet's force traveled the inland water route he knew well — Wood
Creek to Oneida Lake, then the Onondaga (Oswego) River to Lake Ontario, reaching
Sackets Harbor by August 21. The expedition moved in bateaux (flat-bottomed
boats). HistoryOfWar gives the journey as ~430 miles including 84 miles of
portage. [S2, S5]

14. The flotilla of bateaux crossed Lake Ontario and landed without opposition
about one mile from Fort Frontenac on August 25, 1758. [S1, S3]

15. The night after landing, Bradstreet's men established gun batteries and dug
trenches toward the fort. They attempted, without success, to board French ships
anchored before the fort. [S1]

16. On the morning of August 26 the British guns opened fire. Bombardment
continued August 26–27. [S1, S3]

17. Surrender date — DISPUTED:
- August 27 (morning): fort surrendered after a brief cannonade. [S2, S3]
- August 28: Noyan raised the white flag following a brief council of war, after
  French ships attempted to escape but ran aground. [S1]
- Anderson (S2) and Kronoskaf (S3) give the 27th; Wikipedia (S1) gives the 28th.
  The action is bracketed Aug 25 (landing) – Aug 27/28 (surrender). PROMPT and
  scholarly source (S2) use Aug 27.

18. French reinforcements from Montreal were organized only on August 27 and did
not arrive until after September 1 — too late. [S2]

19. The garrison was hopeless: a tiny force in a decayed fort, able to work only
a fraction of its guns, surprised and heavily outnumbered, with no relief in
time. Surrender followed a brief council of war. [S1, S2]

## E. THE HAUL — CAPTURED AND DESTROYED

20. French armed vessels / lake fleet — DISPUTED / range:
- Nine French sloops, described as "Lake Ontario's entire naval force." [S2]
  (Anderson, scholarly standard.)
- 2 armed vessels (i.e., the vessels actively present/defending). [S1]
- Reconciliation: the fort and harbor yielded the French Lake Ontario fleet
  (~9 vessels per Anderson); the "2 armed vessels" (S1) appears to refer to the
  ships actively defending / that tried to escape. PROMPT uses ~9 ships (the lake
  fleet). Flag the 2-vs-9 distinction.

21. Disposition of the vessels: Bradstreet loaded valuables onto two sloops to
carry off, and sank/destroyed the rest. [S2]

22. Cannon: over 60 artillery pieces captured, including British guns that had
been taken from Fort Oswego in 1756. [S1, S2]

23. Stores and trade goods: hundreds of barrels of provisions; bales of furs/deer
skins destined for Montreal; bales of cloth, laced and plain coats and shirts of
all sizes. Bradstreet reported the value of seized goods at "£35,000, or 800,000
livres." [S1, S2]

24. Destruction: Bradstreet's orders were not to hold the fort but to destroy it.
He disabled the cannon, burned buildings and stores, collapsed the walls, loaded
what valuables two sloops could carry, sank the remaining vessels, then withdrew.
[S1, S2]

## F. CASUALTIES

25. British casualties: very light. 11 wounded (S1); Anderson notes the raid cost
"zero Anglo-American lives" (S2). DISPUTED only in trivial detail; uniformly
described as near-bloodless for the British.

26. French casualties: 2 killed; the garrison surrendered (taken prisoner). [S1]

## G. STRATEGIC EFFECT

27. The capture severed one of the two major supply/communication lines between
the eastern French centers and the western territories, cutting supplies to the
western posts and disrupting the fur trade in the *pays d'en haut*. [S1]

28. Bradstreet's own assessment (reported): "their troops to the southward and
western garrisons will suffer greatly, if not entirely starve, for want of the
provisions and vessels we have destroyed." [S2] (See quotes section — verified.)

29. Effect on Fort Duquesne: the loss cut supply lines to Fort Duquesne. The
garrison at Duquesne was low on provisions because supplies intended for it were
destroyed in the Frontenac raid. Fort Duquesne fell to the British (Forbes
Expedition) before the end of 1758; the French abandoned and blew it up in
November 1758. Montcalm regarded the loss of Frontenac as serious. [S1, S5, S6]

30. Context within Pitt's 1758 strategy: the raid came amid William Pitt's
broad 1758 offensive and offset the demoralizing British defeat at Carillon
(Ticonderoga) on July 8, 1758. Bradstreet seized the opening after Ticonderoga to
get the raid approved. [S1, S2]

31. Resonance with Oswego: Frontenac sat across Lake Ontario from Oswego, the
British base Montcalm had destroyed in 1756. Among the 60+ guns captured were
British pieces taken at Oswego two years earlier — a recovery / reversal. [S1, S2]

32. Aftermath for Bradstreet: he immediately lobbied to push on to Fort Niagara
and expand the conquest; rebuffed by Abercromby, he campaigned (anonymous
pamphlet, letters to patrons) against Abercromby. [S2]

## I. UNVERIFIED QUOTES — DO NOT USE

- No clean first-person verbatim quotation from Noyan was located in the sources
  consulted. Do NOT invent a surrender speech or any line for Noyan.
- Do NOT attribute any verbatim line to Montcalm; sources report only that he
  considered the loss "serious" (paraphrase, S5) — render as paraphrase, not a
  quote.
- The Vaudreuil "would not dare to enter Lake Ontario" line (VQ4) is a source
  paraphrase, not a documented verbatim utterance — do not present it inside
  quotation marks as Vaudreuil's exact words.

## J. DISPUTED-FIGURES SUMMARY (for the draft's hedging)

- British force: ~2,600–3,600; use "about 3,000, overwhelmingly provincials."
- French garrison: ~60 effective soldiers / ~110 total with women and children;
  use "about 110."
- Surrender date: Aug 27 (S2/S3, scholarly + prompt) vs Aug 28 (S1); use Aug 27,
  may note the action spanned Aug 25–27/28.
- Lake fleet captured: ~9 sloops (entire Lake Ontario fleet, S2) vs "2 armed
  vessels" actively defending (S1); use the lake fleet (~9), note the distinction.
- Value of haul: 800,000 livres / £35,000 — consistent across sources.
- Cannon: 60+, consistent.
