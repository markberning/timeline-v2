# Newcomer Clarity Audit — The Conquest of Canada

> Persona: sharp 15-year-old, zero prior knowledge. Loosened: common English fine; marquee names (Wolfe, Montcalm, Amherst, Pitt) exempt from side-tags.

## Term-by-Term Verdicts

| Term / Phrase | Verdict | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| "siege" | NEEDS-GLOSS | Used repeatedly without definition. A 15-year-old may guess "surrounding a fort" but never gets the mechanics: you surround it, cut off supplies, and either starve it out or batter its walls. First use (Fort Niagara section) should gloss. |
| "batteries / battery" | NEEDS-GLOSS | "A battery at Point Lévis" — readers don't know a battery is a positioned group of heavy guns; sounds like electric batteries. One-line gloss on first use. |
| "mortars" | CLEAR | Implicitly explained enough by context (artillery). |
| "regulars" | NEEDS-GLOSS | "British regulars" vs. colonial militia — the draft uses both but never draws the line. Regulars = professional full-time soldiers of the crown, trained, uniformed, paid year-round. Militia = part-time colonial volunteers. The distinction matters: regulars hold formation; militia can't (key to the battle). |
| "militia" | NEEDS-GLOSS | Same issue as regulars; they appear together and the contrast is implied, never stated. |
| "honors of war" | NEEDS-GLOSS | Used twice without definition. Means the beaten army earns the right to march out with weapons shouldered, colors flying, drums beating — a coded acknowledgment that they fought with dignity. Without it, the burning-of-the-flags moment loses its punch. |
| "capitulation" | NEEDS-GLOSS | Used in headers and body ("fifty-five articles of capitulation"). Means formal terms of surrender, negotiated in advance. The word sounds legalistic; gloss it once. |
| "carcasses" (incendiary shells) | CLEAR | Defined inline, well done. |
| "spiking … their cannon" | NEEDS-GLOSS | "Spiking and abandoning their cannon in the retreat" — a reader doesn't know spiking means driving a metal spike into the touchhole to permanently disable a gun so the enemy can't use it. |
| "siege train" | NEEDS-GLOSS | "He had only three cannon and no siege train" — a siege train is the specialized heavy artillery (large mortars, battering guns) required to breach fortress walls, not just shoot at soldiers. Without it, you can't knock down walls. The term is the pivot of the whole Sainte-Foy section. |
| "portage trail" | CLEAR | Implied by context (overland path around falls/rapids). Acceptable. |
| "line two ranks deep instead of the usual three" | CLEAR | The tactical implication (wider front, same number of men) is explained immediately. |
| "volleys" | CLEAR | Plain enough in context. |
| "the Haudenosaunee" | CLEAR | Glossed immediately as "the confederacy of Six Nations the British called the Iroquois." |
| "Chevalier de Lévis" | CLEAR | Title not explained but doesn't hurt comprehension. |
| "the Lachine rapids" | CLEAR | Named but not requiring explanation; context (84 men drowned, 46 boats wrecked) makes severity vivid. |
| "scurvy" | CLEAR | Common enough. |
| "regimental colors" | NEEDS-GLOSS | "Burn their regimental colors" — a 15-year-old may not know these are the official battle flags sewn to a regiment's identity, not generic flags; their capture by the enemy is a deep dishonor. One sentence would make the burning meaningful. |
| "the honors of war / colors flying / drums beating" | NEEDS-GLOSS | See "honors of war" above. |
| "L'Anse-au-Foulon" | CLEAR | Name explained immediately as "a cove … about 1.9 miles (3 km) upstream from the city, at the foot of a cliff." The name itself isn't a term that needs glossing; the place is described. Draft uses "L'Anse-au-Foulon" in one place and "Anse-au-Foulon" elsewhere — minor inconsistency. |
| "the Plains of Abraham" | CLEAR | Explained as "a farmer's open field just west of the city walls." |
| "the royal proclamation" | NEEDS-GLOSS (in Meanwhile block only) | The "Meanwhile" sidebar mentions "a royal proclamation closing the newly won west to settlement" — zero context given for what proclamation, who issued it, or why. Fine for a sidebar teaser but could briefly say "King George's 1763 Proclamation." |

## Unexplained Logical Leaps — MUST-FIX

### 1. WHY did the Plains of Abraham force Quebec to surrender? (**LOST**)
This is the single biggest gap. The draft says Wolfe's men win the battle, Montcalm is shot, Vaudreuil pulls the army back upriver — and five days later Quebec surrenders. But a 15-year-old who doesn't already know siege warfare won't understand why a field battle west of the city walls caused the *city itself* to give up. The draft never explains:
- The garrison was left leaderless and undersupplied
- The army that would have defended the city was now gone upriver
- British troops could now invest the landward gates
- The city had no prospect of relief before winter

Without this, "Quebec surrendered five days later" is a bald fact with no logic behind it. **One or two sentences of causal stitching are required.**

### 2. WHY did Sainte-Foy change nothing despite being a French victory? (**NEEDS-FIX**)
The draft does explain this — Lévis had no siege train and waited for ships. That's good. The phrase "it decided nothing" arrives before the explanation, though, which momentarily confuses ("wait, France won, why nothing?"). **Reorder: give the reason (no siege train, waiting for ships) before the verdict.**

### 3. WHY did Montreal's fall end the war in North America? (**NEEDS-FIX**)
The draft says "The war in North America was over" after the Montreal surrender, but readers who don't know geography may not grasp that Montreal *was the last French position*. The draft mentions "the last French stronghold" in the setup paragraph but the connection could be slightly tighter at the payoff: a half-sentence confirmation that with Montreal gone, France had no cities, no army, and no supply line left on the continent.

## Overwhelming / Pacing Issues

- **The bombardment paragraph** (10,000 shells, 40,000 cannonballs, carcasses, ingredients) is dense but its detail serves the horror — not overwhelming, just rich.
- **The Montreal section** packs three converging armies, Lachine rapids, Murray, Haviland, Bougainville, and Île-aux-Noix into a tight space. A reader following all three paths may lose the thread. **SHOULD-FIX: one sentence explicitly recapping "the three-army plan worked because…" before the surrender.**
- **The "Counterweight Removed" section** is the clearest and most graceful section in the draft. No flags.

---

## Summary

### MUST-FIX (comprehension-blocking)
1. **Plains of Abraham → surrender logic** — explain why winning a field battle outside the walls forced the city inside the walls to give up.
2. **"siege" undefined** — first use should gloss.
3. **"regulars" vs. "militia"** — distinction never drawn; matters at the Plains of Abraham.
4. **"siege train"** — the Sainte-Foy pivot turns on this; needs a one-line gloss.
5. **"honors of war"** — the flag-burning climax is meaningless without it.

### SHOULD-FIX (clarity/flow)
1. **"batteries"** — gloss on first use.
2. **"spiking their cannon"** — one clause.
3. **"regimental colors"** — one sentence makes the burning scene land.
4. **"capitulation"** — one-line gloss; used as a section header.
5. **Sainte-Foy verdict reorder** — reason before conclusion.
6. **Pontiac's nation** — clarify "Ottawa nation" vs. Ottawa city.
7. **Montreal section** — half-sentence recap of why three armies ending it = total French defeat.
8. **"the royal proclamation"** — add "King George's 1763 Proclamation" in sidebar.

---

*Total MUST-FIX: 5. Worst single comprehension breaker: the Plains of Abraham → surrender gap — without it, the climax of the chapter is a non-sequitur.*
