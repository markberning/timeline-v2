## Overall Assessment

This is a competent, informed draft. The material is all here — the night march, the pivot, the two generals dead in fifteen minutes, the Cherokee complexity, the ammunition trap — and the draft largely uses it. The voice is in the right register throughout, with several moments that hit genuine Antietam-level crispness. What keeps this at GOOD rather than STRONG overall is a recurring pattern: the draft narrates events at the right altitude but doesn't always land the human gut-punch the material earns. The section that should be the emotional climax (`out-of-ammunition`, the battle resolving) is the weakest of the three: it does the work competently but exits too soon and at too low an emotional temperature. The ending is analytic where it should be gut-level.

The highest-impact fix is a single structural one: the `out-of-ammunition` section needs its last breath — the image of Confederate guns falling silent — to close at the level of a body, not a summary. Everything before it earns that beat.

## Section 1: `the-armies-gather` — The Fight Moves South

**Grade: GOOD**

### Strengths

The opening paragraph is doing real work. Naming Missouri as the prize and the slave state on the knife's edge before a shot is fired is the right orientation — it answers "why should I care?" before the reader can ask it. "The fight that ended at a tavern in the Arkansas hills" is a nice hook; it orients without over-promising.

The Van Dorn character note — "ambitious to the point of recklessness" — earns its place and pays off structurally when his own march beats him. The Price/McCulloch feud is given just the right weight: enough to make you feel that the Confederacy was its own worst enemy in the West even before Van Dorn arrived.

The pullquote that closes the section is pointed and earned: "Van Dorn had assembled more men against Curtis than any Confederate force would ever again bring to bear in the entire Trans-Mississippi."

The "Meanwhile" sidebar on Missouri is strong — it earns its place by zooming out to the strategic prize and names what Curtis holding will mean before the battle has been described.

### Weaknesses and Fixes

**Hook energy drops in the middle.** The opening paragraph hits the right note; then the backstory of Wilson's Creek through the Price/McCulloch feud — three paragraphs of recap — lowers the temperature. This is necessary context but it reads like a timeline summary. Compare to how Antietam's `lost-order` section handles backstory: two short paragraphs to establish Lee's invasion and its stakes, then immediately to the specific scene of Barton Mitchell's cigars. The pacing difference is the discipline of the concrete object.

_Fix:_ The recap from "It had started badly" through "The Confederate bid for Missouri stalled out on its own internal squabbling" is competent but flat. One quick humanizing beat would lift it. The Price/McCulloch feud is the seed of everything — two stubborn men who could have taken Missouri together and couldn't stand each other. That absurdity is the story. One line to feel that: something like the fact that their dispatches to Richmond during the fall of 1861 were as much about each other as about the enemy. This is a beat the draft mentions but doesn't inhabit. _Not an invented fact — derived from the dynamics the draft already describes._

**Curtis is introduced efficiently but lacks one beat of character.** "A trained engineer put in charge of Union forces" and "he moved" is fine, and the detail "moved through the dead of winter" earns its place. But the contrast between Curtis the engineer and Van Dorn the gambler is the dramatic spine of the whole battle, and this section doesn't name that spine yet. The reader doesn't quite feel why these two men are the worst possible matchup for Van Dorn.

_Fix:_ The draft says Curtis "dug in" and "picked a strong defensive line." Hold that thought for where it belongs (section 2) and give Curtis a single line here that names the contrast: a man who solves problems with a shovel, facing a man who solves them with a gamble. The contrast is in the draft; it just isn't stated as the drama.

**The march paragraph is good but undersells the body cost.** "Men arrived frozen, starving, and exhausted" is accurate and efficient. But the forced march is one of the most consequential facts of the battle — it's what turns Van Dorn's numerical advantage into a near-parity. The draft mentions this (effective strength dropping from ~16,000 to ~12,000) but treats it as a military logistics note rather than as the beginning of the army's self-destruction. There's no image of a man on that march.

_Fix:_ The draft already has Price "begged Van Dorn to let the worn-out, hungry men rest." That's a scene. It's currently buried in reported speech in the third paragraph of that section. Surface it: let the reader feel Price looking at his men before the battle even starts. One sentence of what those men looked like on arrival is enough. _Not an invented fact — paraphrase of the sourced detail already in the draft._

**The pullquote placement is slightly early.** The big Van Dorn quote about assembling the largest Trans-Mississippi force sits at the END of the section on troop buildup, right before the march description. But it reads better as a cap on the whole gathering, before Van Dorn throws it away — i.e., just before the "The march that ate the army" subsection. Currently it summarizes buildup; moved, it would set up the irony of the march as a fall from that high point.

## Section 2: `around-the-army` — The Army That Turned Around

**Grade: STRONG**

This is the best section of the draft. The tactical problem is explained so clearly that a reader with zero military background will follow it — and the draft manages that without ever dumbing down.

### Strengths

The lead sentence is the best opener in the draft: "A battle plan usually starts with two armies facing each other. This one started with Van Dorn deciding to face Curtis from the wrong direction — on purpose." That's the Antietam voice. It earns the reader's trust by being precise and a little cheeky at the same time.

"It half-worked, which turned out to be the worst possible outcome." This is strong writing — the payoff to the setup is the irony, and the draft earns it cleanly.

The two-generals-in-fifteen-minutes passage is the best passage in the full draft. McCulloch in black velvet, scouting alone, the shot from a skirmisher, command passing to McIntosh, McIntosh killed in fifteen minutes leading a mounted charge from the front — it's all there, in the right order, at the right pace. The Hébert paragraph compounds the disaster in exactly the right way: the next man in line didn't even know he was in command. The result arriving at Pike, "a lawyer and poet with no real combat experience," is the right beat to close on — it names the human scale of what just happened.

The pullquote in this section works: "Van Dorn had marched all night to hit Curtis from behind. Curtis turned his whole army around in a morning and hit back." It's punchy and earned.

The Stand Watie background is handled well — the Treaty of New Echota, Trail of Tears, the "last Confederate general to surrender" — without feeling like a digression. The draft makes a real case for why it belongs here and earns the Cherokee context before the Foster's Farm passage.

The Foster's Farm passage is the hardest material in the draft to handle — the scalping episode — and the draft handles it with exactly the right scrupulousness: no eyewitness, no assignment of perpetrators, the scale noted (a handful of the ~900 present), Pike's response. The italic closer ("Both armies marched into those woods believing they were the ones doing the surprising. By afternoon, neither side could say for certain who had done what among the trees.") is the right note — it extends the moral fog beyond the single episode.

The "Meanwhile in Indian Territory" sidebar is strong and earns its weight: "a second civil war folded inside the first" is a real insight, not just a quote.

### Weaknesses and Fixes

**Curtis's pivot is described clearly but could hit harder.** "He turned his entire army around" is in the draft, and the tactical detail (Carr to Elkhorn Tavern, Osterhaus and Davis to Leetown) is correct. But the human texture of the moment — an entire army reversing itself in a morning, men who had spent days fortifying facing one direction suddenly turning their backs on all of it — is not there. In Antietam, the West Woods passage gets at this through the body cost ("in roughly twenty minutes, Sedgwick's men took around 2,200 casualties"). The pivot has no equivalent image.

_Fix:_ The draft's pullquote does some of this work: "Curtis turned his whole army around in a morning and hit back." But there's a detail available that would concretize it: the line Curtis had "spent days fortifying" along Little Sugar Creek became his rear in a single morning. Name it explicitly as the thing he gave up, and the reader will feel the audacity of the decision. The detail is already in the draft — it just needs one sentence of emphasis: the fortification becomes the thing he was willing to abandon. _Not an invented fact._

**The "Meanwhile" sidebar for Indian Territory is a half-beat late.** It follows the Foster's Farm passage and the Cherokee context. But the Cherokee Nation's internal split is mentioned in the body text ("a Cherokee regiment whose Unionist soldiers would begin deserting to the Federal side during the battle itself") and then the sidebar covers essentially the same ground. The sidebar feels like a repeat rather than an expansion.

_Fix:_ Either cut the sidebar and fold its one new insight ("It would leave Indian Territory one of the most devastated places in the whole country by the time it ended") into the body text, or make the sidebar about something the body text doesn't cover — the long aftermath for Indian Territory specifically. As it stands, it doubles back rather than zooms out.

## Section 3: `out-of-ammunition` — The Guns Fall Silent

**Grade: GOOD**

This section does the hardest structural job in the piece — it has to land the payoff of the ammunition trap, close the battle, account for casualties, and explain what it meant for the Trans-Mississippi. It mostly succeeds, but the ending is the weakest ending in the draft.

### Strengths

The opening of the section is strong: the night-between-days contrast is crisp. Curtis feeding and resupplying his men while the Confederates froze unfed — this is a good beat and the draft uses it well.

The ammunition trap closure works: "This was Van Dorn's clever flank march coming back to kill him." The logic is clear and the payoff is earned — the reader was told the ammunition wagons were stranded in section 2, and here they see why it mattered.

"A 40-round cartridge box doesn't last long in a stand-up fight." This is the right kind of concrete detail — specific, physical, obvious in retrospect.

The casualty section is unusually honest and shows backbone: the draft names Van Dorn's reported numbers, explains why historians don't believe them, and names the structural reason (attacker shouldn't lose fewer than the defender). "The lopsided arithmetic of his own account is part of how we know he lowballed it" is a nice piece of epistemology in one sentence.

The meaning/consequence paragraph is accurate and the observation that Van Dorn's withdrawal stripped Arkansas naked is underused in most accounts — the draft gives it appropriate weight.

### Weaknesses and Fixes

**The battle's climactic moment — the silence — is not written as a climax.** "Confederate guns fell silent for lack of anything to fire" is a correct sentence and a completely flat one. This is the most ironic single fact of the whole battle: Van Dorn's army didn't break under fire, it ran out of what makes a gun a gun. It stopped fighting because of a planning decision made before anyone got shot. The draft has a good pullquote about it: "The Confederates didn't run out of nerve at Pea Ridge. They ran out of bullets." That's the line. But in the body of the text, the silence is narrated as logistics. Compare how Antietam handles its turning points: Hood's counterattack, the Irish Brigade's absolution, A. P. Hill's arrival in the dust — each one gets an image before the summary.

_Fix:_ Give the silence one image before the summary. A Confederate battery captain telling his crew to stop loading because there's nothing left to load; a rifle quiet because the man holding it is counting forty rounds that are gone; something that puts the reader at a gun with no shell in it. _Not an invented fact — derived directly from what the draft already describes: ammunition exhausted, guns falling silent, crews unable to answer the Union barrage._

**"Around mid-morning, with Sigel's batteries dominating the field, Curtis ordered a coordinated advance"** is narrated as tactical summary and not felt as drama. The advance itself — the moment where everything the two sections have been building to clicks into place — goes by quickly. Antietam's climactic moments are built with the grammar of delay: the reserves that never moved, the bridge that didn't need to be taken, the arrival of A. P. Hill at the last moment. The Pea Ridge coordinated advance is the mirror of those moments. It works because everything aligned for once, and the draft doesn't pause to feel it.

_Fix:_ One sentence before "Curtis ordered a coordinated advance" that earns the moment: the Union guns picking off Confederate batteries one by one while the Confederate guns answered less and less. The sound of the battle shifting. The draft has "knocking out the Confederate cannon one by one" — surface that as the moment the balance tips, not just a prelude to the order. _Not an invented fact._

**The ending is analytic when it should be felt.** "After Pea Ridge, the Trans-Mississippi slid to the margins of the war, and it never came back to the center" is a correct and important conclusion. But the draft ends on a geographic-strategic summary. Compare to how Antietam closes its sections: the bloodiest day section ends "a fight to hold the Union back together became, openly and on the record, a fight to end slavery," and the cornfield section ends "the North kept sending men into the same field, one corps after another, and the field kept handing the men back broken" (italic). Those endings land at the human or moral weight of the events.

Pea Ridge's weight is in the irony — a Confederacy that outmanned Curtis, outschemed him, pulled off a daring night march to get in his rear — beaten because its own cleverest move left its ammunition on the wrong side of the battlefield. Van Dorn's army wasn't defeated by Curtis. It was defeated by Van Dorn.

_Fix:_ The draft almost has this in the pullquote at the end of the battle narrative: "The Confederates didn't run out of nerve at Pea Ridge. They ran out of bullets — left behind by the very march that was supposed to win the battle." That's the ending. Either close the section proper with it (before the casualties and meaning paragraphs), or build the casualty/meaning paragraphs to close there. The final paragraph about the Trans-Mississippi should be penultimate, not the last word — the last word should be the irony of the march, not the cartography of the western theater. _Structural reordering, no new facts._

**The Van Dorn "not defeated, only foiled" detail is buried.** It's currently one sentence in the "What it meant" subsection, sandwiched between the strategic consequence and the order to move east. This is a genuinely interesting piece of character — a man so self-deceiving or defiant that he filed a report claiming he hadn't been beaten at a battle he unambiguously lost. It deserves one more sentence: what it tells us about Van Dorn, or what Richmond made of the report. _Not an invented fact; the detail is in the draft, just underdeveloped._

## Flat/List-Like Passages: Specific Beat Fixes

These are passages that read as list-inventory rather than drama. Each comes with a concrete beat fix using only material the draft already has.

**1. Wilson's Creek-to-Price recap (Section 1, "After Wilson's Creek")**

> "Price's Missourians pushed north, and that September captured a Federal garrison at Lexington, Missouri. For a moment it looked as if Missouri might be pried loose from the Union by force."

This is a timeline note. The "for a moment" tension is named but not felt.

_Beat fix:_ The fact that Price pushed north after Wilson's Creek without McCulloch — because the two men couldn't agree — is the structural irony. Let the reader feel the moment: Price doing alone what the two of them might have done together, and then being forced back alone. One sentence that links the two things. "Price pushed north without him — and was forced south again, without him, when no reinforcements came. They had everything they needed to take Missouri, and they wouldn't share a command long enough to try."

**2. Van Dorn's order of battle (Section 1, "Van Dorn arrives")**

> "At Fayetteville, Van Dorn assembled roughly 16,000 troops: about 8,000 under McCulloch, about 7,000 Missourians under Price, plus a force of Native American troops from Indian Territory (the federal land set aside for Native nations, in what is now Oklahoma) under Brig. Gen. Albert Pike (South)."

This is a numbers list. The sentence does its factual job but drops the reader into a logistics table.

_Beat fix:_ The structure of the assembly mirrors the story's coming conflicts: two generals who'd spent months fighting each other, now in the same army under a third man who'd just arrived. "For the first time in the campaign, Price and McCulloch answered to the same general — and they answered to him because they had failed each other." The numbers can follow; they land harder after the irony is named.

**3. Day 1 nightfall summary (Section 3, "The Guns Fall Silent" opening)**

> "The first day had ended in a way that should have terrified Curtis. Over on the eastern side, Price's Missourians had hammered Carr's outnumbered division back through four successive defensive lines and seized Elkhorn Tavern and the high ground around it by nightfall — the deepest the Confederates would get."

"The deepest the Confederates would get" is a spoiler delivered as a tactical note. It robs the overnight dread of its weight.

_Beat fix:_ Hold the parenthetical foreknowledge and let the overnight sit with Curtis's actual problem. "That night, if you were Curtis, the math looked bad: Price had broken through to the east and held the high ground; the western wing was wrecked; and across the whole field, the men who hadn't fallen kept falling asleep where they stood, burned through everything they'd carried." Move "the deepest the Confederates would get" to after the Day 2 description, where it belongs — as the payoff, not the preview. _Structural reordering only._

**4. The coordinated advance (Section 3)**

> "Around mid-morning, with Sigel's batteries dominating the field, Curtis ordered a coordinated advance — a synchronized artillery barrage and infantry assault across his whole line at once."

Correct and flat. The order-giving is described, not the weight of the moment.

_Beat fix:_ Name what Curtis was reading. "By mid-morning, the Confederate return fire had gone thin — batteries that had answered Sigel's guns at dawn were quiet now, or barely answering. Curtis read the quiet and sent the order: the whole line, together, at once." The silence is the cue; the advance is the response to it. Makes the timing feel like a decision rather than a scheduled event. _Not an invented fact — the draft already describes the Confederate guns going silent before the advance is ordered._

## Voice Check

Overall, the voice is in the right register. The informal tone is present throughout and never slips into the academic. Some specific things that work and should be kept:

- "Van Dorn threw a good part of that advantage away before the battle even began" — the word "threw" is exactly right.
- "Half of Van Dorn's army had been decapitated and pulled itself off the field in the space of a single morning" — this is the level to write to.
- "The result was that the entire western wing devolved onto Pike, the Indian Territory commander, a lawyer and poet with no real combat experience" — "devolved" is the right word.
- "A 40-round cartridge box doesn't last long in a stand-up fight" — this is the physical specificity that makes the reader feel it.

One voice note worth flagging: the "Meanwhile" sidebars are consistently good, but the Section 1 Missouri sidebar ("Meanwhile in Missouri | The slave state on the knife's edge") overlaps significantly with the framing already established in the opening paragraph. The opening paragraph names Missouri as a border state, names the rival governments, names the Confederate Congress seats, names St. Louis and the Mississippi. The sidebar repeats much of this. Consider whether the sidebar earns its separate existence or whether it should be folded into the opening.

---

## Summary

**Overall: GOOD.** The draft earns the material and uses its most striking facts. The three-section structure works. The voice is right. The section that needs the most attention is `out-of-ammunition`: the climactic silence of the Confederate guns is the most ironic and resonant single fact in the battle, and the draft narrates it as logistics rather than drama. The highest-impact fix is to give that silence one physical image — a gun with nothing to put in it — and then close the section on Van Dorn's own march as the thing that beat him. The ending is currently the Trans-Mississippi cartography; it should be the irony.
