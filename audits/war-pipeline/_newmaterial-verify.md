## cotton-diplomacy-final.md

### 1. Seward's Dispatch No. 10 (May 21, 1861) + Lincoln softening it + "wrap the world in flames"
✅ CONFIRMED. The dispatch is dated May 21, 1861, instructing Adams to break off relations if Britain dealt further with Confederate agents. The verbatim fragment "shall cease to be friends and become once more … enemies of Great Britain" is confirmed by the Library of Congress digital scan (loc.gov/item/mal1000900/) and by contemporaneous scholarship (JALA, NEH, civilwarmonths.com). Lincoln did personally review and soften the language, and instructed Adams to share it selectively (verbally to Russell, not in writing). The "wrap the whole world in flames" posture is confirmed by multiple sources (Voltaire Network / Webster Tarpley; JALA review of Kevin Peraino's Lincoln in the World) describing Seward "repeatedly told astonished diplomats and journalists that he would 'wrap the world in flame' if any European power dare lend aid to the rebellion."

**One minor precision note:** Search results show Seward's exact inflammatory phrase as "we from that hour, shall cease to be friends and become once more, as we have twice before been forced to be, enemies of Great Britain." The prose uses "shall cease to be friends and become once more … enemies of Great Britain," which is a clean, accurate ellipsis of this. ✅

### 2. ~4 million enslaved people / "more than one in eight people"
✅ CONFIRMED. 1860 census: 3,953,760 enslaved (U.S. Census Bureau; Wikipedia 1860 United States census). Total population ~31.4 million → enslaved = ~12.6%, i.e., more than one in eight. The prose says "roughly four million" and "more than one in eight people in the entire country." Both accurate.

### 3. Prince Albert softened the Trent ultimatum from his deathbed
✅ CONFIRMED. Albert, gravely ill with typhoid (died December 14, 1861), read the draft ultimatum, judged it too belligerent, rose from his sickbed and softened it — building in the face-saving "Wilkes acted without authority" exit. Multiple sources (Wikipedia "Albert, Prince Consort"; Wikipedia "Trent Affair"; biography.com; europeanroyalhistory.wordpress.com) describe this as his "last great diplomatic contribution" and effectively "his last public act." The prose says he "rose from his sickbed" to soften the wording and that it was "the last thing he ever did in public life." ✅

**VERDICT: CLEAN** (0 errors)

## ironclads-final.md

### 1. Confederate cargo-fraction "New Plan" (1/3 late 1863 → 1/2 by March 1864)
✅ CONFIRMED. The Encyclopedia of Alabama ("Blockade Running in the Civil War") and other sources confirm the Confederate government required runners to reserve one-third of cargo space for government war supplies, raised to one-half by March 1864 (under plan supervisor Colin J. McRae); enforced at Wilmington and Charleston, largely ignored along the Gulf. The prose matches this precisely.

### 2. CSS Virginia's 10-gun armament: 6 nine-inch Dahlgren smoothbores + 4 Brooke rifles
✅ CONFIRMED with clarification. Sources confirmed by Wikipedia "CSS Virginia" and The Mariners' Museum: The battery was 10 guns total — six 9-inch Dahlgren smoothbores + four Brooke rifles (two 7-inch pivot rifles + two 6.4-inch broadside rifles). The prose says "six 9-inch Dahlgren smoothbores and four Brooke rifles" — this is accurate as a summary formulation. The reviser's own note flags the cross-source variance on the Brooke calibers but correctly uses the "six Dahlgrens + four Brooke rifles" formulation, which is consistent with all sources. ✅

**VERDICT: CLEAN** (0 errors)

## new-way-of-war-final.md

### 1. Gatling gun at Petersburg — framed as CONTESTED, not asserted as verified "first combat use"
✅ CONFIRMED (framing is correct). The prose explicitly says: "Whether they truly fired on the enemy is genuinely uncertain — there is no solid documentation that any Gatling saw real combat in the war, and some historians suspect Butler's guns were a different rapid-fire model entirely." HistoryNet ("Dr. Gatling's Wonder Weapon") confirms: "no verifiable proof" that Gatlings saw Civil War combat (William B. Edwards); Butler "neglects to mention this colorful use" despite being notoriously self-promoting; Butler definitely obtained ten Agar Coffee Mill Guns from Washington Arsenal in February 1864, and whether his Gatlings (purchased privately) actually fired in combat remains disputed. The "spring of 1865" / "June 1864" dating discrepancy noted in the ledger is correctly handled by NOT asserting a specific date. Framing is honest and accurate. ✅

### 2. Mark Neely as the most persistent critic of the "total war" label
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia ("Mark E. Neely Jr.") and ResearchGate confirm: Neely published "Was the Civil War a Total War?" in Civil War History (March 1991), described as "one of the three most influential articles on the war written in the last half of the 20th century," with the answer being "no." He is well-established as the leading academic critic of applying the total-war label. ✅

### 3. Harper's Weekly carried wood-engraved versions of Gardner's Antietam photos
✅ CONFIRMED. This is a standard, well-established historical fact corroborated by multiple Civil War photography sources. The prose states "wood-engraved copies of the images later ran in Harper's Weekly, carrying the shock past the gallery doors to readers across the country." ✅

## home-front-final.md

### 1. Confederate substitute prices: $800–$1,500, up to ~$3,000 in specie
✅ CONFIRMED. NCpedia ("Substitutes (Civil War)") states: "prices ranged as high as $3,000 in specie and even higher in Confederate currency," with typical $800–$1,500 range corroborated. The prose: "A substitute in the South typically cost between $800 and $1,500, and as the war went on the going rate ran as high as $3,000 in specie (gold and silver coin)." ✅

### 2. Frederick Law Olmsted as "Secretary-General" of the Sanitary Commission
⚠️ MINOR ISSUE. Multiple authoritative sources (NPS, Wikipedia, HistoryNet, NY Academy of Medicine) consistently use "General Secretary" or "Executive Secretary" — NOT "Secretary-General." The reviser's fact-check notes say it was corrected TO "Secretary-General" (from "executive secretary"), citing Wikipedia and NPS. However, my independent search finds NPS uses "Executive Secretary" for Olmsted in at least one article; the most common form in search results is "General Secretary." "Secretary-General" is used at least once in some sources but is the minority rendering. The medicine-final.md section correctly renders him as "executive secretary" (which is also attested). The two sections use different titles for the same man: home-front calls him "first Secretary-General," medicine-final calls him "executive secretary."

**Assessment:** The title as used in home-front-final.md ("Secretary-General") is not wrong — it is one attested formulation — but it differs from the most common sources (which say "General Secretary") and from how medicine-final.md renders the same fact ("executive secretary"). This is an internal inconsistency and a minor precision issue, not a factual error. The substance is correct.

### 3. Ladies' Aid Societies — South had local societies that never combined into a national organization due to Southern localism
✅ CONFIRMED. Essential Civil War Curriculum ("Women and Soldiers' Aid Societies") confirms: "Given the longstanding distrust of centralization and persistent attitude of localism in the South, no overarching organization like the Sanitary Commission developed there"; Southern societies stayed local/state-level. ✅

**VERDICT: CLEAN** — the Olmsted title variation is a minor formatting inconsistency, not a factual error. (0 ❌)

## medicine-final.md

### 1. "Great Civil War Benefaction" (1862), $75/leg, $50/arm, patents 34→133, James Hanger, Confederate vets excluded
✅ CONFIRMED. National Museum of Civil War Medicine (civilwarmed.org/prosthetics) and ASME ("Civil War and the Birth of the US Prosthetics Industry") confirm all specific details:
- "Great Civil War Benefaction" launched 1862 ✅
- $75 for an artificial leg, $50 for an arm ✅
- 34 patents (1845–1861) → 133 patents (1861–1873) ✅
- James Hanger (Confederate amputee, lost left leg above knee at Philippi, June 3, 1861 — the war's first land action) became a prosthetics manufacturer ✅
- "Because they had engaged in rebellion, Confederate veterans were not eligible for federal subsidies for artificial limbs" ✅

### 2. Susie King Taylor — 33rd USCT, nurse, smallpox, Clara Barton, Sea Islands, only Black woman to publish Civil War memoir
✅ CONFIRMED. NPS, American Battlefield Trust, Wikipedia, and Clara Barton Museum all confirm: Taylor served with the 1st South Carolina Volunteers (later 33rd USCT) for four years and three months, unpaid; enrolled as laundress but taught reading and nursed; walked into a smallpox outbreak because she had been vaccinated; met and worked with Clara Barton in the Sea Islands; first (and only) Black woman to publish a Civil War memoir (Reminiscences of My Life in Camp, 1902). ✅

### 3. USCT disease-death disparity: ~148 vs. ~88 per 1,000
✅ CONFIRMED. Encyclopedia of Arkansas ("Disease during the Civil War") and PMC/NIH sources confirm: USCT disease-death mortality ~148 per 1,000 vs. ~88 per 1,000 for white Union troops. ✅

### 4. 256 enslaved workers at Chimborazo (1864), hired from owners, wages to slaveholder
✅ CONFIRMED. Multiple sources (American Battlefield Trust, NLM "Binding Wounds, Pushing Boundaries," History of American Women blog) confirm: Dr. James B. McCaw (superintendent) reported using 256 enslaved people to care for patients in 1864; they were hired from owners under contract, with wages paid to the slaveholders, not the workers. ✅

### 5. Pvt. Newton Robert Scott, 36th Iowa Infantry, "Dieing Every Day"
✅ CONFIRMED. civilwarletters.com confirms Scott was a Private / Company A clerk in the 36th Iowa Infantry, from Albia, Monroe County, Iowa; the verbatim "Dieing Every Day" phrase is confirmed in his letters to Hannah Cone; he survived, married Hannah, and died at 83 (1925). ✅

**VERDICT: CLEAN** (0 errors)

## usct-final.md

### 1. Robert Smalls / CSS *Planter* (May 13, 1862)
✅ CONFIRMED. Smithsonian Magazine, Post and Courier, PBS, and multiple authoritative sources confirm: Before dawn on May 13, 1862, Smalls and enslaved crew took the *Planter*, stopped to pick up family members, steamed past Confederate checkpoints (including Fort Sumter) with Smalls wearing the captain's straw hat and giving the correct coded signals, then raised a white sheet and surrendered the vessel to the Union blockade — all within under four hours. The "white bedsheet" detail: confirmed by multiple sources (Smithsonian uses "white bed sheet"). Smalls later piloted for the Union Navy and served in Congress. ✅

### 2. Black sailors ~16–20% of the Union Navy
✅ CONFIRMED. Naval History and Heritage Command, Gilder Lehrman Institute, and NPS sources confirm: Black men averaged ~16% of naval personnel over the war, with 1863 figures around 20%, rising to a peak above 20% by late 1864. ~18,000–19,000 total served (some estimates range as high as 20,000). The prose uses "roughly 16 to 20 percent" — accurate and appropriately hedged. ✅

### 3. Douglass citizenship quote — verbatim wording
✅ CONFIRMED with one minor note. The Frederick Douglass Papers Project (item 9183) confirms the July 6, 1863 Philadelphia speech as the source. The confirmed wording is: "Once let the black man get upon his person the brass letters, U.S., let him get an eagle on his button, and a musket on his shoulder and bullets in his pocket, and there is no power on the earth or under the earth which can deny that he has earned the right of citizenship in the United States."

The prose renders it as: "Once let the black man get upon his person the brass letters, U.S., let him get an eagle on his button, and a musket on his shoulder and bullets in his pocket, and there is no power on earth or under the earth which can deny that he has earned the right of citizenship in the United States."

The only difference: the Douglass Papers read "on the earth or under the earth" (with the article "the" before "earth") while the prose has "on earth or under the earth" (no article before the first "earth"). The reviser's own ledger notes this as "a trivial article difference." This is ✅ essentially CONFIRMED — the phrasing is substantively correct and the difference (one missing article) is negligible.

### 4. Dutch Gap Canal (August 1864)
✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia ("Dutch Gap Canal") and American Battlefield Trust ("USCTs at Dutch Gap") confirm: Canal construction was ordered by Butler (Army of the James) beginning August 1864, near Richmond, to allow gunboats to bypass Confederate batteries on the James River. USCT regiments performed the bulk of the labor under Confederate sniper and artillery/mortar fire throughout the operation (August 1864–January 1865). ✅

### 5. Gooding as "New Bedford whaler"
✅ CONFIRMED (with important background context that the reviser handled correctly). Wikipedia and NPS confirm: Gooding was born enslaved in North Carolina but his freedom was purchased in infancy; he grew up in New York City, then worked as a whaler out of New Bedford from 1856. He later told people he was born free in Troy, NY. The prose calls him "a New Bedford whaler" — accurate and deliberately chosen (the reviser's ledger explicitly notes Gooding should NOT be called "formerly enslaved" because his freedom was purchased in infancy and he was raised free). The characterization is correct. ✅

**VERDICT: CLEAN** (0 errors, 1 trivial article-word variant in the Douglass quote)
