# FACT PACK — "An Army from Nothing" (1775–1783)

*American Revolution vertical, WAR-YEARS Off-the-Battlefield theme chapter (route slug `army-from-nothing`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. This is a SOCIETY/INSTITUTION story — how the Continental Army was built, who filled it, what held it together, and how close it came to falling apart — NOT a campaign story. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against the cited sources (Founders Online, Mount Vernon digital encyclopedia, NPS, Library of Congress, American Battlefield Trust, National Museum of the U.S. Army, Journal of the American Revolution; cross-checked against Wikipedia); confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges and "flag" guidance for contested specifics.*

**Boundaries with sibling chapters:** battle narratives belong to the spine/battle pages (Bunker Hill, Trenton, Monmouth, Yorktown may be *referenced* as proof-points, never narrated). Women with the army (camp followers, Molly Pitcher) belong to **rev-women** — here you may note in ONE beat that women traveled with and were rationed by the army, then stop. The Black soldiers' *story* (Dunmore's proclamation, the 1st Rhode Island as narrative) belongs to **rev-slavery** — this chapter carries only the integration-of-the-ranks *facts*. The French army belongs to **rev-french-alliance**.

**Orientation.** In April 1775 there was no American army — there were militias: every able-bodied man with a musket, mustering a few days a year, superb for one desperate day (Lexington and Concord) and structurally useless for a war. The crowd of New Englanders that penned the British inside Boston was an army only by courtesy. On June 14, 1775 the Continental Congress adopted it — the birthday of the United States Army — and the next day unanimously picked the one delegate who had been wearing a uniform to Congress all spring: George Washington of Virginia. What followed was an eight-year experiment in keeping an army alive inside a society that distrusted standing armies, ruled by a Congress that could ask for money but not tax for it. The army dissolved and re-recruited *in front of the enemy* — twice. The patriotic fever of 1775 (the *rage militaire*) burned out within a year, and the long-war army that replaced it was drawn overwhelmingly from the young, the poor, and the landless, including thousands of Black soldiers serving in integrated regiments — a degree of integration the U.S. Army would not see again until the Korean War. The soldiers were paid in a currency that collapsed to nothing ("not worth a Continental"), supplied by a system that broke down spectacularly (Valley Forge, and the worse but less famous Morristown winter), drilled into a real army by a charming Prussian with an invented résumé, and pushed to open mutiny three times in 1780–81. It ended in 1783 not with a parade but with quiet furloughs, back pay in near-worthless paper, and one extraordinary moment at Newburgh when Washington put on his spectacles and talked his officers out of turning on the civilian government. The chapter's recurring eyewitness is Private Joseph Plumb Martin, who enlisted at fifteen and wrote it all down — fifty years later.

## 1. WHAT EXISTED IN APRIL 1775 — THE MILITIA WORLD

- **The militia tradition.** In Massachusetts (and similarly across New England), **every able-bodied man roughly 16 to 60** was required to serve in his town militia, mustering for inspection or training **at least a few days a year** (Massachusetts required at least six training days). The militia was a community institution — neighbors, elected or locally appointed officers, men with farms and trades to return to. (HIGH)
- **Minutemen** — about **one quarter of the militia** in Massachusetts was set apart (from late 1774) as "minute men," younger and more mobile, training far more often (two to three sessions a week was common), ready to turn out "at a minute's warning." (HIGH)
- **Fine for a day, useless for a war.** The militia's structural limits: short obligations measured in days or weeks, no logistics, no sustained discipline, officers chosen by popularity, and men who legally went home when their term ended or the harvest called. April 19, 1775 was the system working exactly as designed — thousands of armed neighbors swarming a British column for one day. A multi-year war was precisely what it was *not* designed for. (HIGH — frame this as design, not cowardice; the militia kept mattering all war as a surge force and home guard.)
- **The army-by-courtesy at Boston.** After Lexington and Concord, New England militiamen stayed and besieged Boston. The force was large — commonly given as roughly **16,000–20,000+ New Englanders** (counts fluctuated daily as men came and went; ~22,000 is cited at the moment of adoption in June). It had no unified command structure beyond inter-colonial cooperation (Artemas Ward of Massachusetts in loose overall charge), enlistments only to the end of 1775, little artillery, almost no gunpowder, and camp sanitation that appalled everyone who saw it. (HIGH on the character; MED on any single headcount — use a range, never one number.)
- **The deep prejudice against standing armies.** British regulars occupying Boston were a living argument, in colonial eyes, for why permanent professional armies endangered liberty. This ideology — armies of free citizen-volunteers, short enlistments, civilian control — shaped (and hobbled) every decision Congress made about its own army for eight years. (HIGH — this is the chapter's structural antagonist alongside poverty.)

## 2. CONGRESS ADOPTS AN ARMY; WASHINGTON CHOSEN (JUNE 1775)

- **June 14, 1775** — the Second Continental Congress voted to adopt the New England forces besieging Boston (plus forces near New York) as a **Continental Army**, and authorized **ten companies of expert riflemen** from Pennsylvania (6), Maryland (2), and Virginia (2) as the first troops raised directly under continental authority. **June 14 is celebrated as the birthday of the U.S. Army.** (HIGH)
- **June 15, 1775** — Congress **unanimously elected George Washington** commander in chief; **John Adams** made the nomination speech (Samuel Adams seconded). Washington formally accepted on **June 16**. (HIGH)
- **Why Washington** (all HIGH):
  - **Virginia.** A New England army with a Virginian at its head turned a regional quarrel into a continental cause. Adams later recalled telling Congress of a "Gentleman from Virginia who was among Us" who "would command the Approbation of all America, and unite the cordial Exertions of all the Colonies better than any other Person in the Union." (HIGH — Adams's wording is from his own later autobiography/diary; present as Adams's recollection.)
  - **Experience.** Washington had commanded the Virginia Regiment in the French and Indian War — about as much military command experience as any native-born American had.
  - **The uniform.** Washington attended the Second Continental Congress (from May 1775) **wearing a military uniform — the only delegate to do so** — the buff-and-blue of the Fairfax Independent Company. It was a deliberate, silent signal of Virginia's (and his own) readiness to fight. (HIGH — well attested; recent scholarship debates exactly *which* uniform and how calculated the theater was, but "he wore a uniform to Congress and was the only delegate to do so" is solid.)
- **He refused pay.** In his June 16 acceptance, Washington declined any salary and asked only that Congress **reimburse his expenses**. He kept meticulous expense accounts for the whole war (the famous ledger survives; the National Archives and the Washington Papers' financial project both hold/transcribe it). (HIGH on the refusal + expense arrangement. CARE: do NOT assert a total dollar figure for the expense account — sources give conflicting totals, e.g. ~$160,074 vs ~$449,261, reflecting different accountings/currencies. If a number is wanted, say "his wartime expenses ran to six figures in the dollars of the day" or omit.)
- **July 3, 1775** — Washington took command of the army at **Cambridge, Massachusetts**. (HIGH)

## 3. CAMBRIDGE — AND THE ARMY THAT DISSOLVED IN FRONT OF THE ENEMY (TWICE)

- **What he found:** a brave, ill-disciplined, unsanitary, gunpowder-poor encampment whose entire enlistment base expired with the calendar year. (HIGH)
- **"An exceeding dirty & nasty people."** To his cousin/estate manager **Lund Washington, 20 August 1775**, Washington wrote that the New England privates **"would fight very well (if properly Officered) although they are an exceeding dirty & nasty people."** In the same letter he savaged the officers: **"their Officers generally speaking are the most indifferent kind of People I ever saw… I have already broke one Colo. and five Captain's for Cowardice, & for drawing more Pay & Provision's than they had Men in their Companies."** (HIGH — verbatim, Founders Online. CONTEXT: a private letter, a Virginia planter's snobbery toward Yankee egalitarianism — officers elected by and indistinguishable from their men. Use it for what it reveals about the culture clash, and note he was simultaneously praising their willingness to fight.)
- **Dissolution #1 — December 31, 1775.** Virtually the whole army's enlistments expired at year's end (men could begin leaving December 1; everyone could leave January 1). By late fall the leakage was severe — Nathanael Greene lost about three-quarters of his brigade. Washington to Joseph Reed, **28 November 1775**: **"Could I have foreseen what I have, & am like to experience, no consideration upon Earth should have induced me to accept this Command."** (HIGH — verbatim)
- **The unprecedented maneuver:** discharge one army and recruit another **within musket shot of the enemy**. Washington to John Hancock: **"to maintain a post within Musket Shot of the Enemy for Six months together… and at the same time to disband one Army and recruit another, within that distance, of Twenty odd British regiments, is probably more than ever was attempted."** (HIGH — verbatim; cited in NPS and New England Historical Society accounts.) Militia were called in to plug the gap; re-enlistment was slow and humiliating; by March 1776 strength had recovered — and the British, never learning how hollow the lines opposite them were, evacuated Boston that month. (HIGH)
- **Dissolution #2 — December 31, 1776.** The army raised for 1776 *also* expired at year's end — this time mid-campaign, days after Trenton, with the war's survival visibly at stake. Washington personally appealed to veteran regiments to stay **six weeks beyond their enlistments for a $10 bounty** (money he pledged on his own authority; Congress backed it). Enough stayed to fight at Princeton. (HIGH on the expiration + bounty appeal; the famous verbatim "My brave fellows…" speech text is a sergeant's much-later recollection — do NOT quote it as verbatim. The *fact* of the appeal is solid.) (CARE — narrate as institutional crisis, not as a Trenton/Princeton battle story.)
- This twice-repeated near-death is what finally pushed Congress toward long enlistments (§5). (HIGH)

## 4. WHO ACTUALLY SERVED — THE SOCIAL HISTORY (the heart of the chapter)

- ***Rage militaire*, 1775.** Historian Charles Royster's term for the "passion for arms" that swept the colonies in 1775 — the fever that put tens of thousands of ordinary householders briefly under arms. **It was short-lived; by 1776 it had effectively vanished and never returned.** The fantasy it carried — a short war won by virtuous citizen-soldiers — died with it. (HIGH — Royster, *A Revolutionary People at War*, 1979)
- **The long-war army was a poor man's army.** As enthusiasm faded and terms lengthened, the men with farms, shops, and families increasingly stayed home (or served short militia stints), and the Continental ranks filled from the bottom of the white social ladder — plus free and enslaved Black men. Scholarship built on muster rolls, tax lists, and pension files (Mark Edward Lender on the New Jersey Brigade; Edward Papenfuse & Gregory Stiverson on Maryland recruits; John Shy; Royster) converges on a profile: (HIGH on the overall pattern)
  - **Young** — typical privates were in their late teens and early twenties; studies and sampled muster rolls put common ages around 17–24, with averages on particular rolls in the low twenties. (MED on any precise average — give "late teens and early twenties," not one number.)
  - **Poor and landless** — in the studied samples, the great majority of long-service privates were drawn from the propertyless or near-propertyless; a frequently cited finding is that **under ~10% owned taxable property**. (MED — these are regional studies generalized; phrase as "studies of the rolls suggest," not census fact.)
  - **Many immigrants, servants, and laborers** — substantial shares of Irish-born and German-born men in several state lines (figures like "a quarter Irish, an eighth German" circulate from the scholarship); indentured servants and even some convicts and British deserters were accepted as the war ground on. (MED — keep proportions soft.)
  - **The yeoman-minuteman image is a myth for the Continental Line.** The small farmer who grabbed the musket over the fireplace describes April 1775 and militia surges — NOT the men who stood in the Line in 1778–83. (HIGH as historiographic correction.)
- **Black soldiers and integration (facts only — story owned by rev-slavery):**
  - An official headcount, the **"Return of the Negroes in the Army," 24 August 1778** (compiled under Adjutant General **Alexander Scammell**), counted **755 Black soldiers serving in 14–15 brigades of Washington's main army** — about **3.6% of that force's rank and file**; in the brigades where they served they were typically ~5–8% of enlisted men. The return did not include the Rhode Island or New Jersey regiments (adding those brings the snapshot to ~950). (HIGH — the document survives; "fourteen" vs "fifteen" brigades varies by transcription, keep loose.)
  - Over the whole war, an estimated **5,000–8,000 men of African descent served on the American side**. (MED — estimate range; use the range.)
  - **They served integrated** — Black and white privates in the same companies, messes, and tents, the **1st Rhode Island Regiment being the notable segregated exception** (Black rank and file, white officers). (HIGH)
  - **The "until Korea" claim — precise form:** the Continental Army was the **last time soldiers of African descent served in racially integrated regular U.S. Army units at any scale until the Korean War era** (desegregation was ordered by Executive Order 9981 in 1948 and substantially implemented during Korea, 1950–53). This form is supported by the U.S. Army's own publications, the American Battlefield Trust, and the Museum of the American Revolution. (HIGH in this form — see HANDLE-WITH-CARE #4 for what NOT to say.)
- **How the ranks were filled after 1776:** cash and land **bounties** (escalating wildly — see §5), **state quotas** under which states drafted from the militia but allowed a drafted man to hire a **substitute** (so the poor frequently served in the place of the propertied), and re-enlistment bonuses. By 1780–81 New Jersey was offering recruits bounties valued near **$1,000** (depreciated dollars) while Pennsylvania's three-year veterans had gotten **$20** in 1776–77 — a disparity that helped detonate the Pennsylvania Line mutiny (§10). (HIGH on mechanisms; MED on specific bounty figures — keep as illustrative contrast.)
- **Camp followers — ONE BEAT ONLY (boundary with rev-women):** women (soldiers' wives and others) traveled with the army throughout, doing laundry, nursing, and sutling, and were officially carried on the ration rolls. Say that, and stop — the story is rev-women's. (HIGH)
- **Scale of service overall:** about **230,000 men served in the Continental Army at some point** across the war (with roughly another ~145,000 militia enlistments alongside — overlapping, since many men did both). (MED — totals are scholarly estimates; use "about"/"roughly.")

## 5. TERMS, ORGANIZATION, AND SIZE

- **One-year armies (1775, 1776).** The army was twice built on enlistments expiring December 31 — the catastrophic design flaw of §3. (HIGH)
- **The "Eighty-eight Battalion Resolve," 16 September 1776.** Congress voted a standing establishment of **88 battalions (~60,000 men on paper)**, apportioned to the states by population, with enlistments for **"three years or during the war."** Inducements: a **$20 cash bounty** and **100 acres of land** for privates and NCOs who enlisted for the duration (land scaled up by rank: ensign 150, lieutenant 200, captain 300, major 400, lieutenant colonel 450, colonel 500 acres). (HIGH)
- **"Three years or the war"** became the backbone term of the post-1777 army — and the ambiguity of that phrase ("three years OR the war — whichever the enlister meant") became the legal core of the Pennsylvania mutiny (§10). (HIGH)
- **The Continental Line.** Each state raised, clothed, and (in theory) paid its own numbered regiments — "the Pennsylvania Line," "the Connecticut Line" — which together formed the Continental Line under Washington and Congress. The states' uneven performance at supplying their own lines created the inequities that fed the mutinies. (HIGH)
- **Paper vs. reality.** The 88-battalion establishment was never close to filled. A Continental regiment's paper strength was ~580–750; field regiments commonly mustered **half or less**. The establishment was cut to 80 battalions (1779), then to ~58–60 regiments for the last years. (HIGH on the pattern; MED on exact establishment counts.)
- **Size over time:** about **230,000 total served**, but **never more than ~48,000 under arms at any one moment, and never more than ~13,000 in one place** (American Battlefield Trust / NMUSA figures). Annual strength peaked around **1776–78** (one standard tabulation has ~89,000 enrolled in 1776 counting militia, and main-army Continental strength peaking near 35,000 in 1778); by the war's end the whole establishment was a fraction of its paper size. (MED — every strength series differs in what it counts; ALWAYS give ranges and the paper-vs-effective caveat. Safe formulations: "on paper… in reality…", "never more than about 48,000 at once.")

## 6. PAY, MONEY, AND SUPPLY — NAME THE MECHANISM, NOT JUST THE SUFFERING

- **The root cause:** under the Articles-of-Confederation-era arrangements, **Congress could not tax.** It could requisition the states (who chronically under-delivered) and it could print money. It printed. (HIGH)
- **A private's pay** was set at **$6⅔ per month** (and was routinely months — eventually years — in arrears, and paid, when paid, in depreciating paper). (MED on the exact figure phrasing; the arrears are HIGH.)
- **The Continental dollar.** Congress emitted some **$200–240 million** in Continental currency, 1775–1779 (it voted to stop the presses at $200 million in late 1779; total emissions including reissues are counted variously). With no tax revenue behind it, plus state paper inflating alongside and aggressive British counterfeiting, it slid: roughly **3:1 against hard money in 1777, ~6–8:1 in 1778, ~40:1 by early 1780**. In **March 1780 Congress itself officially devalued at 40:1**. By **early 1781** street rates ran **75:1 to 100:1+** (well past 100:1 in some places — figures up to 146:1 and beyond appear for spring 1781 by locality), and in **May 1781 the currency simply stopped circulating**. (HIGH on the trajectory and the March 1780 official 40:1; MED on any single year-rate — USE RANGES. The depreciation schedule differed by state and month; never print one clean table as fact.)
- **"Not worth a Continental"** — the collapse minted a genuine American idiom for worthlessness; it long outlived the war. (HIGH as idiom; its exact first attestation is not pinned — present as "Americans started saying," not as a dated coinage.)
- **What this meant for soldiers:** their pay shrank to nearly nothing *while they held it*; their enlistment bounties were honored in the same melting paper; civilians (reasonably) preferred selling food to the British for hard coin or to anyone but the army for promissory certificates. The soldier wasn't just unpaid — he was paid in a substance evaporating in his pocket. (HIGH — this framing is the chapter's economic spine.)
- **The supply machine and how it broke (Valley Forge winter as the case study):**
  - **Quartermaster General Thomas Mifflin**, soured on the job and busy with politics, **effectively stopped performing his duties after mid-1777 and resigned in October/November 1777** — so the army went into winter quarters with the department **leaderless and in chaos**: no forage system, broken wagon contracts, supplies rotting at depots for want of transport. (HIGH)
  - The **Clothier General's department** (James Mease) failed equally — clothing existed in warehouses that never reached the men. (MED on Mease specifics; HIGH that clothing distribution, not absolute scarcity alone, was a core failure.)
  - **Congress, unable to pay, kept trying to buy supplies with certificates** (IOUs) farmers learned to dodge; price controls made it worse. (HIGH)
  - **Fix #1:** **Nathanael Greene was appointed Quartermaster General (March 1778)** — notified March 2, in duty by March 23 — and audited, reorganized, pre-positioned depots, and got the trains moving. (HIGH)
  - **Fix #2 (which created the next failure):** in **1780** Congress, broke, shifted to the **"system of specific supplies"** — requisitioning actual goods (beef, flour, rum) from the states by quota. The states under-delivered; the 1779–81 hunger crises (Morristown, the 1780–81 mutinies) sit directly on this mechanism. (HIGH)
  - The honest summary: **the men starved because the SYSTEM was broken — congressional poverty, a leaderless quartermaster corps, dead currency, and state quotas nobody enforced — not because food didn't exist in America.** (HIGH — this is the required "name the mechanism" beat.)

## 7. VALLEY FORGE (DECEMBER 19, 1777 – JUNE 19, 1778) — HONEST NUMBERS, REAL TRANSFORMATION

- **The basics:** the main army — about **12,000 soldiers** (plus several hundred women and children with the army) — marched into Valley Forge, ~20 miles from British-occupied Philadelphia, on **December 19, 1777**, and marched out **June 19, 1778**. They built some 1,300–2,000 log huts on a defensible plateau. (HIGH; hut counts vary — keep loose or omit.)
- **The honest death toll: roughly 1,700–2,000 dead** over the six months — commonly stated as "as many as 2,000" (some popular sources stretch to 2,500–3,000; stay at ~1,700–2,000). **About one in ten to one in six did not survive.** (MED-HIGH — use the range. NPS uses ~1,700–2,000.)
- **They died of DISEASE, not freezing:** influenza, typhus, typhoid, and dysentery were the killers — and **about two-thirds of the deaths came in the warmer months of March, April, and May 1778**, when crowding, bad water, and camp filth did their work, NOT in the depths of winter. (HIGH — NPS/park records; this inversion of the popular image is a required beat.)
- **The winter itself was actually mild-to-ordinary** by Pennsylvania standards (the catastrophic winter was Morristown two years later, §8). The suffering at Valley Forge was a *supply-system* catastrophe (§6), not a meteorological one. (HIGH)
- **The hunger and nakedness were real:** Washington to Henry Laurens (President of Congress), **23 December 1777**: unless the commissary was fixed, **"this Army must inevitably be reduced to one or other of these three things. Starve—dissolve—or disperse."** Same letter: a field return that day showed **"no less than 2898 Men now in Camp unfit for duty, because they are barefoot and otherwise naked"** — nearly a quarter of the force. (HIGH — verbatim, Founders Online.)
- **The bloody footprints are real but sourced to memory and to the march, not to a frozen tableau:** Washington (to John Banister, **21 April 1778**) wrote of men **"without Shoes, by which their Marches might be traced by the Blood from their feet."** Joseph Plumb Martin recalled men going barefoot **"till they might be tracked by their blood upon the rough frozen ground."** (HIGH on both quotes; see HANDLE-WITH-CARE #1 for how to frame the icon.)
- **Martha Washington was in camp** from February 1778 until the army took the field in June — running headquarters' social/secretarial life, visiting the sick, and (by contemporary testimony) lifting morale. ONE beat; her larger story is rev-women's. (HIGH)
- **Baron von Steuben — the delightful truth (all HIGH unless tagged):**
  - **Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben** arrived at Valley Forge **February 23, 1778**, as an unpaid volunteer. His Prussian career was real but modest: a **captain** and staff officer, out of the army since 1763, unemployed and debt-ridden when the Americans found him; his "Baron" came from later service at a tiny German court, not Prussian nobility of consequence.
  - **Franklin's letter introduced him as "a Lieutenant General in the King of Prussia's service."** The kindest reading: his staff title, rendered in French (*lieutenant général quartier-maître* — deputy quartermaster general), invited the mistranslation; Franklin and Silas Deane, who needed Congress to take their find seriously, did not correct the impression. Congress and Washington received a Prussian lieutenant general; they got a half-pay captain — **who turned out to be exactly what the army needed.** (HIGH on the inflation; the precise mix of mistranslation vs. deliberate embellishment is MED/DISPUTED — tell it as "his credentials were inflated somewhere between Paris and Philadelphia, and nobody who watched him work ever asked for a refund.")
  - **Method:** he started with a **model company** (drawn from Washington's Guard plus drafts from the brigades, ~100–150 men), drilled it personally — a European general sergeant-drilling privates, which astonished everyone — then **cascaded** the trained men outward as instructors to every brigade. He standardized the step, the manual of arms, and above all **bayonet discipline and column-to-line maneuvers**.
  - **He spoke almost no English.** He drilled in French and German with translators (his secretary **Pierre-Étienne Du Ponceau**, and Alexander Hamilton and John Laurens helped render his drills into English); when the men fumbled he swore in German, then French, and when those ran out called for help — the recorded version (via Du Ponceau's circle) has him crying out for an aide to **"come and swear for me in English."** The men loved it. (HIGH on the scene and method; the exact quoted sentence varies by telling — present as reported anecdote, wording approximate. See QUOTES.)
  - **The Blue Book:** in winter 1778–79 he wrote (in French, translated by his aides) the ***Regulations for the Order and Discipline of the Troops of the United States*** — approved by Congress **March 1779**, the U.S. Army's drill manual **until 1814**. (HIGH)
  - He was appointed **Inspector General** (major general) in **May 1778**. (HIGH)
- **Proof it worked:** at **Monmouth, June 28, 1778** — nine days out of Valley Forge — the army maneuvered under fire and traded volleys with British regulars to a standstill. Reference as proof-point only; the battle page owns the story. (HIGH)

## 8. THE OTHER WINTERS — MORRISTOWN 1779–80, WORSE AND LESS FAMOUS

- The army wintered at **Morristown, New Jersey** twice (1777 and 1779–80); the second — the **"Hard Winter" of 1779–80**, with ~10,000–12,000 troops hutted at **Jockey Hollow** — was **the coldest winter of the eighteenth century in the region**: the **only winter on record in which the waters around New York City froze solid** enough to cross with heavy loads, and **some 26–28 snowstorms** swept the camp; a January blizzard left about four feet of snow. (HIGH on "hard winter"/worst-of-century reputation and the harbor freezing; MED on the storm count — "more than two dozen snowstorms" is safe.)
- Washington, spring 1780: **"The oldest people now living in this Country do not remember so hard a winter as the one we are now emerging from."** (HIGH — verbatim)
- Supply was *worse* than Valley Forge (the specific-supplies system, §6, plus the dead currency and snowbound roads); Joseph Plumb Martin's starvation passages (§12) describe **this** winter; the Connecticut Line mutiny (§10) broke out in this camp in May 1780. Yet fewer died than at Valley Forge — partly hard-won competence: better huts, better sanitation, smallpox kept down by inoculation (§9). (HIGH on worse supply + the mutiny link; MED on comparative death tolls — Morristown deaths were in the low hundreds; phrase comparatively, not with a precise count.)
- **Why Valley Forge got the fame anyway:** Valley Forge acquired the redemption arc (suffering → Steuben → Monmouth) and the nineteenth-century mythmakers; Morristown got the worse weather and the mutiny. One sentence of this irony is worth including. (HIGH as historiography.)

## 9. DISCIPLINE, DISEASE, DESERTION — AND THE INOCULATION ORDER

- **The lash.** The original Articles of War (June 1775) capped flogging at the biblical **39 lashes**; the revised Articles of **September 20, 1776** raised the cap to **100 lashes** (against 500–1,000+ possible in the British service). **Washington repeatedly found 100 insufficient and sought authority for up to 500 lashes; Congress never granted it** (and courts-martial sometimes worked around the cap). (HIGH on 39→100 and the British contrast; MED on the 500 request — well-reported via Mount Vernon/scholarship; phrase as "Washington pressed Congress for a 500-lash ceiling and was refused.")
- Capital punishment (hanging or firing squad) was prescribed for mutiny, desertion to the enemy, and the like — used sparingly but pointedly (see §10). (HIGH)
- **Desertion** ran high throughout: the standard scholarly estimate (rooted in Allen Bowman's 1943 study and supported by compilations of deserter advertisements) is an average desertion rate of **20–25%** — roughly **one man in four or five** — concentrated among new recruits, substitutes, and the hungriest seasons. (MED-HIGH — present as the scholarly estimate range.)
- **Disease was the great killer of the war:** for every soldier killed in battle, several died of disease (camp fevers, dysentery, smallpox). (HIGH as ratio-free statement; avoid precise multipliers.)
- **The smallpox inoculation order — a quietly huge call.** On **February 5–6, 1777** (letters to John Hancock and Dr. William Shippen Jr., from Morristown), **Washington ordered the mass inoculation (variolation) of the Continental Army** — every soldier and recruit who had never had smallpox would be deliberately infected with a mild controlled case. (HIGH)
  - Variolation carried real risk (a few percent died of it) and incapacitated men for weeks, so **it was done in secret, in rotations**, lest the British strike an army half-down with deliberate pox. (HIGH)
  - It was arguably **the first mass military immunization program in history**, and it worked: smallpox ceased to be a strategic threat to the army (commonly cited: infection rates falling from ~17% of the army to ~1%). (HIGH on the program and outcome; MED on the 17%→1% figures — use "from rampant to rare" or tag the figures as estimates.)
  - Context beat: smallpox had already wrecked the 1775–76 Canadian expedition — Washington was solving a proven army-killer, not a hypothetical. (HIGH)

## 10. THE BREAKING POINTS — THE MUTINIES OF 1780–81

*(Frame: these were labor strikes by starving, unpaid veterans who kept insisting they were NOT changing sides — and proved it.)*

- **Connecticut Line, May 25, 1780 (Jockey Hollow/Morristown).** After days on fractional rations ("a little musty bread and a little beef about every other day," then nothing — Martin), **two Connecticut regiments paraded under arms without orders**, threatening to march off to extort food. Officers and steady Pennsylvania troops talked/forced them back; in the scuffle **Colonel Return Jonathan Meigs was bayoneted** (he survived). No executions; the grievance was straightforwardly hunger and ~5 months' pay arrears. **Joseph Plumb Martin was a participant-witness** (§12). (HIGH)
- **Pennsylvania Line, January 1, 1781 (Jockey Hollow) — the big one.** Roughly **1,500–2,400 men** of the Pennsylvania Line — long-service veterans held to "three years or the war" readings of their 1776–77 enlistments, paid almost nothing since, on a **$20 bounty** while new recruits got hundreds — killed/wounded several officers in the initial outbreak, elected a **Board of Sergeants**, and marched in good order toward Princeton/Philadelphia to put their case to Congress and Pennsylvania. (HIGH)
  - **They were mutineers, not traitors:** when **General Sir Henry Clinton sent emissaries** (John Mason, guided by James Ogden) offering British pay and pardons, **the mutineers arrested the emissaries** and turned them over; **Mason and Ogden were tried as spies and hanged (January 10–11, 1781)**. (HIGH)
  - **The settlement** (negotiated with Pennsylvania's civilian president **Joseph Reed**, with General **Anthony Wayne** as the army's man on the scene, concluded by late January): men enlisted in 1776–77 for $20 could take **discharge** on their own oath about their term; about **half the Line took discharge** (many re-enlisted later for new bounties), the rest got furloughs/back-pay promises. (HIGH on the shape; MED on exact discharge counts.)
  - **No mutineer was executed.** (HIGH)
- **New Jersey Line, January 20–27, 1781 (Pompton).** ~200 New Jersey soldiers, inspired by the Pennsylvanians' success, mutinied for the same grievances. **This time Washington ordered it crushed:** he sent **General Robert Howe with ~500–600 New England troops**, who surrounded the camp at dawn (January 27), forced unconditional surrender, court-martialed three ringleaders on the spot, and had **two of them — David Gilmore and John Tuttle — shot by firing squads drawn from their fellow mutineers** (the third, George Grant, was reprieved). (HIGH)
  - Washington's logic, stated in his orders/letters: concession had answered the Pennsylvania mutiny; a second successful mutiny would dissolve the army. **"Unless this dangerous spirit can be suppressed by force, there is an end to all subordination in the Army, and indeed to the Army itself."** (HIGH — verbatim from Washington's January 1781 instructions to Howe; widely reproduced. Verify exact wording at integration if quoted: it is from GW's orders to Robert Howe, January 22, 1781.) (MED on the verbatim — safe to paraphrase.)
- The mutinies' real indictment ran against **Congress and the states** — the men's claims were *true*, and everyone, Washington included, said so in private. (HIGH as framing, grounded in his correspondence.)

## 11. NEWBURGH AND THE END (1782–1783)

- **The setup:** with fighting over after Yorktown but peace unsigned, the army sat at **Newburgh/New Windsor, New York** — officers facing demobilization with **years of pay in arrears** and a congressional promise (1780) of **half-pay-for-life pensions** that Congress could not fund and states would not honor. Nationalists in Philadelphia (around Robert Morris, Gouverneur Morris, Hamilton) saw the army's grievance as leverage to force taxing power — a fire they were willing to warm their hands at. (HIGH)
- **The Newburgh Addresses:** on **March 10, 1783** an anonymous paper (written by **Major John Armstrong Jr.**, an aide to General Horatio Gates) circulated, calling a mass officers' meeting and hinting the army should refuse to disband — or refuse to fight — until paid. Washington blocked the irregular meeting and called his own for **March 15, 1783**, in the camp assembly hall (the "Temple"/New Building). (HIGH)
- **March 15, 1783 — the spectacles.** Washington appeared unexpectedly, spoke against the "insidious" anonymous counsel, then fumbled with a letter from a congressman — and put on the reading glasses almost none of them had seen him wear: **"Gentlemen, you will permit me to put on my spectacles, for I have not only grown gray, but almost blind, in the service of my country."** Officers wept; the conspiracy collapsed on the spot; the meeting voted its confidence in Congress. (HIGH on the scene and effect — multiple eyewitnesses. **SOURCING CAVEAT (required if quoted):** the line is NOT in Washington's prepared text; it survives via eyewitness accounts — Samuel Shaw's journal and David Cobb's later recollection — and the wording varies slightly across tellings ("grown gray"/"grown old," "not only… but almost"). Quote it with "as one officer recorded it" framing.)
- Aftermath in one beat: Congress later converted half-pay-for-life to **five years' full pay in "commutation" certificates** — paper that, like everything else, traded far below face. (HIGH)
- **The army dissolves almost unthanked:** preliminary peace ended hostilities in April 1783; in **late May Congress voted to furlough** (not discharge — cheaper, and reversible if peace failed) the men enlisted "for the war," and **on June 13, 1783 the furloughs began**. Soldiers walked home with **three months' pay in "Morris notes"** (Robert Morris's personal-credit paper, ~$800,000 of it) and **final-settlement certificates** for the rest — which most, needing shoes and food for the road, **sold to speculators at a fraction of face value**. Furloughed men were formally **discharged November 3, 1783**; the last British troops left New York November 25; Washington took leave of his officers December 4 and resigned his commission at Annapolis **December 23, 1783**. (HIGH on the sequence; the certificate-speculation beat is HIGH and is the chapter's bitter closing fact. The June 1783 Philadelphia mutiny of new levies that chased Congress to Princeton is OPTIONAL — one clause at most.)
- **Society of the Cincinnati — one beat.** In **May 1783** Continental officers (constitution drafted by **Henry Knox**; Washington as first president general) founded the **Society of the Cincinnati**, a fraternal order named for Cincinnatus — with **membership hereditary in the eldest male line**. The hereditary clause set off a genuine panic about a budding military aristocracy (Aedanus Burke's pamphlet; Jefferson, John Adams, and Franklin all hostile); Washington pushed to abolish the hereditary feature in 1784, the state societies balked, and the reform quietly died. (HIGH — keep to 2–3 sentences; the irony — eight years guarding against a standing army, then a hereditary officers' order — is the point.)

## 12. JOSEPH PLUMB MARTIN — THE CHAPTER'S EYEWITNESS VOICE

- **Who:** born **November 21, 1760** (Becket, Massachusetts; raised by grandparents in Milford, Connecticut). Enlisted **July 1776 at age 15** for a six-month Connecticut levy; re-enlisted **April 1777 for the duration** in the **8th Connecticut Regiment** (Continental Line); later a sapper/miner; served to the **June 1783** furlough — present at the New York campaign, Monmouth, the Connecticut Line mutiny, and Yorktown. Postwar: settled in **Prospect, Maine**; died **May 2, 1850**. (HIGH)
- **The book:** *A Narrative of Some of the Adventures, Dangers and Sufferings of a Revolutionary Soldier*, published **anonymously in 1830** (Hallowell, Maine), rediscovered in the twentieth century (often reprinted as *Private Yankee Doodle* / *Memoir of a Revolutionary Soldier*). It is THE common-soldier source for the war. (HIGH)
- **Reliability caveats (must accompany his use):** written **~47–55 years after the events**, by an old man with a pension claim on file, anonymously, with literary shaping and likely embellishment in places (historians treat the texture and grievances as gold, specific dialogue and some adventures with care). Frame him as "Martin, remembering half a century later." (HIGH)
- **His verbatim-verified lines are in VERIFIED QUOTES below** (Thanksgiving rice-and-vinegar; bloody tracks; the mutiny's "exasperated beyond endurance"; "turned adrift like old worn-out horses").

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **Valley Forge mythology — partly true, partly icon.** TRUE: real hunger, ~1,700–2,000 dead, ~2,898 men too naked/barefoot for duty in one December return, bloody footprints attested by Washington (April 1778) and Martin. ICON: the deaths were from **disease, two-thirds of them in the warm spring months**, not men freezing in snowdrifts; the winter was meteorologically ordinary; nobody starved to death *en masse* in the snow; and the barefoot-blood image has been industrially exaggerated since the nineteenth century. Tell both halves; the redemption arc (Steuben → Monmouth) is real and earned.
2. **Von Steuben's invented credentials — tell it as the delightful truth.** He was a half-pay Prussian *captain* sold to Congress as a *lieutenant general*, via a plausible mistranslation that Franklin and Deane chose not to correct. Don't flatten it to "a fraud" (his competence was real and transformative) or to "a mix-up" (the inflation was convenient and nobody hurried to fix it). His "Baron" is similarly soft. OPTIONAL/SENSITIVE: the rumors that drove him from Europe (allegations of improper conduct with young men at the Hechingen court) are part of modern scholarship but DISPUTED in specifics — omit or handle in one carefully sourced clause, not as established fact.
3. **The Dec 23 1777 vs. Apr 21 1778 quote conflation** — the "blood from their feet" passage is from the **April 1778 Banister letter**; the **December 1777 Laurens letter** has "Starve—dissolve—or disperse" and the 2,898 figure. Popular sources mash them together; we must not.
4. **The "not integrated again until Korea" claim — precise form only.** SAFE: "Black and white soldiers served side by side in the same regular-army units, at a scale the U.S. Army would not see again until the Korean War era." UNSAFE: "the most integrated army until Korea" stated as a measured superlative, or implying *policy* of equality (integration was pragmatic manpower need, and Washington's own policy zigzagged — he initially barred Black enlistment in late 1775 before reversing); also note the segregated 1st Rhode Island exception and that desegregation was *ordered* in 1948 (E.O. 9981) and *implemented* during Korea. The full Black-soldiers story (Dunmore, 1st RI as narrative) belongs to **rev-slavery**.
5. **Joseph Plumb Martin's memoir is a ~50-year-late recollection** — anonymous, 1830, shaped for readers and pension-era grievance. Use him constantly but always badge him ("remembering half a century later"); don't let his dialogue carry load-bearing facts.
6. **Molly Pitcher does NOT belong here.** Any women-with-the-army material beyond the single rationed-camp-followers beat is **rev-women's** — that includes Monmouth's cannon legend.
7. **Numbers that must stay ranges:** total who served (~230,000 Continental, ~145,000 militia — overlapping); peak strength (never more than ~48,000 at once / ~13,000 in one place; "paper vs. effectives" caveat always); Valley Forge deaths (1,700–2,000); Morristown snowstorm count (more than two dozen); currency rates by year (ranges only; the only hard anchor is Congress's own 40:1 devaluation of March 1780); desertion (20–25% scholarly estimate); Black soldiers total (5,000–8,000); Washington's expense-account total (conflicting figures — avoid).
8. **Don't villainize Congress as a cartoon.** Congress wasn't cruel; it was *broke by design* — it had no taxing power, and the states guarded their purses. The same ideology that produced the Revolution (fear of standing armies and central taxation) produced the army's misery. That irony is the chapter's thesis; name the mechanism (§6), don't sneer.
9. **The mutinies were strikes, not treason — but don't romanticize.** The Pennsylvanians hanged Clinton's emissaries to prove their loyalty; the same Washington who conceded to Pennsylvania had two New Jersey ringleaders shot three weeks later by their own comrades' firing squads. Hold both: justified grievances, and an institution that survived by a calibrated mix of concession and killing.
10. **Newburgh's drama has sourcing seams.** The spectacles line is eyewitness-reported, not in the prepared text; the depth of the "conspiracy" (how close to a coup it really was, and how far Hamilton/the Morrises deliberately stoked it) is debated among historians. Tell the scene with its eyewitness sourcing visible; don't assert a fully plotted coup.
11. **The $10 bounty appeal of Dec 31, 1776 is solid; the "My brave fellows" speech text is not** — it comes from a sergeant's recollection published decades later. Paraphrase the appeal; don't quote the speech.
12. **Recruiting-profile percentages are regional studies generalized** (Lender's New Jersey Brigade; Papenfuse–Stiverson's Maryland recruits) — phrase as "studies of the muster rolls suggest," with "under ~10% owned property" and ethnic fractions kept soft.
