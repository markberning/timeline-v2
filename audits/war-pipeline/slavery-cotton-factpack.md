## 1. The world before the boom (the "pressure")
- After the Revolution, slavery looked to some like a declining institution in the
  Upper South: tobacco was exhausting the soil of Virginia and Maryland, and the
  profitability of enslaved labor there was uncertain. (Standard antebellum
  historiography; American Yawp ch. 11 "The Cotton Revolution".)
- The obstacle to cotton: **short-staple (green-seed) cotton** grew well across the
  Southern interior but its sticky seeds were enormously laborious to remove. By
  hand, **a single laborer needed about ten hours to separate one pound of fiber
  from the seeds.** Long-staple (Sea Island) cotton ginned easily but grew only on
  the coast. (Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*.)

## 2. The cotton gin (the turning point's trigger)
- **Eli Whitney** applied for the patent on **October 28, 1793**; it was granted
  **March 14, 1794** (patent 72X). (Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*.)
- His machine could clean about **50 pounds of lint per day** — versus one pound
  per laborer per ten hours by hand. (Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*.)
- **Precise mechanism (if you describe it, use this wording, not "wire hooks /
  slotted screen"):** the gin pulled the cotton fiber through a **comb-like grid of
  slender wires** that were too narrow to let the seeds pass, stripping the lint
  from the seed. (Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*.) Keep any mechanical description short.
- Effect (the paradox to make central): the gin removed the *processing*
  bottleneck, which made short-staple cotton wildly profitable, which made the
  *growing and picking* — still all done by hand — explode in demand. **A machine
  meant to reduce labor multiplied the demand for enslaved labor instead.**
  (Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*; American Yawp ch. 11.)

## 3. The cotton boom (numbers — all sourced)
- US cotton output: **156,000 bales (1800) → 750,000 bales (1830) → 2.85 million
  bales (1850) → more than 4 million bales (1860).** A bale was a compressed bundle
  weighing **400–500 pounds**; 1860's crop was roughly **two billion pounds**.
  (American Yawp ch. 11; Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*.)
- By 1860 cotton was **almost 60% (>60% by some counts) of all US exports**, worth
  on the order of **$200 million** a year (~$220 million of goods moved through New
  Orleans alone, ~3,500 vessels). (Wikipedia, *King Cotton*; American Yawp ch. 11.)
- World share (state these as the two distinct facts they are, don't blur them):
  **by 1860 US cotton was about two-thirds of the world's supply**, and **the US
  supplied roughly 75% of the cotton consumed by Britain** (whose mills were the
  hungry center of the global trade). (Wikipedia, *King Cotton*; American Yawp
  ch. 11.) — pick whichever framing serves the sentence; do not assert "two-thirds"
  and "80%" in the same breath as if simultaneously true.
- The geography: the **Cotton Belt / Black Belt** swept from Georgia and the
  Carolinas across Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana into Texas — new land, worked
  by people moved there by force.

## 4. The enslaved population (numbers — all sourced)
- **~700,000 enslaved people in 1790 → 3,953,760 in the 1860 census** (the last
  federal count of the enslaved; some sources give 3,950,511). (1860 US Census;
  Wikipedia, *1860 United States census*; *Cotton gin*.)
- Distribution in 1860: roughly **2.35 million (about 59%) lived in the Deep
  South**, with about **2.5 million in the Cotton Belt**. Enslaved people were
  **more than half the population of Mississippi and South Carolina.** Across the
  South as a whole, roughly **one in three** people was enslaved. (Freedmen's
  Patrol / 1860 census tabulations; American Yawp ch. 11.)
- **The 1808 cutoff:** the Constitution barred Congress from banning the Atlantic
  slave trade before 1808; the **Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves took effect
  January 1, 1808.** After that, almost no new enslaved people were legally imported.
  (Wikipedia, *Slavery in the United States*, *Act Prohibiting Importation of
  Slaves*.)
- Consequence to make central: cut off from imports, the US enslaved population
  nonetheless **nearly quadrupled — it grew overwhelmingly by natural increase
  (births), the only major slave society in the Americas to grow this way** rather
  than by continued importation. This is what made enslaved people themselves a
  self-replenishing form of wealth. `[VERIFY]` the "only major slave society to
  grow by natural increase" framing with the fact-checker (well-established but
  state it carefully).

## 5. The domestic slave trade — the "Second Middle Passage"
- With imports banned and Upper-South tobacco land tired, the Upper South sold its
  enslaved people *south and west* to the booming cotton frontier. Estimates: about
  **835,000 forcibly relocated 1790–1860** by one count; **roughly 1 million (1
  million or more) moved after 1808.** Commonly summarized as **about a million
  people**, a forced migration historians call the **Second Middle Passage.**
  (Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.) — label as an estimate.
- **Exporting states:** Virginia and Maryland above all (also Kentucky, North
  Carolina, Tennessee, Missouri). **Importing states:** Georgia, Alabama,
  Mississippi, Louisiana, Arkansas, Texas. (Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the US*.)
- **Major firms:** **Franklin & Armfield** (the largest), Bolton, Dickens & Co.
- **Markets:** **New Orleans** was the chief center of the lower-South trade by
  1827 — economists estimate **more than 135,000 people were sold there between
  1804 and 1862.** **Natchez, Mississippi** had the **"Forks of the Road"** market;
  also Richmond, Charleston, Savannah, Memphis. (Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the US*.)
- **Mechanics & human cost:** overland, traders drove **"coffles"** — columns of
  people chained together, walking roughly **20 miles a day.** To be **"sold down
  the river"** meant being shipped via the Mississippi to the Deep South — and
  because the people left behind had no address, no way to send or receive word,
  and no legal claim, it almost always meant a **permanent, total severance: the
  family in Virginia could never know whether you were alive or dead, and would
  never see you again.** The trade routinely **broke apart families** — spouses,
  parents, and children sold separately. (Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the US* —
  emphasizes family separation.) This is the human heart of the section; weight it.

## 5b. A face on the trade — Solomon Northup & Eliza (the named witness)
*(Added so the moral heart of the section is borne by a real, documented person,
not anonymous statistics. All from a primary source.)*
- **Solomon Northup** was a **free-born Black man from Saratoga Springs, New York**
  — a carpenter and violinist — who in **1841 was lured to Washington, D.C.,
  drugged, kidnapped, and sold into slavery.** He spent **twelve years enslaved,
  mostly in Louisiana,** before regaining his freedom in **1853** and publishing his
  memoir, ***Twelve Years a Slave*** (1853). (Wikipedia, *Twelve Years a Slave*;
  American Yawp Reader.)
- He was shipped to **New Orleans** and sold through the pen of the trader
  **Theophilus Freeman.** In that pen was an enslaved woman named **Eliza** and her
  two children, **Randall** (son) and **Emily** (daughter). **A planter from Baton
  Rouge bought Randall alone.** Eliza begged to be sold together with her children;
  Freeman refused.
- **Verbatim quotes (use exactly; the fact-checker will re-verify each — do NOT
  alter a word inside quotation marks):**
  - Randall, led away: **"Don't cry, mama. I will be a good boy. Don't cry,"**
  - Eliza's plea: she wanted to be with her children **"the little time she had to
    live,"** promising **"how very faithful and obedient she would be; how hard she
    would labor day and night."**
  - Freeman's response: he **"damned her, calling her a blubbering, bawling wench,
    and ordered her to go to her place."**
  - Northup's close on the scene: **"What has become of the lad, God knows. It was
    a mournful scene indeed."**
  - Eliza **never saw or heard of her children again.**
  (Source: Solomon Northup, *Twelve Years a Slave*, ch. 6, via the American Yawp
  Reader — "Solomon Northup Describes a Slave Market, 1841.")
- **Use this as the concrete, named human core of the Second Middle Passage beat.**
  Put the people first; the firms/cities logistics go *after* the human material.

## 6. Enslaved people as capital (the consequence)
- By 1860 the estimated market value of the nation's enslaved people exceeded
  **$3 billion** — **more than all the capital invested in US railroads, factories,
  and banks combined**, and worth more than even the South's farmland. Enslaved
  human beings were **the single largest capital asset in the American economy.**
  (Multiple economic histories; Lumen/UNC Press *Black Market*; SFUHS reader.) —
  label "$3 billion" an estimate.
- The point this drives: emancipation was not, to the planter class, a moral
  abstraction to be argued away — it was the threatened destruction of the largest
  pool of wealth in the country. That is *why* the South would fight.

## 7. "King Cotton" and the politics it bred
- **James Henry Hammond**, US Senator from **South Carolina (served 1857–1860)**,
  delivered the **"Cotton is King" speech to the US Senate on March 4, 1858** (the
  "mud-sill" speech). (Wikipedia, *King Cotton*, *James Henry Hammond*.)
  - Cotton-is-king quote — **VERBATIM, exact wording (do NOT drop "No," and do NOT
    drop "to"; trim only with an ellipsis, never alter words):**
    **"No, you dare not make war on cotton. No power on earth dares to make war upon
    it. Cotton is king."**
  - The mud-sill quote (his defense of slavery as society's foundation):
    **"In all social systems there must be a class to do the menial duties, to
    perform the drudgery of life… It constitutes the very mud-sill of society."**
- **King Cotton diplomacy** (set up here, paid off in the "Britain, France & Cotton"
  theme): the conviction that Europe's dependence on Southern cotton was a weapon —
  that a cotton embargo would force Britain and France to recognize and aid the
  South. **It failed** (Britain turned to India, Egypt, and Brazil, and would not
  side publicly with a slaveholding rebellion) — but in 1860 the *belief* in
  cotton's power made the planter class dangerously confident. (Wikipedia, *King
  Cotton*.)

## 8. The throughline for the section (causes-phase opener)
Cotton did not merely make the South rich; it made the South **certain.** A
machine revived a dying institution; an export crop made enslaved people the
country's chief form of wealth; and a class of men came to believe that wealth
made them untouchable. The war's deepest cause is in this section: a society that
had staked everything — its economy, its politics, its self-image — on human
property, and would not give it up. The section should **end by handing off to
"The Road to War"** (the political crisis) without resolving it.
