# FACT PACK — Battle of Carillon (Ticonderoga), July 8, 1758

*French and Indian War. ONLY source. Imperial + Native stakes (the Lake George-Champlain corridor; behind it, Canada). NO slavery angle. Use ranges; the British defeat was self-inflicted (artillery never used; frontal assaults).*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- Main battle July 8, 1758 (broader engagement July 6-8: July 6 = the skirmish in which Lord Howe died; July 8 = the assaults). At/before Fort Carillon, the French fort at Ticonderoga, present-day Ticonderoga, NY (~43.84,-73.39), on the portage between Lake George (south) and Lake Champlain (north), joined by the La Chute River. French name "Carillon"; British renamed it "Ticonderoga" after taking it in 1759. State NY.
- Significance: Montcalm's GREATEST defensive victory — a small French force smashed a British army ~4× its size that threw itself in repeated frontal assaults against a log breastwork + abatis; one of the bloodiest British defeats of the war; a humiliation for British cmdr James Abercromby. Historian L.H. Gipson: no American campaign "ever launched... involved a greater number of errors of judgment."

## 2. BACKGROUND
- Part of the British 1758 offensive under Pitt's strategy: three big attacks — Louisbourg, Fort Duquesne, and Ticonderoga/Carillon (the gateway up the Champlain corridor toward New France). Largest army yet assembled in North America gathered at the south end of Lake George, advanced north down the lake, landed at the north end ~July 5.

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
- British: Maj. Gen. James Abercromby (also Abercrombie) — widely blamed, never led another campaign, recalled by Pitt (letter Sept 18, 1758). 2IC Brig. Gen. George Augustus, Lord Howe — the army's beloved, ablest officer, KILLED July 6 in the skirmish at Bernetz/Trout Brook; his death unhinged the campaign ("the soul of General Abercromby's army seemed to expire"); Wolfe called him "the very best officer in the King's service." Strength ~15,000-16,000 (the largest army yet in North America): ~6,000 British regulars + ~9,000-10,000 provincials/rangers. Artillery present but NOT brought forward to the assault (the central blunder).
- French: Maj. Gen. Montcalm; subordinates Chevalier de Lévis + Bourlamaque. ~3,600-4,000 (mostly French regulars; only a few hundred Canadian militia + ~600 Native allies), vastly outnumbered.

## 4. THE BATTLE
- The French hastily built defensive works on high ground (the Heights of Carillon, ~0.75 mi NW of the fort): a log breastwork above trenches + an extensive abatis (felled trees with sharpened branches facing out, like barbed wire), flank redoubts with cannon, much of it camouflaged with shrubs/trees.
- July 6: a British column blundered into a French detachment in the woods (Battle of Bernetz Brook/Trout Brook); Lord Howe shot and killed instantly; ~150 French casualties there. July 7: Lévis arrived with ~400 reinforcements.
- The faulty reconnaissance: engineer Lt. Matthew Clerk reported the works looked incomplete and could be "easily forced, even without cannon" (they were near-complete and camouflaged); he also urged fortifying Rattlesnake Hill (Mount Defiance) to command the line — Abercromby ignored it. So Abercromby ordered a FRONTAL ASSAULT without bringing up artillery (an attempt to float ~4 six-pounders + a howitzer down the La Chute failed: barges fired on, two sank). He was also anxious about rumored French reinforcements.
- July 8 assaults: British advanced in three columns; intended a single coordinated assault but coordination broke down into piecemeal waves (~12:30pm start; failures by ~2pm and ~2:30pm; a desperate ~5pm push; ~4-7 hours total). The abatis broke up formations and exposed them to murderous fire from the breastwork. The 42nd Highlanders (the Black Watch) pressed hardest, in places reached the works, but could not break through. British never breached the line. Abercromby ordered a full, somewhat panicked retreat back up the lake — despite still vastly outnumbering the French.

## 5. CASUALTIES (contested — ranges)
- British: official return ~547 killed / ~1,356 wounded / ~77 missing (≈1,980); some accounts round to ~2,000 total and push "killed" to ~800-1,000. Use ~1,900-2,000 total casualties; flag "killed" ranges ~500-1,000. The 42nd Black Watch lost roughly half its strength (~600+ of ~900; ~300+ killed).
- French: ~350-380 (July 8 proper); ~500-550 incl. the July 6 skirmish.
- The defeat was self-inflicted (artillery never used; frontal assaults).

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- A stunning French victory against the odds — Montcalm's masterpiece; he raised a large cross on the field (Montcalm's Cross), attributing the win to divine intervention. (The famous Latin inscription and a "What a day for France!" exclamation are [UNVERIFIED] — do NOT quote as fact.)
- Abercromby humiliated, recalled by Pitt, never commanded again, replaced by Jeffery Amherst.
- Lord Howe's death widely mourned; the Province of Massachusetts Bay financed a monument to him in Westminster Abbey (VERIFIED); body buried at St. Peter's, Albany.
- The fort fell the NEXT year (1759): Amherst advanced, the French evacuated/partly destroyed Carillon (July 23-26, 1759), British moved in July 27, 1759 and renamed it Fort Ticonderoga.
- Net 1758: Pitt's other two prongs SUCCEEDED (Louisbourg fell; Fort Duquesne taken), so 1758 was still a net British turn despite Carillon.

## 7. NATIVE PERSPECTIVE / STAKES
- FAR fewer Native allies at Carillon than at William Henry (~600 here): the 1757 William Henry massacre had strained the French-Native alliance, so many western nations did not return for 1758 (standard historiography; treat as standard-but-secondary). Stakes: control of the Lake George-Champlain corridor (the invasion route between the colonies and the St. Lawrence) and the land/trade behind it; Haudenosaunee territory and allegiance a central factor. The British defeat, witnessed by Native observers, complicated future British recruitment of allies. Imperial + Native stakes; NO slavery angle.

## 8. VERIFIED QUOTES (flag shaky)
- On Lord Howe: "the soul of General Abercromby's army seemed to expire" (contemporary). Wolfe: Howe was "the very best officer in the King's service," his loss "one of the greatest that could befall a nation."
- Capt. Charles Lee at the breastwork: "The fire was prodigiously hot," with "the slaughter of officers very great, almost all wounded, the men still furiously rushing forwards without any leaders."
- An officer of the 55th on the Highlanders: "With a mixture of esteem, grief, and envy, I am penetrated by the great loss and immortal glory acquired by the Highlanders."
- [UNVERIFIED, DO NOT USE AS FACT]: Montcalm's "What a day for France!" / the Latin cross inscription; the "Nabbycromby" nickname for Abercromby (popular soldiers' lore).

## 9. MAP GEOGRAPHY (state = New York)
- Fort Carillon/Ticonderoga (~43.84,-73.39) on the La Chute River carrying Lake George's water down to Lake Champlain. Lake George to the south (British advanced north down it, landed at its north end ~July 5); Lake Champlain to the north. The La Chute portage/falls/sawmill (carrying-place around the falls; the failed gun-barge attempt). The Heights of Carillon ~0.75 mi NW of the fort (the abatis + breastwork; the killing ground July 8). Rattlesnake Hill / Mount Defiance (the height Abercromby ignored). Fort Edward (~43.27,-73.58, British base on the upper Hudson, south). Crown Point/Fort St-Frédéric (~44.03,-73.43, on Lake Champlain north of Ticonderoga).
