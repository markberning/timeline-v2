# FACT PACK — "Native Nations Caught Between" (1775–1783)

*American Revolution vertical, WAR-YEARS Off-the-Battlefield theme chapter. SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against the cited sources; confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges and "flag" guidance for contested specifics. FRAMING IS LOCKED: Native nations are ACTORS making strategic choices between two empires of land-hungry colonizers — not a backdrop, not a tragedy reel, not one people. "The Indians sided with the British" is the myth this chapter kills.*

**Orientation.** The F&I vertical already told the deep story of Native diplomacy 1754–1763 — the covenant chain, the play-off system between empires, and the Proclamation Line of 1763 (the Crown's promise that settlement stopped at the Appalachian crest). This chapter picks those threads up tersely and carries them through the Revolution. In 1775 every major nation east of the Mississippi faced the same brutal question with different answers: the rebelling colonists were the very settlers crossing the Line, while the King's government was the only power that had ever — however weakly — tried to hold them back. Both sides courted Native alliance or at least neutrality at the war's start (Albany and Pittsburgh, 1775). Then the war came to Indian country anyway: the Cherokee war of 1776, the Haudenosaunee confederacy's 1777 split and its civil war at Oriskany, the murdered neutrals of the Ohio country (Cornstalk, White Eyes), Washington's 1779 Sullivan campaign and the refugee winter at Niagara, and the Gnadenhutten massacre of 1782. The close is the peace of 1783, which gave away the trans-Appalachian west with no Native signature, presence, or mention — the disaster, as the scholarship puts it, lay in the outcome. Molly Brant's personal story belongs to the Women chapter (one beat only here); battle narrative stays at what-it-was scale.

## 1. THE LANDSCAPE IN 1775 — NATIONS, THE LINE, AND TWO COURTSHIPS

- **Who and where (the map the reader needs).** North: **Abenaki** (northern New England/Quebec borderlands, divided) and the **Stockbridge Mohican** community in western Massachusetts. New York: the **Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Six Nations** — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, Tuscarora — the most powerful confederacy in the Northeast. Ohio country: **Shawnee, Delaware (Lenape), Wyandot, Mingo** and others. South: **Cherokee** (southern Appalachians), **Creek (Muscogee)** (Georgia/Alabama), **Choctaw** and **Chickasaw** (Mississippi/west Tennessee). (HIGH — standard geography; keep nation-by-nation, never "the Indians.")
- **The Proclamation Line of 1763 is the live grievance** (F&I callback, terse). The Crown drew settlement's legal limit at the Appalachians; settlers crossed it continuously and colonial governments barely pretended to stop them. For most nations this is the strategic fact of 1775: **the rebels WERE the encroachers; the Crown was the flawed restrainer.** (HIGH)
- **Sycamore Shoals, 17 March 1775** — weeks before Lexington: Richard Henderson's private Transylvania Company "bought" roughly **20 million acres** of Cherokee hunting grounds (most of Kentucky + middle Tennessee) from senior chiefs (Attakullakulla, Oconostota) for trade goods worth about **£10,000** (the proverbial wagonloads of goods). The purchase was illegal under the Proclamation and under Virginia and North Carolina law — and it happened anyway. (HIGH on date/scale/illegality)
  - **Dragging Canoe** (Attakullakulla's son, war leader of the younger generation) refused the sale and walked out with a warning that the ceded country would be **"dark and bloody"** ground to settle. **Exact wording is unreliable** — accounts vary ("bloody ground… dark and difficult to settle"), and the related folk-etymology that "Kentucky" *means* "dark and bloody ground" is false. Paraphrase the warning; don't put polished words in quotation marks. (DISPUTED on wording; HIGH that he repudiated the sale and warned of war)
- **Both sides initially wanted Native NEUTRALITY, not alliance.** The Continental Congress created **three Indian departments (northern, middle, southern) in July 1775** with treaty commissioners. (MED on the exact July 1775 structure — verify before asserting the date) Congress's 1775 message to the Six Nations framed the war as **"a family quarrel between us and Old England,"** in which "you Indians are not concerned." (MED — widely cited from the July 1775 speech in the Journals of the Continental Congress; verify verbatim before quoting)
- **Albany, August–September 1775:** Continental commissioners met Six Nations delegates, who pledged neutrality. The Oneida had already put the position in their own words that year: **"We are unwilling to join on either side of such a contest, for we love you both, Old England and New."** (HIGH — NPS; minor wording variants exist across printings)
- **Pittsburgh, September 12 – October 21, 1775:** congress between commissioners (Continental and Virginia) and the Ohio nations (Delaware, Shawnee, Wyandot, Mingo, Seneca) — reaffirmed the **Ohio River boundary** from Dunmore's War (1774) and Ohio-country neutrality. (HIGH)
- **Britain's machinery:** the Indian superintendents — **Guy Johnson** (north, Sir William Johnson's nephew/son-in-law and successor) and **John Stuart** (south) — worked the old alliance networks. **Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea)**, Mohawk war leader, sailed with Guy Johnson to **London (1775–76)**, met George III, and put the Mohawk case to the Colonial Secretary, Lord George Germain: loyalty rendered, land grievances unredressed (verbatim in QUOTES). (HIGH)
- **Framing beat:** every nation ran its own foreign policy, and all three options were real — **British alignment** (most who fought: Britain at least nominally restrained settlers), **Patriot alignment** (Oneida, Tuscarora, Stockbridge, some Abenaki and Catawba), and **neutrality** (the Delaware under White Eyes, Cornstalk's Shawnee faction, most Creeks for most of the war). (HIGH)

## 2. THE HAUDENOSAUNEE SPLIT — THE CHAPTER'S SPINE

- **Two years of neutrality first.** The Six Nations held to the Albany pledge through 1775–76; pressure from both sides mounted. (HIGH)
- **The council fire at Onondaga.** The Onondaga were the confederacy's fire-keepers — the central council fire was the symbol and instrument of collective decision. In **January 1777**, after a devastating **smallpox epidemic at Onondaga killed leading sachems**, word was sent that **the central council fire was extinguished** (sources also say "covered" or "raked over" — the ritual phrasing varies; the political meaning is solid: with no functioning central council, **each nation was freed to choose its own course**). (HIGH on the 1777 extinguishing and its meaning; MED on any specific death count — one source's "90 leaders" is uncorroborated, don't use; DISPUTED which ritual verb to quote — paraphrase)
- **The split (1777).** **Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and Onondaga warriors largely went with Britain; the Oneida and Tuscarora largely with the Patriots** — with individuals and villages on both sides of every line. Don't write any nation as unanimous. (HIGH)
- **Why the Oneida chose differently:** decades of close ties to New England, and above all the Presbyterian missionary **Samuel Kirkland**, resident among the Oneida **since 1766**, trusted counselor and conduit to the Patriots (Washington later credited his "uncommon Ascendency" over them). (HIGH)
- **Joseph Brant** returned from London committed to the British alliance and became the most famous Native commander of the war. **Molly Brant** — his sister, Sir William Johnson's widow, and a Mohawk clan matron whose intelligence and influence held much of the Mohawk-British alliance together — gets ONE beat here; her story is the Women chapter's. (HIGH)
- **ORISKANY — 6 August 1777** (what-it-was scale only). A Tryon County militia column under **Gen. Nicholas Herkimer** — about **800 militia plus 60–100 Oneida warriors**, among them **Han Yerry** — marched to relieve besieged Fort Stanwix and was ambushed in a ravine by **Loyalists (Royal Yorkers, Butler) and British-allied Haudenosaunee — Seneca war chiefs Sayenqueraghta and Cornplanter, Mohawks under Brant**. Roughly **385–465 American casualties** out of ~800 — proportionally among the bloodiest days of the entire war. Herkimer, shot in the leg, died ten days later. (HIGH; give the casualty figure as a range)
  - **The load-bearing fact: Haudenosaunee fought Haudenosaunee.** Oneida warriors and Seneca/Mohawk warriors — members of a confederacy built on the Great Law of Peace — killed each other at Oriskany. Haudenosaunee oral history remembers the place as **"A Place of Great Sadness,"** and scholars and the nations alike describe the moment as the confederacy's **civil war**. The reprisals were immediate and internal: **Brant's Mohawks burned the Oneida village of Oriska; Oneidas plundered the Mohawk towns of Tiononderoge and Canajoharie.** (HIGH — the "civil war within the confederacy" framing has solid scholarly and Haudenosaunee support)
- **The frontier war, 1778 — present the sequence plainly, no side's framing.** **Wyoming Valley, Pennsylvania, 3 July 1778:** Maj. John Butler with ~110 Rangers and ~464 allied warriors (mostly Seneca and Cayuga under Sayenqueraghta and Cornplanter) destroyed the Patriot militia — roughly **300 Patriot dead**, many killed in flight or after capture. **Atrocity reports were then wildly inflated by American newspapers** (the burning of women and children in Forty Fort never happened). **Joseph Brant was not at Wyoming** — the "Monster Brant" legend pinned it on him anyway. (HIGH)
  - **Cherry Valley, New York, 11 November 1778:** Loyalist and Seneca/Mohawk force under Capt. Walter Butler with Brant; about **30 noncombatants** — women and children among them — were killed, mostly by Seneca warriors enraged by the year's propaganda and by Patriot raids; **Brant is documented trying to restrain the killing.** (HIGH)
  - These raids are the proximate cause Washington cited for 1779. **Do NOT write Sullivan as justified retaliation, and do NOT write Wyoming/Cherry Valley as motiveless savagery — both framings are partisan. Give the sequence.** (LOCKED framing)

## 3. THE CHEROKEE WAR OF 1776 AND THE CHICKAMAUGA

- **Summer 1776:** Dragging Canoe and the younger warriors — over the objection of the older chiefs — struck the trans-Line settlements (Watauga, Holston, Carolina backcountry), aiming to push the squatters off the ceded lands. The attacks largely failed at the fortified stations. (HIGH on the campaign; keep details light)
- **The Patriot answer was systematic town destruction.** In late 1776, coordinated columns invaded the Cherokee country: **Col. Andrew Williamson** (South Carolina, ~1,800) burned the Lower Towns, **Gen. Griffith Rutherford** (North Carolina, ~2,400) the Middle Towns, **Col. William Christian** (Virginia, ~1,800) the Overhill Towns. **Towns burned: at least 36 (Williamson + Rutherford alone), with full-campaign counts running to ~50+** — plus crops, orchards, and stores, on the eve of winter. Use the range. (HIGH on the campaign; counts vary by source — RANGE ONLY)
- **The older chiefs bought peace with land.** **Treaty of DeWitt's Corner (20 May 1777):** the Lower Cherokee ceded almost all remaining land in South Carolina. **Treaty of Long Island of the Holston (20 July 1777):** the Overhill Cherokee ceded lands east of the Blue Ridge and in Tennessee/Virginia. (HIGH)
- **Dragging Canoe refused and seceded.** He led the unreconciled warriors and their families south-west to **Chickamauga Creek** (near present Chattanooga), built **11 new towns**, and as the **Chickamauga Cherokee** fought on — with British supply, then beyond Britain's surrender entirely, until **1794**: a 19-year war of resistance that outlasted the Revolution itself by a decade. (HIGH)
- Framing: this is the generational split inside one nation — accommodation vs. resistance — both strategies rational responses to the same squeeze. (HIGH)

## 4. THE OHIO COUNTRY — NEUTRALITY MURDERED

- **Cornstalk (Shawnee).** The principal advocate of Shawnee neutrality after Dunmore's War. In the fall of 1777 he came to the American **Fort Randolph** (Point Pleasant, at the Kanawha/Ohio confluence) — by the standard account to warn the garrison that most nations were tipping British and that he might be unable to hold his own people ("run with the stream"). Capt. **Matthew Arbuckle detained him as a hostage**. On **10 November 1777**, after a soldier was killed nearby, **militiamen stormed the cabin and murdered Cornstalk, his son Elinipsico, the Delaware Red Hawk, and a fourth man — men held under what amounted to a flag of truce.** Virginia's Gov. Patrick Henry denounced the murders and offered a reward; **the accused were tried and acquitted; no one was ever punished.** (HIGH on the murder, date, victims, and impunity; MED on the exact wording of his warning — paraphrase)
  - Effect: the murder gutted the Shawnee neutralists; most Shawnee moved to the British war camp, while a minority withdrew west rather than fight. (HIGH)
- **The Treaty of Fort Pitt, 17 September 1778 — the FIRST formal treaty between the United States and any Native nation.** Signed at Fort Pitt by US commissioners Andrew and Thomas Lewis and Delaware leaders **White Eyes (Koquethagechton), Captain Pipe (Hopocan), and John Kill Buck Jr. (Gelelemend)**. The Delaware granted American troops passage through their country toward Detroit and allowed a fort (Fort Laurens followed that winter — MED on naming the fort, verify if used); the US **guaranteed Delaware territorial rights** and — Article VI — held out **statehood**: a Native state, **"whereof the Delaware nation shall be the head, and have a representation in Congress"** (full verbatim in QUOTES), conditional on Congress's approval. **Congress never acted on it. No Native state was ever created.** (HIGH; treaty text via Avalon/NARA)
- **White Eyes — the disputed death.** The pro-American Delaware leader joined Gen. Lachlan McIntosh's expedition as guide/negotiator in early **November 1778** — within weeks of signing the treaty — and the army **reported he died of smallpox**. Years later **George Morgan**, the US Indian agent who knew him best, wrote to Congress that White Eyes had been **"treacherously put to death" by American militia and the murder covered up** to keep the Delaware in the alliance. **Historians generally accept that he was murdered; the circumstances are lost.** Present it at exactly that evidence level: official story smallpox, credible insider testimony of murder and cover-up, details unrecoverable. (DISPUTED manner of death — HIGH that the dispute itself is the documented record)
- **The spiral.** With White Eyes dead and the treaty's promises unmet, the Delaware alliance rotted; Captain Pipe took most Delaware to the British by 1781; an American force destroyed the Delaware capital Coshocton (April 1781 — MED, verify if used). The pacifist **Moravian Delaware** mission towns on the Muskingum were left exposed between both fires — the setup for §6. (HIGH on the arc)

## 5. THE SULLIVAN EXPEDITION (1779) AND THE WINTER AT NIAGARA

- **Washington's orders are verbatim and unambiguous.** To Maj. Gen. John Sullivan, **31 May 1779**: the expedition's objects were **"the total distruction and devastation of their settlements"** [sic — period spelling] and the seizure of prisoners; ruin the crops in the ground and prevent more planting; the country **"may not be merely overrun but destroyed"**; and hear no peace overtures **"before the total ruin of their settlements is effected."** (HIGH — Founders Online / LOC; full passages in QUOTES)
- (Prelude, April 1779: Col. Van Schaick's raid destroyed the Onondaga towns — striking the fire-keepers themselves. MED — verify date/details if used.)
- **The campaign, June–October 1779:** ~4,400 Continentals under Sullivan and James Clinton swept the Seneca and Cayuga heartland of central New York. One real engagement (**Newtown, 29 August** — a one-beat mention at most). By **Sullivan's own report** the army destroyed **some 40 towns and approximately 160,000 bushels of corn**, plus vast stores of vegetables and fruit orchards (girdled and cut — orchards take a generation to regrow). Total expedition combat deaths were small on both sides; the weapon was hunger. (HIGH)
- **The refugee winter, 1779–80.** The burned-out families went to the British post at **Fort Niagara**: a recorded **5,036 refugees by 21 September 1779**. The winter of 1779–80 was **among the most severe of the century** — snow feet deep for weeks, game dying in the woods. **An unknown number — credibly hundreds — died of starvation, exposure, scurvy, and disease.** Larger death-toll percentages circulating online are extrapolations; don't use them. (HIGH on the 5,036 and the killing winter; DISPUTED on any death total — say "unknown, likely hundreds")
- **What it did and didn't do.** Sullivan executed the destruction but **did not knock the Haudenosaunee out of the war — 1780–81 brought the heaviest British-allied raiding of the whole war** to the New York frontier, now launched from Niagara by men with burned homes. Avoid "broke the Iroquois" — what broke was the homeland, not the resistance. (HIGH)
- **"TOWN DESTROYER" — handle the name precisely; it PREDATES Sullivan.** The Seneca/Iroquois name **Conotocarious** ("Town Destroyer" / "devourer of villages") was given in the 1670s to **John Washington, George's great-grandfather**, after militia treachery against Susquehannock and Doeg envoys; the name was remembered and **applied to George Washington in 1753** when he appeared on the Ohio frontier (young Washington knew of and even used the name himself — MED on his own use, verify if asserted). **After 1779 the old name acquired its permanent, bitter weight.** (HIGH on the two-generation history — Mount Vernon, Wikipedia/Town Destroyer)
- **Cornplanter said it to Washington's face.** The Seneca leader (a commander at Oriskany and Wyoming) addressed President Washington in **1790**: "when your army entered the country of the Six Nations, we called you the Town Destroyer; and to this day, when that name is heard, our women look behind them and turn pale, and our children cling close to the necks of their mothers" (full text + variant note in QUOTES). This is the chapter's strongest single voice — a man who fought the whole war telling the first president what his name means. (HIGH)
- **The Oneida paid too — kill the "good Indians get spared" implication.** In **July 1780** a British-allied force led by **Joseph Brant** burned the principal Oneida town, **Kanonwalohale**, after the Oneida refused to defect; the Oneida and Tuscarora spent the rest of the war as **refugees in squalid camps around Schenectady**. America's first allies ended the war homeless too — and New York State stripped most of their land in the decades after anyway. (HIGH)
- **The North, one beat:** the **Stockbridge Mohican** company served the Patriot cause from Bunker Hill onward and was cut to pieces at **Kingsbridge (31 August 1778)** — Daniel Nimham and his son Abraham killed; within a generation the weakened community was pushed west off its Massachusetts land. Abenaki communities split, some scouting for each side. (HIGH on Stockbridge; keep Abenaki general)

## 6. GNADENHUTTEN — 8 MARCH 1782

- **Who they were:** pacifist **Moravian Christian Delaware (Lenape) and Mohican converts** of the mission towns (Gnadenhutten, Salem, Schoenbrunn) on the Muskingum in eastern Ohio — neutral by religious conviction, suspected by both sides. In fall 1781 the British-allied Wyandot/Delaware forcibly removed them to the Sandusky ("Captives' Town"), where they starved; in **February 1782 over 150 were allowed back** to their old towns to gather the corn still standing in their fields. (HIGH)
- **The massacre:** on **7–8 March 1782**, ~160 **Pennsylvania militiamen under Col. David Williamson** — name the perpetrators plainly: Pennsylvania militia — rounded up the unarmed harvesters at Gnadenhutten, accused them of raiding (the charge was false; the real raiders had passed through), **held a vote, and the majority voted to kill them**; a minority refused and stood apart. The condemned spent the night in prayer and hymns. On 8 March the militia bludgeoned and scalped **96 people — about 28 men, 29 women, and 39 children** (breakdown MED; the 96 total HIGH) — in two buildings the killers called the slaughterhouses, then burned the town. **Two boys survived**, one scalped and left for dead. **No one was ever punished.** (HIGH on all bolded facts)
- **Aftermath, one beat:** in June 1782 a follow-on expedition against Sandusky under **Col. William Crawford** (Williamson second-in-command; some Gnadenhutten participants in the ranks) was routed; Crawford — who had not been at Gnadenhutten — was captured and **executed by Delaware warriors on 11 June 1782, explicitly in retribution for Gnadenhutten**. State it and move on; no torture detail. (HIGH)

## 7. THE GULF DIMENSION — ONE HONEST BEAT

- When **Spain entered the war (1779)** and Bernardo de Gálvez rolled up British West Florida (**Mobile 1780, Pensacola May 1781**), the southern interior nations were squeezed between a third pair of empires: **hundreds of Choctaw warriors fought in the British defense of Mobile and Pensacola** (a smaller Choctaw faction aided Spain — division within the nation, as everywhere); the **Chickasaw**, long-standing British partners, patrolled the Mississippi against American movement and skirmished with George Rogers Clark's posts, while keeping their distance from the doomed coastal sieges. The **Creek** towns stayed officially neutral and internally divided (pro-British under leaders like Emistisiguo — killed 1782 — and the rising Alexander McGillivray; other headmen pro-American). British defeat handed their diplomacy to Spain and the new United States simultaneously. (HIGH on the spine; don't pad beyond this)

## 8. THE PEACE THAT IGNORED THEM (1783–1784)

- **Treaty of Paris, 3 September 1783:** Britain ceded everything east of the Mississippi (between the Great Lakes and Florida) to the United States. **No Native nation was present, consulted, or mentioned — not one word, no signatories, no provision for Britain's allies or for neutrals.** The land "given" was overwhelmingly land no American army occupied or had conquered. (HIGH)
- Native reaction at the British posts was immediate fury — recorded by British officers at Niagara: the nations held that **they were a free people, allied to the King but never subjects, and that the King had ceded what was never his to cede.** (HIGH as documented sentiment; exact reported phrasings vary — paraphrase, or verify the specific Maclean/Brant reports before quoting)
- **The "conquered peoples" posture.** The new United States treated British-allied nations as defeated enemies whose lands were forfeit by conquest. **Treaty of Fort Stanwix, October 1784:** US commissioners, dictating terms to a confederacy delegation (with hostages taken as surety), punished the four British-aligned Haudenosaunee nations and forced cession of their Ohio Valley claims and a strip of western New York. The Haudenosaunee council later repudiated the treaty as unauthorized — the US enforced it anyway. One forward beat; the full dispossession story belongs to later chapters. (HIGH)
- **The standard scholarly verdict (Calloway et al.).** Colin Calloway, *The American Revolution in Indian Country* (1995): **"The real disaster of the American Revolution for Indian peoples lay in its outcome."** His chapter on 1783 is titled **"The peace that brought no peace."** The shape of the verdict: whichever side a nation chose — Britain, the Patriots, or neutrality — the Revolution ended in invasion, burned towns, refugee camps, and a peace that erased them from the map of decision; the nations **lost at the peace table a war most had not lost in the field**. Patriot-allied Oneida and Stockbridge were dispossessed barely slower than British-allied Seneca and Cherokee. That equal-outcome point is the chapter's close. (HIGH — quote verified; framing is mainstream scholarship)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **THE MONOLITH MYTH is the thing to kill.** Never "the Indians" as one actor with one policy. Every section names the nation, and inside nations names the faction (Dragging Canoe vs. the older chiefs; Cornstalk's neutralists vs. the war party; Oneida vs. Seneca; Choctaw pro-British vs. pro-Spanish). "Most who fought chose Britain" is true and explainable (the Line); "the Indians sided with the British" is false.
2. **"Town Destroyer" predates Sullivan — be precise or be wrong.** Conotocarious was bestowed on John Washington (great-grandfather) in the 1670s and applied to George Washington from 1753. The 1779 campaign didn't coin the name; it made it true a second time and gave it its lasting weight. Cornplanter's 1790 address is the documentation of what it meant *after* Sullivan.
3. **Cornstalk and White Eyes — keep the evidence levels distinct.** Cornstalk's murder is documented fact (date, victims, mob, impunity all solid). White Eyes is a *credible murder claim against an official smallpox story* — Morgan's "treacherously put to death," generally accepted by historians, details lost. Don't flatten the second to the certainty of the first.
4. **Wyoming/Cherry Valley → Sullivan: present the SEQUENCE, adopt neither framing.** "Sullivan was justified retaliation for massacres" and "Sullivan was unprovoked genocide" are both partisan shapes. The record: real Patriot dead at Wyoming (~300) and Cherry Valley (~30 noncombatants); real, documented atrocity-inflation in the American press (Forty Fort burnings invented); then verbatim orders for total destruction of forty towns of mostly noncombatants. Tell it in order, plainly.
5. **"Monster Brant" is a myth on the specifics:** Brant was NOT at Wyoming, and at Cherry Valley he is documented trying to restrain the killing. Use Brant as what he was — a strategist and commander — not the gothic villain of 19th-century print.
6. **The Oneida are not "the good Indians."** They chose alliance for their own strategic and religious-network reasons; they fought at Oriskany and Saratoga; and they paid catastrophically anyway — Kanonwalohale burned by Brant's force (July 1780), years in refugee camps, then dispossessed by New York after the war. Their fate is the proof of the chapter's closing point, not a counterexample.
7. **Gnadenhutten: name the perpetrators plainly — Pennsylvania militia under David Williamson — and tell it without gore inventory.** The vote, the night of hymns, the 96, the two boys: that is the story. Equally: the Crawford execution gets one unflinching sentence, no torture-wallowing, and note Crawford himself wasn't at Gnadenhutten.
8. **Dragging Canoe's "dark and bloody ground":** the warning is real; the verbatim is not recoverable; "Kentucky means dark and bloody ground" is a false etymology. Paraphrase. Also resist romanticizing him — and resist the alternative cliché that the older chiefs "sold out": both factions were running rational strategies with bad options.
9. **Council fire phrasing:** the January 1777 extinguishing at Onondaga (after the smallpox deaths of sachems) is documented and its constitutional meaning (nations freed to act severally) is the point. The ritual verb ("extinguished" / "covered") varies by source — don't put one in quotation marks. Skip the uncorroborated "90 leaders dead" figure.
10. **Neutrality was a strategy, not fence-sitting** — and the war's grimmest pattern is that the *neutrals* kept getting killed by Americans: Cornstalk (murdered under detention), White Eyes (probably murdered), the Moravian Delaware (massacred). Let that pattern surface without editorializing; the facts carry it.
11. **No "savage" trope in either direction; violence honest both ways.** Haudenosaunee/Loyalist raids killed civilians (Cherry Valley); American campaigns burned the food of whole nations on the edge of winter (Cherokee 1776, Sullivan 1779) and militias murdered prisoners and pacifists. The British armed and used allies, then abandoned them at Paris. Nobody in this chapter is a prop.
12. **Don't over-credit Sullivan militarily:** the campaign destroyed the homeland but intensified the frontier war (1780–81 raids were the worst of the war). "Broke the power of the Iroquois" is a textbook cliché the evidence doesn't support.
13. **Calloway's line is about the OUTCOME** — the peace and what followed, not only wartime destruction. Use the quote as written; don't paraphrase it into "the war destroyed them."
14. **Numbers stay as ranges:** Cherokee towns burned ~36 to 50+; Oriskany American casualties ~385–465; Sullivan ~40 towns / ~160,000 bushels (Sullivan's own report — flag as his count); Niagara refugees 5,036 recorded (Sept 1779) with winter deaths unknown ("likely hundreds"); Gnadenhutten 96 (the 28/29/39 breakdown is MED); Wyoming Patriot dead ~300 (the "1,000+ refugee deaths" claim is unverified — don't use).
