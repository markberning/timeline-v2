## the-river-and-the-cotton · Red River, 1864 · The river and the cotton

### What a Union army was doing 150 miles up a Louisiana river

To understand the Battle of Mansfield you first have to understand what a Union army was doing where it was — deep in the pine country of northwest Louisiana, a long way from anywhere that usually shows up in a Civil War story. The answer is the **Red River Campaign**, the Union’s big, doomed push of the spring of 1864 to drive up the Red River toward **Shreveport** (the headquarters of the Confederate Trans-Mississippi Department — the Confederacy’s whole command west of the Mississippi River) and on toward Texas. This was the **Trans-Mississippi Theater**: the war west of the great river, a sprawling, secondary front that rarely got the South’s best armies or the North’s best generals, but where a great deal of cotton, cattle, and contested ground sat.

It was a **combined expedition** — army and navy moving together. **Maj. Gen. Nathaniel P. Banks (North)**, a Massachusetts political general (a former governor and Speaker of the U.S. House who got his rank from his clout, not from West Point), marched the **Army of the Gulf** overland up the river road. Alongside him, steaming up the Red River itself, came **Rear Adm. David Dixon Porter (North, naval)** and his **gunboat fleet** — a flotilla of armored river warships, floating artillery batteries with engines, meant to shell anything on the banks and carry the army’s heavy lifting. By the first of April, Banks had pushed about **150 miles up the river**, occupying Grand Ecore and Natchitoches, with Shreveport the prize ahead.

The campaign had four tangled aims, and they matter, because the tangle is the point. Banks was supposed to **take Shreveport** and break the Confederate army in west Louisiana; to **plant the U.S. flag in Texas** as a warning to French-occupied Mexico, where Emperor Maximilian had some 25,000 French troops and Washington was nervous; to **stand up a pro-Union state government** in Louisiana under Lincoln’s lenient “ten percent” reconstruction plan. And — the one that hung over all the rest — to **seize cotton**.

Here is where the war’s real cause stops being abstract and becomes a thing you could touch and weigh. One of the stated objectives of this expedition was to grab **as much as 100,000 bales of cotton** off the plantations strung along the Red River, for the hungry textile mills of the North and the U.S. Treasury. That cotton did not grow itself. It was the great staple of the Deep South, and it was grown — every boll of it — by **enslaved people**, on those river plantations, under the lash, for the profit of the men who claimed to own them. The Confederacy was a republic founded to keep four million human beings in bondage and to defend the slave economy that produced exactly that cotton; the wealth Banks was reaching for *was* the wealth of slavery. Strip away the maps and the marching, and a Union army was driving up a Louisiana river toward the signature crop of the slave economy, while a Confederate army gathered to defend it. The slavery the war was fought over is not hiding in the background of this campaign. It is sitting in warehouses along the river in the form of bales.

And the greed was not subtle. Banks let **cotton speculators ride along with the army** like camp followers with ledgers; Porter’s gunboats seized cotton as “prizes of war,” and Porter himself made a great deal of money selling it. The whole operation reeked of cotton money so badly that after the war a congressional investigation aired the scandal, and historians have argued ever since over whether the cotton or the military goals really drove the thing — Ludwell Johnson’s book on the campaign is literally titled *Politics and Cotton in the Civil War*. We don’t need to settle that debate to make the simpler point: the object two armies maneuvered over in the Louisiana pines was the slave-grown crop itself.

> **MEANWHILE — the other men on the river.** There is a sharp counter-image to the cotton, and it was marching on the same campaign. Banks (North) had, back on May 1, 1863, as commander of the **Department of the Gulf**, created the **Corps d’Afrique** — regiments of Black soldiers, infantry and cavalry and artillery, many of them formerly enslaved Louisianans. Units raised from that effort and from the broader U.S. Colored Troops served in the Red River Campaign: engineer regiments, a cavalry brigade, fresh companies of freedmen recruited in occupied Alexandria that very April. So the same Louisiana soil that grew the slave-cotton Banks coveted was also producing the free Black soldiers now marching in Union blue against the army defending slavery. One river, two cargoes: the bales of a slave economy, and the men who had walked out of it carrying rifles.

## the-narrow-road · April 8, morning · One road through the pines

### Why a smaller army could ambush a bigger one

The fight that broke the campaign goes by two names, and you should hold both. The Union called it the **Battle of Mansfield**, after the DeSoto Parish town it was fought about three miles below. The Confederates called it the **Battle of Sabine Crossroads**, after a road junction near the field. (The National Park Service even keeps a third name, **Pleasant Grove**, for the rear-guard stand at the end of the day.) Same ground, same afternoon — the two-name habit of a war where each side wrote its own history.

The man waiting for Banks was **Maj. Gen. Richard Taylor (South)**, commanding the small Confederate Army of Western Louisiana — and, as it happens, the son of a U.S. president, Zachary Taylor. Taylor had been falling back for weeks, badly outnumbered if you counted every soldier on both sides. But Taylor had been counting something else: not the totals, but the **road**.

Because everything here turns on one narrow road. Banks’s army was advancing **north** toward Mansfield and Shreveport up a single stage road that ran through **dense pine forest** — a kind of forest tunnel, trees crowding both shoulders, no room to spread out. An army that size needs room to deploy its strength side by side; on one track through the woods it can only go in a long thin file, and that is exactly what Banks’s did. His **column** — the army strung out in marching order — ran for **two to three miles or more**: the **cavalry division** (a mounted division, here a force of roughly 3,300 to 3,500 horsemen) under **Brig. Gen. Albert L. Lee (North)** at the very head, then behind it the army’s wagon train — somewhere around **320 to 350 supply wagons** clogging the single lane, hauling the food and ammunition and the speculators’ hopes — and only *then* the infantry, miles back, packed nose to tail and unable to get past the wagons in front of them.

Sit with the mechanism, because it is the whole battle. Banks may have had **20,000 to 30,000 men** in the expedition — the sources scatter badly, from “21,000” up toward “30,000” — but the number that decided the day was the number he could actually get into the fight, and that was only about **12,000**. One officer described the army as having “almost a day’s march between the advance guard and the rear.” The wagons were a cork in a bottle. Taylor, meanwhile, had only some **8,800 to 11,000 men** total — but he could bring nearly all of them to bear on the *front* of Banks’s column, the part that had emerged from the woods. At the actual point of contact, it was the **Union infantry that was outnumbered, roughly two to one.** The army that was smaller everywhere managed to be bigger in the one place that mattered. That is how a single narrow road through the pines let a smaller force rout the head of a larger one.

About **noon**, Lee’s cavalry van (North) shoved Confederate skirmishers back and rode out of the tree line into a **large clearing** — open farmland about three miles south of Mansfield, with a low rise called **Honeycutt Hill** in the middle of it. Lee deployed his troopers across the rise and sent back for infantry. He had a bad feeling about the whole thing, and he had already said so to Banks: he warned, in effect, that they could not push on without bringing on a full battle in which they’d be soundly beaten. Banks decided to fight there anyway.

> **MEANWHILE — the man on the stump.** While the Union van massed across the clearing and called nervously for help, Taylor (South) did something that tells you he knew exactly what he had. He sat on a tree stump and smoked a cigar. Then he rode his line adjusting it, and finally sat his horse with one leg hooked casually over the saddle, watching the Federals pile up in front of him. For about two hours the two sides faced off across the open field in what one account calls a race to see who could get more men to the front first — and Taylor, holding the woods on three sides of the clearing, was winning that race without lifting a finger. He was waiting on purpose.

## the-rout · April 8, ~4 p.m. · The crescent closes

### Two hours of waiting, then a collapse

Taylor had arranged his roughly 9,000 men in a rough **crescent** — bent like a horseshoe — along the **western, northern, and eastern tree lines** rimming the clearing, with the open field in front and the Union column pouring into it. **Brig. Gen. Alfred Mouton’s (South)** division held the **left**, east of the road; **Maj. Gen. John G. Walker’s (South)** Texas infantry division held the **right**, west of the road; **Brig. Gen. Thomas Green’s (South)** cavalry covered the flanks. The Federals, deploying in the clearing, were forming a line with the woods curling around three sides of them and did not fully grasp it.

Around **four o’clock in the afternoon**, after the long wait, Taylor ordered the assault. **Mouton’s division (South)** charged first, east of the road, straight at the Union line along the rail fence and Honeycutt Hill — and ran into a wall of fire. The charge was bloodily repulsed in its first rush, and Mouton himself was **killed**, leading his men, along with several of his regimental commanders. Command of the division passed on the spot to **Brig. Gen. Camille Armand Jules Marie, Prince de Polignac (South)** — and that is not a typo: a genuine French nobleman, a prince, had crossed an ocean to command a brigade of Texans for the Confederacy, and now inherited a whole division by battlefield promotion in the middle of a firefight in the Louisiana woods.

West of the road, **Walker’s Texas division (South)** did the thing the crescent was built to do: it **wrapped around the Union flank** and rolled the line up from the side, while **Green’s (South)** cavalry pressed the edge. A battle line can absorb a blow to its front; it cannot absorb being folded in from the end, where each man’s flank and rear are suddenly open. The Union line **broke** — and then the road that had strung Banks’s army out turned a defeat into a disaster.

Because the broken Union front fled **south, back down the single road** — and slammed straight into its own **wagon train**, the 300-odd wagons still jammed nose to tail in the lane behind them. There was nowhere to go. The retreat collided with its own baggage and congealed into a stampede. Men **threw away their rifles and cartridge boxes** to run faster, sprinting back through a Confederate crossfire pouring in from both tree lines. **Brig. Gen. Thomas E. G. Ransom (North)**, commanding the leading infantry detachment, was **wounded** trying to rally the wreck. The numbers tell the shape of it: the captured-and-missing figure dwarfs the killed, which is the fingerprint of a rout rather than a stand-up fight. The Union lost roughly **2,100 to 2,235** men here — but only about **113 killed and some 580 wounded**, against around **1,500 captured or missing**, swept up wholesale as the line dissolved. The Confederates also took about **20 cannon, 156 wagons, and a thousand horses and mules** off the jammed road.

Banks (North) rode into the flood and tried to stop it, calling on his men not to desert him — and the men ran past him anyway. That contrast is the whole afternoon in one image: the political general pleading on the road while his army poured around him toward the rear.

> **MEANWHILE — “the battle is won.”** Late in the day, word reached Taylor (South) that caution was coming down the wire — orders or reinforcements meant to keep him from committing to a general engagement he wasn’t supposed to fight. By the story that has come down, Taylor’s answer was that it was too late for any of that: the battle, he said, was already won. Whether those were his exact words or a line tidied up by later tellers, the sentiment was simply true. The head of Banks’s army was already running.

## what-it-cost · The reckoning · The campaign breaks on a back road

### A lopsided win, a death close to home, and the end of the drive

**OUTCOME: Confederate victory** — and one of the most lopsided in the whole Trans-Mississippi war. **Maj. Gen. Richard Taylor (South)**, outnumbered across the theater, had concentrated his army against the head of **Maj. Gen. Nathaniel Banks’s (North)** strung-out column and shattered it. The disproportion even flips the usual Civil War arithmetic, where the attacker normally bleeds worse: here the *attacker* got off light. Taylor lost roughly **1,000 men** — the figure reported by **Gen. Edmund Kirby Smith (South)**, since precise Confederate returns were never recorded — while Banks lost about double that and saw some 1,500 of his men marched off as prisoners. The defender’s line collapsed and was captured wholesale; the attacker counted his losses in the hundreds.

The rout did not run clean to Mansfield, though, and the reason is a fourth Union line. Two or three miles back down the road to the south, **Brig. Gen. William H. Emory’s (North)** fresh division — roughly 5,000 men of the XIX Corps, infantry who had not yet been in the fight — formed a new line across the road and the woods (the stand the Park Service files as **Pleasant Grove**). Taylor’s pursuing Confederates, flushed and disordered from chasing a broken enemy two miles through timber, hit this unbroken wall around six o’clock and could not push through it. After more than an hour, **nightfall ended the fighting**; a Confederate try after dark to turn Banks’s flank came to nothing. Emory’s backstop is the only reason the catastrophe had a southern edge at all.

It is worth pausing on the man who didn’t walk off the field. **Brig. Gen. Alfred Mouton (South)**, killed leading the opening charge, was no outsider to this ground. He was a **Louisiana native**, born at Opelousas in 1829, the **son of a former Louisiana governor**, **West Point–trained** before he resigned to become a civil engineer and a sugarcane planter, and wounded earlier in the war at Shiloh. He died about three miles from a Louisiana town, on Louisiana soil, defending a Louisiana slave society his planter family belonged to — the cotton-and-cane economy this whole campaign was, at bottom, a fight over. The cause that opened this story closed over his body.

The morning after, Banks (North) fell back to Pleasant Hill. There, the next day, Taylor attacked again and was repulsed by Sherman’s loaned veterans under **Brig. Gen. Andrew J. Smith (North)** — a tactical Union win that Banks promptly threw away by retreating regardless, abandoning the drive on Shreveport. (That second day, Pleasant Hill, April 9, is its own battle and its own story; the point that matters here is that Mansfield had already decided the campaign, and Pleasant Hill only confirmed it.) The expedition reversed course and withdrew toward Alexandria and down the river.

Every one of the campaign’s four aims died with it. No Shreveport. No U.S. flag planted in Texas. No 100,000 bales of cotton — the slave-grown prize that had drawn speculators up the river slipped away entirely. No expanded pro-Union government. The cotton-speculation scandal followed Banks into a postwar congressional investigation and effectively ended his military career. The whole bungled expedition tied up tens of thousands of Union troops and, by some historians’ reckoning, may have stretched the war out by months. A smaller army had used a single narrow road through the pines to wreck a bigger one — and to keep, for a while longer, the slave economy whose great crop two armies had marched into the woods to fight over.

> **MEANWHILE — what the bales were.** It is tempting to file Mansfield as a battle “about a river town,” or about a road, or about a general too vain to listen to his cavalry. All true enough. But sit with what the column was reaching for when it strung itself out on that road. The 100,000 bales were not a neutral commodity waiting in a warehouse — they were the congealed labor of enslaved people, the product and the proof of the slave society the Confederacy existed to defend. The Union’s drive up the Red River chased that wealth; the Confederate army in the woods guarded it; and marching on the very same campaign were Black soldiers of the Corps d’Afrique who had walked out of that slavery and turned around to fight it. Two armies maneuvered over the crop of bondage, on the same back road, at the same time. The cotton is not a footnote to the battle of Mansfield. It is what the battle was for.
