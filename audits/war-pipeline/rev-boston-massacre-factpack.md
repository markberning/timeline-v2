# FACT PACK — "The Townshend Acts & the Boston Massacre" (1767–1770)

*American Revolution vertical, CAUSES arc, chapter 3 (route slug `boston-massacre`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against the cited sources; confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges and "flag" guidance for contested specifics.*

**Orientation.** Ch.2 ended with the Stamp Act repeal (1766) paired with the Declaratory Act — Parliament gave up the tax but kept the asserted power "in all cases whatsoever." Ch.3 opens with Parliament trying again. In 1767 Charles Townshend, Chancellor of the Exchequer, levied a new set of *external* duties (collected at the ports) on glass, lead, paint/painters' colors, paper, and tea, deliberately exploiting the colonists' own earlier external-vs-internal distinction — and earmarked the revenue to pay royal governors and judges directly, prying them loose from the colonial assemblies' purse. The colonists refined their principle (no tax *for revenue*, external or internal, without consent), revived the cross-colony boycott, and — after the customs seizure of Hancock's *Liberty* — got an army: the 14th and 29th Regiments occupied Boston in October 1768. Sixteen months of friction between a garrisoned army and a hostile town climaxed on the night of March 5, 1770 in the killings on King Street that patriots branded the "Massacre." That same day in London, Lord North moved to repeal most of the Townshend duties but keep the tea tax as a token of Parliament's right. The boycotts collapsed; a real lull followed (1770–1773). The surviving tea duty is the fuse to the next chapter — do not narrate it here.

## 1. THE TOWNSHEND ACTS (1767)

- **Charles Townshend** — Chancellor of the Exchequer; born **27 August 1725**, died **4 September 1767** of a fever, aged **42**; took the Exchequer in **August 1766** under Pitt the Elder's ministry. (HIGH)
- Renowned orator; his reckless, brilliant **"Champagne Speech"** to the Commons (**8 May 1767**) earned him the nickname **"Champagne Charlie"**; Horace Walpole described it as lasting "an hour, with torrents of wit, ridicule, vanity, lies, and beautiful language." (HIGH — Walpole quote verified)
- Townshend **died suddenly in September 1767, before the storm broke** in the colonies — he never saw the resistance his acts provoked. (HIGH)
- The "Townshend Acts" are a *cluster* of laws passed mid-1767 (plus one 1768 act), not a single statute. The five commonly grouped: (HIGH on dates below)
  - **Revenue Act of 1767** — royal assent **29 June 1767**. The core measure: import duties on **glass, lead, paints/painters' colors, paper, and tea** brought into the colonies. (HIGH)
  - **Commissioners of Customs Act** — **29 June 1767**. Created the American Board of Customs Commissioners (below). (HIGH)
  - **Indemnity Act of 1767** — **2 July 1767**. Lowered the duty the East India Company paid in Britain on tea (to make British tea more competitive against smuggled Dutch tea). (HIGH)
  - **New York Restraining Act of 1767** — **2 July 1767**. Suspended the New York Assembly for refusing to fully comply with the 1765 Quartering Act. (HIGH; note: this is a punitive measure, not a tax — mention only as context for "assemblies dissolved/suspended" theme.)
  - **Vice Admiralty Court Act of 1768** — **8 March 1768**. Expanded the vice-admiralty (no-jury) court system; created district courts (incl. Boston). (HIGH)
- **KEY FRAMING — the external-duty maneuver.** These were deliberately **external** duties, collected at the ports. Townshend was exploiting the distinction the colonists had themselves drawn during the Stamp Act crisis (external/trade duties allegedly acceptable, internal/direct taxes not). He meant to call their bluff. (HIGH — this is the chapter's central irony; well attested.)
- **KEY FRAMING — the purpose was pointed.** The revenue was earmarked to **pay the salaries of royal governors and judges directly**, freeing those officials from the colonial assemblies' power of the purse (the assemblies had historically controlled official pay and thus leverage). This — not the dollar amount — was the alarming part. (HIGH)
- **American Board of Customs Commissioners** — a **five-man** board, seated at **Boston, November 1767**. Enforced shipping laws and collected duties; could act without consulting the Treasury Board in London; raised the number of customs officers, built a colonial coast guard, paid informers. (HIGH)
  - Revenue context (1768–1774): American customs averaged ~**£30,000/year** at a cost of ~£13,000; of proceeds, one-third to the British treasury, one-third to the colony's governor, one-third to the seizing officers. (MED — single-source figure; use cautiously, optional.)
- **Writs of assistance** — general (open-ended) search warrants the new customs regime relied on, letting officers search private property for smuggled goods without a specific cause. (HIGH — these long predate 1767, famously argued against by James Otis in 1761; the Townshend regime renewed their salience.)

## 2. THE COLONIAL RESPONSE (1767–1768)

- **The refined principle.** The colonists rejected Townshend's external gambit, refining their position from the Stamp Act–era external/internal line to: **no tax for revenue — external or internal — without consent.** The *purpose* (revenue from Americans without their assemblies' consent), not the collection point, was the test. (HIGH)
- **John Dickinson, *Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania*** — **twelve letters**, the first appearing in the *Pennsylvania Chronicle* on **2 December 1767** (one source: 30 November 1767 — minor discrepancy, use 2 Dec); ran over ~ten weeks into early **1768**; collected as a pamphlet (completed ~**8 February 1768**), reprinted in **19 of 23** colonial English-language newspapers and in seven American pamphlet editions, plus London, Dublin, Paris. (HIGH)
  - Tone: **moderate, lawyerly, hugely influential** — Dickinson (a wealthy Philadelphia lawyer, the "Pennsylvania Farmer" persona) argued Parliament could regulate trade but could **not** tax for revenue; he urged petition and restraint, not violence or independence. (HIGH — keep him as the moderate voice, contrast with Samuel Adams.)
- **Massachusetts Circular Letter** — drafted by **Samuel Adams** (with **James Otis**); approved by the Massachusetts House **11 February 1768**; sent to the speakers of the other colonial assemblies urging joint resistance to the Townshend taxation. (HIGH)
  - **Lord Hillsborough** (Wills Hill, Earl of Hillsborough — the first **Secretary of State for the Colonies**, post created 1768) ordered the Massachusetts House to **rescind** it and instructed other governors to dissolve any assembly that endorsed it. (HIGH)
  - The Massachusetts House refused to rescind, **30 June 1768**, by a vote of **92 to 17**. **"The Massachusetts 92"** became a patriot emblem of resistance. (HIGH)
  - Governor **Francis Bernard** then **dissolved the Massachusetts assembly**; royal governors dissolved several other assemblies that took up the letter. (HIGH)
- **Renewed nonimportation boycotts (1768–1770).** Boston merchants signed the **Boston Non-Importation Agreement on 1 August 1768**; New York joined in 1768; **Philadelphia joined only in 1769** (after stockpiling imports first). The coordinated boycott was the colonists' proven Stamp Act–era weapon, now revived. (HIGH)
- **Daughters of Liberty / homespun.** Women's role was central and visible: **spinning and weaving parties ("spinning bees")** became politically charged public events; **homespun cloth** became a badge of patriotism (substituting for boycotted British textiles); the Daughters of Liberty urged women to buy only from merchants honoring the boycott. (HIGH — frame as genuine economic-political participation, not decoration.)
- **Enforcement strain.** In **August 1769** the *Boston Chronicle* published merchants caught violating the boycott — public shaming as enforcement; also signaled the strain that would later crack the agreements. (MED)

## 3. THE OCCUPATION OF BOSTON (1768)

- **Customs commissioners harassed.** Bostonians made the new customs board's life difficult from its November 1767 seating onward; intimidation and crowd pressure were routine. (HIGH)
- **The *Liberty* affair (June 1768).** John Hancock's sloop ***Liberty***: (HIGH on the spine; specifics MED where noted)
  - **9 May 1768** — customs officers boarded *Liberty* and found **25 pipes of Madeira wine**, far less than her capacity (suggesting cargo had been run ashore untaxed). (MED — the "pipes" count and the smuggling inference)
  - A month later a customs official changed his story (claiming ~100 casks, only a quarter declared), with the Royal Navy warship **HMS *Romney*** now in the harbor. (MED)
  - **10 June 1768** — customs officials (Harrison and Benjamin **Hallowell**) seized *Liberty* (marked her mast with the broad arrow) and had her towed out under *Romney*'s guns. A **riot** erupted on the waterfront; the customs house was attacked; officers were roughed up. (HIGH on the seizure + riot)
  - The customs commissioners **fled — first to *Romney*, then to Castle William** (the island fort in the harbor) for safety. (HIGH)
- **Britain sends regulars.** In response to the *Liberty* riot and Boston's defiance, the ministry dispatched troops. **On 1 October 1768 the 14th and 29th Regiments of Foot landed at Long Wharf** and marched up King Street into the town — "with insolent Parade, Drums beating, Fifes playing, and Colours flying." (HIGH)
  - Scale: about **1,200 soldiers** disembarked from ~14 naval vessels (some 50-gun); a detachment of the **59th Regiment** and a train of **artillery (2 cannon)** also landed. In **November 1768** the **64th and 65th Regiments** arrived, pushing the garrison **well over 2,000** men. (HIGH on 1,200 / regiments; MED on the rounded 2,000+)
- **An army in a peacetime city.** Regular troops were garrisoned in a town not at war — quartering disputes, off-duty soldiers competing with locals for scarce work, daily insults and brawls. This sustained, grinding friction (Oct 1768 → March 1770) is the powder keg, not a single spark. (HIGH — emphasize the *duration*; the Massacre is sixteen months into an occupation.)

## 4. THE BOSTON MASSACRE (March 5, 1770)

- **Date/time:** evening of **5 March 1770**; the shooting around **~9:30 p.m.** (HIGH)
- **The spark.** A wigmaker's apprentice, **Edward Garrick** (~13), taunted a British officer (**Captain-Lieutenant John Goldfinch**) over an unpaid bill near the Custom House. The lone sentry, **Private Hugh White** (29th Regiment), told the boy to be respectful; insults flew; **White struck Garrick on the head with his musket.** A crowd began to gather. (HIGH)
- **Crowd grows.** Church bells rang (normally a fire alarm), drawing more people into King Street; the crowd swelled around the single sentry. (HIGH)
- **The relief party.** **Captain Thomas Preston** marched a relief party to White's aid: **a corporal and six/seven privates of the 29th Regiment**, with fixed bayonets. Named soldiers: Cpl. **William Wemms** and Privates **Hugh Montgomery, John Carroll, James Hartigan, William McCauley, William Warren, Matthew Kilroy.** (HIGH — note "eight soldiers" total counting White + the relief party is the figure used at trial.)
- **Crowd size — DISPUTED.** Estimates range **from ~50/60 ("a mob of sixty") up to 300–400.** Patriot accounts large, Preston's account small. There are **200+ surviving eyewitness accounts**, and they vary widely. **Give a range; do not assert one number.** (DISPUTED)
- **The provocation.** The crowd **pelted the soldiers** with snowballs, ice, oyster shells, sticks/clubs and other objects, jeered, and **dared/taunted them to fire** ("Fire!"). The soldiers were genuinely menaced and cornered against the Custom House. (HIGH — this provocation is essential and is what the Revere engraving omits.)
- **The shooting — DISPUTED order.** In the chaos **Private Hugh Montgomery was knocked down** (by a thrown object/club) and, regaining his feet, **fired** — apparently **without a clear order**; other soldiers then fired raggedly. **Whether Preston ordered "Fire," whether a soldier fired after being struck, or whether someone in the crowd shouted "fire," remains disputed** — the trial turned on exactly this, and Preston was acquitted. **Flag as disputed; the safe statement is that the soldiers fired without a proven order from Preston.** (DISPUTED)
- **The dead — FIVE colonists** (HIGH on names; ages/details as noted):
  - **Crispus Attucks** — dockworker/sailor of African and Wampanoag descent (b. ~1723); **died on the spot / among the first to fall.** (HIGH he died instantly; see §6 on heritage + mythology + the "led the crowd" dispute.)
  - **Samuel Gray** — a ropemaker; **died instantly.** (HIGH)
  - **James Caldwell** — a sailor/mariner; **died instantly.** (HIGH)
  - **Samuel Maverick** — a **17-year-old** apprentice; **died early the next morning, 6 March 1770.** (HIGH)
  - **Patrick Carr** — an Irish immigrant (leather-breeches maker), ~30; **died of his wounds on 14 March 1770** (about nine days later). (HIGH)
- **Wounded:** about **6 others** wounded (sources commonly give 11 total hit: 5 dead + 6 wounded; some say "8 others / 11 total"). Use "**six others wounded**" or "**eleven hit in all**." (MED — minor source variance on the wounded count.)
- **The funeral.** The five (Maverick and Carr added as they died) were given a massive public funeral and interred together; the procession was one of the largest gatherings in colonial Boston to that point. The victims' shared grave is at the **Granary Burying Ground**, Boston. (HIGH)

### The propaganda war
- **Paul Revere's engraving — *"The Bloody Massacre Perpetrated in King Street, Boston, on March 5th 1770, by a Party of the 29th Reg^t."*** — advertised/issued **late March 1770** (Revere advertised it **26 March 1770**). It is **deliberate, factually false propaganda** and must be used *as* propaganda, captioned as such. (HIGH)
  - **Copied from Henry Pelham.** Pelham (a young Boston artist; Copley's half-brother) designed the scene first (his print titled ***The Fruits of Arbitrary Power***). Revere saw Pelham's sketch, **copied it, and rushed his own version to print first without crediting Pelham.** Pelham's furious letter to Revere is dated **29 March 1770.** (HIGH)
  - **The lies in the image:** an **orderly firing line** of redcoats firing **on command** (an officer — read as Preston — with **raised sword ordering fire**); a defenseless, passive crowd with **no snowballs/clubs, no provocation shown**; the scene set in **daylight** (it was night); a sign reading **"Butcher's Hall"** added to the Custom House; a musket firing from a **Custom House window** ("a gun from the window"). All editorializing/false. (HIGH)
- **The word "Massacre" itself** is loaded contemporary framing — see HANDLE-WITH-CARE. (HIGH)

## 5. THE TRIAL & THE PARTIAL REPEAL (1770)

- **John Adams defends the soldiers.** **John Adams** (with **Josiah Quincy Jr.**, and Robert Auchmuty for Preston) defended the accused British soldiers, on principle — to uphold the right to a fair trial. **Samuel Quincy** and **Robert Treat Paine** prosecuted. (HIGH)
  - Adams worried about the cost to his reputation but later judged the defense **"one of the best Pieces of Service I ever rendered my Country"** (also called it "one of the most gallant, generous, manly and disinterested Actions of my whole Life"). (HIGH — verified quotes)
  - **"Facts are stubborn things"** — Adams's closing argument opened: *"Facts are stubborn things; and whatever may be our wishes, our inclinations, or the dictates of our passions, they cannot alter the state of facts and evidence."* (HIGH — verified)
- **The verdicts** (HIGH):
  - **Captain Thomas Preston — tried late October 1770, ACQUITTED** (the prosecution couldn't prove he ordered the volley).
  - **The eight soldiers — tried late November 1770** (verdict ~5 December): **six acquitted; two convicted of manslaughter** — **Hugh Montgomery and Matthew Kilroy.**
  - The two convicted **pleaded benefit of clergy**, escaping execution; they were **branded on the thumb** and released. (HIGH)
  - (A separate trial of four civilians accused of firing from the Custom House, **December 1770**, ended in acquittals.) (MED — include only if useful.)
  - **Patrick Carr's dying deposition** helped the defense: his surgeon (**Dr. John Jeffries**) testified Carr said the soldiers fired in self-defense / bore "no malice" and "fired to defend himself." (HIGH)
- **The partial repeal — SAME DAY as the Massacre.** On **5 March 1770** (the very day of the King Street killings, though London had no word of them yet) **Lord North** moved in the House of Commons to **repeal most of the Townshend duties.** The repeal received **royal assent 12 April 1770** (Repeal Act, 10 Geo. III c. 17). (HIGH)
  - **The tea duty was deliberately KEPT** — North argued the tea tax must be retained to assert "the right of taxing the Americans," consistent with the Declaratory Act of 1766. (HIGH — verified North reasoning.)
- **The boycotts collapse / the lull.** With the duties cut to tea alone, the appearance of victory plus boycott fatigue cracked nonimportation: it collapsed first at **Albany, Providence, Newport (May 1770)**, then **New York (July 1770)**, **Philadelphia (12 September 1770)**, **Boston (12 October 1770)**, **Charleston (13 December 1770).** A **relative calm followed (1770–1773).** (HIGH — this is the anti-inevitability beat.)
- **Set up, don't narrate, the next chapter.** The **surviving tea duty** is the lone unresolved tax — the fuse to the Tea Party (1773). State that it remained; do **not** narrate the Tea Party here. (HIGH)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **The word "MASSACRE" is loaded contemporary propaganda.** What happened was a **chaotic, violent nighttime street confrontation** — a small relief party, cornered against the Custom House, fired into a **hostile, pelting, taunting crowd**. It was **not a planned or one-sided slaughter of innocents.** Present it honestly: the soldiers were genuinely menaced; *and* five townspeople (none with firearms) died. Both halves are true; hold them together. Don't adopt "massacre" as the app's own neutral description without flagging it as the patriots' framing.
2. **Revere's engraving is propaganda and factually false in specifics** (orderly line firing on command, officer ordering fire, defenseless crowd, no snowballs/clubs, daylight, "Butcher's Hall," gun from the window) and was **copied from Henry Pelham's design.** Flag it explicitly; use it *as* propaganda, captioned as such — never as an accurate depiction.
3. **The colonists were DIVIDED.** Many Bostonians were uneasy about the mob's harassment of soldiers and customs officers; John Dickinson's moderation and John Adams's defense both reflect a population that was far from unanimous. Feeds the war's **"first civil war" / Britons-against-Britons** thread. Avoid a unified-patriot-people frame.
4. **John Adams defending the soldiers = the rule-of-law angle.** Treat it as **genuinely principled** (a patriot ensuring British soldiers got a fair trial), and note he **worried about the cost to his reputation** — not as a stunt or a contradiction.
5. **Crispus Attucks** was real — of **African and Wampanoag descent** (b. ~1723; widely believed to have escaped enslavement near Framingham, Mass., c. 1750; worked as a sailor/whaler/dockhand). He was **heavily mythologized later** — abolitionists led by **William Cooper Nell** made him "the first martyr of liberty"; the first **"Crispus Attucks Day"** was held at Faneuil Hall in **1858**. State plainly what's known; **do not hagiographize or invent biographical detail.** The honest tension worth surfacing (without anachronism): **a man of color was among the first to die in a cause that would not free the enslaved** — a Revolution-wide framing point, not a claim about his motives.
6. **Did Preston order "Fire"? — DISPUTED.** The trial turned on it; Preston was acquitted because it couldn't be proven. Safe statement: the soldiers fired **without a proven order from Preston** (Montgomery, knocked down, apparently fired first; "fire" may have been shouted by the crowd). Don't assert Preston ordered the volley.
7. **Crowd size — DISPUTED.** ~50/60 to 300–400 across 200+ varying accounts. Use a **range**, never a single figure.
8. **Did Attucks "lead" the crowd? — DISPUTED/propagandized.** Some accounts (and later legend) put him at the front leading the press toward the soldiers; this is contested and partly mythological. Safe statement: he was **at or near the front and among the first (often called the first) to fall.** Don't assert he led an assault.
9. **No tyrant caricature of the British side.** Townshend's external-duty maneuver was a **reasoned political bet** (exploit the colonists' own distinction; fund officials independently); North's keep-the-tea-tax was a **calculated minimum assertion of principle**, not cruelty. Present the British as reasoning actors.
10. **AVOID inevitability.** After the partial repeal there was a **genuine lull (1770–1773)** — not continuous escalation. Independence was **still not the goal**; most colonists still saw themselves as aggrieved Britons seeking redress.
11. **Minor numeric variances to keep loose:** the *Liberty* wine count (25 pipes vs ~100 casks claim); wounded count (commonly "six," sometimes phrased "8 others / 11 total"); customs-revenue figures (single-source). Phrase softly.
