## BEAT 1: The Context — Five Days After Appomattox

- **Lee's surrender:** Robert E. Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Ulysses S. Grant at Appomattox Court House, Virginia, on **April 9, 1865**. Richmond, the Confederate capital, had fallen days earlier (Union forces entered April 3). The war was effectively, though not formally, over. Lincoln was shot **five days** after Appomattox.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"; Ford's Theatre, "Lincoln's Assassination"

- **Washington's celebration:** Washington was in the middle of euphoric celebration over the war's end when Lincoln was killed — illuminations, bonfires, serenades. The assassination landed in the middle of national jubilation, which sharpened the shock.
  — Source: Library of Congress, "Assassination of President Abraham Lincoln"

- **Lincoln's last public address (April 11, 1865):** From a window of the White House, to a crowd celebrating the victory, Lincoln delivered what became his final speech. It was largely about the hard problem of Reconstruction — specifically, whether to recognize the new free-state government of Louisiana. In it he became **the first U.S. president to publicly endorse voting rights for any Black Americans**, advocating a limited Black suffrage.
  — Source: Abraham Lincoln Online, "Last Public Address"; OUPblog, "Why Lincoln's last speech matters"; History.com, "What Lincoln Said in His Final Speech"

- **Verbatim — the suffrage line (from the April 11 address):**
  > "It is also unsatisfactory to some that the elective franchise is not given to the colored man. I would myself prefer that it were now conferred on the very intelligent, and on those who serve our cause as soldiers."
  — Source: Abraham Lincoln Online, "Last Public Address" (full text); History News Network

- **Booth in the crowd:** John Wilkes Booth was present at the April 11 speech, with conspirator Lewis Powell. Booth was enraged by the suffrage endorsement. His reaction is recorded in two forms (see Verified Quotes): a version emphasizing his racism ("That means [n-word] citizenship") and the famous threat, **"That is the last speech he will ever make."** Historians (e.g., the History News Network piece "Yes, John Wilkes Booth did Speak Those Notorious Words") accept that Booth said words to this effect that night.
  — Source: Ford's Theatre, "Lincoln's Assassination"; History News Network, "Yes, John Wilkes Booth did Speak Those Notorious Words"

- **Lincoln's reported premonition/dream:** Lincoln's friend, sometime bodyguard, and former law associate **Ward Hill Lamon** recorded that, a few days before the assassination, Lincoln described a dream to a small group including Mary Todd Lincoln. In the dream Lincoln wandered the White House toward "mournful sounds of distress," reached the East Room, and found a covered corpse on a catafalque guarded by soldiers, with mourners weeping. He asked a soldier, "Who is dead in the White House?" and was told, "The President… he was killed by an assassin."
  — Source: History.com, "Did Abraham Lincoln Predict His Own Death?"; TeachingHistory.org, "Premonition of Death"

  **[VERIFY / CONTESTED]** Lamon's account was first published in the 1880s, ~20 years later, and reconstructed from notes; historians Don E. and Virginia Fehrenbacher cite internal inconsistencies and doubt its veracity. **Author guidance: present as "a story Lamon told" / "reportedly," not as established fact.** It is a vivid, much-repeated piece of the legend, but it is not solidly documented.

## BEAT 2: John Wilkes Booth — the Actor Turned Assassin

- **Birth:** **May 10, 1838**, near Bel Air, Maryland (Harford County). Son of the renowned British-American Shakespearean actor **Junius Brutus Booth** and Mary Ann Holmes.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John Wilkes Booth"

- **The acting dynasty:** A member of America's most famous theatrical family. His brother **Edwin Booth** was one of the era's greatest actors — and a Unionist. (Note for contrast: Edwin had once, by chance, saved the life of Lincoln's son Robert Todd Lincoln on a train platform — a much-cited irony, **[VERIFY]** the date/details before asserting.) Sister Asia Booth Clarke wrote a memoir of him.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John Wilkes Booth"

- **His own fame:** A celebrated leading man in his own right, handsome and athletic, reportedly earning around **$20,000 a year** by the late 1850s. He performed frequently at Ford's Theatre and knew the building intimately. His final stage appearance was **March 18, 1865.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John Wilkes Booth"

- **Politics — Confederate and pro-slavery:** Strongly pro-Confederate and pro-slavery. He **attended John Brown's execution on December 2, 1859**, having borrowed a Richmond Grays militia uniform to gain access. When Lincoln was elected (Nov 6, 1860), Booth drafted a long speech decrying Northern abolitionism. He remained in the North acting through the war but seethed with Confederate sympathy.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John Wilkes Booth"

- **The plot's evolution — KIDNAPPING first:** Booth's original scheme (developed across **1864 into early 1865**) was to **kidnap** Lincoln and spirit him to Richmond, to be **ransomed for Confederate prisoners of war** — the Union had largely halted prisoner exchanges, draining Confederate manpower. Booth made an unexplained trip to **Montreal in October 1864** and met Confederate agents there. He recruited a cell for the abduction.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John Wilkes Booth"; Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **The failed kidnapping attempt (March 17, 1865):** The conspirators planned to abduct Lincoln as he returned from a performance at Campbell General Hospital, but Lincoln changed his plans and attended a ceremony at the National Hotel instead. The abduction never happened.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **The shift to ASSASSINATION:** After **Richmond fell (April 3)** and **Lee surrendered (April 9)**, kidnapping became pointless — there was no Confederacy left to ransom prisoners to. Booth pivoted to killing Lincoln. The April 11 suffrage speech hardened his resolve.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"; Ford's Theatre

- **The goal was decapitation, not just regicide:** Booth conceived the April 14 plan as a triple strike to **throw the U.S. government into chaos** by killing the president, vice president, and secretary of state at once (see Beat 4).
  — Source: Ford's Theatre, "Lincoln's Assassination"; Britannica, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

## BEAT 4: The Coordinated Plot — Night of April 14

- **The 8:45 pm briefing:** At a meeting (~8:45 pm) Booth assigned the targets:
  - **Booth → President Lincoln**, at Ford's Theatre.
  - **Lewis Powell → Secretary of State William H. Seward**, at Seward's home (Herold to guide Powell, who didn't know the city).
  - **George Atzerodt → Vice President Andrew Johnson**, at the Kirkwood House hotel.
  - Target time: **shortly after 10 pm**, to strike simultaneously.
  — Source: Ford's Theatre, "Lincoln's Assassination"; Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **The point:** Kill the top three officials in the line of authority at once and decapitate the government. (Note: the statutory line of succession in 1865 did not run president→VP→Secretary of State the way modern law does, but Booth plainly meant to throw the government into disarray.)
  — Source: Britannica; Ford's Theatre

### The Powell attack on Seward (~10:10 pm)

- **Why Seward was vulnerable:** Seward was bedridden at home, recovering from a **carriage accident** that had fractured his jaw and arm; his jaw was held by a metal/leather splint.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **The attack:** Powell came to the door claiming to deliver medicine from Seward's physician. Servant **William Bell** answered. Seward's son **Frederick W. Seward** (Assistant Secretary of State) blocked the stairs; Powell drew a revolver, aimed at Frederick's head — **the gun misfired** — and Powell bludgeoned Frederick unconscious with it. Powell then forced into the bedroom and **stabbed Seward repeatedly in the face and neck with a Bowie knife**, slicing his cheek open. **The jaw splint is widely credited with deflecting the blade from Seward's jugular.** Seward survived, badly scarred.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **Others wounded by Powell:** Seward's son **Augustus Seward**, Army nurse/Sergeant **George F. Robinson**, and State Department messenger **Emerick Hansell** (stabbed in the back as Powell fled). Powell reportedly shouted "I'm mad! I'm mad!" as he ran. **Herold had already fled, leaving Powell to escape alone through an unfamiliar city** — which led to his arrest days later.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

### The Atzerodt failure

- **Atzerodt lost his nerve entirely.** He had rented a room at the Kirkwood House directly above Johnson's, and arrived with a gun and knife — but went down to the bar, got drunk, asked the bartender about Johnson, then wandered off, **threw away his knife**, and did nothing. Andrew Johnson was never attacked.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **The Booth calling card:** Earlier that day Booth had left a note/card for Johnson at the hotel: **"Don't wish to disturb you. Are you at home? J. Wilkes Booth."** (Its meaning has been debated ever since — it later complicated suspicions about Johnson.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

## BEAT 5: Ford's Theatre — The Assassination

- **The play and date:** **Good Friday, April 14, 1865.** The play was the comedy ***Our American Cousin***. By the time Booth struck, the show was well into its last act.
  — Source: Ford's Theatre; Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **In the state box:** President Lincoln, **Mary Todd Lincoln**, Army officer **Major Henry Rathbone**, and Rathbone's fiancée **Clara Harris** (daughter of U.S. Senator Ira Harris of New York). General Grant and his wife had been invited but declined — a much-noted near-miss.
  — Source: History.com, "Abraham Lincoln's Assassination"; Wikipedia

- **The unguarded door:** Lincoln's assigned Metropolitan Police bodyguard, **John Frederick Parker**, left his post at the box during the performance (he reportedly went to a tavern). The presidential box was left **unguarded**, letting Booth — who knew the theater well — walk right up to it. Booth also wedged the outer door of the box so it could not be opened from behind.
  — Source: Ford's Theatre; Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **Booth's entry (~10:10–10:15 pm):** Booth, a celebrity who could move freely backstage and front-of-house, entered the box behind Lincoln. He carried a **single-shot Philadelphia Deringer pistol** in one hand and a **dagger** in the other.
  — Source: Ford's Theatre; Wikipedia

- **The shot (~10:15 pm):** Booth fired one bullet point-blank into the **back of Lincoln's head, behind the left ear.** "The bullet entered Lincoln's skull behind his left ear, passed through his brain, and came to rest near the front of the skull after fracturing both orbital plates." (See CONTESTED for the Derringer caliber dispute.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **Rathbone stabbed:** Major Rathbone lunged at Booth; Booth **slashed/stabbed Rathbone in the left arm** with the dagger, cutting deep.
  — Source: Wikipedia; History.com

- **The leap and the broken leg:** Booth **jumped from the box (~12 feet) to the stage.** His spur (some accounts: his boot) caught on the **Treasury flag / U.S. flag bunting** draped on the box, and he landed awkwardly, **breaking the fibula of his left leg.**
  — Source: Wikipedia; Ford's Theatre; History.com

- **"Sic semper tyrannis!"** — Booth shouted from the stage. The phrase ("Thus always to tyrants" — the Virginia state motto) is the standard account, but witnesses disagreed on his exact words (some heard "The South is avenged!" / "Revenge for the South!"). He then fled out the back, mounted a waiting horse, and escaped into the night.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"; Ford's Theatre. **[CONTESTED — see quotes section: also whether he said it from the box or the stage.]**

## BEAT 6: The Petersen House — The Death Watch

- **Carried across the street:** Lincoln, mortally wounded and unconscious, was carried out of Ford's Theatre and across Tenth Street to the boardinghouse of tailor **William Petersen.** Because the **6'4" Lincoln was too tall for the small bed**, he was laid **diagonally** across it in a first-floor back bedroom.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"; Ford's Theatre

- **The death watch:** Lincoln never regained consciousness. A rotating crowd of physicians (first on the scene at the theater was Army surgeon **Charles Leale**; others included Surgeon General **Joseph K. Barnes** and Lincoln's family physician **Robert K. Stone**), cabinet members, and family kept vigil through the night. Secretary of War **Edwin M. Stanton** effectively ran the government — and the manhunt — from the back parlor.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"; Library of Congress

- **Death:** Lincoln died at **7:22 am, April 15, 1865.** One physician described "no apparent suffering, no convulsive action… [only] a mere cessation of breathing."
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **Stanton's line:** At or just after the moment of death, Stanton is famously said to have pronounced **"Now he belongs to the ages."** **[CONTESTED — ages vs. angels: see Verified Quotes.]**
  — Source: Walter Stahr, "Lincoln's Death, Stanton's Words"; RealClearPolitics; Adam Gopnik, *Angels and Ages*

- **Johnson sworn in:** Vice President **Andrew Johnson** was sworn in as the **17th president** later that morning (between ~10–11 am) by Chief Justice **Salmon P. Chase.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

## BEAT 7: The Manhunt — 12 Days

- **The escape route:** Booth, with **David Herold**, crossed the **Navy Yard Bridge** out of Washington into southern Maryland within about half an hour of the shooting. (Sentry **Silas Cobb** let them pass despite a curfew on civilian crossings.) They retrieved supplies at Surrattsville.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **Dr. Mudd sets the leg:** They reached the home of **Dr. Samuel A. Mudd** in the early hours of April 15; Mudd **splinted Booth's broken left leg** and gave him crutches. (Mudd's degree of foreknowledge is debated to this day — see CONTESTED.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **Hiding in the swamp:** Confederate sympathizer **Thomas Jones** hid the pair in a **pine thicket / Zekiah Swamp** for about five days before helping them cross the Potomac into Virginia.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **The reward:** The War Department posted **$100,000 total** — **$50,000 for Booth, $25,000 each for John Surratt and David Herold** — on the April 20, 1865 wanted broadside.
  — Source: Library of Congress, "$100,000 reward!" broadside; Wikipedia

- **The Garrett farm (April 24–26):** Booth and Herold arrived at **Richard H. Garrett's tobacco farm** in **Caroline / King George County, Virginia** (near Port Royal/Port Conway). On **April 26, 1865**, soldiers of the **16th New York Cavalry** surrounded the tobacco barn where the two were hiding. **Herold surrendered; Booth refused.** The soldiers set the barn on fire.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"; History.com

- **Booth shot:** Sergeant **Boston Corbett** shot Booth through a gap in the barn wall — strikingly, "in the back of the head about an inch below the spot where his [Booth's] shot had entered the head of Mr. Lincoln," severing his spinal cord and paralyzing him. (Corbett claimed he fired against orders to take Booth alive.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **Last words:** Booth, paralyzed, was carried to the Garrett farmhouse porch and lived about three more hours, dying the morning of **April 26.** Unable to move his hands, he asked a soldier to raise them before his face, looked at them, and whispered **"Useless… useless."**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John Wilkes Booth"; Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **Booth's diary:** Found on his body, his diary included the self-justifying line: **"Our country owed all her troubles to him, and God simply made me the instrument of his punishment."**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John Wilkes Booth"

## BEAT 8: Justice — The Tribunal and the Gallows

- **A military tribunal, not a civilian court:** The accused conspirators were tried by a **nine-officer military commission**, not in civilian court — a hugely controversial choice (the war was over and the civilian courts were open). Presided over by Maj. Gen. **David Hunter**; the only lawyer on the panel was Maj. Gen. **Lew Wallace** (later author of *Ben-Hur*). The prosecution was led by Judge Advocate General **Joseph Holt** with **John A. Bingham.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **Scale:** The trial ran about **seven weeks** with **roughly 366 witnesses.** Verdicts (guilty on all) came **June 30, 1865.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **The four hanged (July 7, 1865), at the Old Arsenal Penitentiary (now Fort Lesley J. McNair), Washington:**
  - **Mary Surratt** — **the first woman executed by the U.S. federal government.**
  - **Lewis Powell** (Paine)
  - **David Herold**
  - **George Atzerodt**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"; Washingtonian; LincolnConspirators.com

- **The prison sentences:**
  - **Dr. Samuel Mudd** — life imprisonment
  - **Samuel Arnold** — life imprisonment
  - **Michael O'Laughlen** — life imprisonment (**died in prison, 1867**, in a yellow fever epidemic)
  - **Edman Spangler** — **6 years**
  Mudd, Arnold, and Spangler were **pardoned by President Johnson in February 1869.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **The Surratt controversy:** Mary Surratt's guilt is debated to this day. The evidence that she knowingly participated (vs. merely owning the boardinghouse where the plot was hatched, and running an errand to her tavern the day of the assassination) was circumstantial and partly rested on the testimony of informer **Louis Weichmann** and tavern-keeper **John Lloyd.** **Five of the nine commission members signed a petition recommending clemency** (life imprisonment instead of death) on account of her sex and age; **President Johnson said he never saw it** (Holt said he delivered it) — a dispute that has never been resolved. She was hanged anyway.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Mary Surratt"; Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

- **John Surratt's escape and trial:** Mary's son **John Surratt** fled to Canada, then Liverpool, then served in the **Papal Zouaves** in Italy under an alias; he was recognized, escaped, and was finally captured in **Egypt in late 1866.** Tried in **civilian court in 1867** (after the Supreme Court's *Ex parte Milligan* decision cast doubt on military trials of civilians); the **jury deadlocked** and he was **released**, never reconvicted — a sharp contrast with his mother's fate that fed the controversy over the military tribunal.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln"

## BEAT 9: Aftermath / Meaning

- **Andrew Johnson becomes president (Reconstruction PREVIEW — keep brief):** The Tennessee Unionist Democrat Lincoln had added to the 1864 ticket now held the presidency. His clashes with the Republican Congress over the terms of reuniting the country would make Reconstruction far harsher and more chaotic than Lincoln's hoped-for "soft peace," and would end in Johnson's impeachment. **(Hand off to the Reckoning section — do not narrate further.)**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln" (aftermath); standard historiography

- **The poisoned peace:** Lincoln had signaled leniency toward the defeated South ("With malice toward none…"). His murder — by a Confederate partisan — hardened Northern opinion, discredited any conciliatory approach, and removed the one figure with the standing to manage a gentle reunion. The assassination is widely argued to have **damaged the prospects of a "soft peace"** and embittered Reconstruction.
  — Source: Britannica, "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln" (impact); standard historiography. **[Author guidance: this is interpretive — frame as the consensus historical judgment, not a hard fact.]**

- **The funeral train:** Lincoln's body, **embalmed** (the era's new embalming made the long display tour possible), was carried by a special train dubbed **"The Lincoln Special"** from Washington beginning **April 21, 1865**, on a roughly **1,654-mile** (often rounded to "about 1,700 miles") journey to **Springfield, Illinois**, where he was buried **May 4, 1865.** The route deliberately **retraced, in reverse, the 1861 journey he had taken to Washington for his inauguration**, passing through ~400 communities in 7 states.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "State funeral of Abraham Lincoln"; History.com, "Abraham Lincoln's Funeral Train"; HistoryNet

  **[VERIFY — distance figure]** Sources commonly cite **~1,654 miles over ~13 days**; "1,700 miles" is a common rounding. The user's brief says "1,700-mile journey" — **prefer "roughly 1,700 miles" or cite ~1,654.** Both are defensible; flag the exact number.

- **Mass mourning:** Roughly **1.5 million people viewed the body**, and an estimated **7 million saw the train or hearse pass.** At about 13 major stops the coffin was taken off, processioned through draped streets, and laid in state for public viewing. **Willie Lincoln's** coffin (Lincoln's son, died 1862) traveled on the train to be reburied with his father at Springfield.
  — Source: History.com, "Abraham Lincoln's Funeral Train"; Wikipedia, "State funeral of Abraham Lincoln". **[VERIFY — the 1.5M / 7M figures are widely repeated estimates, not exact counts; flag as "an estimated."]**

- **Lincoln as martyr / secular saint:** Killed on **Good Friday**, in the hour of victory, the previously polarizing wartime president was instantly transformed into a national **martyr** — a "secular saint." The funeral pageant, the martyrdom framing, and the timing fused into a near-religious cult of "Father Abraham" that reshaped his memory for generations.
  — Source: standard historiography (e.g., Library of Congress assassination essay; Ford's Theatre). **[Author guidance: interpretive consensus — frame as such.]**

## CONTESTED / FLAGGED (author guidance)

1. **Derringer caliber — .41 vs .44.** Sources differ: Booth's pistol is most authoritatively a **.44-caliber single-shot Philadelphia Deringer** (per the History.com / standard museum account), but the Wikipedia "John Wilkes Booth" article in one place says **.41 caliber.** The actual surviving weapon at Ford's is a Henry Deringer pocket pistol. **Author guidance: "a single-shot Deringer pistol" avoids the dispute; if a caliber is wanted, use ".44-caliber" and flag that .41 appears in some sources.**

2. **Booth's exact words on stage.** "Sic semper tyrannis!" is standard but contested; witnesses also reported "The South is avenged," and there's dispute over whether he spoke from the box, the stage, or both. **Use "Sic semper tyrannis!" as the famous line; note witnesses disagreed.**

3. **Stanton: "ages" vs. "angels."** The line may have been "ages," "angels," or never said at all — it surfaces in the Hay/Nicolay account ~25 years later and James Tanner's stenographic note ("angels"). Adam Gopnik (*Angels and Ages*) treats the dispute as essentially unresolvable. **Use "Now he belongs to the ages" (the canonical form) but note the angels/ages dispute and the doubt it was said at all.**

4. **Booth's reaction quote (Apr 11) — "make" vs. "give," and the racist preamble.** Wording varies. **Use "That is the last speech he will ever make"; you may note Booth's reaction was explicitly racist (the suffrage line is what set him off).**

5. **Lincoln's premonition dream.** Lamon's account is late and doubted by the Fehrenbachers. **Attribute to Lamon / "the story goes," never assert as fact.**

6. **Dr. Mudd's guilt/foreknowledge.** Whether Mudd knew he was treating the assassin (vs. an innocent doctor setting a stranger's leg) has been argued for 160 years; his descendants long sought to clear his name. **Present as genuinely contested.**

7. **Mary Surratt's guilt + the clemency petition.** Her guilt is debated; the clemency-recommendation petition (signed by 5 of 9 commissioners) and whether Johnson ever saw it is an unresolved dispute (Holt vs. Johnson). **Present both as contested.**

8. **Funeral-train distance (~1,654 vs. "1,700 miles") and mourner counts (~1.5M / 7M).** These are common roundings/estimates, not exact. **Use "roughly" / "an estimated."**

9. **Edwin Booth saving Robert Todd Lincoln.** The much-cited story that John Wilkes Booth's brother Edwin once saved Lincoln's son from a train-platform accident is real but worth **[VERIFY]** for exact details before asserting. **Author guidance: include only if verified; it's a great irony but get the facts right.**

10. **Succession line in 1865.** Don't imply the modern president→VP→Secretary of State succession order justified the targets; the plot's logic was chaos/decapitation, not statutory succession.
