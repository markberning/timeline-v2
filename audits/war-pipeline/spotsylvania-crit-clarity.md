# Spotsylvania — NEWCOMER / CLARITY critic gate

> War content pipeline, step 3 critic. Gate: CLARITY. Cold read as a zero-knowledge but
> sharp 15-year-old: every term defined on first use, no unexplained leaps, every named
> actor side-tagged AND scene-set, no overwhelming passages. Verdict per item: CLEAR /
> NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST, tagged MUST-FIX or SHOULD-FIX. This gate does NOT judge facts.

## Headline verdict: PASS WITH FIXES (very clear draft; one MUST-FIX, several SHOULD-FIX)

This draft is unusually disciplined about inline definition — it teaches *corps*,
*works*, *salient*, *enfilade*, *court house*, *flank*, *cavalry screen*, and *minié
ball* all on first use, in voice, without slowing down. A sharp newcomer can follow the
whole battle. The remaining problems are: one undefined-on-first-use term (*foot-race*
appears in the title before it's explained — minor), a few **named actors who arrive
without a job title or scene** (the reader is told what *side* they're on but not who
they *are*), and a couple of dense clock/number passages that risk overwhelming. None
of these break comprehension; they're polish that makes a good draft airtight.

### NEEDS-GLOSS / scene-setting gaps

- **"foot-race" (section title, `the-race`).** The term appears in the section subtitle
  before the body explains the race. A sharp newcomer will infer it from context within
  two paragraphs, so this is minor — but the *title* uses the term cold. SHOULD-FIX:
  either the body's "it became a race in the most literal sense" lands fast enough to
  cover it, or lightly rephrase the title. Low priority.

- **"First Corps," "Second Corps," "V Corps," "VI Corps," "II Corps" — the numbering
  switches systems mid-piece.** Confederate corps are named with words ("First Corps,"
  "Second Corps"); Union corps are named with Roman numerals ("V Corps," "VI Corps," "II
  Corps"). This is historically correct (it's how the two armies actually numbered
  themselves), but a newcomer has no way to know that, and may read "First Corps" and "V
  Corps" as two unrelated things or wonder where Union "First through Fourth" went.
  SHOULD-FIX: a single clarifying half-sentence the first time both appear ("the
  Confederates numbered their corps in words, the Union in Roman numerals") would
  prevent a quiet confusion. The reader can survive without it, but it's a free fix.

- **Named actors who arrive with a side-tag but no JOB or scene** — this is the most
  common clarity gap. The reader is told *which side* but not *who the person is* or
  *why they matter*:
  - **Maj. Gen. Martin McMahon (North)** (`laurel-hill`) — introduced only as Sedgwick's
    "chief of staff," which is fine, but he's given a Maj. Gen. rank-tag for a one-line
    walk-on; CLEAR enough.
  - **Brig. Gen. Gershom Mott (North)** (`the-mule-shoe`) — appears as "a division under
    Brig. Gen. Gershom Mott" whose supporting attack "wilted." The reader gets what he
    *did* (failed to support Upton) but he's a name with no prior existence. CLEAR
    functionally (his role is self-evident from the action), no fix needed.
  - **Maj. Gen. Edward "Allegheny" Johnson (South)** — well scene-set ("whose division
    held the apex"). CLEAR.
  - **Brig. Gen. Francis Barlow (North)** (`the-bloody-angle`) — "Brig. Gen. Francis
    Barlow's division poured over the works" — appears with no introduction at all. The
    action carries him, so it's CLEAR, but he's the man who overran the apex and breaks
    Lee's line; a 3-word role-tag wouldn't hurt. SHOULD-FIX (minor).
  - **McGowan, Ramseur (South)** (`the-bloody-angle`) — appear as "the South Carolinians
    of Brig. Gen. Samuel McGowan... the men of Brig. Gen. Stephen Ramseur... brigade
    after brigade." Here the *un*-introduced rush is arguably the point (the chaos of
    feeding men into a breach), and the prose signals that with "brigade after brigade
    thrown straight into the breach." CLEAR — the blur is intentional and reads as
    intentional.
  - **Brig. Gen. George "Maryland" Steuart (South)** — introduced as "one of his brigade
    commanders." CLEAR.
  - **Maj. Gen. John B. Gordon (South)** — "flung counterattacking brigades into the
    gap" — role is self-evident from action. CLEAR.
  - **Maj. Gen. Henry Halleck (North)** — glossed ("the army's chief of staff in
    Washington"). CLEAR.
  - **Pvt. John Huff (North)** — scene-set ("a dismounted Union trooper"). CLEAR.
  - **Maj. Gen. Fitzhugh Lee (South)** — appears as "took over the cavalry"; the reader
    may briefly conflate him with Robert E. Lee given the shared surname two paragraphs
    after R.E. Lee is active. SHOULD-FIX: one clause distinguishing him (e.g., "Lee's
    nephew") prevents a same-name stumble. Minor.
  - **Lt. Col. Horace Porter (North)** — scene-set ("Grant's aide"). CLEAR.
  - **Brig. Gen. Thomas Rosser (South)**, **2nd Ohio Cavalry (North)**, **23rd USCT
    (North)** — all scene-set in `the-23rd`. CLEAR.

- **"redoubt" — NOT present.** (The brief flagged it; the draft never uses it. No
  action.)

### Passages that risk OVERWHELMING

- **The guns paragraph (`the-mule-shoe`, "There was one more piece...").** MUST-FIX for
  clarity, not just storytelling. This single paragraph asks the newcomer to track, in
  order: Lee's wrong guess about Grant disengaging → ~29 guns pulled out → Johnson's
  midnight request → the order to return them → "crawled" → "half past three in the
  morning" → Hancock landing "about half an hour later" → Alexander's verdict. That's
  five actors and three timestamps in one paragraph, and the *causal* point (the guns
  were on the road when the attack hit) is at the very end. A newcomer can lose the
  thread. SHOULD-FIX→treat as MUST-FIX for legibility: lead with the plain-language
  consequence ("Lee accidentally left his weakest point without cannon at the exact
  moment it was attacked"), THEN the clock detail. Same facts, front-loaded conclusion.
  (Storytelling gate flags the same paragraph independently — converging signal.)

- **The strength/casualty numbers (`the-23rd`, "roughly eighteen thousand... twelve to
  thirteen thousand").** Rendered as words, as ranges, in a single clean comparative
  sentence. This is the *right* way to do numbers for a newcomer — CLEAR, no fix.

- **The Bloody Angle horror paragraph.** Long, but each sentence is a single concrete
  image building on the last (muzzle-to-muzzle → soaked powder → hand-to-hand → men
  climbing over the dead). It reads fast despite its length. CLEAR — not overwhelming,
  because it's images not data.

### Unexplained leaps

- **"the now-familiar pattern" / "the same pattern" (`the-mule-shoe`, `the-23rd`).** By
  the time these appear, the draft HAS established the pattern (attack → repulse → slide
  southeast), explicitly, in `the-race` ("the pattern almost never varied"). So the
  callbacks are earned. CLEAR.
- **"a near-replay of a moment in the Wilderness" / "It was nearly a replay of a moment
  in the Wilderness days earlier" (`the-bloody-angle`).** The reader is told it happened
  before but not what happened (the Widow Tapp / "Lee to the rear" episode). This is a
  forgivable terse cross-reference — the meaning ("his men wouldn't let him risk
  himself") is fully carried by the current scene, so the Wilderness callback is
  flavor, not load-bearing. CLEAR.
- **The Corinth callback (`the-23rd`, "The railroad junction at Corinth had its
  contraband camp...").** LOST for a reader who hasn't read the Corinth section. This is
  an orphaned cross-reference: "contraband camp" is never glossed here, and the parallel
  only works if you already know the Corinth story. SHOULD-FIX: gloss "contraband camp"
  in one clause or cut the callback — the "different ground, same point" beat stands on
  its own. (Storytelling gate flags this too.)
