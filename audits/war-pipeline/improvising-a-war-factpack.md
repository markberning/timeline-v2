## §1 — Two Republics, Unequal: the strategic asymmetry

**The framing fact: this is a war over slavery.** Eleven states seceded and raised
armies to preserve and extend the enslavement of roughly four million people; the
Confederacy was formed for that purpose, and said so in its own founding documents.
The author MUST state this plainly in the chapter's own voice (NAME THE CAUSE rule,
ship-blocking) — the two huge armies of 1861 were being raised, fundamentally, over
slavery. [The South's own constitution/Cornerstone Speech are quoted in the **Two
Governments** theme — LINK ACROSS for the primary-source case.]

**Population (1860 census / war start).**
- Union ≈ **22 million** people; Confederacy fewer than **9 million**, of whom nearly **4 million were enslaved** (≈ one-third of the South's population, and people the South would not arm). [ushistory.org; ncanchor.org; Battlefields.org]
- Split shares often cited as **Union ~71% / Confederacy ~29%** of national population. [ncanchor.org]
- Strategic point: the South's free, mobilizable manpower pool was a fraction of the North's — and a huge share of its population was the very people the war was being fought to keep enslaved.

**Industry & manufacturing (1860).**
- The North manufactured **~97% of the country's firearms, ~96% of its railroad locomotives, ~94% of its cloth, ~93% of its pig iron**, and over 90% of its boots and shoes. [nps.gov "Industry and Economy during the Civil War"]
- The South was overwhelmingly agricultural; the industrial revolution had largely stopped at the Mason–Dixon line. [nps.gov]

**Railroads (the logistics asymmetry).**
- North ≈ **21,000–22,000 miles** of track; South ≈ **9,000 miles** (often cited as **~71% / ~29%**). [Statista; civilwartalk summary of standard figures]
- The South had roughly **one-third of the freight cars, one-fifth of the locomotives, one-tenth of the telegraph stations**, and a small fraction of locomotive production. [ushistory.org]
- Strategic meaning: railroads were the supply nervous system of a continental war; the North could move and feed armies the South could not match — and Southern track was a patchwork of different gauges, hard to run as one network.

**Navy.** The U.S. Navy was small but it was a *national* navy with ships, yards, and
officers; the Confederacy started with **essentially no navy** and had to improvise
one. This is what made the blockade (§4) a Union instrument from day one. [general;
see history.state.gov blockade]

**The Confederacy's real advantages — why "weaker" did NOT mean "doomed."**
- **It only had to survive.** "The South could 'win' the war by not losing; the North could win only by winning." A draw was a Confederate victory. [Bevin Alexander; standard strategic framing]
- **Interior lines.** Defending its own homeland, the South could shift forces along shorter internal routes to concentrate against an invader, while the Union had to operate on a vast exterior perimeter. [Confederate strategy summaries; mycivilwar.com]
- **Vast territory & home ground.** The Confederacy covered land comparable in scale to Western Europe; the Union had to invade, conquer, AND garrison ALL of it. Fighting on the defensive, on home soil, with shorter supply lines, magnified Southern strength.
- **The King Cotton bet.** The South assumed cotton was so vital to Britain and France that they'd intervene to break the blockade. (It was a bad bet — Britain had a cotton surplus in 1861 — but in 1861 it shaped Southern confidence.) [mshistorynow.mdah.ms.gov; LINK ACROSS to **Britain, France & Cotton** for the full story.]

**The asymmetric problem, stated.** The Union had to do the HARDER thing — conquer
and hold a continent; the Confederacy had to do the EASIER thing — endure and
outlast. Numbers favored the North; the *nature of the task* partly evened it. That
tension is the chapter's spine.

> **Clarity note for the author:** gloss "interior lines" (shorter routes inside your
> own territory, letting you shift troops faster than an enemy circling the outside)
> and "exterior lines" on first use — they're the load-bearing strategic terms here.

## §2 — Raising Armies from Almost Nothing

**The starting army was tiny.**
- As of **January 1, 1861**, the U.S. Regular Army on paper was **16,403 officers and enlisted men** — and only about **14,658 present for duty**. (Hence the common shorthand "**~16,000**.") [Armies in the American Civil War, Wikipedia; CMH "Regular Army Before the Civil War"]
- Most of it was scattered in tiny frontier posts in the West, not concentrated for a continental war. [CMH]
- The Confederacy had **no regular army at all** — every Confederate unit had to be raised from scratch by the states. [Armies in the American Civil War, Wikipedia]

**Lincoln's first call (April 15, 1861).**
- Three days after Fort Sumter surrendered, Lincoln (North) issued **Proclamation 80**, calling for **75,000 militia** to serve **90 days** (three months — the maximum the Militia Act of 1795 allowed) and convening Congress in special session for **July 4, 1861**. [Proclamation 80, Wikipedia; senate.gov; visitthecapitol.gov]
- Their stated first duty: to "repossess" federal property seized by the seceded states. [Proclamation 80]
- **The 90-day number IS the chapter's irony engine:** the law's three-month cap, plus the universal assumption of a short war, is exactly why the first Union army's enlistments were expiring as it marched to Bull Run (§5). The illusion was baked into the paperwork.

**How both sides actually built armies — the volunteer-regiment system.**
- The basic unit was the **regiment** (~1,000 men at full strength, usually fewer). [Battlefields.org "Regiments of the Civil War"]
- In 1861 a prominent local man would raise a regiment in his own community, and the men often **elected their own company and regimental officers**; states (governors) appointed/commissioned regimental officers. [globalsecurity.org "USV"; Armies in the American Civil War]
- Units often supplied their own uniforms and even weapons; the result was wild inconsistency. [globalsecurity.org]
- Both sides used this system; the Confederacy, with no regular army, relied on it entirely. [Armies in the American Civil War]

**The amateurism of 1861.** These were citizen-soldiers led largely by amateur
officers, drilling for weeks, not trained for the scale of war coming. Both armies
that collided in July 1861 were "very green recruits." [Britannica "First Battle of
Bull Run"; History.com] The competence the war would demand had to be *learned*,
expensively, in the field — that learning curve is the through-line of the whole
pillar.

> **Clarity note:** gloss "regiment," "militia," and "regular army" (full-time
> professional soldiers vs. citizen-volunteers called up for a term) on first use.

## §3 — The First Grand Plan: Scott and the "Anaconda"

**The man.** **General-in-Chief Winfield Scott (North)** — the U.S. Army's senior
soldier, a hero of the War of 1812 and the Mexican–American War, by 1861 elderly
(in his mid-70s) and physically infirm. [Anaconda Plan, Wikipedia] He was the one
professional in the room thinking about the war as a *whole*.

**The plan (May 1861).**
- Scott sketched it in a **letter to Maj. Gen. George B. McClellan (North) on May 3, 1861**, with a fuller outline dated **May 21, 1861**. [Anaconda Plan, Wikipedia]
- Two parts: **(1)** a rigorous **naval blockade of all Confederate ports**, and **(2)** a strong column — Scott proposed roughly **80,000 men** — driving down the **Mississippi River** to cut the Confederacy in two. [Anaconda Plan, Wikipedia]
- The logic: **envelop and strangle** the South economically — "bring them to terms with less bloodshed than by any other plan." It was a strategy of squeezing, not of a single knockout battle. [Anaconda Plan, Wikipedia]

**Why it was derided — and why that matters.**
- Critics mocked it as too **slow and passive**; opponents likened it to "the coils of an **anaconda** suffocating its victim" — and the snake image stuck, giving the plan its popular (originally derisive) name. [Anaconda Plan, Wikipedia]
- The press piled on: the *Chicago Tribune* ran the plan down (e.g. a "Torpid Anaconda" jab, July 15, 1861). [National Civil War Museum; americanheritage.com]
- The public cry was "**On to Richmond!**" — the demand for a quick, decisive march on the Confederate capital, not a years-long squeeze. The mood of 1861 was *action*. [americanheritage.com] **This is the chapter's central 1861 tension:** the one general thinking in *years and systems* vs. a public and press wanting it over in *weeks*.
- Scott, "aged and infirm," **retired before the end of 1861** (Nov. 1, 1861), replaced by McClellan as General-in-Chief. [Anaconda Plan, Wikipedia]

**The payoff to flag (don't overstate for 1861).** Mocked in 1861, the plan's two
pillars — the blockade and seizing the Mississippi — DID become the backbone of
eventual Union victory (the river fully split the Confederacy at **Vicksburg, July
1863**; the blockade tightened for years). The chapter can note Scott was righter
than his critics — strategically, not as a literal adopted "plan." [Anaconda Plan,
Wikipedia; Britannica] *LINK DOWN (forward) to Vicksburg: `/war-civil-war/western/vicksburg`.*

## §4 — The Blockade Proclaimed (the instrument that took years to bite)

- **April 19, 1861** — Lincoln (North) issued **Proclamation 81**, declaring a **blockade of the ports** of the seceded states (South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Florida, Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas); **extended April 27** to add **Virginia and North Carolina**. [Proclamation 81, American Presidency Project; history.state.gov]
- Scale of the task: the Confederate coastline ran roughly **3,500 miles** with dozens of ports and inlets — and the U.S. Navy was far too small to seal it in 1861. The blockade was a *legal declaration* long before it was a physical reality. [history.state.gov "Blockade of Confederate Ports"]
- **Strategic double-edge / the diplomacy wrinkle:** declaring a "blockade" (rather than merely closing your own ports) implicitly granted the Confederacy **belligerent status** under international law — a point that mattered for European recognition. [Proclamation 81 analysis, EBSCO; history.state.gov] *Touch lightly; the full diplomacy story lives in **Britain, France & Cotton**.*
- **Why it belongs in a strategy chapter:** the blockade was the patient, unglamorous half of Scott's plan — a strategic instrument proclaimed in week one of the war that would take **years** to actually choke Southern trade. It's the counter-example to "On to Richmond" — strangulation, not a knockout. [history.state.gov]

> *LINK ACROSS to **Ironclads & the Blockade** (`/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/ironclads`) for the naval combat + technology; this section is the blockade as a 1861 STRATEGIC DECISION only.*

## §5 — The Border States: the Strategic Prize (Kentucky, Maryland, Missouri)

**Why they were decisive (strategy, not sentiment).** The slaveholding states that
did NOT secede — Kentucky, Maryland, Missouri, Delaware — sat between the two sides
and held railroads, rivers, manpower, and the approaches to Washington and the
Ohio/Mississippi line. Losing them could have made the Union's task militarily
impossible. [Border states, Wikipedia; nps.gov "The Border States"]

**Lincoln's own words — the load-bearing quote (VERIFIED, exact).**
- From Lincoln's **letter to Senator Orville H. Browning, September 22, 1861**:
  > "I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game. Kentucky gone, we can not hold Missouri, nor, as I think, Maryland. These all against us, and the job on our hands is too large for us."
  [House Divided / Dickinson — "Letter to Orville Browning (Sept. 22, 1861)"; *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln*, Vol. 4]

**Kentucky's neutrality — the strategic tightrope.**
- Governor Beriah Magoffin (KY) proclaimed Kentucky **neutral on May 20, 1861** — refusing troops to BOTH sides. [Kentucky Declaration of Neutrality, Wikipedia]
- Lincoln (North) deliberately handled Kentucky with great care — declining to force the issue and let either side appear the aggressor.
- The neutrality broke when the **Confederacy moved first**: Maj. Gen. **Leonidas Polk (South)** occupied **Columbus, Kentucky, on September 4, 1861** — a strategic blunder that let the Union respond as the defender. **Brig. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant (North)** seized **Paducah** on September 6, and the legislature ordered only the Confederates out, tilting Kentucky toward the Union. [Kentucky in the American Civil War, Wikipedia]

**Maryland & Missouri (brief, strategic).**
- **Maryland** surrounded Washington, D.C., on three sides; losing it would have isolated the capital. Lincoln moved hard (including suspending habeas corpus along the rail line) to keep it. [Border states, Wikipedia]
- **Missouri** controlled the confluence of the Missouri and Mississippi rivers and St. Louis's arsenal; it was contested by force in 1861 — which is what §6 (Wilson's Creek) is about.

> **Clarity note:** define "border states" (slave states that stayed in the Union) and
> "neutrality" on first use; this is exactly the kind of phrase the newcomer gate flags.

## §6 — The War Opens in the West: Missouri, Wilson's Creek, and Frémont's Misstep

**Wilson's Creek (Aug. 10, 1861) — what it MEANT (do NOT re-tell tactically).**
- The biggest 1861 battle west of the Mississippi; ~5,400 Union troops under **Brig. Gen. Nathaniel Lyon (North)** vs. a combined ~10,000+ Confederate and Missouri State Guard force under **Brig. Gen. Benjamin McCulloch (South)** and **Maj. Gen. Sterling Price (South)**, ~10 miles southwest of Springfield, Missouri. [Battle of Wilson's Creek, Wikipedia; Britannica]
- **Nathaniel Lyon (North) was killed — the first Union general to die in the Civil War.** [Nathaniel Lyon, Wikipedia; npg.si.edu]
- Tactically a Confederate victory (Union ~24% casualties to Confederate ~12%); strategically, the South won the field but Lyon's aggressive 1861 campaign had already done the larger job of **keeping Missouri from joining the Confederacy.** [Battlefields.org; Britannica]
- **Strategic meaning for the chapter:** it proved the war would be fought across a *continent*, not just on the Richmond–Washington line — a second, western front opened in the war's first summer. Often called "the **Bull Run of the West**." *LINK DOWN: `/war-civil-war/trans-mississippi/wilsons-creek`.*

**Frémont's premature emancipation — the politics of slavery as a war measure, TOO EARLY.**
- **Maj. Gen. John C. Frémont (North)**, commanding in Missouri, on **August 30, 1861** declared **martial law** and issued a proclamation **confiscating the property of armed rebels and FREEING their slaves.** [Frémont Emancipation, Wikipedia]
- This was **16 months before** Lincoln's own Emancipation Proclamation — and politically explosive: Lincoln (North) feared it would drive the slaveholding **border states (especially Kentucky)** into the Confederacy. [Frémont Emancipation, Wikipedia]
- Lincoln asked Frémont to modify it; Frémont refused unless ordered. Lincoln then **publicly ordered it rescinded/modified on September 11, 1861**, limiting it to conform to the (narrower) Confiscation Act. [Frémont Emancipation, Wikipedia]
- **Strategic point for the chapter:** in 1861, **military necessity (hold the border states) outranked emancipation** in Lincoln's calculus — emancipation was the right end at the wrong moment. This is the *strategic* face of the war's central issue: even the cause the war was over had to wait on the strategy of keeping Kentucky. [Frémont Emancipation, Wikipedia] *LINK ACROSS (forward) to the **Emancipation** theme: `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/emancipation`.*

> **Framing note (NAME THE CAUSE):** the Frémont episode is a natural place to make
> the cause unmistakable in the chapter's own voice — Lincoln didn't object to the
> *idea* of freeing enslaved people; he objected to the *timing* relative to holding
> the border. The war's object — slavery — is right on the surface here.

## §7 — First Bull Run: the Death of the Short-War Illusion

**Do NOT re-tell the battle tactically — carry only its STRATEGIC meaning.**
- **First Bull Run / First Manassas, July 21, 1861**, near Manassas, Virginia. The first major battle of the war. [First Battle of Bull Run, Wikipedia; Battlefields.org]
- The "**picnic battle**": Washington civilians — including U.S. senators — rode out with picnic baskets and opera glasses to *watch* the Union army win, expecting a short, glorious afternoon. [senate.gov "Senators Witness the First Battle of Bull Run"; History.com]
- It ended in a **Union rout** — green Union troops broke and fled back toward Washington in chaos. [Battlefields.org; Britannica]
- Casualties (CONTESTED in fine detail; standard figures): Union ~**2,896** (≈460 killed, 1,124 wounded, 1,312 captured/missing); Confederate ~**1,982** (≈387 killed, 1,582 wounded, 13 missing); roughly **~850 killed** total — shocking for 1861, trivial by what was coming. [Battlefields.org; Britannica] *Flag the range; cite as approximate.*
- The Union army that fought it was largely the **90-day men** of Lincoln's April call (§2) — enlistments literally running out. [History.com; standard accounts]

**What it MEANT (the chapter's hinge).** Bull Run "changed the nation's understanding
of what the war would entail, dispelling illusions of a short conflict." [History.com;
americanheritagepartners] It was the day the 90-day dream died — for the public, the
press, and Lincoln. The strategic consequence: the North turned to raising **long-term
armies** (Congress had already authorized large volunteer forces; after Bull Run the
commitment hardened) and to the patient, continental war Scott had described and
everyone had mocked. *LINK DOWN: `/war-civil-war/eastern/bull-run`.* (The roster hook
is already: "The first big battle of the war — and the day the dream of a short one died.")

> **Connective payoff:** §3 (Scott mocked for a slow war) + §7 (Bull Run proves the
> slow war was right) is the chapter's single best arc — set them up to pay off
> together.

## §8 — Two Untested Commanders-in-Chief: Lincoln and Davis

**The paradox to lead with.** On paper, the South had the better-prepared war
president — and it didn't matter the way everyone expected.

**Jefferson Davis (South) — the professional.**
- **West Point graduate (Class of 1828, ranked 23rd).** U.S. Army officer **1828–1835**. [Jefferson Davis, Wikipedia]
- **Mexican–American War:** colonel of the **1st Mississippi Rifles** (the "Mississippi rifle" regiment); **wounded in the heel at the Battle of Buena Vista (February 1847)**, where his regiment helped halt a Mexican assault — a genuine combat hero. [Jefferson Davis, Wikipedia]
- **U.S. Secretary of War, 1853–1857** (under Pierce) — ran the Army, championed modernization and a transcontinental railroad. [Jefferson Davis, Wikipedia]
- **U.S. Senator** (Mississippi). Elected **provisional Confederate president Feb. 9, 1861; inaugurated Feb. 18, 1861.** [Jefferson Davis, Wikipedia]
- Telling detail: **Davis had wanted a military command, not the presidency.** [Jefferson Davis, Wikipedia] He tended to involve himself deeply (often too deeply) in military matters and clashed with his generals.

**Abraham Lincoln (North) — the amateur.**
- **Almost no military experience:** a brief, combat-free stint as a captain/private in the Illinois militia in the **Black Hawk War (1832)** — famously joked his only battles were with mosquitoes. [Essential Civil War Curriculum "Lincoln and Davis as Commanders in Chief"]
- A largely self-taught prairie lawyer; **but a fast, analytical learner** — he read works on military strategy, studied his generals' successes and failures, learned from his own mistakes, and applied hard common sense. Historians widely judge that, despite the experience gap, **Lincoln became the more effective commander-in-chief.** [Essential Civil War Curriculum; standard scholarship]

**The command structures (early 1861).**
- **North:** Lincoln (North) over the aging General-in-Chief **Winfield Scott (North)** (→ replaced by **George B. McClellan (North)**, Nov. 1861). Lincoln would cycle through generals for years before finding Grant.
- **South:** Davis (South), the soldier-president, kept tight personal control of strategy and worked through commanders like **P.G.T. Beauregard (South)** and **Joseph E. Johnston (South)** — relationships that were often friction-filled from the start.

**Early mistakes & strengths to flag (1861, not the whole war).**
- Both *under-planned for length* — the 90-day call (Lincoln) and the short-war assumption (both) are the shared 1861 error.
- Lincoln's early *strength* was political-strategic: the border-state restraint (§5) and overruling Frémont (§6) — subordinating military romance to strategic necessity.
- Davis's early *strength* was the coherent **"offensive-defensive"** doctrine — defend the homeland on interior lines, strike north when opportunity offered. [Confederate strategy summaries] His early *weakness* was a tendency to micromanage and to feud with subordinates.

> **Clarity + framing note:** define "commander-in-chief" (the civilian head of the
> armed forces — the president) and "General-in-Chief" (the top *uniformed* officer)
> on first use; readers conflate them. NAME-THE-CAUSE reminder: this section is about
> two men raising armies — be explicit, once, that what they were raising armies *for*
> was, on the Confederate side, the preservation of slavery and, on the Union side,
> the preservation of the Union (which would BECOME a war for emancipation, §6's
> seed). Don't let the "two leaders" framing flatten into both-sides symmetry.

## CONTESTED / [VERIFY] FLAGS

1. **Bull Run casualty figures.** Standard totals are Union ~2,896 / Confederate ~1,982, ~850 killed overall — but exact counts vary by source. Cite as approximate; do not present a single precise total as definitive. [Battlefields.org; Britannica]
2. **Railroad mileage (North ~21,000–22,000 vs. South ~9,000).** Figures vary slightly by source and by what counts as "Confederate" track (border-state lines blur it). The ~71/29% split and the rough magnitudes are solid; treat the exact mile counts as approximate. [Statista; ushistory.org]
3. **Regular Army size "~16,000."** Precise figure: 16,403 on paper / 14,658 present for duty, Jan 1 1861. Use "about 16,000" in prose; the present-for-duty number is the more honest operational figure. [Armies in the American Civil War, Wikipedia]
4. **Scott's "80,000 men" for the Mississippi column.** Wikipedia's Anaconda article gives "perhaps 80,000"; Scott's own letters phrase it as an estimate. Present as Scott's *proposed* figure, not a hard plan. [Anaconda Plan, Wikipedia]
5. **"Anaconda Plan" was never an officially adopted, named plan.** It was Scott's proposal, derisively nicknamed; the Union never formally adopted "the Anaconda Plan" as such, though its two pillars shaped strategy. Author must not overstate it as an executed master-plan. [Anaconda Plan, Wikipedia; Britannica]
6. **Lincoln–Browning quote.** Verified word-for-word against House Divided / *Collected Works* Vol. 4: "I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game. Kentucky gone, we can not hold Missouri, nor, as I think, Maryland." Note the spelling "can not" (two words) in the source; modern renderings sometimes use "cannot." Date Sept. 22, 1861.
7. **Frémont rescission date.** Aug. 30, 1861 (proclamation) → Sept. 11, 1861 (Lincoln's public order to modify). Both dates consistent across sources. [Frémont Emancipation, Wikipedia]
8. **Wilson's Creek troop numbers.** Union ~5,400; Confederate/State Guard "more than 10,000" — figures vary; Lyon's force was clearly badly outnumbered. Cite as approximate. Lyon as "first Union general killed in the war" is firmly established. [Battle of Wilson's Creek, Wikipedia; Britannica]
9. **"~97% of firearms / ~93% pig iron" etc.** From NPS; these are 1860 manufacturing-share figures and are widely repeated, but they are *shares of national output*, not Union-vs-Confederate ratios per se — phrase carefully ("the North made nearly all the nation's…"). [nps.gov]
10. **Population "22 million vs. <9 million."** Solid as round figures; the "nearly 4 million enslaved" within the Southern total is the key qualifier and must travel with it. [ushistory.org; Battlefields.org]
11. **Davis "wanted a military command."** Sourced to Wikipedia's Davis article; widely attested in scholarship. Safe to state, but it's a characterization, not a quoted document.
