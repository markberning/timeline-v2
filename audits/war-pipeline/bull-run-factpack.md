## 2. CASUALTIES

### Standard figures (most commonly cited by ABT, Wikipedia, Britannica)

| Side | Killed | Wounded | Captured/Missing | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Union** | 460 | 1,124 | 1,312 | **2,896** |
| **Confederate** | 387 | 1,582 | 13 | **1,982** |
| **Combined** | 847 | 2,706 | 1,325 | **~4,878** |

Sources: ABT (battlefields.org); Wikipedia infobox; Britannica

### Scholarly range / source variation

- Wikipedia infobox also cites alternate Union figures: 481 killed / 1,011 wounded / 1,216 captured = **2,708 total Union**
- Historynet gives slightly rounded figures: Union ~2,700 (480 killed, 1,000 wounded, 1,200 missing); Confederate ~2,000
- Encyclopedia Virginia gives Confederate as 1,897 (378 killed / 1,489 wounded / 30 missing) — slightly lower
- Combined range across sources: **~4,700–4,878**
- The Wikipedia infobox itself notes variant figures, indicating no single authoritative tally; standard scholarly citation is the ABT/Wikipedia 2,896 / 1,982 split

### Contextual shock
- Both sides expected the battle to be brief and decisive — a Northern public confident of quick victory
- ~847 Americans killed in a single day — more than all U.S. combat deaths in the entire Mexican-American War (1,733 total battle deaths over two years)
- Coming after months of "On to Richmond" cheerleading, the scale stunned Washington

### Notable individual casualties
- **Col. James Cameron** (US, killed) — brother of Secretary of War Simon Cameron (Wikipedia)
- **Brig. Gen. Barnard E. Bee** (CS) — mortally wounded on Henry House Hill, died July 22, 1861 (Wikipedia; ABT)
- **Col. Francis S. Bartow** (CS, killed) — first Confederate brigade commander killed in the war (Wikipedia)
- **Brig. Gen. Edmund Kirby Smith** (CS) — seriously wounded in neck and shoulder shortly after arriving from the Valley; command passed to Col. Arnold Elzey (Wikipedia)
- **Judith Carter Henry** (civilian) — only confirmed civilian fatality of the battle; see Section 4

## 3. WHAT CAME BEFORE

### The "90-day war" illusion
- After Fort Sumter (April 12–13, 1861), Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers with **90-day enlistments** (Wikipedia; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- The 90-day window enshrined an assumption that one sharp battle would end the rebellion
- By mid-July 1861, those enlistments were expiring — two Union regiments literally marched back to Washington *during* the battle rather than re-enlist (Wikipedia)

### "Forward to Richmond" — press pressure
- Starting June 26, 1861, Horace Greeley's *New York Tribune* ran "**FORWARD TO RICHMOND!**" as a daily editorial demand (Essential Civil War Curriculum; eyewitnesstohistory.com)
- Public opinion, Congress, and Lincoln himself pressed for action before the 90-day window closed

### McDowell's reluctance / Lincoln's famous reply
- McDowell argued his army was not yet ready; he needed more time to train (Wikipedia; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- He was overruled. Lincoln (or Scott — see Section 10, Contested Facts) replied with the famous line about both armies being equally green
- McDowell's own force was so raw that soldiers broke ranks during the approach march to pick blackberries and get water (Wikipedia)
- McDowell's plan required synchronized movements that his inexperienced army could not execute (Wikipedia; Britannica)

### Confederate capital at Richmond
- The Confederate government moved its capital from Montgomery, Alabama to **Richmond, Virginia** in May 1861 — putting it roughly 100 miles from Washington and making it the obvious Union strategic target (Wikipedia)

### The two Confederate armies
- **Beauregard** held **Manassas Junction**, a critical railroad hub where the Orange & Alexandria and the Manassas Gap railroads met — guarding the main overland approach to Richmond (Wikipedia; Encyclopedia Virginia)
- **Johnston** held the **Shenandoah Valley** near Winchester (and later Harpers Ferry area), facing Union General Robert Patterson with ~11,000–12,000 men
- Beauregard's position at Manassas Junction was specifically chosen to block the Union advance while exploiting the rail connection to the Valley (Wikipedia; Essential Civil War Curriculum)

### Patterson's failure
- McDowell's plan depended entirely on Patterson keeping Johnston occupied in the Valley
- Patterson was ordered to demonstrate against Johnston, preventing any rail movement east
- Patterson failed to press Johnston aggressively; Johnston screened his departure with J.E.B. Stuart's cavalry, marching his infantry on July 18–19 to **Piedmont Station** (present-day Delaplane, Virginia) on the Manassas Gap Railroad (Wikipedia; Encyclopedia Virginia)
- Patterson reported to Washington that he had succeeded in containing Johnston — while Johnston's troops were already boarding trains (Wikipedia infobox: Patterson "reported to Washington: 'I have succeeded...in keeping General Johnston's force at Winchester'" [VERIFY exact wording — attributed in Wikipedia but primary source document not independently confirmed in this research session])
- Patterson was relieved of command July 27, 1861; his enlistment expired and he was never tried by Congress (Wikipedia)

### First strategic use of railroads in warfare
- Johnston's infantry marched to Piedmont Station; the **Manassas Gap Railroad** carried them ~34 miles east to Manassas Junction — **the first time in history that railroads were used to transport troops to a major battlefield** (Encyclopedia Virginia; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- Troops began arriving July 19; Johnston himself arrived July 20; the last brigade (Kirby Smith/Elzey) arrived by rail on the afternoon of July 21, during the battle itself — its arrival proved decisive (Wikipedia; search results)
- This was noted immediately by military observers on both sides as a new dimension of warfare

### The picnic-goers
- Hundreds of Washington civilians — including U.S. senators, congressmen, and their families — rode out from the capital in carriages with picnic baskets and opera glasses, expecting to watch a quick Union victory (Wikipedia; Senate.gov; WETA Boundary Stones)
- Senators present: Zachariah Chandler (Michigan), Ben Wade (Ohio), Henry Wilson (Massachusetts), James Grimes (Iowa) (Senate.gov)
- Representative Alfred Ely (R-NY) was among the spectators; he was captured (see Section 5)

## 4. THE BATTLE

### McDowell's plan
- **Feint:** Tyler's division would demonstrate against the Confederate right/center at **Stone Bridge** (crossing of the Warrenton Turnpike over Bull Run) and at Blackburn's Ford, fixing Beauregard's attention
- **Main attack:** Hunter's and Heintzelman's divisions would conduct a long flanking march north and west, crossing Bull Run at **Sudley Springs Ford**, then swing south to roll up the Confederate left flank and cut off Manassas Junction (Wikipedia; Britannica; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- The plan was sound in conception but depended on timing McDowell's untrained army could not deliver

### The signal corps and early warning
- Confederate signal officer **Capt. Edward Porter Alexander** used wig-wag (semaphore) flag signaling — the first combat use of this technique — to relay a message when he spotted the Union flanking column's glinting bayonets: "**Look out for your left, your position is turned**" (Wikipedia)
- This alerted Col. Nathan Evans on the Confederate left, who had fewer than 1,100 men at Stone Bridge

### Morning phase: Matthews Hill (approx. 6:00–11:30 a.m.)
- Col. Nathan Evans, realizing Stone Bridge was a feint, repositioned most of his command to **Matthews Hill** north of the Warrenton Turnpike to intercept the Union flanking column (Wikipedia; ABT)
- Evans was reinforced by Brig. Gen. Barnard Bee's and Col. Francis Bartow's brigades
- The Union flanking column (Hunter, Heintzelman) did not reach Sudley Springs until ~9:30 a.m. — a two-hour delay that cost surprise (Wikipedia)
- Fierce fighting on Matthews Hill; Maj. Roberdeau Wheat's Louisiana Tigers launched a spoiling attack; Wheat was seriously wounded (Wikipedia)
- **Col. William T. Sherman** (US), commanding a brigade under Tyler, discovered an unguarded ford upstream from Stone Bridge and crossed around 10:00 a.m., striking the Confederate right flank on Matthews Hill (Wikipedia)
- Confederate line on Matthews Hill collapsed after ~11:30 a.m. and retreated south to Henry House Hill

### Midday phase: The rally on Henry House Hill (approx. 11:30 a.m.–4:00 p.m.)
- **Brig. Gen. Thomas J. Jackson** (CS) had arrived near noon with his Virginia brigade and positioned five regiments on the **reverse slope** of Henry House Hill — protecting them from direct artillery fire while establishing 13 guns on the crest (Wikipedia)
- Wade Hampton's Legion and J.E.B. Stuart's cavalry provided flank support (Wikipedia; ABT)
- As the Confederate forces fell back from Matthews Hill, Brig. Gen. Barnard Bee reportedly pointed to Jackson and uttered what became the most famous line of the battle (see Section 8, Quotes)
- Bee was mortally wounded shortly after speaking and died the next day, July 22 — he never clarified his meaning (Wikipedia; TeachingHistory.org)

### The artillery duel and capture of Union guns
- Union artillery captains **James B. Ricketts** and **Charles Griffin** advanced their batteries (11 guns total) onto Henry House Hill
- A Confederate battalion in blue uniforms (the 33rd Virginia Infantry) was mistaken for Union troops; Griffin held his fire; the Confederates overran and captured both batteries around 3:00 p.m. (Wikipedia)
- The guns changed hands multiple times in brutal close fighting

### Judith Carter Henry — the battle's only civilian fatality
- **Judith Carter Henry**, approximately 85 years old, was a bedridden widow living in the farmhouse directly on Henry House Hill (Wikipedia; Smithsonian; NPS)
- Her family attempted to carry her to safety but she begged to be returned home
- Union artillery, targeting Confederate sharpshooters believed to be using the house, opened fire; a shell tore through the bedroom wall, severing one of her feet and inflicting multiple wounds; she died later that day (Wikipedia; Smithsonian)
- She is considered the first known civilian fatality of the American Civil War (Smithsonian Magazine)

### The arrival of Johnston's last reinforcements — the decisive moment
- Brig. Gen. **Edmund Kirby Smith**'s brigade (the last of Johnston's Army of the Shenandoah to arrive from the Valley) reached the battlefield by rail and entered the fight on the Confederate left around 3:30–4:00 p.m. (Wikipedia; search results)
- Kirby Smith was almost immediately wounded (seriously, in neck and shoulder); command passed to **Col. Arnold Elzey**, who led the brigade in a charge that crushed Union Col. Oliver O. Howard's brigade on Chinn Ridge (Wikipedia)
- Simultaneously, Col. Jubal Early's brigade struck Howard's flank
- This combined assault broke the Union right and triggered the general collapse

### The rebel yell
- During the Confederate counterattack on Henry House Hill and the subsequent charge, Confederate troops raised a distinctive high-pitched battle cry that became known as the **rebel yell** — associated with Jackson's order to "yell like furies" (Encyclopedia Virginia; Wikipedia; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- First appearance in major combat is attributed to this battle, July 21, 1861 (Encyclopedia Virginia; multiple sources)
- [NOTE: Jackson's specific "yell like furies" order is sourced to an 1865 London *Times* article and an 1866 Jackson biography; it does not appear in Jackson's own official reports — see Section 10, Contested Facts]

## 5. THE ROUT

### Collapse of Union lines
- Union troops began retreating around 4:00 p.m. (Wikipedia; ABT)
- An artillery shell overturned a Union wagon on the **Cub Run Creek bridge**, blocking the main road and triggering panic (Wikipedia; ABT; WETA)

### The Great Skedaddle
- The orderly retreat became a rout — soldiers discarding weapons and equipment and streaming back toward Washington (Wikipedia; ABT; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- Named by the Southern press "**The Great Skedaddle**" (Wikipedia; multiple sources)
- McDowell ordered Col. Dixon S. Miles' division to act as rear guard, but it proved impossible to rally the army short of Washington (Wikipedia; ABT)
- The roads were also clogged with the fleeing civilian spectators who had come to watch — their carriages tangled with army wagons, heightening the panic (Wikipedia; Senate.gov; WETA)

### The senators
- Senator **Zachariah Chandler** (Michigan) reportedly tried to block the road and stop the retreat (Senate.gov)
- Senator **Ben Wade** (Ohio) picked up a discarded rifle and threatened to shoot any soldier who ran (Senate.gov)
- Senator **Henry Wilson** (Massachusetts) had his buggy destroyed by a Confederate shell; escaped on a stray mule (Senate.gov)
- Senator **James Grimes** (Iowa) narrowly avoided capture and vowed never to return to a battlefield (Senate.gov)

### Alfred Ely captured
- **Rep. Alfred Ely** (Republican, Rochester, NY) had come to the battlefield to check on the 13th New York Volunteers, which he had helped recruit
- As Confederate forces advanced, Ely was captured and brought before Confederate officers; he identified himself as "the Honorable Alfred Ely, Representative in Congress from New York" (Emerging Civil War; Local History Rocs)
- He was taken to Richmond and held in **Libby Prison** alongside captured Union officers (Emerging Civil War)
- Released in a prisoner exchange on Christmas Day 1861 for former U.S. Minister to France Charles James Faulkner (Emerging Civil War)
- Only member of Congress to reach Richmond that day — as a prisoner of war (Senate.gov)

### Confederate failure to pursue
- Despite Jefferson Davis arriving on the battlefield and urging pursuit, Beauregard and Johnston did not effectively follow up their victory (Wikipedia)
- Beauregard's attempt to use Bonham's and Longstreet's brigades to intercept the retreat failed; Bonham encountered artillery fire from McDowell's rear guard and found the road to Centreville blocked (Wikipedia)
- The Confederate army was nearly as disorganized by its victory as the Union army was by its defeat — the shared exhaustion of green troops (Wikipedia; multiple sources)

## 7. WHAT IT MEANT

### Death of the short-war illusion
- The "90-day war" narrative was destroyed in a single afternoon (Wikipedia; ABT; Britannica; multiple)
- The day after the battle, Lincoln signed legislation authorizing **500,000 three-year volunteers** — a signal the war would be long (Wikipedia)
- Congress accepted 11,000 Pennsylvania Reserves for federal service on July 25 — a measure previously resisted (Wikipedia)

### Union reorganization
- McDowell was relieved July 25, replaced by **Major General George B. McClellan**, who had won minor victories in western Virginia (Wikipedia; ABT; Britannica)
- McClellan was tasked with building and training what would become the **Army of the Potomac** — a process that took months and kept the war from another major battle in the East until the Peninsula Campaign of 1862
- The **Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War** was established by Congress to investigate military affairs — partly a response to Bull Run (Wikipedia)

### Confederate overconfidence
- The easy victory bred a dangerous complacency in the South; some historians argue this contributed to the Confederacy's eventual defeat by creating false expectations (Wikipedia; multiple)
- Beauregard and Johnston did not pursue — partly exhaustion, partly disorganization, possibly a missed strategic opportunity (Wikipedia)

### Jackson's legend born
- "Stonewall" Jackson became the South's first authentic hero of the war — the legend of the man who held when others fled was born at Henry House Hill on July 21, 1861 (Wikipedia; ABT; multiple)

### The railroad revolution
- The decisive use of the Manassas Gap Railroad to move Johnston's army established that railroads were not merely logistical infrastructure but **operational weapons** — the first time in history a major battle was decided in part by rail mobility (Encyclopedia Virginia; Essential Civil War Curriculum)
- This lesson was absorbed by both sides and shaped Civil War strategy for the next four years

### Northern shock
- George Templeton Strong, Union diarist: "**Today will be known as BLACK MONDAY. We are utterly and disgracefully routed, beaten, whipped by secessionists**" (Wikipedia)
- The defeat sobered Northern public opinion; the unrealistic optimism of spring 1861 was extinguished

## 8. NOTABLE QUOTES

### "There stands Jackson like a stone wall"
**Attributed to Brig. Gen. Barnard Bee (CS), Henry House Hill, July 21, 1861**

Most commonly cited version: *"There is Jackson standing like a stone wall! Rally behind the Virginians!"*

Alternative versions recorded: *"There stands Jackson like a stone wall"* / *"There is Jackson standing like a damned stone wall"*

- **Sources:** Multiple period accounts; widely cited in Civil War literature; TeachingHistory.org; Wikipedia; ABT
- **Dispute:** The meaning has never been definitively established. Bee was mortally wounded moments after speaking and died July 22 without clarifying his intent.
  - **Praise interpretation:** Bee was rallying his own broken brigade by pointing to Jackson's steadfast Virginians as an example — "look how Jackson holds, rally behind him" (TeachingHistory.org; Wikipedia)
  - **Rebuke interpretation:** Maj. Chase Whiting (CS) reported that Jackson had refused to advance to support Bee's troops under assault; Bee was expressing furious frustration — "Jackson stands there like a stone wall doing nothing while we're being slaughtered" (Wikipedia; TeachingHistory.org; HistoryNet "Barnard Bee" article)
- **Verdict:** Permanently contested. Bee's death forecloses certainty. Both interpretations have scholarly proponents.

---

### "You are green, it is true, but they are green also"
**Popular attribution: President Abraham Lincoln to Gen. McDowell, before the battle**

Full popular version: *"You are green, it is true, but they are green also; you are all green alike."*

- **Source / attribution dispute:** Widely attributed to Lincoln in popular Civil War literature. However, historian John J. Hennessey (*The First Battle of Manassas: An End to Innocence*) attributes the quote to **General Winfield Scott**, not Lincoln. The Encyclopedia of Virginia also credits Scott. McDowell's own account refers to it as "the answer" in a context suggesting a conversation with Scott. (Search results — multiple sources)
- **Verdict:** [CONTESTED] The quote almost certainly occurred — the sentiment is consistent with what both Lincoln and Scott said at the time — but the attribution to Lincoln rather than Scott appears to be a widespread popular misattribution. Cite as "attributed to Lincoln (or Winfield Scott — attribution disputed)."

## 9. GEOGRAPHY FOR A MAP

### Compass orientation
North up. The battlefield lies in northern Virginia. Washington, D.C. is to the northeast (~25–30 miles). The Shenandoah Valley (Johnston's origin point) is to the west/northwest.

### Key features

| Feature | Role in battle | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bull Run** | The stream/creek that gives the Union name to the battle; Confederate defensive line along its south bank | Flows roughly northwest-to-southeast through the area; Union had to cross it to attack |
| **Manassas Junction** | Confederate logistical hub; the strategic prize | Intersection of the Orange & Alexandria Railroad and the Manassas Gap Railroad; ~5 miles south of the main battlefield |
| **Warrenton Turnpike** | Main east-west road across the battlefield | Ran from Centreville (Union base) west across Stone Bridge and past Henry House Hill; the axis of Union advance and the retreat route |
| **Stone Bridge** | The Warrenton Turnpike crossing of Bull Run | Confederate held; Tyler's division feinted here while the main Union force flanked north |
| **Sudley Springs Ford (Sudley Ford)** | The Union flanking column's crossing point | ~3 miles north of Stone Bridge; Union forces crossed here starting ~9:30 a.m.; farther north than ideal, adding delay |
| **Matthews Hill** | Morning fight; Confederate delaying position | North of the Warrenton Turnpike; Evans, Bee, and Bartow slowed the Union advance here until ~11:30 a.m. |
| **Henry House Hill** | The decisive ground; afternoon fighting | South of the Warrenton Turnpike; elevated plateau where Jackson made his stand; the Henry House sat on its crest |
| **Chinn Ridge** | Final Union right flank collapse | West/northwest of Henry House Hill; where Elzey's and Early's brigades struck Howard's brigade ~4:00 p.m., triggering the rout |
| **Blackburn's Ford** | A secondary Bull Run crossing to the south | Tyler demonstrated here on July 18 (the "skirmish at Blackburn's Ford" — an early probe); also where Wilmer McLean's farm was located |
| **Cub Run Creek bridge** | Site of the Union collapse | On the road from the battlefield back to Centreville; artillery fire overturned a wagon here, blocking the retreat and turning withdrawal into rout |
| **Piedmont Station** | Johnston's railroad embarkation point | In the Shenandoah Valley; where the Manassas Gap Railroad met Johnston's march route (~34 miles by rail from Manassas Junction) |

### Flanking march direction
McDowell's flanking force (Hunter + Heintzelman) marched from Centreville northwest along a back road, then northeast to Sudley Springs Ford — a wide arc north of the Confederate left. After crossing, they swung south and southeast, hitting Matthews Hill from the north/northwest. The Confederate left (originally facing north along Bull Run) was thus rolled from their western/northern flank toward the Warrenton Turnpike.

## 10. CONTESTED / UNCERTAIN FACTS

### "Stone wall" — praise or rebuke?
- Permanently unresolved; see Section 8. Both interpretations are defensible. Bee is dead; no other firsthand account definitively resolves it.
- Author note: the praise interpretation is the popular/dominant one; the rebuke argument is the scholarly minority position but has respectable historians behind it (Whiting's account).

### "You are green" — Lincoln or Winfield Scott?
- Popular attribution is Lincoln; scholarly sources (Hennessey; Encyclopedia of Virginia) attribute it to Winfield Scott
- McDowell's own account is ambiguous; context suggests Scott is the more likely speaker
- [CONTESTED — author should attribute as "Lincoln or Scott (attribution disputed)" or follow Hennessey and assign to Scott]

### Casualty figures
- No single authoritative tally; scholarly range is Union 2,708–2,896 / Confederate 1,897–1,982 / Combined ~4,600–4,878
- The ABT figure (Union 2,896 / CS 1,982) is the most commonly cited standard reference
- Source of variation: different accounting of "captured" vs. "missing"; whether wounded who later died are counted as killed

### Rebel yell origin
- The attribution of the rebel yell's origin to this specific battle, and to Jackson's specific "yell like furies" order, is traditional but not confirmed in Jackson's own reports; the first documented use in combat is generally accepted as Bull Run but the specific order text is from post-war secondary sources [VERIFY]

### Patterson's "I have succeeded" telegram
- Quoted in the Wikipedia article as Patterson reporting "I have succeeded...in keeping General Johnston's force at Winchester"; this specific wording was not independently confirmed against a primary source in this research session [VERIFY against Patterson's papers / OR: *A Narrative of the Campaign in the Valley of the Shenandoah* (1865)]

### Were there other civilian casualties?
- Judith Henry is described as the "first known" or "only confirmed" civilian fatality; the Smithsonian Magazine article uses "first known" rather than definitively "only." [VERIFY — the NPS Manassas NBP is the authoritative source for battlefield civilian casualties]

### Johnston's rank / seniority dispute
- After the battle, Johnston believed his seniority (as former U.S. Army general) entitled him to the top Confederate rank, but Jefferson Davis ranked him fourth among the Confederacy's full generals — a dispute that created lasting friction between Johnston and Davis.
