# FRAMING/FAIRNESS CRITIQUE — "The Peace and Its Fuse"
French and Indian War, Phase 5

---

## FIRST-CHECK RESULT

### 1. Native nations as first-class actors?
**PASS — with one MUST-FIX gap.**
Neolin and Pontiac are handled well: Neolin gets his own section, his vision is described as "a coherent vision of renewal and of taking back a homeland, a spiritual program with a political edge" — not primitive religion. Pontiac (Odawa) is tagged correctly, his agency is foregrounded, and the draft explicitly corrects the "supreme mastermind conspiracy" older framing. The three-part structural cause (Amherst's contempt, Neolin's vision, loss of the French counterweight) is present.

**The gap:** The long dispossession — what the British victory *accelerated* — is named in the final "The Fuse" section, but only in one paragraph at the end. The framing reads: "The deepest cost fell on the Native nations, and it was not a grievance but a catastrophe." This is good language. But the casualty block in "Bushy Run and the Stalemate" buries Native losses: "at least 200 Native warriors died, with the toll on Native communities certainly higher and largely unrecorded." The phrase "largely unrecorded" is accurate and honest — but the draft gives no signal that the civilian toll on Native communities during the siege waves (Fort Sandusky, Michilimackinac, etc.) was real and substantial, only the warrior count. The asymmetry (450 colonial civilians named; Native civilian toll absent) is mild but visible.

### 2. Fort Pitt smallpox blankets — documented intent, contested effect?
**PASS — this is the draft's strongest section.**
The handling is exemplary. Trent's journal entry (June 24 — *before* Amherst's letter) is quoted verbatim and its chronology is correctly noted ("done at Fort Pitt without orders from above, and it actually came before the more famous correspondence"). Amherst's July letter and Bouquet's July 13 reply are both quoted; Amherst's July 16 "extirpate this execrable race" is quoted in full without softening. The effect is then correctly handled: "Some have concluded the attempt spread smallpox... others have argued it was a total failure." The closing formulation — "The intent is documented. The effect is not." — is exactly right. No minimization, no sensationalism.

**No fix needed here.**

### 3. Human cost named throughout?
**LARGELY PASS — one SHOULD-FIX.**
The human cost is present across the chapter (frontier violence, forts burned, garrisons killed, 450 colonial civilians, 4,000 settlers displaced). The Proclamation Line's betrayal is named. The "catastrophe" language for Native nations at the close is apt. However, the casualty block gives Native warrior deaths as "at least 200" and stops. The draft does not mention that Pontiac's War — even setting aside the blanket episode — coincided with smallpox spreading "through the region on its own," and that Native communities bore epidemic losses that dwarfed battle casualties. The dispossession is named as a long-term consequence but not the immediate human cost of the disease environment the war created.

## FINDINGS

### FAIR
- **Neolin's section ("The Master of Life"):** Fully respects his agency, theology, and political coherence. Not romanticized, not reduced to provocation.
- **Pontiac (Odawa) framing:** Correctly rejects the "mastermind conspiracy" older narrative. His role as a "powerful regional leader whose early successes inspired others" is the current scholarly consensus and is cleanly stated.
- **Amherst's contempt:** Named directly and structurally linked to the war's cause. "He regarded the Indigenous nations with open contempt" is plain and accurate.
- **Proclamation Line double-edge:** The draft correctly calls it "the first British acknowledgment that the Native nations held rights to their own land" and then immediately notes it was "ignored by colonists from the day it was signed and would be erased entirely by the revolution to come." That's honest.
- **"Rebellion/uprising" language:** The draft uses "uprising" twice and "rebellion" zero times in scare quotes — and explicitly corrects the framing: "This was not a riot. It was a coordinated military offensive." That pre-empts the sanitization. The peace terms section adds: "Historians have called it hardly a surrender at all." Both are good.
- **Choiseul's calculation:** Handled with appropriate source caution — "a calculation historians attribute to him rather than his exact words." Correct.

### TILTED — SHOULD-FIX

**1. Native civilian toll invisible in the casualty block.**
The "Bushy Run and the Stalemate" section lists: 400 British soldiers, 50 captured/tortured, 450 colonial civilians, 4,000 settlers displaced — then "at least 200 Native warriors died, with the toll on Native communities certainly higher and largely unrecorded." The parenthetical acknowledgment is honest but the structural asymmetry is real: colonial civilian deaths are counted; Native civilian deaths get a phrase. The smallpox epidemic (spreading "on its own" as the draft notes) would have killed far more than the 200 warriors. A sentence acknowledging that the epidemic toll in Native villages during and after the war is unknown but was almost certainly the largest single human cost of the whole conflict would correct the imbalance without overclaiming.

**2. The "meanwhile" box tilts colonial.**
The closing "--- meanwhile ---" block is framed entirely around the Road to Revolution. That's the box's designated role, but it means the chapter's very last word is the colonial grievance thread. The Native catastrophe section ("The Fuse," second-to-last paragraph) is structurally strong but the box tips the endpoint back toward British/colonial framing. Not a MUST-FIX — the main text handles it — but the box could note the Native thread briefly (one clause) to avoid the final impression being purely a colonial-grievance hand-off.

### TILTED — MUST-FIX

**1. "The largest Native uprising the British had yet faced" — phrasing risk.**
The lead sentence of "Pontiac's War" reads: "The crisis that hardened the Proclamation Line into law was the largest Native uprising the British had yet faced." The word "uprising" here is used without quotation marks or explicit correction, and "the British had yet faced" frames it from the British vantage point. The draft corrects this framing later ("This was not a riot. It was a coordinated military offensive."), but the section-opening sentence lets the colonial framing in before it pushes back. Suggest: open the section from the Native nations' vantage — what *they* faced and why they moved — rather than from what the British encountered. The correction comes too late in the paragraph to fully neutralize the opener.

**2. Dispossession named but not *timed*.**
The closing "The Fuse" paragraph says the British victory "accelerated the long dispossession that the next war, and the new nation that won it, would carry to its end." That's the right claim — but it's abstract. The reader has seen the Proclamation Line's betrayal described, but there is no sentence that connects the dots: *after 1763, the pace of settler encroachment on Native land west of the Appalachians accelerated even before the Revolution, because the Proclamation was never enforced.* Washington's private correspondence (quoted in the draft) shows this — he was arranging to claim land the Proclamation was supposed to protect. The draft quotes that but doesn't draw the conclusion explicitly: the Proclamation was unenforced *from day one*, which means the "recognition of Native rights" it supposedly represented was functionally null immediately. The dispossession wasn't deferred to the Revolution; it began the moment the ink dried. One sentence would close this gap.
