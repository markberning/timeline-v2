## MUST-ADD

### 1. The Congressional response to Bull Run — July 22–25, 1861 legislation

**What's missing.** The draft says, twice, that Bull Run caused the North to turn to
"long-term armies" rather than 90-day militias (§ raising-armies and § bull-run-
illusion). But the *concrete mechanism* — what Congress actually did, and when — is
never stated. The reader learns the lesson was learned but not what learning it looked
like in practice.

**Why it matters.** The July 22–25, 1861 Acts are the precise institutional payoff of
Bull Run's lesson. Congress, one day after the battle, authorized the president to
accept up to 500,000 volunteers on three-year (or war-duration) enlistments — turning
the army from a 90-day experiment into a genuine long-war force. This is the chapter's
own thesis (both sides built for a short war; 1861 proved it would be long) completing
its loop. Without it, the strategic consequence of Bull Run stays abstract.

**Fact to add (sourced):** On July 22 and July 25, 1861 — the day after Bull Run and
four days later — Congress passed successive acts authorizing the President to raise up
to 500,000 volunteers on three-year (or war-duration) enlistments, replacing the 90-day
militia framework. (American Presidency Project / GlobalSecurity.org; Union army,
Wikipedia.)

**Placement:** One sentence at the end of § bull-run-illusion, after "turned to raising
long-term armies." No new section needed — it's the mechanical close of the 90-day arc
already threaded through the chapter.

### 2. Fort Sumter's role in flipping the upper South

**What's missing.** The draft opens with Fort Sumter's surrender (April 14, 1861) as
the war's starting gun and a link to the dossier, but never states the single most
consequential strategic fact about it: the bombardment and Lincoln's subsequent call for
75,000 troops forced Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Arkansas to choose sides
— and all four joined the Confederacy. That instantly doubled the Confederacy's
industrial and manpower base beyond the seven original Deep South states, and it put
the new Confederate capital (Richmond) 100 miles from Washington.

**Why it matters.** The chapter's entire strategic asymmetry section rests on the
Confederate vs. Union resource gap — yet the gap was not fixed at secession. The
Confederacy that fought Bull Run was a larger, more viable entity than the one that
fired on Sumter, precisely because of what Sumter triggered. The upper-South
secessions also explain why the Richmond–Washington corridor became the war's dominant
eastern theater: without Virginia's secession you have no Richmond to march "On to."
This is a strategic fact, not a cause/secession story (which correctly lives in OTBF).

**Fact to add (sourced):** Lincoln's April 15 call for 75,000 troops — the direct
response to Sumter — prompted Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Arkansas to
secede and join the Confederacy by June 1861, bringing the Confederate state count from
7 to 11 and adding the industrial and agricultural weight of the upper South. (Battle
of Fort Sumter, Wikipedia; American Battlefield Trust; Smithsonian.)

**Placement:** A sentence or two in § raising-armies or § two-republics, where the
resource asymmetry is established. It reframes the 71/29 split as the post-Sumter
reality, not the pre-war baseline.

## CONSIDER

### A. McClellan's western Virginia campaign — the Union's one 1861 success and what it cost

**What's present.** Wilson's Creek is well covered; the western front as continental
fact is made. McClellan appears only as the man who received Scott's May 3 Anaconda
letter and later replaced him as General-in-Chief.

**Why it's worth a sentence.** Before Bull Run, McClellan ran the Union's one unambiguous
1861 military success: the western Virginia campaign (Rich Mountain, July 11, 1861),
which secured the B&O Railroad corridor, held the mountain counties for the Union, and
set the stage for West Virginia's eventual statehood. Crucially, it made McClellan a
newspaper hero — the North's answer to Confederate victories — and is the direct reason
Lincoln turned to him after Bull Run. The chapter already names McClellan twice
(receiving Scott's letter; replacing Scott as General-in-Chief) without explaining why
he was the man chosen. That gap slightly undercuts the two-commanders section.

**Assessment.** Not a blocking omission — the chapter doesn't promise a McClellan
biography — but one sentence connecting "western Virginia" to "why Lincoln picked him"
would close a small hole in the logic of the command narrative.

### B. The Trent Affair (November 8, 1861)

**What's missing.** On November 8, 1861, USS San Jacinto stopped the British mail
packet RMS Trent and seized Confederate envoys James Mason and John Slidell bound for
London and Paris. Britain mobilized troops to Canada; war with Britain was a genuine
near-term possibility. Lincoln backed down and released the men in December ("One war
at a time"). The affair is fully resolved within 1861.

**Why it's strategically relevant.** The chapter already touches the King Cotton
diplomatic bet and the blockade's belligerent-status wrinkle, both of which link across
to the Britain/France & Cotton OTBF theme. The Trent Affair is the 1861 moment when
the diplomatic risk of the blockade was most acute — a Union captain's action nearly
dragged Britain into the war on the Confederate side in the war's first year. It is
also a clean illustration of Lincoln's "strategic restraint" theme, which the chapter
develops through the Kentucky chess game.

**Assessment.** This one sits at the scope boundary. The chapter's own scope note says
the full diplomatic story lives in the Britain/France & Cotton OTBF theme (already
linked). If that theme covers the Trent Affair adequately, a link-across is enough and
no text is needed here. Check what the OTBF theme covers; if the Trent Affair is there,
leave a two-word cross-reference at most. If it isn't, one sentence here (the diplomatic
near-miss of 1861, resolved by Lincoln's restraint, full story linked) earns its place.

### C. The cordon defense vs. offensive-defensive tension within Confederate strategy

**What's present.** The chapter names Davis's "offensive-defensive" doctrine in
§ two-commanders as his early strategic strength. This is correct and well-handled.

**What's missing.** The chapter does not note that in 1861 Davis *couldn't* fully
implement the offensive-defensive because political pressure from governors and the
Confederate public forced a dispersed "cordon defense" — spreading small armies around
the entire Confederate perimeter to guard every state. This is why the Confederacy was
vulnerable to Union breakthroughs along its flanks in early 1862, and it's the gap
between Davis's stated doctrine and his actual 1861 dispositions.

**Assessment.** A genuinely enriching strategic nuance, but not a blocking gap. The
chapter is about 1861 improvisation, not a full strategic treatise. One sentence
(something like "the political reality of 1861 forced Davis toward a dispersed cordon
rather than the concentrated offensive-defensive he preferred") would sharpen the
Davis paragraph. Flag as SHOULD-CONSIDER for the revision pass.
