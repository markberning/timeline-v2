✅ CONFIRMED — Wikipedia, American History Central.

**Montgomery, Alabama as Confederate capital [VERIFY item 3]:**
✅ CONFIRMED — Montgomery, Alabama was the first Confederate capital (Feb 4 – May 29, 1861). Multiple sources confirm this explicitly. The state name is accurate.

**Robert Toombs lone dissenter; "hornet's nest" warning; from memoirs/diaries not a transcript [VERIFY item 4 — Mason-Dixon gloss; see also Toombs quote]:**
The draft renders Toombs's warning as: "it would strike a hornet's nest, rouse the quiet North into a swarm, and lose the Confederacy every friend it had above the Mason-Dixon line."

The sourced versions of the Toombs quote (Wikipedia, American History Central) read: "It will lose us every friend at the North. You will only strike a hornet's nest. Legions now quiet will swarm out and sting us to death. It is unnecessary. It puts us in the wrong. It is fatal."

The draft's paraphrase captures the substance accurately. The "Mason-Dixon line" gloss: the Mason-Dixon Line is the historically established cultural boundary between the antebellum North and South (Maryland/Pennsylvania line, colloquially extended), in common use in Civil War contexts. Using it here as a synonym for "the North" is standard and not misleading — Toombs was warning about losing Northern friends, and the Mason-Dixon line is precisely the conventional shorthand for that boundary. It does not need cutting.

✅ CONFIRMED (substance) / ✅ ACCEPTABLE (Mason-Dixon gloss — historically standard usage, not a fabrication)

The draft correctly notes the Toombs quote comes from memoirs/diaries, not a verbatim transcript. That caveat is in the text. No correction needed.

✅ CONFIRMED — Wikipedia, NPS: Leroy P. Walker telegraphed on April 10.

✅ CONFIRMED — Wikipedia, NPS, ABT.

✅ CONFIRMED — ABT primary sources, Wikipedia: exact quote, well-attested.

**Anderson's parting words: "If we never meet in this world again, God grant that we may meet in the next":**
✅ CONFIRMED — Wikipedia, Confederate aide accounts.

**Roger Pryor: former Virginia congressman, urged a blow, offered first shot, declined — "I could not fire the first gun of the war," rowed away before bombardment:**
✅ CONFIRMED — Wikipedia (Roger Atkinson Pryor), NPS (George S. James page), multiple sources.

**Confederacy "nine weeks old" [VERIFY item 8]:**
⚠️ MINOR IMPRECISION — needs acknowledgment. The draft says "A government that had existed for nine weeks decided to start the bloodiest war in American history."

The CSA provisional government convened Feb 4, 1861; the provisional constitution was adopted Feb 8. The decision to fire was April 9–10. From Feb 8 to April 9 = 60 days = ~8.5 weeks. From Feb 4 to April 9 = 64 days = ~9.1 weeks.

"Nine weeks" is close but slightly fast if counting from Feb 8 (the constitution's adoption). From Feb 4 (first meeting) it rounds to about 9 weeks. The draft's fact ledger notes this is its own arithmetic. "About nine weeks" or "barely two months" would be safer. "Nine weeks" as stated is defensible (rounding from the Feb 4 convention date) but on the generous end if dating from Feb 8. This is a minor issue — the overall "very young government" characterization is accurate and unambiguous.

Verdict: ⚠️ MINOR — recommend changing to "barely two months" or "about nine weeks" with the note that it depends on which founding date you use; or simply verify Feb 4 → April 9 = ~9 weeks (which it does).

## Additional Claim — Not Self-Flagged, Found in Independent Check

**The draft says Beauregard fired at 4:15 a.m. (via Roger Pryor section):**
The fact pack says Roger Pryor was at Fort Johnson when he declined and "the bombardment began at ~4:15 a.m." per the factpack's Roger Pryor entry. The draft's bombardment section correctly gives 4:30 a.m. as the time of the signal mortar (consistent with Wikipedia and all primary sources). The 4:15 figure appears only in the factpack's Pryor section and may reflect when Pryor was offered the shot, not when firing began. The draft uses 4:30 consistently in the bombardment narrative — this is correct.
✅ No error in draft.

**The draft describes the floating battery as "sheathed in railroad iron and boiler plate":**
The factpack says "two layers of railroad iron + four layers of boiler iron." The draft's description ("railroad iron and boiler plate") is accurate in substance.
✅ CONFIRMED.

**The draft says the Confederacy "nine weeks old" in the "Meanwhile in Montgomery" sidebar, but "barely two months old" earlier in the same section:**
The draft says "The Confederacy was barely two months old" (the-decision section) and "A government that had existed for nine weeks" (the Montgomery sidebar). These are used in different places but refer to the same gap of ~8.5–9 weeks. They are not contradictory (two months ≈ eight weeks), but "barely two months old" and "nine weeks" give slightly different impressions of the same interval. This is a minor internal consistency note, not a factual error.
⚠️ MINOR — recommend making them consistent ("barely two months" or "about nine weeks" in both places).
