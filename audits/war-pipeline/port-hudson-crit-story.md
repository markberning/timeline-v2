# Port Hudson — STORYTELLING critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

> Judging the draft **as a story** (the #1 goal). Accuracy/zero-hallucination is a hard
> floor handled by the fact-checker gate — flagged here only where a storytelling choice
> rests on a wobbly fact. Voice/shape benchmark: shipped Shiloh `section-narrative.tsx`.
> Grades: STRONG / GOOD / NEEDS WORK / REWRITE. Selective coverage is GOOD, not a defect.

---

## OVERALL: GOOD (ship-ready after a focused revise pass)

This is a strong, genuinely moving draft with a clear spine and a real emotional center.
The Native Guard / Cailloux material is the best writing in the file and earns the whole
battle a reason to exist. The strategic frame ("the last lock") is clean and the structure
is well-chosen: river-run → doomed charges → starving siege → meaning. Selective coverage
is exactly right — it does not drown in play-by-play, and it disciplines the Vicksburg
cross-ref instead of re-telling it.

What holds it back from STRONG overall is texture, not architecture. Against the Shiloh
benchmark the draft is **noticeably thinner on the human, sensory, ground-level detail
that makes Shiloh land** — Shiloh gives you Grant under a tree with a cigar, a boot quietly
filling with blood, men shot among their cook-fires. Port Hudson tells us the siege was
visceral more than it lets us feel it, and the two assault sections lean abstract ("the
ground taught him," "the works had won") where Shiloh would put a man's face on it. The
Cailloux beat is the exception and proves the rest of the draft can reach that bar.

Two things must improve before ship (below). The rest is should-improve polish.

## §1 `the-last-lock` — The River's Last Lock — **GOOD**

**Hook & stakes:** Strong open. *"the Confederacy held exactly one stretch of the
Mississippi River, and it was holding it with two clenched fists"* is a real house-voice
hook — concrete, visual, immediately stakes the whole campaign. The "last lock" / "cork in
the bottom of the bottle" metaphor is consistent and earns its keep. The Red River
"artery… sawn off" logic is explained mechanism-first, exactly the Shiloh standard ("Take
Corinth and you cut that east-west artery in two").

**Human dimension:** Thin but acceptable for a scene-setter. Gardner gets one good
human hook — *"a New York native by birth who had married into a Louisiana family and
thrown in with the Confederacy"* — which is the kind of complicating detail Shiloh loves
(Sherman "whispered about as having lost his nerve"). Good.

**Play-by-play tedium:** None — this is orientation, well-paced.

**Voice:** On target. *"a position built to kill anyone who came at it head-on. Which is
exactly what the Union army was about to do."* is a clean Shiloh-style button (cf. "He was
about to be very nearly proven wrong.").

**Pacing/dual POV:** The "Meanwhile, in Mississippi" Vicksburg pairing is the right call
and well-disciplined. One soft spot: *"their fort's fate had already been chained to his"*
slightly over-telegraphs the ending — fine, but it's the second foreshadow button in the
section (after "which is exactly what the Union army was about to do"), so it reads a touch
pat. **SHOULD-IMPROVE.**

**Fact flag (storytelling-adjacent):** the "~13 heavy guns" and the 6,800–7,500 garrison
spread are both `[VERIFY]` — fine to keep vague, the prose already hedges "sources
disagree." No story problem.

## §2 `the-night-run` — Farragut Runs the Guns — **GOOD**

**Hook & stakes:** Good framing — *"the navy tried to handle it the navy's way — by simply
steaming past."* Clear why it matters (cut the Red River traffic) and clear why it sets up
the land siege.

**Human dimension / visceral:** This is where the section underperforms its own material.
*"about three hours of close-range slaughter on the water"* is the right phrase but the
draft then **summarizes the slaughter instead of showing one second of it.** The single
most cinematic image available — the frigate *Mississippi* aground under the guns, set
ablaze, burning all night until the fire reaches her magazine — is delivered as a clause in
a list: *"was set ablaze to keep her out of Confederate hands, and burned through the night
until her fire reached her magazine."* Shiloh would slow down here. The magazine going up is
a *detonation lighting the river* — that's a sentence on its own, and the section is poorer
for not landing it. **SHOULD-IMPROVE** (high-value): give the *Mississippi*'s end one beat
of its own; let the bonfires on the bank and the ship's fire on the water be felt.

**Play-by-play tedium:** The seven-vessel inventory in ¶2 is the one place the draft risks
ship-list tedium. It's justified (the lashing-together detail pays off) but could trim a
name or two without loss.

**Voice:** Good. *"a knife held against the South's western artery"* lands.

**Pacing/dual POV:** The "Meanwhile, upstream" coda is solid and does real work — turns a
6-of-7 failure into a proof-of-concept that "pointed straight at the land battle of May."
Good connective tissue.

## §3 `the-doomed-charges` — The Charges Into the Ravines — **STRONG** (the Native Guard / Cailloux beat) / **GOOD** (the framing assaults)

This is the heart of the piece and the reason the battle is worth a chapter. Graded high.

**Hook & stakes:** Good. *"He did not understand the ground. Five days into the siege he
ordered the first attack, and the ground taught him."* is a clean, ominous button.

**Human dimension — the Native Guard charge:** **STRONG, and the best writing in the
draft.** This is where the file reaches the Shiloh bar and exceeds it emotionally.

- *"it was on that day, on the Confederate right, that the larger meaning of the war walked
  onto the field."* — earns its weight.
- The charge itself is *felt*: *"They crossed it, were torn apart, fell back, reformed, and
  went forward again — repeatedly… They did not break and run. They went in, and went in
  again, into fire that could not be survived in the open."* The asyndeton ("torn apart,
  fell back, reformed, and went forward again") does the work; you feel the repetition of
  bodies into fire.
- Cailloux: the death is rendered with exactly the restraint Shiloh uses for Johnston's
  bleeding-out. *"A shot shattered his left arm, and he kept going, waving his men forward
  with the sword in his right hand, until an artillery shell struck him and killed him."*
  Clean, no melodrama, devastating.
- The 47-days-on-the-field beat is the gut-punch of the whole article and it is perfectly
  placed: *"a captain of the United States, killed leading a charge, left to lie on the
  ground he died taking, because of the color of his skin."* This is the line the section
  is built around and it lands.

One caution: the explicit "here is the thing to understand about why these men were
fighting… it is the whole point of the war" paragraph (¶ after the regiments are named) is
**doing necessary cause-naming work** (and satisfies the standing slavery-naming
requirement — good, mandatory, keep it). But it is the one place the section *tells* the
meaning rather than letting the 47-days image carry it. It's not wrong — the directive
requires slavery be named in the section's own prose — but it could be tightened so the
essay-voice doesn't briefly interrupt the scene's momentum. **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** trim by
~20%, lean harder on the concrete (these men charging the works of the people who had owned
them) and less on the abstract restatement.

**Human dimension — the framing assaults (May 27 / June 14):** **GOOD but abstract.** Here
the section is weakest relative to Shiloh. The casualties are delivered as ledger lines —
*"Banks had lost on the order of 1,995 men — roughly 293 killed, 1,545 wounded, 157
missing"* — and the verdicts are summary abstractions: *"the ground taught him," "The works
had won," "the same story written in more blood."* These are good *sentences* but they
describe slaughter from the map table. Shiloh never lets a bloodbath stay abstract — it
gives you the boot filling with blood, the cook-fires. The June 14 section *almost* fixes
this with the Fitts paraphrase — *"the ditch at the foot of the parapet filled with the
living and the dead, the survivors clawing at the face of the earthwork within a few yards
of the muzzles"* — which is the **single most visceral non-Cailloux image in the draft and
proves the section can do it.** **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** push one ground-level human image into
the May 27 general-assault prose the way the Fitts line does for June 14, so the doomed
charges feel doomed and not merely tallied.

**Play-by-play tedium:** Well-controlled. The four brigade-commander names (Weitzel,
Grover, Augur, Sherman) in ¶2 are a list, but they pay off the "supposed to be
coordinated… came apart instantly" point, so it's earned. No tedium.

**Voice:** Strong throughout.

**Pacing/dual POV:** Excellent. The "Meanwhile, in the North" coda — the charge traveling
north as news and changing minds, opening the floodgates to ~200,000 Black enlistments — is
the perfect payoff and ties the failed tactical charge to the won strategic war. *"The
charge that failed to take a single yard of earthwork helped win the larger war."* is a
top-tier button.

**Fact flags (story rests on them):** the Cailloux death details, the 47-days, the "one of
the first" framing, and both opinion-shift quotes are all `[VERIFY]` paraphrases. The
*story* is leaning hard on these — if the fact-checker can't confirm the 47-days or the
shattered-arm/sword/shell sequence, the emotional spine bends. Flagged for the fact gate,
not a story defect in itself.

## §4 `the-starving-siege` — Forty Days in the Wilderness of Death — **GOOD**

**Hook & stakes:** Good pivot — *"Banks stopped trying to take Port Hudson with men's
bodies and started taking it with engineering and time. This was the part the manuals were
written for."*

**Human dimension / visceral starvation:** **GOOD but under-delivers the brief's "visceral"
bar.** The descent is there and the escalation is well-staged: *"They ate the beef first.
Then, when the beef was gone, they ate the mules. Then the dogs and the rats."* The
escalation rhythm works. But it stays at the inventory level — *what they ate* — and never
drops to *one man's* hunger, one sensory detail of the heat and the trenches. The brief
specifically asks that "the starving siege [be] visceral," and right now it's vivid-by-list
rather than felt-in-the-body. Compare Shiloh's wounded "lying out in the dark between the
lines" in the thunderstorm — Shiloh embodies; this catalogs. The "beer from sugar and
molasses" is a great period detail and should be kept; the scurvy/dysentery/malaria line is
correctly grim but delivered clinically. **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** land one embodied image — a
single soldier, the smell of the trenches in the Louisiana July, the diary voice in close-up
— so the reader's stomach turns rather than just registering the menu.

**Play-by-play tedium:** None. The siege engineering (saps, three mines) is summarized at
exactly the right altitude.

**Voice:** Strong. *"a weapon as deadly as any cannon," "The Mississippi summer did not care
which uniform a man wore."*

**Pacing/dual POV:** Very good. The Vicksburg-falls reveal is staged well — *"What finally
ended it came not from the trenches but from upstream"* — and the *"Gardner was guarding a
door whose wall had been torn down"* image is excellent and pays back the "last lock"
through-line. The "Meanwhile, upstream and east" coda (the July 1863 turning-point pairing,
Port Hudson as "the quiet coda… the last lock clicking open after the key had already
turned") is genuinely good. *"They simply hadn't known it until the news came down the
river."* is a fine closer.

## §5 `the-meaning` — The River Whole, and a Question Answered — **GOOD**

**Hook & stakes:** Good — the two-things structure (split the Confederacy / answered the
question) is clear and the river-whole payoff lands.

**Human dimension:** The Cailloux funeral material is the right way to close — it carries
the human thread past the surrender. *"a vast procession through the city for a man who had
been born enslaved and died a captain of the United States, leading free men against the
system that had owned him."* and the Father Maistre defiance are strong, and the "second
line" forerunner note is a lovely resonant detail. Good.

**Play-by-play tedium:** None.

**Voice:** Good, though this section does the most *telling* of its own meaning of any in
the draft — which is appropriate for a closing "what it meant" section, but it does restate
the Native Guard / 200,000 point that §3's coda already made. There's **mild redundancy**
between §3's "Meanwhile, in the North" coda and §5's second half: both make the "would Black
men fight → answered → ~200,000 enlistments" argument. **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** differentiate
them — let §3's coda own the *immediate news/opinion shift* and §5 own the *long aftermath
(the funeral, the martyr, the legacy)* — so the reader doesn't hear the same thesis twice.

**Pacing:** The section has both a body, an explicit **"Outcome: Union victory"** block, and
a *"Meanwhile, across the war"* coda — three closings in a row. That's a lot of landing gear.
The Outcome block and the final coda say overlapping things (both rehearse "five days after
Vicksburg… because Vicksburg fell… ~200,000 Black men"). For a section graded on pacing this
is the one real drag — the ending repeats itself. **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** tighten so there's one
clean close, not three. (If the Outcome block is a required structural field, trim the prose
coda's overlap with it.)

**Dual POV:** Note — this whole battle is structurally single-POV-light. We get the Union
attackers and the strategic frame richly, and Gardner's garrison through the starvation, but
we rarely sit *inside* the Confederate defenders' experience the way Shiloh inhabits both
Grant's and Johnston's/Beauregard's heads. That's a defensible choice (the Native Guard
makes the Union side the moral center), and "selective is GOOD," so this is not a defect —
but if a single Confederate-defender human beat were added anywhere, §3 or §4 is where it
would most enrich the dual POV.

## MUST-IMPROVE (ship-blocking on storytelling grounds)

1. **Put a face on the framing assaults (§3).** The May 27 / June 14 slaughter is currently
   delivered as casualty ledgers and map-table verdicts ("the ground taught him," "the works
   had won"). The June 14 Fitts paraphrase proves the section *can* embody it. Push at least
   one ground-level human image into the May 27 general-assault prose so the "doomed charges"
   read as doomed men, not totals. Right now the only fully-embodied combat in the whole
   article is the Native Guard's — every other charge is seen from the map.

2. **Make the starvation visceral (§4) per the brief.** Land one embodied, sensory image —
   a single soldier, the heat and stench of the July trenches, the diary voice in close-up —
   instead of (only) the menu of mule/dog/rat. The brief explicitly requires the starving
   siege be *visceral*; it is currently vivid-by-catalog and stops at the body's edge.

## SHOULD-IMPROVE (polish; not ship-blocking)

3. **§2:** Give the USS *Mississippi*'s burning-and-magazine end one beat of its own — it's
   the most cinematic image available and it's buried in a clause.
4. **§3:** Trim the explicit "why these men were fighting" essay paragraph ~20% (keep the
   mandatory slavery-naming) so it doesn't pause the scene's momentum; lean concrete.
5. **§5 + §3 coda:** De-duplicate the "would Black men fight → ~200,000" thesis between §3's
   coda and §5 — give §3 the immediate news/opinion shift, §5 the long aftermath.
6. **§5 ending:** Three stacked closings (body + Outcome block + "Meanwhile, across the war")
   overlap heavily; tighten to one clean close.
7. **§1:** One fewer foreshadow button ("chained to his" vs. "which is exactly what the
   Union army was about to do" — keep one).
