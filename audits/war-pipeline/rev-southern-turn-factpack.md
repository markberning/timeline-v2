## 1. GREENE TAKES THE SOUTH (Dec 1780)

- **The wreckage he inherited.** After Camden (16 Aug 1780) destroyed Gates's army and Charleston's fall (May 1780) had already swallowed one whole American army, the Southern Department was a third disaster from collapse. Washington's own choice for the command — **Maj. Gen. Nathanael Greene**, the Rhode Island Quaker-raised ex-ironmaster — got it in October 1780 (Congress had picked the previous three southern commanders itself; this time it let Washington choose). (HIGH)
- **2–3 December 1780, Charlotte, North Carolina:** Greene arrived 2 December and formally took command from Gates on 3 December. What he found: a force on paper of ~2,300–2,400, fewer than ~1,500–1,600 fit and equipped for duty, hungry and half-naked. (HIGH on dates; strength figures MED — use "around 2,300 on paper, barely 1,500 fit to fight" as a softened range.)
- **The quartermaster as strategist.** Greene had spent 1778–1780 as the Continental Army's **Quartermaster General** — the unglamorous job of wagons, depots, forage, and river transport. He ran the southern war like a supply officer: before fighting anyone he sent engineers (Kosciuszko among them) to survey the **rivers** — the Dan, the Yadkin, the Catawba — and ordered **boats built and staged**. The campaign that followed was won as much by ferries and flatboats as by muskets. (HIGH on the QM background and river surveys; the framing is the chapter's thesis.)
- **Dividing a weaker army — the textbook sin.** In mid-December Greene split his inferior force in the face of a superior enemy: **Brig. Gen. Daniel Morgan** (the Virginia rifleman, veteran of Quebec and Saratoga) was detached (orders 16 Dec 1780) with ~600 Continentals plus militia to operate west of the Catawba, while Greene took the rest to a "camp of repose" at **Cheraw**, South Carolina. The logic: a divided army could eat (forage two districts instead of one), could threaten the British posts from two directions, and forced **Cornwallis** to divide too — because if Cornwallis massed against either wing, the other was loose behind him. (HIGH — the 16 Dec orders to Morgan are published; the rationale is Greene's own.)
- **Cornwallis's answering split:** he sent **Lt. Col. Banastre Tarleton** (the feared 26-year-old cavalry commander of the British Legion, "Bloody Ban" to the backcountry after Waxhaws) with ~1,100 men to destroy Morgan, while Cornwallis himself moved north to cut Morgan off. That set up Cowpens. (HIGH)

## 2. COWPENS (17 Jan 1781) — Morgan's masterpiece, what-happened scale

- **The ground:** the Cow Pens, a backcountry grazing ground in upcountry South Carolina near the Broad River — open woods, gentle rises, the river miles at Morgan's back. Morgan chose to stand with a river behind him, partly (by his own later account) so his militia could not melt away. (HIGH on the ground; the militia-can't-run rationale is Morgan's own later telling — MED, attribute.)
- **Numbers as RANGES:** Morgan ~**800–1,900** total depending on how much arriving militia is counted (older accounts ~1,000; modern research, esp. Babits, pushes higher); the core was ~300–400 Continentals under **Lt. Col. John Eager Howard** plus Virginia/Carolina/Georgia militia under **Andrew Pickens** and ~80–100 dragoons under **Lt. Col. William Washington** (the commander-in-chief's second cousin). Tarleton ~**1,050–1,150**. NEVER print a single hard number for Morgan's force. (HIGH on the dispute itself; figures as ranges.)
- **The mechanism — the two-volley militia line.** Morgan built the battle around what militia actually could and couldn't do. Three lines: (1) skirmishers/sharpshooters out front in the trees to bleed Tarleton's dragoons; (2) **Pickens's militia line, asked for exactly two volleys** — "two fires" aimed low (officers and sergeants the preferred marks, by tradition) — and then *permitted in advance* to retire around the left to reform behind the third line; (3) Howard's Continentals on the main rise, with Washington's cavalry hidden in reserve. The genius: a militia retreat that always before meant rout was here **scripted**, so when the British saw the second line "break" and surged forward in disorder, they were running uphill into fresh regulars. (HIGH — NPS Cowpens account; the "look for the epaulets" wording is traditional, don't quote it as an order.)
- **The pivot:** Howard's right was ordered to refuse the flank, the order was misheard, and the whole Continental line began marching to the rear in good order — Morgan rode in, picked a new line, and on command the Continentals **faced about and fired a point-blank volley** into the pursuing British, then went in with the bayonet; Washington's cavalry burst onto the British right/rear and Pickens's reformed militia came back around the other flank: a **double envelopment**, the war's cleanest. Tarleton's Legion cavalry refused his order to charge again and fled; Tarleton himself cut his way out after a brief mounted clash with William Washington. Under an hour, by most accounts. (HIGH on the sequence; the Tarleton–Washington personal clash is well attested but details are dramatized in tellings — keep to a clause.)
- **Tarleton's losses as RANGES:** ~**100–110 killed, ~200+ wounded, ~500–600 captured** — in total roughly **80–90% of his force destroyed or taken** (NPS: 110 dead, over 200 wounded, 500 captured). Morgan's losses: commonly given as low as **12 killed, 60 wounded** (NPS) and as high as ~25/124 (other tallies) — give as "a few dozen." Also taken: both British field guns, the colors of the 7th Regiment, Tarleton's baggage. (HIGH on the magnitude; exact figures MED — ranges mandatory.)
- **What it meant:** in 40-odd minutes Cornwallis lost his light troops — the fast quarter of his army — which is what made everything after (the burned baggage, the failed pursuit) necessary. Morgan, his health broken (sciatica/rheumatism), left the army weeks later; he'd fought his last battle. (HIGH)

## 3. THE RACE TO THE DAN & GUILFORD COURTHOUSE (Jan–Mar 1781)

### Cornwallis burns the baggage
- Cornwallis, reinforced by Leslie to ~2,500–3,000, set out to catch Morgan and retake the Cowpens prisoners. At **Ramsour's Mill** (late January 1781) he made the campaign's most desperate call: he **burned his own baggage train** — tents, wagons, provisions, the rum casks staved in, officers' baggage included, keeping only ammunition, salt, hospital stores, and a few wagons — to turn his whole army into light infantry. A British army in the American interior, stripped of its supply line by its own commander's order. (HIGH — multiple sources incl. NC DNCR markers and JAR; exact date given variously ~25–28 Jan, use "late January.")
- Morgan got over the **Catawba** fords hours-to-days ahead of the pursuit (the river then rose behind him); Greene rode cross-country with a tiny escort to join Morgan and steered both wings northeast. The two wings reunited at **Guilford Courthouse**, then ran for the **Dan River** on the Virginia line. (HIGH)

### The race to the Dan (early Feb)
- ~10–15 miles a day in freezing rain, mud-frozen roads, rivers rising; an American rear guard of picked light troops (under **Otho Holland Williams**, with Lee's Legion) screened the army, sometimes marching within musket shot of the British van. (HIGH; keep weather/misery to attested generalities.)
- The trick that won it: Cornwallis believed the lower Dan ferries couldn't carry an army and raced for the **upper fords**; but Greene's staged **boats** (the quartermaster's campaign — collected on his December orders) were waiting at the lower crossings, **Irwin's Ferry and Boyd's Ferry**. On **14 February 1781** the last Americans — Williams's rear guard — crossed into Virginia and the boats went with them. Cornwallis reached the bank to find a river he could not cross and a country he could not feed an army in. (HIGH — date and ferries well attested.)
- Cornwallis fell back to Hillsborough, raised the royal standard, and called on loyalists to rise; the response was thin (the loyalist beat belongs to §5/civil-war pill — Pyle's defeat, 25 Feb, where Lee's Legion shattered a body of mounted loyalists, killing ~90+, sits here at ONE clause if used, owned by the civil-war chapter). Greene, refitted and reinforced with Virginia and Carolina militia, **recrossed the Dan on 22–23 February** to keep the loyalists down and shadow Cornwallis. (HIGH on the recrossing; Pyle figures MED.)

### Guilford Courthouse (15 Mar 1781) — the Pyrrhic win
- Greene, now ~**4,400–4,500** (the majority militia, many days from home), offered battle on ground he chose at Guilford Courthouse, NC; Cornwallis attacked with ~**1,900–2,100** veterans. Greene copied Morgan's three-line scheme onto bigger, wooded ground: NC militia at a fence line (asked, again, for two volleys), Virginia militia in the woods behind, Continentals on the hill by the courthouse. The lines were too far apart to support each other — a real flaw, say it plainly. (HIGH; strengths as ranges.)
- The fighting ran ~90 minutes through three positions, ending in close melee near the Continental line — the Guards and the 1st Maryland mixed hand-to-hand, William Washington's cavalry slamming in. (HIGH)
- **The grapeshot moment — EVIDENCE LEVEL, not legend level.** The famous story: Cornwallis ordered Lt. McLeod's guns to fire **grapeshot into the melee**, hitting his own Guards along with the Americans, to break the crisis. Source: **Henry "Light-Horse Harry" Lee's memoirs, written ~30 years later**, repeated by most later writers. The modern battlefield study (Babits & Howard, *Long, Obstinate, and Bloody*, 2009 — built on ~1,000 pension files) argues the guns' actual target was **Washington's charging cavalry**, and that few if any Guards were hit — i.e., the deliberate fire-into-his-own-men version is likely embellishment. WRITE IT AS: the guns fired into the chaos at the crisis, the field cleared, and the story that Cornwallis knowingly shredded his own Guards comes from one American memoir decades on — modern research doubts it. (DISPUTED — handle exactly this way.)
- Greene, true to doctrine, **broke off and retreated in order**, abandoning his guns; Cornwallis held the field. (HIGH)
- **The bill:** British **~532 casualties — 93 killed, over 400 wounded, ~26 missing — roughly a quarter (25–27%) of the army engaged**, including a crippling cut of officers and Guards. American losses ~79 killed, ~185 wounded — plus a thousand-odd militia "missing," i.e., gone home, which is what militia did and not a rout statistic; explain, don't inflate. (HIGH — figures consistent across NPS/Wikipedia/Babits.)
- **The Fox line — VERIFY-THE-CHAIN status.** When news reached London (early June 1781), **Charles James Fox** — the Whig opposition leader — is reported to have told the Commons: **"Another such victory would ruin the British Army!"** (consciously echoing Plutarch on Pyrrhus). The attribution is standard in the scholarship (it titles the NPS-historian battle study, Baker's *Another Such Victory*), BUT the exact wording rests on the era's reconstructed parliamentary reporting, not a verbatim transcript. USE AS: "Fox is reported to have said," never as a verified verbatim quote. (MED — substance solid, wording reported.)
- **What Cornwallis did with his victory:** with hundreds of wounded, no supplies (he'd burned them), and no loyalist rising, he marched 200-odd miles AWAY from the war he'd been fighting — to the coast at **Wilmington** to refit — and there made the decision that built this chapter's second half: rather than return to South Carolina, he marched north into **Virginia** (April–May 1781), on his own theory that the southern colonies couldn't be held until Virginia was subdued. **Clinton in New York was not asked.** Clinton later wrote that had Cornwallis hinted at the plan, "I should certainly have endeavoured to stop you." (HIGH on the movements and the Clinton line's substance; the quoted phrase is from Clinton's May 1781 letter as reproduced in standard accounts — MED verbatim, safe paraphrased.)

## 4. THE TWO-FRONT SPRING & SUMMER (Apr–Sept 1781)

### Front one — Greene reclaims the interior (one beat each; loses battles, wins the map)
- **Greene's pivot south.** After Guilford, instead of chasing Cornwallis to the coast, Greene marched back INTO South Carolina — at the chain of British posts (Camden, Ninety Six, Augusta, the forts on the rivers) — while the partisans (Marion, Sumter, Pickens, Lee's Legion) took the small posts one by one. (HIGH)
- **Hobkirk's Hill, 25 April 1781** (a mile and a half from Camden): **Lord Rawdon** (the 26-year-old British commander in South Carolina) surprised Greene's camp; the American line broke at the wrong moment and Greene lost the field again. **Camden was evacuated by Rawdon anyway on 10 May** — the post was untenable with the countryside gone. (HIGH)
- **Ninety Six, 22 May–19 June 1781**: Greene besieged the loyalist-garrisoned star fort; Kosciuszko ran the approaches; a final assault on **18 June** failed and Greene withdrew when Rawdon marched to relieve it. **The British then abandoned Ninety Six anyway** (early July) — relieved it, and left. (HIGH)
- **Eutaw Springs, 8 September 1781**: Greene attacked Lt. Col. **Alexander Stewart** northwest of Charleston; the Americans swept the British camp, the attack dissolved in the plunder of it (the camp-looting collapse is standard in the accounts — say it plainly, it's human), a British counterattack held the field — proportionally among **the bloodiest battles of the whole war** (each side lost roughly a quarter of its force; ~500–700 casualties per side in the standard tallies — RANGES). The British then fell back to Charleston for good. (HIGH on date/shape/significance; casualty figures MED — ranges.)
- **The pattern, stated once:** Hobkirk's Hill lost — Camden evacuated. Ninety Six not taken — Ninety Six abandoned. Eutaw Springs lost on the field — the field's owner retreats to the coast. By September the British in the lower South hold **Charleston, Savannah, and the ground their cannon can see**, and nothing else. Greene never won a battle in the campaign; he won the campaign. (HIGH — this is the chapter's Greene thesis, and it's true.)

### Front two — Cornwallis into Virginia (Lafayette's shadow campaign)
- **Virginia was already burning.** In **January 1781** a British raiding force under **Benedict Arnold** — three months a British brigadier after his treason — sailed up the James and **burned much of Richmond (5–6 January)**, warehouses, mills, and stores, with **Gov. Thomas Jefferson** watching the smoke from across the river, the militia response a couple hundred men, many unarmed. ONE BEAT; Arnold's turn itself belongs to earlier chapters. (HIGH — Encyclopedia Virginia, Richmond accounts.)
- **Lafayette's shadow campaign.** Washington sent the **Marquis de Lafayette** south with ~1,200 New England/New Jersey light infantry (spring 1781) to deal with Arnold; when Cornwallis arrived from Wilmington (**20 May 1781**, joining the British forces in Virginia — now ~7,000+ men), the 23-year-old Lafayette commanded the only American field force in the state, perhaps 3,000–4,000 with militia. He did the Greene thing: refused battle, hovered, retreated, kept his small army alive while Cornwallis marched the length of central Virginia. One sharp scrape at **Green Spring (6 July)** — Anthony Wayne's advance walked into Cornwallis's ambush near Jamestown and cut its way out — ONE clause if used. (HIGH on the campaign shape; Lafayette's strength MED — ranges. The famous Cornwallis sneer "the boy cannot escape me" could NOT be verified to any primary source — treat as apocryphal, omit or badge hard.)
- **The Charlottesville raid — one beat.** Cornwallis sent **Tarleton** to grab Virginia's government, which had fled to Charlottesville: on the night of **3–4 June 1781** a militia captain named **Jack Jouett**, who spotted the column on the road, rode ~40 miles through the back trails to warn them; Tarleton's dragoons reached **Monticello minutes behind Jefferson's departure** (a neighbor's second warning had him off the mountain just in time). Seven legislators were caught; the government — and Jefferson, his governorship expiring that same week — escaped over the Blue Ridge. (HIGH — Monticello's account; "minutes" per the Hudson warning, phrase as "minutes, by the family's account.")
- **Cornwallis digs in at the water's edge.** Under Clinton's (contradictory, slow-arriving) orders to take a naval station on the Chesapeake, Cornwallis settled his ~7,000–9,000 men at **Yorktown and Gloucester Point**, on the York River, in **August 1781** — a deep-water anchorage, perfectly safe so long as the Royal Navy held the sea. (HIGH; strength as range — ~8,000 at the siege is the common figure.)

## 5. THE PULSE — THE SAME SUMMER, EVERYWHERE AT ONCE (the heart of the chapter)

*This is the McPherson section: tell it braided, week against week. Each strand below is ONE beat unless marked; the off-the-battlefield chapters own the arcs.*

- **January: the army nearly quits.** On **New Year's Day 1781** the **Pennsylvania Line** — ~1,500–2,400 of the Continental Army's hardest veterans — **mutinied at Morristown**, killed/wounded officers in the scuffle, elected a Board of Sergeants, and marched on Philadelphia over years of no pay, no clothes, and disputed enlistments. They were NOT defecting: emissaries Clinton sent to recruit them **were handed over and hanged as spies**. Settled by negotiation (about half the Line discharged); when the **New Jersey Line** tried the same on 20 January, Washington had it surrounded and **two ringleaders shot by firing squads drawn from their fellow mutineers**. The war's sixth winter: the side that would win the year almost dissolved in its first week. ONE-TWO beats; `army-from-nothing` owns the arc. (HIGH)
- **Spring: the ask of France.** The same months, the cause's finances and supply ran on French coin and French arms; Rochambeau's ~5,000+ French regulars had sat at Newport since mid-1780 waiting for naval superiority to make them usable. `french-alliance` owns this arc. (HIGH, terse)
- **May 21–22, Wethersfield, Connecticut:** Washington and **Rochambeau** met to plan the summer. **Washington wanted New York** — the great prize, Clinton's base — and the conference minutes say so; Rochambeau deferred on paper and then wrote to **de Grasse** in the West Indies making clear the Chesapeake might serve better (his letter flagged Virginia's distress and the winds). The honest version: the Yorktown campaign began as a plan Washington didn't want. (HIGH — Founders conference memorandum 23 May 1781; Rochambeau's 28 May letter per the standard accounts.)
- **August 5, Cap-Français (Saint-Domingue):** Adm. **the comte de Grasse** made the decision that armed everything — he answered that he was coming to the **Chesapeake**, not New York, with his **entire fleet (28 ships of the line)**, ~3,000–3,300 French troops under Saint-Simon borrowed from the islands, and money raised in **Havana** to pay for the campaign — and he **sailed that day**, taking the back route through the Bahama Channel to stay unseen. He set his own deadline: he had to be back in the Caribbean by mid-October. (HIGH on the fleet/troops/route/sailing date — his decision letter was written 28 July and the fleet weighed 5 August; the Havana silver detail MED, commonly told as ~500,000 pesos raised in a day — soften to "money raised in Havana.")
- **August 14, Washington's headquarters:** the news arrives via Newport. Washington's diary, same day: *"Matters having now come to a crisis and a decisive plan to be determined on — I was obliged … to give up all idea of attacking New York; & instead thereof to remove the French Troops & a detachment from the American Army to the Head of Elk to be transported to Virginia."* The pivot took him about a day. (HIGH — verbatim at founders.archives.gov, Diaries, 14 Aug 1781.)
- **The march and the lie (late Aug–early Sept).** ~2,000–2,500 Continentals + ~4,000–5,000 French swung south from the Hudson — while Washington ran a deception to pin **Clinton** in New York: fake staging camps in New Jersey, boats collected as if for a Staten Island assault, **a field of brick bread ovens built at Chatham, NJ** (a French bakery actually baking — the proof of a long stay), and **fictitious dispatches written to be captured**. Clinton did not conclude the army was gone south until about **2 September** — the day it paraded through Philadelphia. Washington, years later, admitted the whole kit: *"much trouble was taken and finesse used to misguide and bewilder Sir Henry Clinton … by fictitious communications, as well as by making a deceptive provision of Ovens, Forage and Boats in his Neighborhood."* (HIGH — JAR "The French Bread Connection"; the quote is Washington to Noah Webster, 31 July 1788 — re-verify verbatim at founders before printing in full.)
- **September 5, the Chesapeake** — the hinge, and the `war-at-sea` chapter OWNS the battle; the spine carries only this: de Grasse's 24 ships came out to meet **Graves's 19**; two hours of broadsides off the Virginia Capes, tactically messy, nobody sunk that day — and then de Grasse kept the British fleet at sea for days while **Barras slipped in from Newport with the siege artillery**, and Graves, his ships battered (one, HMS *Terrible*, scuttled), **turned back to New York**. The bay was shut. Cornwallis's safe anchorage became a trap with water on three sides. (HIGH on shape/consequence; keep all naval detail to this beat — pill to war-at-sea.)
- **War-weariness, both capitals, the same season.** In London the war was six years old, the debt swollen, and the coalition now included France, Spain, and the Dutch Republic — Britain fighting in the Channel, the Caribbean, Gibraltar, and India at once, with **no ally in Europe**; in June 1781 **Fox's motion against the American war was beaten in the Commons** but the opposition was growing in the country ("almost every man is now against the American war," one member put it that summer); on **12 December 1781** — Yorktown's news not yet landed — a motion to end the war already drew **179 votes against 220**. The British public's war was dying before Cornwallis surrendered; Yorktown killed it. (HIGH on the strategic isolation and the 12 Dec division numbers — JAR; the June motion MED on its exact terms/date; the "almost every man" line MED — attribute loosely or paraphrase.)
- **And underneath it all, the southern civil war.** The same twelve months in the Carolina backcountry were the war's ugliest: after **Kings Mountain (Oct 1780)** the cycle of reprisal hangings, plundered farms, and neighbor-on-neighbor killing ran through 1781 — Pyle's loyalists cut down by Lee's Legion in February, loyalist raiders burning out rebel settlements in the fall, prisoners executed on both sides. ONE BEAT at this altitude, in the civil-war theme's framing: this is the war Greene's map-winning was slowly strangling, and `civil-war-among-themselves` owns it. (HIGH as a one-beat characterization; NO specific atrocity claims beyond Pyle without the theme chapter's vetted facts.)
- **The pulse, stated once (the chapter's thesis paragraph):** For Yorktown to happen, ALL of it had to happen — a French admiral choosing the Chesapeake with his whole fleet and his own deadline; an American army that had mutinied in January still marching in September; a British command divided between a general in Virginia and a general in New York who'd stopped trusting each other; a Parliament losing its stomach; and a southern interior the British had already effectively lost to Greene and the partisans. Remove any strand and 1781 is just another year. (Directive — this is the McPherson showcase paragraph; keep it factual, no destiny register.)

## 6. YORKTOWN (28 Sept – 19 Oct 1781) — the siege, what-happened scale

- **The investment.** The allied army marched from Williamsburg on **28 September** and closed around Yorktown: ~**8,800 Americans (Continentals + Virginia militia) and ~7,800 French regulars** against Cornwallis's ~**8,000** (ranges vary; with de Grasse's fleet and sailors the French share of the total effort was the larger — keep the proportions honest per the `french-alliance` framing: the siege artillery was French, the fleet was French, the engineers running a textbook European siege were French). On the night of **29–30 September Cornwallis abandoned his outer works** — pulling into the town's inner line on the strength of Clinton's letter promising relief from New York — and the allies walked into the abandoned positions. (HIGH on dates/shape; strengths as the standard ranges above.)
- **The parallels — siegecraft as clockwork.** Night of **6 October**: the **first parallel** (a trench line ~600–800 yards out, two miles long) dug in one rainy night, French engineers directing. **9 October**: the batteries opened — the French at about 3 p.m., the American battery at about 5 p.m., **Washington by tradition firing (or touching off) the first American gun** — and from then on the bombardment was continuous; French hot shot burned **HMS Charon** in the river that night. **11 October**: the **second parallel** begun, ~300 yards out — but it couldn't be completed to the river while two British strongpoints held the left: **Redoubts 9 and 10**. (HIGH — NPS siege chronology; the Washington-first-gun detail is NPS-carried but badge "by tradition.")
- **The night of October 14 — keep the division of labor PRECISE.** Two simultaneous bayonet assaults after dark, muskets unloaded, against the two redoubts: **Redoubt 9 was stormed by ~400 FRENCH troops** (the Régiment Royal Deux-Ponts and Gâtinais grenadiers/chasseurs, **Lt. Col. Guillaume de Deux-Ponts** commanding); **Redoubt 10, nearer the river, by ~400 AMERICAN light infantry under Lt. Col. Alexander Hamilton** (with John Laurens circling the rear; Hamilton had lobbied Washington for the command). Both fell in under half an hour; American losses at 10 were ~9 killed/~25–31 wounded, French losses at 9 heavier (~15 killed/~77 wounded — the 9 garrison was bigger and the abatis less broken). The second parallel reached the river that night and the allied guns moved to point-blank. NEVER write this as an American-led set piece with French support — it was one assault each, the French one the harder. (HIGH on the division/commanders/sequence; casualty figures MED — ranges.)
- **October 16 — the last two throws.** Before dawn, a British **sortie** spiked a few guns in the second parallel (back in action within hours). That night Cornwallis tried his only remaining move: **ferrying the army across the York to Gloucester Point** to break out overland — the first wave crossed, and **a sudden storm scattered the boats** and ended it; the troops were brought back the next morning. (HIGH — NPS chronology; the escape attempt is fully verified, not legend.)
- **October 17 — the drummer.** With the inner town wrecked, sickness spreading, ammunition near out, and the relief fleet still in New York, at about 10 a.m. **a British drummer boy beat the parley on the parapet and an officer waved a white handkerchief**; the guns stopped. (Note the date echo only if the war-at-sea/Saratoga material is live: it was four years to the day after Saratoga's surrender — verify before using; Saratoga's convention was signed 17 October 1777. Usable as one clause, HIGH.)
- **October 18 — the Moore House.** Commissioners haggled terms; Washington refused the **honors of war** (colors cased, no American/French tune to be played by the garrison) — the same humiliation Clinton had imposed on Lincoln's army at Charleston in 1780, returned with interest. (HIGH — the honors-of-war reciprocity is well documented in the articles.)
- **October 19 — the surrender.** About 2 p.m. the garrison marched out between the mile-long allied lines — French on one side, Americans on the other — colors cased. Numbers: **~7,000–8,000 surrendered** (the army ~7,000 soldiers, plus naval personnel; with ~240 guns taken — give "more than 7,000" or the range, never one number). **Cornwallis did not come** (pleading illness); his deputy **Brig. Gen. Charles O'Hara** offered the sword **first to Rochambeau, who pointed him across to Washington; Washington, matching deputy with deputy, directed him to Maj. Gen. Benjamin Lincoln** — the man who had surrendered Charleston. (HIGH — multiply attested; the precise choreography varies slightly by eyewitness, keep to this skeleton.)
- **"The World Turned Upside Down" — TRADITION, badge it.** The story that the British bands played the tune comes from later recollection (the anecdote's main source is decades after the fact); contemporaries record the garrison's drums and slow march, but **no contemporary account names the tune**. Write: "by tradition," never as fact. (DISPUTED-as-fact / firmly traditional.)
- **Casualties of the siege itself** were modest for what it decided: allied ~**75–90 killed, ~300 wounded**; British ~**150–300 killed, ~300–600 wounded** — figures vary widely; use "a few hundred on each side, dwarfed by the 7,000 taken." (MED — ranges only.)

## 7. CLOSE — THE NIGHT THE WAR WAS DECIDED BUT NOT OVER (Nov 1781)

- **The news crossed the Atlantic in five weeks.** On **25 November 1781** the dispatches reached London — to **Lord George Germain's** house first, then minister to minister through the city to **Lord North**. North's reaction — pacing, "as he would have taken a ball in his breast" — and his reported words belong to the treaty chapter; here, at most one clause, badge as reported (the scene comes via Germain's telling as preserved in Wraxall's memoirs). The King's speech opening Parliament two days later had to be rewritten around it. (HIGH on the date/relay; the North scene is REPORTED, secondhand, decades-published — `treaty-of-paris` owns it.)
- **What was and wasn't over.** The war did not end at Yorktown: Britain still held New York, Charleston, and Savannah with ~30,000 troops; the navy fought on (and won big in the Caribbean at the Saintes, April 1782); Greene's army still watched Charleston; frontier killing went on into 1782. What ended was **the British will to reconquer thirteen colonies** — within four months the Commons voted against continuing the offensive war and North's government fell. That story — the votes, the fall, the negotiation — is the next chapter's. END THE SPINE HERE: on the messenger in Pall Mall, the night the war was decided but not yet over. (HIGH; no narrating ahead beyond the one-sentence handoff.)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **Greene's aphorism — source and PLACEMENT.** The verified-verbatim version is "We fight get beat and fight again" (to Washington, 1 May 1781). The famous "rise, and fight again" version is the **La Luzerne letter (28 Apr 1781)**. Both date from AFTER Hobkirk's Hill — late April/May 1781 — so do NOT plant the quote at Greene's December takeover as a mission statement; it's a mid-campaign summation. If the prose wants it early, frame as "he would later put it."
2. **The Guilford grapeshot is EVIDENCE-LEVEL, not fact-level.** The Cornwallis-fired-into-his-own-Guards version traces to Henry Lee's memoirs (~30 years later); Babits & Howard's pension-file study argues the guns targeted Washington's cavalry and few if any Guards fell to them. Tell the guns firing into the crisis as fact, the own-men version as the famous later story modern research doubts.
3. **The Fox chain.** Substance well attested, wording from reconstructed parliamentary reporting — "is reported to have said," with the Pyrrhus echo available as color. Same care for Clinton's "endeavoured to stop you" and ALL eighteenth-century Commons "quotes."
4. **Cowpens numbers are genuinely contested.** Morgan's force runs ~800 to ~1,900 across the scholarship (Babits's modern reconstruction is the high end); Tarleton ~1,050–1,150; losses ~110 dead / 200+ wounded / 500–600 captured. Ranges ONLY, every time. Same for Eutaw Springs (~quarter of each force; tallies vary) and the Yorktown siege casualties.
5. **Redoubts 9 and 10 — the division of labor is a precision tripwire.** The FRENCH (Deux-Ponts/Gâtinais) took **9**; the AMERICANS under Hamilton took **10**. The French assault was the larger and costlier. Any wording that folds the night into "Hamilton stormed the redoubts" fails the gate.
6. **"The World Turned Upside Down" is TRADITION.** No contemporary account names the tune; the anecdote surfaces decades later. "By tradition" mandatory. Likewise badge: Washington firing the first siege gun ("by tradition"); Morgan's militia-and-the-river rationale ("by his own later account"); Buttrick-style traditional battle cries generally.
7. **"The boy cannot escape me" (Cornwallis on Lafayette) could not be verified to any primary source** — standard-issue in popular accounts, absent from the documented record so far as this research found. OMIT, or at most "a line later put in his mouth."
8. **The ovens were REAL ovens — keep the deception's parts straight.** The Chatham bake-ovens were genuinely built and genuinely baked (the French bakery was working cover, not a prop façade); the fake part was what they implied. The fictitious-dispatches element is attested including by Washington's own 1788 letter. Don't inflate into "a fake army of mannequins" folklore; the documented kit is: ovens, boat-collecting, staging camps, planted papers. Clinton's certainty the target was New York lasted until ~2 September.
9. **De Grasse's agency — don't Americanize the decision.** Washington wanted New York into mid-August; Rochambeau steered; de Grasse CHOSE the Chesapeake, brought everything, set the deadline, and raised money in the islands to fund the campaign. The honest proportions at Yorktown: French regulars ≈ the American Continentals, plus the entire fleet, the siege train, and the engineers. Per the `french-alliance` framing, the spine must not sand this down. (Equally: no French-triumphalist inversion — Greene's year and Washington's march were the other half.)
10. **No destiny register.** In June 1781 the allied plan was still New York; in September Clinton still expected to relieve Cornwallis; the de Grasse window was weeks wide and self-imposed. Tell 1781 forward — as a chain of choices and near-misses that happened to close — never as the inevitable last act. The war also does NOT end at Yorktown (New York/Charleston/Savannah held; the Saintes was a British naval victory five months later); the chapter ends on decision, not conclusion.
11. **The militia "missing" at Guilford were mostly men going home** — a structural fact of militia service, not a rout statistic; explain once, don't let the 1,000+ figure imply mass desertion under fire.
12. **One-beat discipline.** Hobkirk's Hill, Ninety Six, Eutaw Springs, Green Spring, Arnold's Richmond raid, the Charlottesville raid, Pyle, the mutinies — each is ONE beat (two for the mutiny pair at most). The moment any of them grows a scene, it's stealing from the pulse.
