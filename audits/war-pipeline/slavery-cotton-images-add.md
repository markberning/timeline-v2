# Slavery & the Cotton Economy — ADDITIONAL Verified Image Manifest

Section source page: `src/app/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/slavery-cotton/page.tsx`.
The section currently ships **1 hero + 1 inline figure** (`/war-img/cotton-gin.jpg`,
the Sheppard "First Cotton-Gin" engraving, under **Eli Whitney's box**). This manifest
adds **7 more inline figures** to bring the section to ~9 images total, spread across
headings that currently lack one. **None duplicates the existing cotton-gin image.**

**Verification method.** Every chosen `tile.loc.gov/storage-services/.../<id>.jpg` URL
was HTTP-fetched from this sandbox (`curl`) and returned `200 image/jpeg` with the pixel
dimensions listed below (decoded with PIL). The LOC catalog JSON API
(`loc.gov/item/<id>/?fo=json`) and the `loc.gov/pictures/item/` HTML pages were blocked
from this sandbox (Cloudflare 403 / 429), so titles, creators, dates, and rights
statements were confirmed through the LOC catalog records surfaced in web search
(titles + reproduction numbers + "Rights Advisory" lines), and the canonical
`tile.loc.gov` image URLs were derived from each item's LC reproduction number /
service path and then byte-fetched to confirm they serve the actual image. **No
CC-licensed image is used; all seven are Library of Congress items.**

**PD basis legend.** Six of seven carry the LOC statement "no known restrictions on
publication"; all are wood engravings, prints, or photographs made 1815–1897 and
published well before 1929 → public domain. (The one item with an unevaluated LOC
rights line was **rejected** — see SUMMARY.) The Detroit Publishing levee negative was
made 1880–1897 and published pre-1929 → PD.

## FIGURE 1 — Enslaved people on a cotton plantation, Edisto Island, 1862

- **filename:** `slavery-cotton-plantation-hands.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/ppmsca/39500/39590r.jpg`
- **orientation:** landscape (640 × 438)
- **caption:** The "hands" the boom demanded were people. Enslaved men, women, and children gathered around a cart at James Hopkinson's cotton plantation on Edisto Island, South Carolina, photographed in 1862 — one of the earliest real photographs of plantation slavery in the Deep South, made only because Union troops had just taken this stretch of coast.
- **credit:** Henry P. Moore · albumen print · 1862 · Library of Congress, Prints & Photographs Division · public domain
- **placement:** `after **The machine that backfired**`
- **subject-verification:** LOC item 2015650290 / LC-DIG-ppmsca-39590, "Negro slaves 1862 Edisto Island, S.C. (plantation of James Hopkinson)," photographer Henry P. Moore. The LOC record describes a group of enslaved African Americans posed at the Cassina Point plantation of James Hopkinson — exactly the "growing and picking cotton had to be done by human hands… and 'hands' meant enslaved people" paragraph. PD: 1862 photo, LOC "no known restrictions."

## FIGURE 2 — Cotton bales on the New Orleans levee

- **filename:** `slavery-cotton-levee-new-orleans.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/det/4a20000/4a26000/4a26900/4a26989v.jpg`
- **orientation:** landscape (1024 × 697)
- **caption:** Cotton bales stacked the length of the New Orleans levee, the great clearinghouse where the South's crop met the world. By 1860 some 3,500 vessels and roughly $220 million in goods moved across these docks in a year — the visible end of an economy whose hidden end was the auction block.
- **credit:** William Henry Jackson · Detroit Publishing Co. · glass negative · 1880–1897 · Library of Congress, Prints & Photographs Division · public domain
- **placement:** `after **The boom**`
- **subject-verification:** LOC item 2016817562 / LC-DIG-det-4a26989, "Cotton on the levee, New Orleans," Detroit Publishing Co. (photographer W. H. Jackson). Depicts bales of cotton heaped on the New Orleans levee — the literal cotton-on-the-docks subject the "boom" / export-figures paragraphs describe. PD: made 1880–1897, published pre-1929, LOC "no known restrictions."

## FIGURE 3 — A slave coffle passing the Capitol

- **filename:** `slavery-cotton-coffle-capitol.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/cph/3a00000/3a06000/3a06200/3a06246r.jpg`
- **orientation:** landscape (640 × 514)
- **caption:** A coffle of enslaved people — chained together at the wrist, driven on foot toward the cotton frontier — marched past the unfinished United States Capitol, the building that protected the trade that bound them. The line covered roughly twenty miles a day; the families it walked away from would, more often than not, never hear of them again.
- **credit:** unattributed · wood engraving (from *A Popular History of the United States*) · 1876 · Library of Congress, Prints & Photographs Division · public domain
- **placement:** `after **The door closes, and the population grows anyway**`
- **subject-verification:** LOC item 98510280 / LC-USZ62-2574, "A slave-coffle passing the Capitol," published in *A Popular History of the United States* (Scribner, Armstrong, 1876–81); depicts shackled enslaved people in a coffle before the domeless 1815 Capitol — exactly the overland-coffle, forced-migration mechanism the section's domestic-trade arc opens onto. PD: published 1876, LOC "no known restrictions."

- **filename:** `slavery-cotton-auction-south.jpg`

Davis," *Harper's Weekly*, v. 5 no. 237, 1861 July 13, p. 442; a wood engraving of enslaved people being auctioned before a crowd — the auction-block / family-separation core of the Northup–Eliza beat. PD: published 1861, LOC "no known restrictions."

## FIGURE 5 — Price, Birch & Co., dealers in slaves, Alexandria

- **filename:** `slavery-cotton-slave-pen-alexandria.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/cph/3a20000/3a28000/3a28400/3a28447r.jpg`
- **orientation:** landscape (640 × 477)
- **caption:** The slave pen of Price, Birch & Co. on Duke Street in Alexandria, Virginia, its sign reading "DEALERS IN SLAVES." This brick building was a holding jail in the machinery of the trade — the same Franklin & Armfield operation that ran the business at near-corporate scale earlier leased it — photographed after Union troops seized it and posted a guard at the door.
- **credit:** unattributed · albumen photograph · c. 1862–1865 · Library of Congress, Prints & Photographs Division · public domain
- **placement:** `after **The Second Middle Passage**`
- **subject-verification:** LOC item 2006683273 / LC-DIG-cph-3a28447, "Front of 'slave pen,' Alexandria, Va."; the canonical Civil War photograph of the Price, Birch & Co. slave-trading premises at 1315 Duke St. (earlier the Franklin & Armfield "Negro jail"), with a Union guard out front — exactly the brand-name-firm / slave-market-building beat the domestic-trade paragraph names. PD: wartime photograph, LOC "no known restrictions."

## FIGURE 6 — Slave auction at Richmond, Virginia

- **filename:** `slavery-cotton-richmond-auction.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/cph/3a10000/3a17000/3a17600/3a17645r.jpg`
- **orientation:** landscape (640 × 383)
- **caption:** A sale in progress at Richmond, Virginia — one of the Upper South's busiest markets for selling people south. By 1860 the nation's four million enslaved people were worth an estimated three billion dollars, more than every railroad, factory, and bank in the country combined. Scenes like this one were the form that fortune actually took.
- **credit:** unattributed · wood engraving (*The Illustrated London News*, Sept. 27, 1856) · 1856 · Library of Congress, Prints & Photographs Division · public domain
- **placement:** `after **The richest thing in America**`
- **subject-verification:** LOC item 98510266 / LC-USZ62-15398 (cph.3a17645), "Slave auction at Richmond, Virginia," *The Illustrated London News*, 1856 Sept. 27, p. 315; a wood engraving of an auction at the Richmond market — visualizing "enslaved human beings were the single largest capital asset in the American economy." PD: published 1856, LOC "no known restrictions."

## SUMMARY

**7 additional inline figures proposed, all verified public domain, all byte-confirmed
`200 image/jpeg` from `tile.loc.gov`.** Combined with the existing hero
(`slavery-cotton-hero.jpg`, Eyre Crowe "Slaves Waiting for Sale, Richmond" 1861) and the
existing cotton-gin figure, the section reaches **9 images total** (1 hero + 8 inline),
inside the ~8–10 target.

Placement spread (headings that previously had NO image now do):
- **The machine that backfired** → Fig 1 (Hopkinson plantation, enslaved field hands)
- **The boom** → Fig 2 (cotton bales on the New Orleans levee)
- **The door closes, and the population grows anyway** → Fig 3 (slave coffle passing the Capitol)
- **The Second Middle Passage** → Fig 4 (slave auction) + Fig 5 (Alexandria slave pen)
- **The richest thing in America** → Fig 6 (Richmond auction)
- **“Cotton is king”** → Fig 7 (Hammond portrait)

Untouched headings (deliberately left without a NEW figure): **A fortune locked behind a
sticky seed** and **A society that staked everything on human property** are abstract
framing beats with no contemporaneous photographic subject; **Eli Whitney's box** already
carries the existing cotton-gin figure.

**REJECTED:** LOC "In the cotton field" lithograph (item 93503993, H. L. Stephens,
c. 1863) — visually apt for a field-picking subject, but its LOC record reads "Rights
status of image not evaluated" rather than "no known restrictions," so it fails the
explicit-PD-basis bar; Figure 1 (the 1862 Hopkinson photograph, LOC "no known
restrictions") carries the plantation-labor subject instead. The "Scenes on a cotton
plantation" Waud engraving (item 96513748) was passed over because LOC serves only a
single-page b&w film-copy half (cph.3c16623), not a clean full master. No modern
CC-licensed photographs of antique objects were used.
