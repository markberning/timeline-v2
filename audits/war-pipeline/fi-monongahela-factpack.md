# FACT PACK — Battle of the Monongahela (Braddock's Defeat), July 9, 1755

*French and Indian War. Every fact tagged with source. Contested numbers given as ranges. This pack is the sole permitted source for the author.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **Primary name:** Battle of the Monongahela. Alternates: "Braddock's Defeat," "Battle of Braddock's Field," "Battle of the Wilderness." [WP-Mon]
- **Date:** July 9, 1755; attack began ~1:00 p.m. [WP-Mon; BB]
- **Location:** Near present-day Braddock, PA, ~10 miles upstream/east of present-day Pittsburgh, on the Monongahela River, ~10 miles short of Fort Duquesne. Coords 40.404, -79.869. [WP-Mon; ABT]
- **Campaign:** The Braddock Expedition — British attempt to take Fort Duquesne (French) at the Forks of the Ohio. One prong of a four-pronged 1755 British offensive. [WP-Brad; ABT]

## 2. STRATEGIC BACKGROUND
- **Stakes:** control of the Ohio Country — the forks where the Allegheny + Monongahela join to form the Ohio River (today Pittsburgh); gateway to the interior and the fur trade. [WP-Brad; ABT]
- **Fort Duquesne:** French fort at the Forks; garrison commander Claude-Pierre Pécaudy de Contrecœur. [WP-Brad; WP-Mon]
- **Prelude:** Washington's 1754 Jumonville Glen skirmish + Fort Necessity surrender showed French/Native dominance and prompted Britain to send regulars. [WP-Brad]
- **Braddock appointed Commander-in-Chief, North America (1755).** [WP-Brad; WP-EB]
- **Braddock Road:** expedition left Fort Cumberland, MD, May 29, 1755; huge problem moving heavy cannon across the Alleghenies; ~2 miles/day at first; cut road ~110 miles toward Fort Duquesne. Franklin procured wagons. [WP-Brad]
- **Flying column:** Braddock split the force — fast ~1,300-man column under him went ahead; ~800-man supply column under Col. Thomas Dunbar trailed far behind. [WP-Brad; ABT]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
**British:** Maj. Gen. Edward Braddock (1695-1755), rigid, insisted on European linear tactics, dismissed wilderness-warfare warnings. Regiments: 44th Foot (Sir Peter Halkett) + 48th Foot (Dunbar), from Ireland. Provincials from MD/NY/NC/SC/PA/VA. Flying column ~1,300-1,400 (some sources 1,459/1,469). George Washington, 23, volunteer aide-de-camp, no formal command, severely ill with dysentery, rejoined July 8. Other officers: Lt. Col. Thomas Gage (advance guard ~300); Sir Peter Halkett (KIA); Capt. Robert Orme (aide, wounded); Horatio Gates (present); Lt. John Fraser (chief of scouts); Daniel Boone (wagoner, escaped by cutting team loose). [WP-Mon; WP-Brad; ABT; BB; MV]

**French & allied:** Field cmdr Capt. Daniel-Hyacinthe-Marie Liénard de Beaujeu (war paint/regalia to lead the Native warriors out; killed in opening volleys). 2IC Capt. Jean-Daniel Dumas (took command, rallied force). Charles-Michel Mouet de Langlade organized Native allies; Contrecœur sent the sortie. Composition most-cited: 891 total = 108 French marines + 146 Canadian militia + 637 Native warriors. Native nations: primarily western Great Lakes (Ottawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi) + Ohio-country (Shawnee, Lenape/Delaware, Mingo) + Wyandot; named leaders cited incl. Pontiac (Ottawa), Shingas (Lenape). [WP-Mon; WP-Brad; ABT; BB]

## 4. THE BATTLE — PLAY-BY-PLAY
- Column forded the Monongahela twice on July 9 to bypass a narrows; crossed in good order, "flags flying and fife and drum beating," spirits high. [BB; ABT]
- A meeting engagement, NOT a planned ambush: Beaujeu's force arrived too late to set an ambush; collided unexpectedly with Gage's advance guard, who opened fire. [WP-Mon; WP-Brad]
- Beaujeu killed in first volleys; Dumas steadied the force; French + Native warriors fanned into woods/ravines on both flanks, firing from cover. [WP-Mon]
- British collapse: Gage's guard fell back into the main body, bunching troops on the road; regulars held linear formation firing volleys at unseen enemy; confusion caused friendly fire; flanks enveloped. War-whoops spread panic. [WP-Mon; WP-Brad]
- Braddock: multiple horses shot under him; after ~3 hours shot through the chest/lung (possibly by his own men — disputed) and fell; resistance collapsed; carried off. [WP-Mon; WP-EB; BB]
- Rout back across the Monongahela in panic. [WP-Mon; ABT]
- Washington: rode through fire, two horses shot under him, four bullet holes in his coat, unhurt; formed rear guard covering the retreat; later rode ~30-40 miles to bring up Dunbar. [MV; WP-Brad; BB]
- French/Native did not pursue — stopped to loot/scalp; some captives tortured/killed that night. [WP-Mon]

## 5. CASUALTIES
- **British:** of ~1,459 engaged, ~976 killed or wounded (~two-thirds) in 3-4 hours. WP: 456 killed, 422+ wounded. Officers: 63 of 86 killed or wounded (26 killed + 37 wounded). Camp-follower women: of ~50, only ~4 returned. [ABT; MHN; WP-Mon; BB]
- **French/allied:** very light, disputed — WP infobox 39 killed/57 wounded; official French report 8 French/Canadian + 15 Native killed (~23); BB "fewer than 30 killed." Beaujeu the one certain notable death. Range ~23-40 killed. [WP-Mon; WP-Brad; BB]
- **Braddock died of wounds night of July 13, 1755, four days later,** during the retreat (near Uniontown / Fort Necessity). [WP-Mon; Britannica; WW]

## 6. AFTERMATH & MEANING
- Braddock asked Washington to oversee burial; chaplain wounded, Washington read the service. Buried in the middle of the cut road; wagons/horses driven over the grave to hide it from desecration. Remains found 1804 during road repair, IDed by uniform buttons. [NPS-Grave; WP-Mon; WP-EB; ABT]
- Dunbar took command of survivors, judged campaign hopeless, destroyed supplies + cannon (burned ~150 wagons), withdrew to Philadelphia — leaving the frontier exposed. [WP-Mon; ABT]
- French captured Braddock's military papers (a propaganda/diplomatic coup — war-planning in nominal peace), his artillery train, and stores — later used against other forts. [MHN]
- Frontier raids devastated MD/PA/VA borders. [ABT]
- "One of the most disastrous defeats in British colonial history"; awakened London to the scale needed. [WP-Mon]
- Washington's reputation made (the "providential survival"). [MV]
- Set up the British "Years of Disaster" 1755-57 (editorial label). Fort Duquesne stayed French until 1758 (Forbes Expedition), rebuilt as Fort Pitt → Pittsburgh. [WP-Mon; ABT]

## 7. CAUSE / STAKES (F&I WAR — NO slavery framing)
- Fought over control of the Ohio Country / trans-Appalachian interior, the fur trade, and imperial dominance between Britain and France. [WP-Brad; ABT]
- Native nations squeezed between two expanding empires; many Ohio + Great Lakes nations allied with France against land-hungry British colonists. Braddock alienated potential Native allies (ABT: told them "no savage should inherit the land"), leaving him ~8 Native scouts. The imperial contest was also a contest over Native land and autonomy. [ABT; WP-Mon]

## 8. VERIFIED QUOTES
- **Washington to John Augustine Washington, July 18, 1755 (firmly sourced):** "By the all-powerful dispensations of Providence, I have been protected beyond all human probability and expectation; for I had four bullets through my coat, and two horses shot under me, yet escaped unhurt, altho' death was levelling my companions on every side of me." [MV]
- **Washington, same letter:** the regulars "were struck with such a panic that they behaved with more cowardice than it is possible to conceive." [WP-Brad]
- **Braddock dying words (reported, secondhand, wording varies):** "Who would have thought it?" and to Orme "We shall better know how to deal with them another time." Quote as reported, not verbatim-fixed. [WW; WP-EB; BB]
- **"No savage should inherit the land" / troops fight as "gentlemen"** — reported/secondhand, characterizes attitude; treat as reported. [ABT]

## VERIFIED ADDITIONS (from critic gates, coordinator-checked — safe to use)
- **Shingas–Braddock land exchange (pre-battle, well-documented via Shingas's own account / the "Examination of Delawares"):** Before the campaign, the Lenape (Delaware) war chief **Shingas** and other Ohio leaders met Braddock and asked plainly what the English would do with the Ohio land if they won. Braddock answered that the English would inhabit and inherit the land and that "no savage should inherit the land." Asked again, he gave the same answer; he said he did not need their help. Shingas and most of the Ohio warriors then withheld their support (many later fought against the British). USE this as the SOURCE of the "no savage" line (frame as Braddock's own bigoted answer to a sovereign leader's direct question). NOTE: Shingas's *presence as a combatant at the Monongahela itself* is DISPUTED — do NOT state he fought in the battle; use him only in the pre-battle exchange.
- **Pontiac's presence at the battle is UNDOCUMENTED ("no direct evidence" — Wikipedia).** Do not assert he was there; drop or hedge clearly.
- **Prisoner torture specifics (James Smith captivity narrative, well-documented):** Captured British/colonial soldiers were taken back toward Fort Duquesne and a number were tortured and burned to death at the stake on the riverbank that night. A captive colonist, **James Smith**, witnessed it from inside the fort. The French command did not stop it. Use with restraint, not gratuitously; it distributes the moral weight honestly (French complicity named).
- **The four-pronged 1755 British offensive (textbook):** Braddock's drive on Fort Duquesne was the flagship of four simultaneous British efforts in 1755: (1) Braddock → Fort Duquesne (this battle, catastrophic failure); (2) William Johnson → Crown Point / Lake Champlain (fought to a draw at the Battle of Lake George); (3) William Shirley → Fort Niagara (stalled, never reached it); (4) Robert Monckton → Fort Beauséjour in Nova Scotia/Acadia (the one clear success, leading into the Acadian expulsion). Use one sentence of this for strategic scale.
- **Alliance shift after the defeat (well-documented):** Braddock's destruction proved British weakness; it pushed wavering Ohio nations toward the French and unleashed years of devastating raids on the Pennsylvania/Maryland/Virginia frontier (the Delaware and Shawnee carried much of it). Safe to state the consequence.
- **Gage → Lexington resonance (textbook):** Lt. Col. Thomas Gage, whose advance guard was overrun here, would two decades later (as the British general in Boston) give the orders that began the Revolution at Lexington in 1775. Optional one-clause resonance.
- **Tactical mechanism (analysis, no new fact):** the British linear system (ranks firing volleys on command) required an enemy willing to stand in the open and trade fire; against warriors firing from cover who never massed into a target, it had no answer. Safe to state plainly for a lay reader.
