## 1. Identity / Date / Location / Theatre

- **Name:** Battle of the Wilderness. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Dates:** **May 5–7, 1864** (three days; the heaviest fighting was May 5–6, with May 7
  the disengagement and the night march south). **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Location:** **Spotsylvania County and Orange County, Virginia** — the "Wilderness of
  Spotsylvania," a dense second-growth forest a few miles **west of Fredericksburg** and
  overlapping the **Chancellorsville** battlefield of a year earlier. Coordinates
  ~38°19′N 77°45′W. **[WIKI] [ABT] [NPS]**
- **Theatre:** **Eastern Theater.** The **opening battle of Grant's 1864 Overland
  Campaign** — the drive on Richmond. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Result:** **Inconclusive / tactically a draw** (with a Confederate edge — Lee bloodied
  a far larger army on his chosen ground and lost roughly half as many men). The
  **strategic** result favored the Union: Grant did NOT retreat (see §5, §9). **[WIKI] [ABT]**

### Why the Wilderness mattered — the ground as the weapon (strategic spine)
- **The first clash between Grant and Lee.** Ulysses S. **Grant (North)**, newly made
  **general-in-chief of all U.S. armies** (Lt. Gen., March 1864), chose to travel **with
  George G. Meade's (North) Army of the Potomac** rather than command from Washington —
  putting him in the field directly across from Robert E. **Lee (South)** and the **Army
  of Northern Virginia** for the first time. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Grant's new strategy:** unlike every prior Union commander in the East, Grant intended
  to fight Lee **continuously**, refusing to fall back and refit after a bloody day. The
  Overland Campaign was a war of attrition aimed at **destroying Lee's army**, not
  capturing a place. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Lee chose the Wilderness on purpose.** Grant's army (~118,000) badly outnumbered Lee's
  (~66,000). Lee deliberately struck inside the **tangled, second-growth thicket** to
  **neutralize Grant's numbers and his artillery** — terrain where lines of sight closed to
  a few yards, cannon could not be brought to bear, and a smaller army could fight a larger
  one to a standstill. **[NPS] [WIKI]** Lee meant to hit Grant **while the Union army was
  strung out crossing the Wilderness**, before it could clear the woods into open country.
  **[ABT]**
- **The terrain (the defining feature):** the Wilderness was **cut-over woodland** — a
  region whose original timber had been stripped over decades to feed **local iron
  furnaces**, leaving a dense, **near-impenetrable** tangle of scrub oak, pine, and
  underbrush. **[NPS]** *(The iron-furnace cause of the overgrowth is the standard
  explanation; the hard, verified claim is the dense second-growth thicket itself —
  [VERIFY] the furnace detail's exact phrasing before stating it as a flat fact.)* NPS
  describes the fighting as **"bushwhacking on a grand scale"** and **"isolated combat
  between small units,"** with whole formations **"confused by the bewildering forest."**
  **[NPS]**
- **Same ground as Chancellorsville:** the battle was fought across and beside the
  **Chancellorsville (May 1863) battlefield**, where Lee had won his masterpiece a year
  earlier — and where Stonewall **Jackson (South)** had been shot by his own men. The
  Wilderness would echo that irony almost exactly (see §6). **[NPS] [WIKI]**

## 4. Geography (for map accuracy)

Relative positions (north = up):

- **Two roughly parallel east–west roads** carried the battle, both running **out of the
  Wilderness toward Orange Court House in the west** and toward **Fredericksburg in the
  east.** They are **~2 miles apart**, the dense woods between them. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
  1. **Orange Turnpike (the northern axis)** — **Ewell's Corps (South)** advanced east down
     it and collided with **Warren's V Corps (North)**, later joined by **Sedgwick's VI
     Corps (North)**. The clearing of **Saunders Field** sits on this road — one of the few
     open spaces, and a focal point of May 5 fighting (and a woods fire). **[WIKI] [ABT]**
  2. **Orange Plank Road (the southern axis)** — **A.P. Hill's Corps (South)** advanced east
     down it and met **Getty's division + Hancock's II Corps (North)**. This is where
     **Longstreet's May 6 counterattack** struck. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Brock Road** — the key **north–south** road; its **intersection with the Orange Plank
  Road** was **the crucial crossroads** of the battle. Holding it kept the Union army's two
  wings connected and held open the road **south toward Spotsylvania Court House.** Getty's
  division raced to seize it on May 5 before Hill could. Hancock's men built **breastworks
  along the Brock Road**, and it was these works Longstreet's attack drove them back onto.
  **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Widow Tapp farm / Tapp Field** — a small clearing **on the Orange Plank Road**, just
  **behind / west of** Hill's line. At dawn May 6, with Hill's corps collapsing,
  **Lt. Col. William Poague's (South) 12 guns** stood in this open field and fired **canister**
  to hold off Hancock's advance until Longstreet arrived — the **artillery stand that bought
  time.** It is also where the **"Lee to the rear" / Texas Brigade** episode happened. **[WIKI]**
- **The unfinished railroad cut** — an **unfinished/graded railroad bed running roughly
  parallel to and south of the Orange Plank Road.** Lee's engineer **Martin L. Smith (South)**
  found it open and unguarded; it gave Longstreet a **covered route around the Union (Hancock's)
  left/southern flank** for the May 6 flank attack. Place it **south of the Plank Road,
  Confederate attack arrow curling up and east into Hancock's exposed flank.** **[WIKI]**
- **Germanna Ford / Germanna Plank Road (NE)** — the **Rapidan River** crossing the Union
  army had used to enter the Wilderness; the road runs **northeast** back toward the river.
  On May 7 Grant ordered the **pontoon bridges at Germanna Ford taken up** — the visible
  sign he was **not** recrossing the Rapidan to retreat. **[ABT]**
- **Map orientation summary:** Two parallel east–west roads (**Turnpike north, Plank Road
  south**), woods between; **Brock Road** crossing them **north–south**, the **Plank Road ×
  Brock Road crossroads** as the pivot. Confederate corps push **east** down both roads
  (Ewell on the Turnpike vs. Warren/Sedgwick; Hill→Longstreet on the Plank Road vs.
  Hancock). **Tapp Field** clearing just west of the Plank Road line. **Unfinished railroad**
  south of the Plank Road = Longstreet's flank-march arrow. **Saunders Field** clearing on
  the Turnpike. **Rapidan River + Germanna Ford to the NE** (the retreat route Grant did
  NOT take); **Spotsylvania Court House to the SE** (where Grant marched instead). North = up.

## 5. Sequence & turning points

**Setup:** Grant's army crossed the **Rapidan** and pushed into the Wilderness, intending to
march through it fast into open ground. Lee struck while the Union army was strung out in the
woods, gambling the terrain would erase Grant's numbers. **[WIKI] [ABT] [NPS]**

### May 5 — first contact, the two roads
- **Orange Turnpike (north):** **~morning** **Warren's V Corps (North)** collided with
  **Ewell's Corps (South)** near **Saunders Field.** Meade ordered an attack despite the
  dreadful terrain; **Ewell's men, in hasty earthworks, repelled it.** Attacks faltered in
  the dense woods — gaps opened between regiments, units lost direction, friendly-fire
  incidents occurred. Fighting see-sawed with **neither side gaining a decision.** **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Orange Plank Road (south):** **A.P. Hill's Corps (South)** advanced east; Union cavalry
  detected it, and **Getty's division (North)** raced to hold the **vital Plank Road × Brock
  Road crossroads** before Hill could seize it. **~4:15 p.m. Getty attacked**, **Hancock's
  II Corps (North)** arriving to join in. Brutal close-range fighting in the brush ran into
  the night with **no decision.** **Brig. Gen. Alexander Hays (North) was killed** here. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **End of May 5:** a confused, bloody draw; Hill's Third Corps ended the day **poorly
  positioned and disorganized** on the Plank Road, having fought all afternoon. **[WIKI]**

### May 6 — the day of the great attacks
- **Dawn (~5 a.m.) — Hancock's assault breaks Hill:** **Hancock and Getty (North) attacked**
  down the Plank Road and **drove A.P. Hill's (South) tired, disorganized corps back in
  confusion.** For a moment Lee's right was on the verge of collapse. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **The Tapp Field stand:** at the **Widow Tapp farm**, **Poague's (South) 12 guns** fired
  canister into the Plank Road to slow Hancock's advance and buy time. **[WIKI]**
- **"Lee to the rear" / "Texans always move them":** **Longstreet's First Corps (South)**
  arrived **~6 a.m.** after a hard night march (his corps had been ~10 miles off at the
  battle's start). His vanguard was **John Gregg's (South) Texas Brigade.** Lee, seeing his
  line saved, rode forward to lead the counterattack himself, **waving his hat and shouting
  "Texans always move them!"** The soldiers **refused to advance until Lee went back**,
  some seizing his horse Traveller's reins; Longstreet persuaded him to a safer spot. This is
  the famous **"Lee to the rear"** moment (one of a couple in the war). **[WIKI]**
  *(Author: it's "Lee to the rear" by tradition; the verified primary detail is the soldiers
  halting his horse and the "Texans always move them" cry. [VERIFY] before printing "Lee to
  the rear!" as a literal shouted quote — treat it as the episode's name, not a sourced quote.)*
- **Longstreet's counterattack:** divisions of **Field (South, left)** and **Kershaw (South,
  right)** drove the Union line back between Tapp Field and the Brock Road. The **Texas
  Brigade was wrecked** — by one account only ~250 of ~800 men came through unscathed.
  **Wadsworth (North) was mortally wounded** as Field's men drove his command back. **[WIKI]**
- **The unfinished-railroad flank march (the masterstroke):** **~10 a.m.** Lee's engineer
  **Martin L. Smith (South)** reported an **unfinished railroad bed south of the Plank Road**
  that led around Hancock's exposed left. **~11 a.m. Moxley Sorrel (South)** led **four
  brigades** (senior brigade commander **Mahone, South**) in a **surprise flank attack** that
  **rolled up the Union line.** Hancock's flank was unguarded (an order to Gibbon to guard/
  strike there miscarried — Gibbon insisted he never received it). Hancock fell back to his
  **Brock Road breastworks.** Hancock later said the attack rolled up his line **"like a wet
  blanket."** **[WIKI]**
- **Longstreet wounded (the Jackson echo):** riding forward to press the advantage on the
  Orange Plank Road, **Longstreet (South) was shot by his own men** — Mahone's **12th
  Virginia**, separated in the smoke, was mistaken for Federals by its own brigade, and the
  two Confederate bodies of troops fired on each other, catching Longstreet's mounted party
  in the crossfire. Longstreet was **hit through the throat/shoulder; Brig. Gen. Micah
  Jenkins (South) was mortally wounded** in the same volley. The attack **stalled** with
  Longstreet down. See §6 for the full circumstances and the Chancellorsville parallel.
  **[WIKI] [WIKI-JENKINS] [ECW] [ABT]**
- **The Brock Road assault, ~4–4:15 p.m.:** Lee organized a renewed attack on Hancock's
  Brock Road works; the Confederates **briefly planted flags on / breached the breastworks**
  (helped by a fire in the log works), but were **repulsed within about an hour** — **Col.
  Samuel S. Carroll's brigade (North)** counterattacking, **Mott's division (North)** having
  given way first. The works held. **[WIKI]**
- **Orange Turnpike (north), evening — Gordon's flank attack:** **~5:30 p.m. Brig. Gen. John
  B. Gordon (South)** struck the **exposed Union right (Ricketts's division, North)** —
  having argued for the attack over **Jubal Early's (South)** initial objections. It crumpled
  the Union flank, **captured Brig. Gens. Shaler and Seymour (North)**, and **nearly bagged
  Sedgwick (North)** himself — but **darkness and the dense woods** let the Union line
  re-form before it became a rout. **[WIKI]**

### May 7 — the turn of the campaign
- **The armies entrenched; no major infantry assault.** Grant judged Lee's earthworks too
  strong to storm and chose to **maneuver, not retreat.** **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **THE HINGE:** **Grant did NOT recross the Rapidan** — the move every prior Union commander
  had made after a bloody check by Lee. He ordered the **pontoon bridges at Germanna Ford
  taken up** and the army to **march southeast, around Lee's right, toward Spotsylvania Court
  House** (~10 miles), keeping the army **between Lee and Richmond** and forcing the next
  fight on his own terms. **[ABT] [WIKI]** It was the **first time the Army of the Potomac
  stayed on the offensive after an opening battle with Lee in Virginia.** **[WIKI]**
- **The night march & the cheer:** on the night of May 7 the exhausted army filed out of its
  trenches and, reaching a fork, found Grant **turning the column south, not north.** As the
  soldiers realized they were **advancing, not retreating, they broke into cheering** — and
  on the march, sang. This is the emotional pivot of the whole campaign: the army understood
  this commander was **different.** **[WIKI] [ABT]**
  - **Lee read it correctly:** he predicted Grant would head for **Spotsylvania Court House**
    and **got there first**, digging in — opening the next battle (Spotsylvania, May 8–21),
    which is out of scope of this pack. **[WIKI] [ABT]**

## 6. Human cost — named people / vivid documented detail

- **Lt. Gen. James Longstreet (South), wounded May 6 — the Jackson echo (handle precisely):**
  - **The circumstances:** after his flank attack rolled up Hancock, Longstreet rode forward
    on the **Orange Plank Road** with a group of officers. Mahone's **12th Virginia Infantry**
    had become **separated** in the woods; as it moved back toward the road, **its own brigade
    mistook it for Union troops** and the two Confederate bodies **fired on each other**,
    catching Longstreet's party in the crossfire. **[WIKI] [WIKI-JENKINS] [ECW]**
  - **The wound:** a bullet passed **through his throat and into/through the right shoulder**
    (sources vary on exact path — **[VERIFY]** "throat and shoulder" wording). He **survived**,
    but was out of action for **about five to six months.** **[WIKI] [ABT] [ECW]**
  - **The Chancellorsville parallel (the irony — do NOT overstate):** Longstreet was shot by
    his own men **near the same ground, almost exactly a year after Stonewall Jackson (South)
    was shot by his own men at Chancellorsville** (May 2, 1863). Verified specifics: it was
    **one year and four days later** [ABT], **roughly four miles** from Jackson's wounding
    site [ABT, via secondary]. **Both** were corps commanders, **both** shot by their own
    troops in these same woods, **both** during a successful attack on a Union flank.
    **[ABT] [WIKI]** *(The "four miles / one year and four days" figures come from ABT and
    closely-sourced secondary writing — solid, but [VERIFY] the exact mileage before printing
    a precise "four miles." The safe, fully-verified statement is "by his own men, in the same
    woods, almost exactly a year after Jackson met the same fate nearby.")*
  - **Longstreet's parting order:** as he was carried off he turned command to **Field**,
    saying **"Press the enemy."** **[WIKI] — [VERIFY]** exact wording before quoting verbatim.
- **Brig. Gen. Micah Jenkins (South), killed May 6** — struck in the **forehead** by the same
  friendly-fire volley that wounded Longstreet; a **Minié ball lodged in his brain**, and he
  **died after about five hours**, never regaining lucidity. **[WIKI-JENKINS]** *(The "shot in
  the forehead / lingered ~5 hours" detail is from his Wikipedia page; safe to use, but
  [VERIFY] the 5-hour figure if printed precisely.)*
- **Brig. Gen. James S. Wadsworth (North), mortally wounded May 6** on the Plank Road front,
  as Longstreet's counterattack drove his division back; he died days later. **[WIKI]**
  *(A wealthy New Yorker and former gubernatorial candidate who took a combat command —
  context, [VERIFY] biographical specifics before using.)*
- **Brig. Gen. Alexander Hays (North), killed May 5** on the Plank Road front, shot while
  **addressing the 63rd Pennsylvania.** **[WIKI]**
- **Brig. Gens. Alexander Shaler and Truman Seymour (North), captured** in Gordon's evening
  flank attack May 6. **Maj. Gen. Sedgwick (North) was nearly captured**, his horse hit and
  a color-bearer beside him shot. **[WIKI]**
- **THE WILDERNESS FIRES (the battle's defining horror — attribute carefully):**
  - Muzzle flashes and shellfire set the **dry leaves and underbrush ablaze**; fires spread
    through the woods **between the lines**, and **wounded men too hurt to crawl away were
    burned to death** where they lay — men of **both sides.** **[WIKI] [NPS] [EV]**
  - At **Saunders Field**, a fire caught during the fighting; soldiers fought **hand-to-hand
    at the guns** while **"the field caught fire and men from both sides were shocked as their
    wounded comrades burned to death."** **[WIKI]** *(Quoted phrasing is Wikipedia narration —
    paraphrase it; do not attribute to a person.)*
  - **NPS:** **"Fires blazed through the forest, sending hot, acrid smoke rolling into the
    air and searing the wounded trapped between the lines — a fitting conclusion to a grisly
    engagement."** **[NPS]**
  - **Horace Porter (North)**, Grant's aide-de-camp, left the most-quoted eyewitness line —
    see §7 #4. **[VERIFY]** exact wording before printing inside quotation marks.

## 8. THE CAUSE — SLAVERY (ship-blocking) + the local hook

> **NAME THE CAUSE (locked, ship-blocking).** The section MUST, in its own prose, name
> **slavery** as the thing the war — and each side — was ultimately fought over. Omission is
> the default Lost Cause failure; "Grant vs. Lee in a thicket" framing alone fails the gate.

### The documented throughline
- The Confederacy fought to preserve and extend **slavery**; Lee's **(South)** Army of
  Northern Virginia was the shield of a slaveholding republic. Grant's **(North)** new
  strategy — to grind that army down and **never let it rest** — was aimed squarely at
  **destroying the military force keeping slavery alive.** The Overland Campaign was the
  beginning of the end **of that institution**, paid for in the blood of the Wilderness.
- The relentlessness is itself the cause-link: Grant could **trade ~18,000 men for ~10,000
  and keep advancing** because the Union was fighting to **end** the slaveholders' rebellion
  for good — not to win a battle and go home.

### STRONG LOCAL HOOK — the 23rd USCT, raised from these counties **[ABT-USCT] [ECW]**
The single most concrete cause-hook for the Wilderness is that **Grant's army contained
Black soldiers who had themselves been enslaved on this exact ground:**
- The **4th Division of the IX Corps** (under **Brig. Gen. Edward Ferrero, North**, in
  **Burnside's** corps) was an **all-Black division of United States Colored Troops (USCT)** —
  the first Black combat division to march with the Army of the Potomac, paraded past
  **President Lincoln (North)** in Washington on its way to the front. **[ABT-USCT] [ECW]**
- Its **23rd USCT** regiment was recruited from **"free men and ex-slaves from … Stafford,
  Spotsylvania, Orange, Culpeper, and Caroline counties and the city of Fredericksburg"** —
  i.e., **the very counties the Wilderness battlefield sits in** (Spotsylvania and Orange).
  Men who had been **property in this neighborhood** now carried Union rifles back into it.
  **[ABT-USCT]**
- The **23rd USCT became "the first colored troops to fight in 'directed combat' against
  Confederate General Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia"** — at the intersection of
  **Catharpin and Old Plank Roads**, **May 15, 1864**, days after the Wilderness, as the
  campaign moved to **Spotsylvania.** They **"drove back Rosser's cavalry."** **[ABT-USCT]**
  *(Note the precise scope: this first directed combat was **May 15 near Spotsylvania**, not
  inside the May 5–7 Wilderness fight itself — during the Wilderness the division was guarding
  the army's wagon trains. The author must say "days later, as the campaign moved south,"
  not place USCT infantry in the main Wilderness lines. [VERIFY] kept tight on the date/place.)*
- **Ferrero (North) reported** the action proved **"these men were ready and able to fight
  their former oppressors."** **[ABT-USCT]** *(Paraphrase of/quote from Ferrero's report;
  [VERIFY] exact wording before printing verbatim.)*

**Author directive:** name slavery plainly as the cause where the section sets its stakes —
Lee's army was the shield of a slave society, Grant's strategy was built to break it — and

**formerly enslaved men from Orange and Spotsylvania counties**, who days later became the

literal stake in the war's outcome were marching back across the ground of their bondage.

## 9. Significance

- **The strategic turning point of the war in the East.** Tactically the Wilderness was a
  bloody draw with a Confederate edge — Lee stopped a far larger army on his chosen ground
  and bled it for half his own losses. **But Grant refused to retreat**, and that refusal
  changed everything: for the first time, a Union army **kept coming after Lee hit it.** **[WIKI] [ABT] [NPS]**
- **Opened the Overland Campaign** — a continuous, six-week grind (Wilderness → Spotsylvania
  → North Anna → Cold Harbor → the siege of Petersburg) that pinned Lee's army and never let
  it recover the initiative. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
- **Proved Grant's attrition strategy.** The Confederacy **could not replace** the men it lost;
  the Union could. The disproportion that looked like a Union defeat (~18k vs. ~10k) was, by
  Grant's arithmetic, a step toward Confederate collapse. **[WIKI] [NPS]**
- **Crippled the Army of Northern Virginia's high command.** Lee lost **Longstreet (South),
  his "Old War Horse,"** to a wound for the rest of the campaign's critical phase — a friendly-
  fire loss eerily echoing Jackson's a year before. The army that won at Chancellorsville with
  Jackson's flank march fought the Wilderness without either of its great wing commanders. **[ABT]**
- **The morale pivot for the Union army.** The night march south, and the cheering when the
  soldiers realized they were **advancing**, marked the moment the Army of the Potomac stopped
  expecting to lose to Lee. **[WIKI] [ABT]**
