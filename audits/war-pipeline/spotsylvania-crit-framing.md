## Lost Cause / soft-pedaling hunt

### Romanticization of the Confederacy — **CLEAN.**
- Lee is shown as a commander who **misread Grant and handed him the battle by accident** (the
  artillery blunder), whose men "wouldn't have it" when he tried to lead a charge — human, fallible,
  not marble. No "marble man" hagiography.
- "Lee to the rear!" is rendered as a documented episode of soldiers protecting their commander, with
  an explicit deflation ("the army would not spend its commander to buy back a trench") — it is NOT
  staged as noble Confederate gallantry. Good.
- The Army of Northern Virginia is named plainly as **"the shield of that slave society"** and "the
  army that was fighting to keep them and their families enslaved." No euphemism about what it was for.

### False balance / "both sides were just brave" — **CLEAN.**
- The "tactical draw, both sides claimed it" framing is immediately corrected to the real meaning:
  *"a strategic disaster for the South, because it proved the new arithmetic."* The draft refuses the
  lazy "everyone fought hard, nobody won" equivalence.
- The closing explicitly rejects abstraction: *"saying plainly what these two armies were fighting
  for."* It does not give the Confederate cause a sympathetic gloss.

### Euphemism — **ONE minor flag (SHOULD-FIX).**
- The draft uses **"holding Black people as property"** and **"listed as property in the records of
  these towns"** and **"enslaved" / "formerly enslaved"** — all correct, direct, non-euphemistic
  language. Good.
- **SHOULD-FIX (very minor):** the phrase *"a regiment of Black soldiers"* and the surrounding prose
  are fine, but one spot leans on "the slaveholders' republic" / "slaveholders' army" as the operative
  noun. That is accurate and pointed — not a defect — but worth a sentence ensuring the *enslaved
  people themselves* are the grammatical subject of liberation, not merely the object the South
  "wanted to keep." The draft actually does this well already ("Men who had been listed as property...
  drove off Confederate cavalry on the soil they had been enslaved on"). No change strictly required;
  flagged only so the author confirms the agency framing stays foregrounded. **Effectively CLEAN.**

### Presentism — **CLEAN.**
- The draft does not anachronistically lecture; it lets the 1864 facts (men returning in uniform to
  the county that enslaved them) carry the moral weight. No 21st-century overlay.

### Unfair sympathy / sanitizing or gratuitous horror — **CLEAN, well-calibrated.**
- The Bloody Angle horror is concrete (the oak cut down by bullets, the trench filling with mud and
  the dead) but NOT gratuitous — it serves the point about the scale of killing, then stops. The
  Smithsonian stump is used as evidence, not gore for its own sake.
- The human cost is given to **both** sides without privileging Confederate suffering. Sedgwick (North)
  and Stuart (South) are paired as "each army lost a beloved senior commander," which is fair
  even-handedness about *grief*, not false balance about *cause*.
- The enslaved soldiers are rendered with dignity and agency, not as symbols or victims-only: they
  "charged the army that was fighting to keep them and their families enslaved," they "settled the
  question of what it was all for by picking up a rifle." Active, not passive. Good.

### Naming the enemy correctly — **CLEAN.**
- North = Union, South = CSA, applied consistently (per the war vertical's side-tag rule). The
  Confederacy is never softened into "the South" as a neutral region when the moral point is at stake;
  it is "the slaveholders' republic," "the slave society."
