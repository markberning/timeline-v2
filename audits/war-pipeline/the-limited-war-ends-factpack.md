## CHAPTER 2 THESIS (the spine the author is serving)

1862's enormous battles killed the dream of a short, limited, conciliatory war — and
turned a war to *restore the Union* into a war *against slavery*. By year's end both
sides understood it would be a long war of destruction, and emancipation had become a
Union war aim. The chapter's through-line: **the West cracks first (rivers + Grant) →
the casualty shock (Shiloh) → the East stalemates and Lee seizes the initiative
(Peninsula → Seven Days → Second Bull Run → first invasion) → Antietam checks the
invasion and unlocks the Proclamation → the war's purpose changes → Fredericksburg
closes a brutal year → the whole machine is straining and the "hard war" has begun.**

## §1 — The plan for a short war, and the man who wouldn't move

**Job:** establish the strategic situation at the start of 1862 — both sides' grand
plans, and the central Eastern problem (McClellan builds a magnificent army and
won't fight with it). Sets up the contrast the rest of the chapter pays off: the
West acts, the East sits.

- **The Anaconda Plan (the Union grand strategy).** Gen. Winfield Scott (North),
  general-in-chief at the war's start, proposed a strategy the press mocked as the
  "Anaconda Plan": **blockade the Southern coast** and **seize the Mississippi River**
  to cut the Confederacy in two and strangle it economically, rather than smash it in
  one big battle. Derided early as too slow, it became the actual shape of Union
  victory. [Wikipedia: Anaconda Plan; Wikipedia: Capture of New Orleans — "Union
  strategy under Winfield Scott's Anaconda Plan"]
- **McClellan and the Army of the Potomac.** Maj. Gen. George B. McClellan (North)
  took command of the main Eastern army after the First Bull Run defeat (July 1861)
  and **built and drilled it into the formidable Army of the Potomac** — a superb
  organizer and trainer, beloved by his men. [Wikipedia: George B. McClellan]
- **The "cautious general" problem.** McClellan was "deliberate and cautious in the
  extreme, and from early in the conflict he consistently overestimated the strength
  of Confederate troops facing him." One scholarly summary: McClellan "always (1)
  overestimated the size of the enemy by a factor of two and (2) underestimated the
  size of his own strength by the same factor." His detective chief **Allan
  Pinkerton (North)** fed him inflated enemy numbers, but the habit was McClellan's
  own. [Wikipedia: George B. McClellan; "The McClellan Mistake," thehighgroundatgettysburg.org]
- **Lincoln's growing frustration.** Lincoln (North) diagnosed McClellan with "a case
  of the slows." His exasperated quip, widely reported: *"If General McClellan does
  not want to use the army, I would like to borrow it for a time."* (Jan. 10, 1862.)
  [history.com: McClellan; the-past.com, "Firing the Generals"] **[VERIFY exact
  wording/date — the "borrow it" line is well attested but phrasings vary slightly.]**
- **General War Order No. 1 (Jan. 27, 1862).** Out of patience, Lincoln issued
  **General War Order No. 1**, ordering all Union land and naval forces to begin a
  general advance by **Feb. 22, 1862** (Washington's Birthday). The order worked in
  the West — Grant moved — but McClellan still did not move. [history.com & House
  Divided (Dickinson College): General War Order No. 1, Jan. 27, 1862]
- **The Confederate strategy: the "defensive-offensive."** The Confederacy under
  President Jefferson Davis (South) generally fought a **defensive** war to protect its
  territory and outlast Northern will — striking offensively when an opening appeared
  (as Lee would in the fall). The South did not need to *conquer* the North; it needed
  only to *survive* and make the war too costly for the North to continue. *(Standard
  framing; the author should state it plainly as the strategic logic, not assert a
  single sourced quote.)* **[VERIFY — keep as characterization, not a quoted claim.]**

**NAME-THE-CAUSE anchor for this section:** the Union was at war to restore the Union;
the Confederacy had seceded and was fighting to preserve slavery. The author should
state, plainly and early, *what the two grand strategies were ultimately in service
of* — see the dedicated NAME-THE-CAUSE section below.

## §2 — The West cracks first: the rivers and the rise of Grant

**Job:** while the East sits, the West moves — and the key insight is geographic: the
**rivers** are the highways into the Confederate heartland. Establishes Grant and
"Unconditional Surrender." **LINK DOWN to the Fort Donelson dossier; tell the
strategy, not the tactics.**

- **The river insight.** "Union commanders realized that control of the major rivers
  would be the key to success in the Western Theater." The **Tennessee** and
  **Cumberland** rivers were natural invasion roads running south into the Confederate
  interior — guarded, respectively, by **Fort Henry** and **Fort Donelson**.
  [Battlefields.org; history.navy.mil: Forts Henry and Donelson]
- **Fort Henry (Feb. 6, 1862).** Brig. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant (North) and Flag Officer
  Andrew H. Foote's (North) gunboats took Fort Henry on the Tennessee River — Foote's
  ironclad gunboats did most of the work before the infantry arrived. [Wikipedia:
  Battle of Fort Henry; National Portrait Gallery]
- **Fort Donelson (Feb. 11–16, 1862).** Grant moved ~12 miles overland to Fort
  Donelson on the Cumberland and took it after several days' fighting. **Casualties:
  Union ~2,691 (507 killed, 1,976 wounded, 208 captured/missing); Confederate ~13,846
  — but of those only ~327 killed and ~1,127 wounded; the rest (~12,392) were
  CAPTURED** when the garrison surrendered. The mass surrender is the point: Grant
  bagged an entire army. [Wikipedia: Battle of Fort Donelson; Battlefields.org]
- **"Unconditional Surrender."** When the Confederate commander asked for terms, Grant
  replied: *"No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender can be accepted.
  I propose to move immediately upon your works."* The North seized on it; Grant's own
  initials (U.S.) were rechristened **"Unconditional Surrender" Grant**, and he was
  promoted to major general. [Battlefields.org: "Ulysses S. Grant: The Myth of
  'Unconditional Surrender' Begins at Fort Donelson"; Wikipedia: Fort Donelson]
- **The strategic payoff.** The twin captures were "the first significant Union
  victories of the war" and opened the heartland: they unhinged the Confederate
  defensive line, and **Kentucky and much of Tennessee — including Nashville, the
  first Confederate state capital to fall — passed into Union control.** [Wikipedia:
  Fort Donelson; Britannica; history.navy.mil]
- **People:** Grant (North), Foote (North); the Confederate commanders are best left
  to the dossier.

the surrender drama).

## §3 — Shiloh and the casualty shock: the war becomes total

**Job:** the emotional and strategic hinge of the whole chapter. Shiloh is where the
true cost of the war became visible and the "short war" illusion died on both sides.
**LINK DOWN to the Shiloh dossier; here it's about the SHOCK and what it changed.**

- **Date & place.** Battle of Shiloh (Pittsburg Landing), Tennessee, **April 6–7,
  1862.** A surprise Confederate dawn attack on April 6 nearly drove Grant's army into
  the Tennessee River; reinforced overnight, the Union counterattacked on April 7 and
  won the field. [Wikipedia: Battle of Shiloh; Britannica]
- **The casualty shock — the load-bearing fact.** Total casualties **~23,000** (Wikipedia
  aggregate **~23,746**: **Union 13,047** — 1,754 killed, 8,408 wounded, 2,885
  missing/captured; **Confederate 10,699** — 1,728 killed, 8,012 wounded, 959
  missing/captured). [Wikipedia: Battle of Shiloh]
- **Why the number broke the country's nerve.** Shiloh's casualties "exceeded
  casualties from all of the United States' previous wars" combined — more than the
  battle casualties of the Revolution, the War of 1812, and the Mexican–American War
  *together*. [Battlefields.org: Shiloh; Wikipedia: Battle of Shiloh — "About 20,000
  men were killed or wounded at Shiloh, while earlier major battles at Manassas …
  Fort Donelson, and Pea Ridge combined to only 12,000."] **[VERIFY phrasing —
  "battle-related casualties" vs "deaths"; the strongest defensible claim is that
  Shiloh's casualties EXCEEDED THE COMBINED CASUALTIES of America's prior wars. Don't
  overstate as "more deaths than all prior wars" without that qualifier.]**
- **Albert Sidney Johnston killed (April 6).** Confederate commander **Gen. Albert
  Sidney Johnston (South)** was shot in the leg leading the attack mid-afternoon and
  **bled to death from a severed artery** — the **highest-ranking soldier killed in
  combat on either side in the entire war.** Command passed to **Gen. P.G.T.
  Beauregard (South).** [Wikipedia: Battle of Shiloh]
- **Grant's own conversion — the chapter's thesis in one man's words.** In his
  memoirs Grant (North) wrote that up to Shiloh he had believed the rebellion would
  "collapse suddenly and soon" if a decisive victory could be won — but after Shiloh,
  *"I gave up all idea of saving the Union except by complete conquest."* This is the
  cleanest single statement of the chapter's thesis: a Union general realizing, in
  April 1862, that this would be a long war of destruction, not a short one.
  [Grant, *Personal Memoirs*, quoted at en.m.wikipedia.org: Ulysses S. Grant and the
  American Civil War; Battlefields.org] **[VERIFY exact wording against Grant's
  *Personal Memoirs* before quoting verbatim.]**
- **The night exchange (color, optional).** Sherman (North) to Grant after the first
  day's near-disaster: *"Well, Grant, we've had the devil's own day, haven't we?"*
  Grant: *"Yes. Lick 'em tomorrow, though."* [Wikipedia: Battle of Shiloh] **[VERIFY —
  popular and well-attested but reconstructed dialogue; flag as traditionally reported.]**
- **Strategic meaning.** Shiloh "convinced Union leaders that the war was not going to
  end quickly in the west." The casualty shock is the moment the *system* — manpower,
  medicine, public morale — first registers the war's true scale (carry forward to §7).
  [Wikipedia: Battle of Shiloh]

**LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/western/shiloh` (the two-day battle in full + the
human cost). **Also LINK DOWN, in §7, to:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/medicine`
(the medical system Shiloh's ~17,000 wounded overwhelmed).

## §4 — The East stalemates, Lee seizes the initiative: Peninsula → Seven Days → Second Bull Run

**Job:** the East's 1862 story in one strategic arc — McClellan's giant set-piece
campaign to take Richmond, its collapse the moment Lee takes command, and Lee then
flipping from defense to offense and opening the road north. Several battle dossiers
hang off this section; **LINK DOWN to each, tell the strategy.**

- **The Peninsula Campaign (March–July 1862).** McClellan (North) finally moved — but
  not overland. He shipped the Army of the Potomac by water to **Fort Monroe** at the
  tip of the Virginia Peninsula and advanced up it toward **Richmond**, the Confederate
  capital, intending to take the city in one grand turning movement. [Wikipedia:
  Peninsula campaign; history.com: Peninsula Campaign]
- **The slows again — the Siege of Yorktown.** Confronted by Confederate works across
  the Peninsula, McClellan stopped to lay a formal **siege of Yorktown** rather than
  attack; the Confederates simply withdrew (May 4, 1862) before his siege guns opened.
  [Wikipedia: Peninsula campaign]
- **How close the Union got.** McClellan pushed to the very outskirts of Richmond —
  Union forces were blunted at Drewry's Bluff **just ~7 miles (11 km) from the
  Confederate capital**, close enough that soldiers later spoke of hearing Richmond's
  church bells. [Wikipedia: Peninsula campaign] **[VERIFY the "church bells" detail
  before using as a quoted specific — the 7-mile distance is solid; the bells are a
  widely-repeated but softer claim.]**
- **The command change that decided the campaign (June 1, 1862).** Confederate
  commander **Gen. Joseph E. Johnston (South)** was wounded by a shell fragment at the
  Battle of **Seven Pines / Fair Oaks (May 31, 1862)**. The next day, **June 1, 1862,
  Gen. Robert E. Lee (South) took command of the army he would rename the Army of
  Northern Virginia** — the single most consequential personnel change of the Eastern
  war. [Wikipedia: Peninsula campaign; Wikipedia: Seven Days Battles]
- **The Seven Days Battles (June 25 – July 1, 1862).** Lee immediately went on the
  attack to drive McClellan away from Richmond, in a week of battles: **Beaver Dam
  Creek / Mechanicsville (June 26), Gaines's Mill (June 27), Savage's Station (June
  29), Glendale / White Oak Swamp (June 30), and Malvern Hill (July 1).** [Wikipedia:
  Seven Days Battles]
  - **Gaines's Mill (June 27)** was **Lee's first victory** — the attack that broke
    McClellan's nerve and turned the campaign. **LINK DOWN TO:**
    `/war-civil-war/eastern/gaines-mill`.
  - **Malvern Hill (July 1)** was the bloody end: the **Union held the field and won
    the day tactically — Confederate assaults were slaughtered by massed Union
    artillery — yet McClellan kept retreating anyway**, the paradox that the dossier's
    own hook names ("won the field and lost the campaign"). **LINK DOWN TO:**
    `/war-civil-war/eastern/malvern-hill`.
- **Seven Days casualties.** Approx. **Union ~15,855** (1,734 killed, 8,066 wounded,
  6,055 missing/captured); **Confederate ~20,204** (3,494 killed, 15,758 wounded, 952
  missing/captured). Lee won strategically but at a *higher* cost than his enemy — a
  pattern (Confederate offensives bleeding the smaller army) the author can carry
  forward as a manpower-system theme into §7. [Wikipedia: Seven Days Battles]
- **The strategic result.** Lee **drove McClellan back to Harrison's Landing on the
  James River**; the campaign to take Richmond in one stroke was dead. "A psychological
  victory for the Confederacy, signaling that Richmond was out of danger." Lee himself:
  *"Our success has not been as great or complete as we should have desired."*
  [Wikipedia: Seven Days Battles] **[VERIFY Lee quote wording.]**
- **Second Bull Run / Second Manassas (Aug. 28–30, 1862).** With Richmond safe, Lee
  turned north against a *new* Union army — the Army of Virginia under the boastful
  **Maj. Gen. John Pope (North)** (who had reportedly announced his "headquarters in
  the saddle"). Lee split his army, sent **Maj. Gen. Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson
  (South)** to strike Pope's supply base and then hold a defensive line, and on Aug. 30
  unleashed **Lt. Gen. James Longstreet's (South) ~25,000 men in the largest
  simultaneous mass assault of the war**, crushing Pope's flank and routing him back
  toward Washington. **Casualties: Union ~14,462; Confederate ~7,298.** [Wikipedia:
  Second Battle of Bull Run]
- **Lee's confidence peaks.** Second Bull Run was a crushing Confederate victory that
  cleared Virginia of major Union armies and **opened the road north** — the
  springboard for Lee's first invasion of the Union (→ §5). **LINK DOWN TO:**
  `/war-civil-war/eastern/second-bull-run`.
- **People:** McClellan (North), Pope (North), Lincoln (North); Lee (South), J.E.
  Johnston (South), Jackson (South), Longstreet (South).

## §5 — Antietam: the bloodiest day, the invasion stopped, the war's purpose unlocked

**Job:** the chapter's strategic climax. Antietam is where Lee's first invasion is
checked and where Lincoln gets the "victory" he needs to change what the war is FOR.
**LINK DOWN to the Antietam dossier for the tactics; here it's about the strategic
hinge and the pivot to emancipation (which §6 develops).**

- **Lee's first invasion — the Maryland Campaign (Sept. 1862).** Riding the momentum
  of Second Bull Run, Lee (South) crossed the Potomac into **Maryland** — his first
  major offensive on Union soil. Strategic aims: feed his army off Northern farms,
  pull the war off war-ravaged Virginia, encourage Maryland (a slaveholding border
  state) to join the Confederacy, and — with a battlefield win on Northern ground —
  push **Britain and France toward recognizing the Confederacy** and pressure
  Lincoln before the fall elections. [Wikipedia: Battle of Antietam] *(The diplomacy
  thread is owned by the "Britain, France & Cotton" theme,
  `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/cotton-diplomacy` — link down, don't re-cover.)*
- **The Lost Order (Special Order 191).** Two Union soldiers found a copy of **Lee's
  Special Order 191** wrapped around three cigars — revealing that **Lee had divided
  his army** into widely separated columns. McClellan (North) held a near-miraculous
  intelligence windfall — and **delayed ~18 hours** before acting on it, squandering
  the chance to destroy Lee's scattered army in detail. The episode is the perfect
  capstone to the chapter's McClellan-caution thread. [Wikipedia: Battle of Antietam]
- **Antietam (Sept. 17, 1862) — the bloodiest single day in American history.** **Total
  casualties ~22,727: Union 12,410 (2,108 killed, 9,549 wounded, 753
  missing/captured); Confederate 10,316 (1,546 killed, 7,752 wounded, 1,018
  missing/captured).** [Wikipedia: Battle of Antietam]
  > **CONTINUITY FLAG (corpus-level):** the home-spine hook for Antietam in
  > `civil-war-roster.ts` reads *"23,000 fell in twelve hours"* and the First Bull Run
  > hook also references a "23,000"-style figure; the **Antietam dossier and standard
  > scholarship use ~22,700.** The author of THIS chapter should use **~22,000–23,000**
  > consistently and the same figure the Antietam dossier ships, so the number reads
  > the same everywhere (the pipeline's continuity rule). Recommend: cite **~22,700**
  > and round-describe as "about 23,000." **[VERIFY against the shipped Antietam
  > dossier's exact number before drafting.]**
- **The strategic outcome — tactical draw, strategic Union check.** Tactically
  Antietam was roughly a **draw** (appalling losses, no breakthrough), but
  **strategically it was a Union victory: Lee's invasion was stopped and he withdrew
  back across the Potomac into Virginia.** [Wikipedia: Battle of Antietam]
- **McClellan fails to pursue — and is fired.** McClellan (North) **declined to pursue
  Lee for over a month** despite repeated orders, citing supply and equipment
  shortages; Lincoln (North) relieved him of command on **Nov. 5, 1862.** The chapter's
  McClellan arc ends here. [Wikipedia: Battle of Antietam]
- **The hinge to emancipation (developed in §6).** Antietam was the **battlefield
  "victory" Lincoln had been waiting for** to issue the Emancipation Proclamation
  without it looking like an act of desperation (→ §6). [Wikipedia: Battle of
  Antietam; Wikipedia: Emancipation Proclamation]

## §6 — The war's purpose changes: from restoring the Union to ending slavery

**Job:** the THESIS payoff and the NAME-THE-CAUSE heart of the chapter. Antietam's
strategic check let Lincoln redefine the war. Keep this focused on the **military /
war-aim hinge**; **LINK DOWN to the Emancipation theme** for the document itself, its
limits, and its meaning.

- **The preliminary Proclamation (Sept. 22, 1862).** Five days after Antietam, Lincoln
  (North) issued the **preliminary Emancipation Proclamation**, warning that enslaved
  people in any state still in rebellion on **Jan. 1, 1863**, would be declared free.
  [Wikipedia: Emancipation Proclamation]
- **Why he waited for a victory.** Secretary of State **William Seward (North)** had
  advised Lincoln to **hold the Proclamation until a Union battlefield victory** —
  otherwise it would look like "our last shriek, on the retreat," a desperate measure
  by a losing government. Antietam gave him that victory. This is the precise reason
  the military and the moral threads of 1862 are *one* story: **without the strategic
  check at Antietam, the Proclamation waits.** [Wikipedia: Emancipation Proclamation]
- **The final Proclamation (Jan. 1, 1863).** Lincoln issued the final Emancipation
  Proclamation on Jan. 1, 1863. *(This date falls in the next chapter's window — this
  chapter ends 1862 — so reference it forward lightly and link down; the Emancipation
  theme owns it.)* [Wikipedia: Emancipation Proclamation]
- **What it did, in war terms (the operational angle — keep it brief, the theme owns
  the detail).** It changed the legal status of **~3.5 million enslaved people** in the
  rebelling states; it **applied only to areas in rebellion** (not the loyal border
  states or Union-occupied zones); and crucially for the *military* story, it
  **authorized the enlistment of Black men in the Union Army** — eventually ~200,000.
  [Wikipedia: Emancipation Proclamation]
- **The war-aim shift — state it plainly.** The Proclamation "would redefine the Civil
  War, turning it from a struggle to preserve the Union to one focused on ending
  slavery." [Wikipedia: Emancipation Proclamation] This is the chapter's thesis,
  consummated: a war begun to *restore the Union* now also had emancipation as a
  declared Union war aim, and the Union army became, in fact, an army of liberation as
  it advanced.
- **People:** Lincoln (North), Seward (North).

**LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/emancipation` (the document, its
limits, its reception, the meaning — do not re-cover here).

## §7 — The river/naval war, Fredericksburg, and the machine under strain

**Job:** close the year. Bundle the naval/river breakthroughs (which the East
narrative crowds out but which advanced the Anaconda strategy decisively), the futile
bloodbath at Fredericksburg that closed the year on a note of Northern despair, and
the chapter's system-level coda (the "hard war" turn; medicine and logistics
overwhelmed). **LINK DOWN to four dossiers + two themes.**

### The naval & river war — the Anaconda tightens
- **Ironclads change naval war (Battle of Hampton Roads, March 8–9, 1862).** On
  **March 8**, the Confederate ironclad **CSS *Virginia*** (rebuilt on the burned hull
  of the **USS *Merrimack***) destroyed the wooden Union warships **USS *Cumberland***
  (rammed and sunk) and **USS *Congress*** — proving wooden navies obsolete in an
  afternoon. On **March 9**, the Union ironclad **USS *Monitor*** (designed by **John
  Ericsson**), with its revolving gun turret, fought the *Virginia* to a draw in **the
  first battle between ironclad warships in history.** [Wikipedia: Battle of Hampton
  Roads] **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/ironclads`.
- **The fall of New Orleans (April 1862).** Flag Officer **David G. Farragut (North)**
  ran his fleet **past Forts Jackson & St. Philip in the dark (night of April 24,
  1862)** and took **New Orleans — the Confederacy's largest city and greatest port,
  at the mouth of the Mississippi** — almost without a land fight; **Maj. Gen. Benjamin
  Butler (North)** occupied it (formally May 1, 1862). A massive blow to the
  Confederacy and a major step in seizing the Mississippi. Casualties in the *capture
  itself* were minimal. [Wikipedia: Capture of New Orleans] **LINK DOWN TO:**
  `/war-civil-war/naval/forts-jackson`.
- **Island Number Ten (Feb. 28 – April 8, 1862).** A Confederate river fortress at a
  bend of the Mississippi near the Missouri–Tennessee border blocked the Union advance
  downriver. Maj. Gen. **John Pope (North)** and Flag Officer **Andrew Foote (North)**
  took it by **digging a bypass canal** and **running gunboats past the batteries in a
  thunderstorm** (USS *Carondelet*, April 4); the garrison surrendered (~April 7–8),
  with several thousand captured (sources: "not more than 5,350 present," likely
  **fewer than ~4,500 captured**). It **opened the upper Mississippi to the Union Navy
  as far as Fort Pillow.** [Wikipedia: Battle of Island Number Ten] **LINK DOWN TO:**
  `/war-civil-war/trans-mississippi/island-number-ten`.
- **The strategic thread to draw:** New Orleans (from the south) + Island Number Ten,
  Forts Henry/Donelson (from the north) = **1862 is the year the Mississippi-and-rivers
  half of the Anaconda Plan went from a mocked idea to a closing vise.** By year's end
  the Confederacy held only the middle stretch of its great river. *(Vicksburg, the
  last lock, is 1863 — the next chapter; reference forward lightly.)*

### Fredericksburg — the year ends in futility
- **Fredericksburg (Dec. 11–15, 1862; main assault Dec. 13).** McClellan's replacement,
  **Maj. Gen. Ambrose Burnside (North)**, threw his army in repeated frontal assaults
  **uphill against Lee's (South) and Longstreet's (South) men entrenched behind a stone
  wall along the sunken road at Marye's Heights.** Wave after wave was shot down on the
  open ground; **not a single Union soldier reached the wall.** [Wikipedia: Battle of
  Fredericksburg]
- **Casualties — a lopsided slaughter.** **Union ~12,653 (1,284 killed, 9,600 wounded,
  1,769 missing/captured); Confederate ~5,377 (608 killed, 4,116 wounded, 653
  missing/captured)** — roughly **two-to-one against the attacker**, the most futile
  Union slaughter of the war to date. [Wikipedia: Battle of Fredericksburg]
- **Lee's famous line (verified).** Watching the carnage from his command height, Lee
  (South) said: *"It is well that war is so terrible, or we should grow too fond of
  it."* [Wikipedia: Battle of Fredericksburg]
- **Northern war-weariness.** The defeat **devastated Union morale** and deepened
  war-weariness as the bloody year closed. Pennsylvania Gov. Andrew Curtin (North) to
  Lincoln: *"It was not a battle, it was a butchery."* Lincoln, reportedly: *"If there
  is a worse place than hell, I am in it."* [Wikipedia: Battle of Fredericksburg]
  **[VERIFY both quotes — Curtin "butchery" and Lincoln "worse place than hell" are
  well attested but flag as reported.]** **LINK DOWN TO:**
  `/war-civil-war/eastern/fredericksburg`.

### The machine under strain — the "hard war" begins
- **From conciliation to hard war.** Early Union policy aimed to wage a *limited,
  conciliatory* war — McClellan (North) himself argued it "should not be, at all, a War
  upon the population; but against armed forces." 1862's bloodshed shattered that. By
  late 1862 the war was taking on the character of **"hard war"**: targeting not just
  Confederate armies but the **resources, property, and slave labor** that sustained
  them. Gen. Henry Halleck (North) ordered commanders to "handle that class without
  gloves, and take their property for public use…. It is time that they should begin
  to feel the presence of the war." [padresteve.com, "From Limited War to Total War";
  the John Sherman / Halleck correspondence quoted therein] **[VERIFY the Halleck
  quote's exact wording/date if used verbatim.]**
- **The Second Confiscation Act (July 17, 1862).** Congress passed the **Second
  Confiscation Act**, authorizing seizure of rebel property and **freeing the enslaved
  people of disloyal owners who came under Union control** — a legal milestone on the
  road from "restore the Union" to "destroy slavery," and a marker that the hard-war
  turn and the emancipation turn were the *same* turn. [Wikipedia: Confiscation Act of
  1862; Senate.gov: The Confiscation Acts]
- **Manpower as a system.** Note the pattern the chapter has surfaced: Confederate
  *offensive* victories (Seven Days, Second Bull Run) cost Lee's *smaller* army as many
  or more men than the Union lost — a bleeding the South could less afford. The numbers
  the chapter has been tracking (Shiloh ~23,700; Seven Days ~36,000 combined; Antietam
  ~22,700; Fredericksburg ~18,000) are not box scores — they are a **manpower system
  beginning to crack on the side with fewer men to lose.** *(Author should frame this
  analytically, as a system, per the chapter's brief.)*
- **Medicine and logistics overwhelmed.** The scale of 1862's casualties swamped a
  medical and supply system built for a small peacetime army — Shiloh alone left
  ~17,000 wounded; Antietam ~17,000 more in a single day. **LINK DOWN TO:**
  `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/medicine` (disease, field hospitals, the
  human cost as a system) **and** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/new-way-of-war`
  (rifle + railroad + telegraph: the tools that outran the tactics and made the
  casualties so vast).

# NAME-THE-CAUSE — ship-blocking material (give the author what to say)

**The rule (locked 2026-05-30, `audits/war-content-pipeline.md` + `feedback_name_the_cause`):**
every Civil War section must, in its OWN prose, name **slavery** as the cause the war
was ultimately fought over. The framing gate FAILS the section if "slavery" never
appears. Omission — not euphemism — is the default Lost Cause failure. For a strategic
chapter this is *especially* easy to miss, because the surface subject is plans,
rivers, and casualty math.

**This chapter is actually the strongest possible place to name the cause, because its
own thesis IS the cause.** Give the author these documented anchors:
1. **The war-aim shift is the spine of the chapter (§6).** State plainly: the Union
   began the war to *restore the Union*; over 1862 — and explicitly with the
   Emancipation Proclamation that Antietam unlocked — **ending slavery became a Union
   war aim.** The Proclamation "would redefine the Civil War, turning it from a
   struggle to preserve the Union to one focused on ending slavery." [Wikipedia:
   Emancipation Proclamation]
2. **Name what the Confederacy was fighting FOR, up front (§1/§4).** The Confederacy
   had seceded and built its armies to **preserve and extend slavery** — the same
   plain statement the shipped Corinth and Antietam sections make in their own voice
   (e.g., Corinth: *"The Confederacy existed for one purpose: to preserve and extend
   slavery."*). The strategic plans of 1862 were plans in service of that.
3. **The hard-war turn and the emancipation turn are the same turn (§7).** The Second
   Confiscation Act (freeing the enslaved of disloyal owners, July 1862) and the
   Proclamation show the Union consciously turning the war *against the institution
   that caused it.* [Wikipedia: Confiscation Act of 1862]
4. **Use Grant's own words as the human hinge (§3).** *"I gave up all idea of saving
   the Union except by complete conquest"* — a Union general recognizing in April 1862
   that the slaveholders' rebellion would have to be destroyed, not negotiated with.

**Author instruction:** do not let plans, rivers, and casualty arithmetic crowd the
cause out of the chapter's own voice. One or two clear sentences early (what the two
sides were ultimately fighting over) plus the §6 war-aim payoff satisfies the gate —
and is also just true to the chapter's thesis.

# CONTESTED / [VERIFY] FLAGS

1. **Antietam casualty figure — continuity, not just accuracy.** Standard scholarship
   / the Antietam Wikipedia infobox = **~22,727**. The home-spine hook in
   `civil-war-roster.ts` says **"23,000 fell in twelve hours."** Use **~22,000–23,000**
   and MATCH the number the shipped Antietam dossier uses, so it reads the same across
   the corpus. **[VERIFY against the shipped dossier before drafting.]**

2. **Shiloh "more than all previous American wars" framing.** Defensible claim:
   Shiloh's casualties **EXCEEDED the COMBINED casualties of the Revolution, the War
   of 1812, and the Mexican–American War.** Do NOT overstate as "more *deaths* than all
   prior wars" without that "combined casualties" qualifier. [Battlefields.org; Wikipedia]

3. **Grant's "complete conquest" quote.** *"I gave up all idea of saving the Union
   except by complete conquest"* — from Grant's *Personal Memoirs*. Verify exact wording
   against the *Memoirs* text before quoting verbatim; it's load-bearing for the thesis.

4. **Sherman–Grant "devil's own day" night exchange (Shiloh).** Well attested but
   reconstructed dialogue; flag as "as the story is told" if used.

5. **Lincoln's "borrow the army" quip.** *"If General McClellan does not want to use
   the army, I would like to borrow it for a time"* — attested (often dated Jan. 10,
   1862) but phrasings vary. Verify wording/date before quoting.

6. **Lee's Seven Days self-criticism** *("Our success has not been as great … as we
   should have desired")* — verify exact wording.

7. **Fredericksburg quotes** — Lee's *"It is well that war is so terrible …"* is solid
   [Wikipedia]. Curtin's *"It was not a battle, it was a butchery"* and Lincoln's *"If

8. **"Church bells of Richmond" (Peninsula).** The **~7-mile distance** is solid
