# Wilson's Creek — Lost Cause / Framing Gate

**Gate:** Lost Cause distortion hunt
**Draft:** `audits/war-pipeline/wilsons-creek-draft.md`
**Date:** 2026-05-30

---

## Overall Verdict

**CONDITIONALLY PASS — two MUST-FIX, two SHOULD-FIX.**

The draft is substantially sound. It does not romanticize the secessionist cause, does not use "states' rights" as a euphemism, does not give false both-sides sympathy on the core moral question, and does not sanitize the guerrilla war. The biggest structural problem is a specific one: Missouri is named as a slaveholding state in passing, but the section never connects that fact to *why* the secessionists were fighting — the reader can finish this piece thinking the crisis was a purely political/strategic "which side do you pick" question rather than a struggle over whether a slave state would join a slaveholders' rebellion. That omission must be fixed. A secondary problem is that Sterling Price and the Missouri State Guard get a romanticizing "scrappy underdog" frame without the draft noting who exactly those men were fighting to put in charge of Missouri and why.

## Finding-by-Finding

---

### 1. Missouri's slaveholding character named but its *causal weight* is absent

**DISTORTED — MUST-FIX**

The draft names Missouri as a slaveholding state twice:

> "A border state was a slave state that stayed in the Union — and Missouri was the biggest prize of them all." (§ *The border state*)

> "By the end of the war it would send roughly 40,000 men to the Confederacy and about 60,000 to the Union — a state genuinely split down the middle…" (§ *The border state*)

And in the "Meanwhile" box:

> "a fight over which of these two governments got to keep the state" (§ *Meanwhile in Missouri — A state with two governments forming*)

**The problem:** the draft correctly labels Missouri a slave state, but the word "slaveholding" does no work — it floats as descriptor and then disappears. The section never explains that the Confederate faction (Jackson, Price, the State Guard) was fighting specifically to preserve Missouri as a *slave* state within a *slaveholders'* confederation — that the fork in the road was not merely "Union vs. Confederate" in the abstract but "slavery continues vs. the Union that was beginning to threaten it." The "fight over which government gets to keep the state" framing is geopolitically correct but morally flattened: a reader finishes this piece not knowing that the government Jackson was trying to install was one committed to slavery by constitutional charter.

The Camp Jackson section makes the secessionists look like local power brokers maneuvering over an arsenal, not men who were trying to deliver a slave state to a slaveholders' confederacy. The intro paragraph uses "border state" and immediately pivots to rivers and railroads — framing the crisis as purely a logistics-and-geography story.

**Fix:** Add one to two sentences — in the "border state" section or the "Camp Jackson" section — that explicitly connect Missouri's slave-state status to what Jackson and Price were fighting for: not just "control of Missouri" but control on behalf of a Confederate order built on slavery. Something as simple as: "The secessionists Jackson was serving were the Confederate States of America, whose constitution explicitly protected slavery and whose vice president had just declared it the cornerstone of their new nation — and they wanted Missouri because it was a slave state they believed they could bring in." Without this, the section reduces the Missouri crisis to a pure power struggle with the slavery question named but causally invisible.

### 2. Sterling Price and the Missouri State Guard — scrappy-defender framing without context

**TILTED — MUST-FIX**

> "Price organized his State Guard along the south face of the hill, and the battle became a brutal uphill grind: Missourians climbing into the muzzles of Lyon's line, formations three and four ranks deep firing while prone, kneeling, and standing all at once, the fighting closing to shotgun range and at one point to within twenty feet of the Union cannon." (§ *The hill that earned its name*)

> "Price wanted to march north toward the Missouri River and the heart of the state…" (§ *The victory they couldn't spend*)

> "Meanwhile in the Confederate camp | A two-headed command" — describes Price's men as "Missouri State Guard" and the command friction as "the original sin of the Confederate war west of the Mississippi."

**The problem:** Price and his Missouri State Guard are framed throughout as a military organization with objectives (take Missouri, march north) and grievances (McCulloch wouldn't cooperate). They are not romanticized in purple prose, and the draft doesn't call them heroes — but it also never tells the reader *what* the Missouri State Guard was: a militia raised by a governor who had just defied a democratic state convention vote against secession, reorganized for the express purpose of delivering Missouri to the Confederacy. The men climbing Bloody Hill were fighting to overturn Missouri's democratically expressed choice to stay in the Union — and to hand a slave state to a slave-state confederation. The "scrappy Missourians vs. a bigger Confederate bureaucracy" narrative of the command-friction section inadvertently makes Price sympathetic without supplying the moral context. "The original sin of the Confederate war west of the Mississippi" is used to mean "command disorganization," not "the effort to force a slave state into a slaveholders' rebellion against the will of its own people."

**Fix:** A sentence in the "border state" or "Camp Jackson" section noting that the State Guard was the militia Jackson raised specifically to override the convention vote and bring Missouri into the Confederacy would anchor the rest. The command-friction "Meanwhile" box could also briefly note that what Price was marching north *for* was to flip a state that had already voted to stay — not just a generic "hold Missouri."

### 3. Lyon's aggression at Camp Jackson — handled fairly

**FAIR**

> "The man who refused to let that happen was a short, red-bearded, perpetually furious Regular Army officer named Nathaniel Lyon (North), and the way he refused was to start a war before anyone gave him permission to." (intro)

> "On May 10, 1861, he surrounded Camp Jackson with 6,500 troops and forced its 700-man garrison to surrender without a shot fired. The shooting came afterward, and it came in the streets." (§ *Camp Jackson*)

> "The Camp Jackson Affair did the opposite of what calm men might have wanted: it lit secessionist feeling on fire." (§ *Camp Jackson*)

> "He decided instead to do the most aggressive thing a heavily outnumbered general can do: he would attack the bigger army first…" (§ *End of the tether*)

This is well-handled. Lyon is explicitly framed as provocative ("start a war before anyone gave him permission to"), the riot aftermath is stated plainly (at least 28 civilians dead, close to a hundred wounded), and the draft acknowledges that Camp Jackson energized the secessionist cause rather than quelling it. The draft does not sanitize Lyon by omitting the civilian deaths or framing them as unavoidable. It presents him as both effective and reckless, which is accurate. No false balance is applied — the draft does not equate Lyon's aggression with the secessionist cause itself.

### 4. Guerrilla war aftermath — not sanitized, but slightly thin

**TILTED — SHOULD-FIX**

> "Bushwhackers and jayhawkers — homegrown partisan raiders on both sides — turned Missouri into a landscape of ambushes, burned farms, and neighbor murdering neighbor for years, well into Reconstruction." (§ *Missouri's fate*)

> "…and bred some of the country's most infamous outlaws in its aftermath" (§ *Missouri's fate*)

> "Bands of bushwhackers and jayhawkers rode the back roads settling scores under cover of the war, and the regular armies could never fully stamp them out. The savage, merciless partisan fighting that defined Missouri for the rest of the war — and bred some of the country's most infamous outlaws in its aftermath — began here, in the bloody summer that Wilson's Creek opened." (§ *Meanwhile — The guerrilla war coming*)

**The problem:** "burned farms, and neighbor murdering neighbor" and "savage, merciless" are accurate and not whitewashed. The draft does not romanticize the bushwhackers as colorful frontier fighters. However, the "both sides" phrasing ("bushwhackers and jayhawkers — homegrown partisan raiders on both sides") without any distinction slightly flattens the asymmetry: the bushwhacker-secessionist violence in Missouri was significantly motivated by resistance to emancipation and by Confederate-aligned guerrillas who specifically targeted Black Missourians, Unionist families, and Free State settlers. The jayhawker violence had its own atrocities but different origins. Treating them as mirror images of each other — "both sides raided" — is not false, but it does suppress the slavery-rooted dimension of what the pro-Confederate guerrillas were fighting for. It also doesn't note that this irregular war was one reason Missouri's enslaved people faced ongoing terror well into the war.

**Fix:** One sentence noting that while both sides committed atrocities, the pro-Confederate guerrilla violence was specifically aimed at keeping slavery in place — against Union families, freed people, and abolitionists — would prevent the "both sides did bad things" frame from implying moral equivalence.

### 5. "A state genuinely split down the middle" — potentially flattening

**TILTED — SHOULD-FIX**

> "By the end of the war it would send roughly 40,000 men to the Confederacy and about 60,000 to the Union — a state genuinely split down the middle, which is exactly why both sides fought so hard and so dirty to control it." (§ *The border state*)

**The problem:** The 40k/60k soldier ratio is accurate and appropriately sourced. But describing Missouri as "genuinely split down the middle" without noting *what* the split was about risks the Lost Cause framing that the Missouri Confederate cause was just a reasonable political preference rather than a commitment to keeping slavery. The sentence as written implies a kind of symmetrical disagreement: reasonable Missourians just happened to land on different sides. A reader could take away that this was a conventional political division (like a 50-50 election split) rather than a split in which one side was fighting for a slaveholders' confederation.

This is a should-fix rather than must-fix because the draft does name Missouri as a slave state and does note that the secessionist side wanted to "grab the arsenal" for the South. But the "split down the middle" phrasing is the kind of framing that, without more context, could give the Lost Cause narrative (the war as a conflict between equally legitimate positions) a foothold.

**Fix:** A brief qualifier — e.g., "split down the middle on whether to stay in a Union that was beginning to threaten slavery" or "on whether to take Missouri into the Confederacy and keep slavery safe there" — would give the split its actual shape.

### 6. Casualty-rate framing, Confederate command, and tactical handling

**FAIR**

The casualty figures are presented with appropriate source-range acknowledgment and the focus is on the rate (not a glorification of either side's fighting prowess). The Confederate command failure is explained structurally and there is no romanticization of McCulloch or Price as tragic heroes. The "empty win" section is particularly strong — it correctly frames the Confederate victory as strategically useless, undercuts any "they won the battle" glory, and attributes the failure to command dysfunction.

### 7. Planters' House quote handling

**FAIR**

> "Rather than concede to the state of Missouri the right to dictate to my government in any matter however unimportant, I would see you, and you, and you, and you, and you, and every man, woman, and child in the state dead and buried. This means war." — as remembered by a Confederate aide writing in 1886; the exact wording is disputed, but the ultimatum was real.

The attribution is appropriately hedged. The quote is used to illustrate Lyon's character, not to glorify him. The uncertainty is clearly stated.
