# STORYTELLING CRITIC · Battle of Princeton (rev-princeton)

Draft: `audits/war-pipeline/rev-princeton-draft.md`
Bar: `audits/war-pipeline/fi-carillon-draft.md` (shipped, gated)
Lane: storytelling only. Accuracy/confidence handling is the fact critic's lane; where a fix touches a sourced line I flag the constraint.

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

The voice is at the house bar. "Held together with paper and promises," "What followed at
twilight settled nothing and cost plenty," "Neither commander had expected the other to
exist," "The fox he was going to bag in the morning had spent the morning eating his rear
guard" — these are shipped-quality lines, and the three-section arc the brief asks for
(trap → collision/peak → meaning) is structurally present and lands clean. Section 3 in
particular earns its meaning without a victory lap: "for once the grand Victorian sentence
is roughly fair" and the closing "it never afterward came so close to dying again" are
exactly the right restraint. No meta-narration of the chapter-about-itself kind, no
drama-bold, no em-dashes in our prose. Mawhood and the 17th Foot read as genuinely
impressive, not as scenery.

What keeps it from a clean PASS: the night march — the one passage the carillon
"imagine being one of them" standard was built for — is told in summary register, not
inhabited; and the climax of section 2 is interrupted mid-peak by a parenthetical
sourcing footnote.

---

## BLOCKER

### B1. The night march is reported, not lived. (Section 1, ¶ "The execution was a piece of theater")

The deception half of the paragraph is fully realized (the tended fires, the noisy
digging, the rag-wrapped wheels). Then the march itself — the section's centerpiece, the
held breath the title is writing a check for — gets this:

> "The march was miserable and slow, men stumbling over stumps in the dark, and in one
> account a militia unit briefly panicked at a cry that Hessians had surrounded them.
> But it was never discovered."

That is a summary of a held breath, not a held breath. Compare the carillon bar at the
abatis: "Imagine being one of them. You hit a belt of felled trees too thick to run
through… you cannot go forward at a walk, let alone a charge." The night march is that
passage's natural home in this battle, and the draft walks past it in a sentence and a
half.

**Fix (insert an inhabited beat between "filed off silently to the east" and the
discovery/dawn payoff — every ingredient is already in the draft, no new facts needed):**
roughly — *Imagine being one of the men in that column. No talking, no pipes, no lights;
twelve miles of frozen, stump-strewn track in the dark, the guns rolling beside you on
muffled wheels, strangely quiet on the iron-hard road. Behind you, over your shoulder,
the glow of your own campfires on the horizon, burning for an audience of British
sentries. Every farm dog, every alert vedette, is the end of the army; one cry about
Hessians sends a militia unit briefly clawing into panic in the dark. And the column
walks on, hour after hour, toward the rear of an army that thinks it is asleep.* Then the
existing payoff ("When the sky began to lighten…") lands twice as hard.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. The "Parade with us" caveat interrupts the climax. (Section 2, the rally)

The peak of the whole battle — Washington reined up thirty yards from the British line —
is broken mid-stride by a 27-word parenthetical footnote:

> "He is said to have called, 'Parade with us my brave fellows!…' (the line rests on
> later recollections, not a January 1777 document, but his rallying the line in person
> under close fire is reported by men who stood in it)."

The badge is required; the courtroom brief in the middle of the charge is not. The
Fitzgerald hat anecdote two sentences later shows the draft already knows how to badge
inside the story's voice ("One nineteenth-century anecdote has his aide…" — perfect).
Match that register and move the load-bearing half of the caveat OUT of the peak.

**Fix:** keep "He is said to have called" (that alone is a badge), cut the parenthetical,
and let the verification ride on the beat that follows, e.g. after the smoke clears:
*"The words are a later memory; the ride between the lines is not — men who stood in that
line reported it, and one of them painted it."* That last clause also hands off to Peale,
who is the very next sentence, so the seam disappears. (Fact critic should confirm the
caveat's substance survives; storytelling only needs it out of the quote's lungs.)

### S2. Section 2's opening is a recap plus an org chart before the story restarts. (¶1)

The first paragraph re-runs section 1 (campfires, rags, 12–14 miles) and then delivers
the wing split in a single 70+ word sentence: "…split into two wings: Major General John
Sullivan with the main body angling right… and a smaller wing under Major General
Nathanael Greene, led by Brigadier General Hugh Mercer's brigade with Brigadier General
John Cadwalader's Philadelphia Associators (the city's volunteer militia) behind, peeling
left toward the Post Road (the main Trenton highway) to break the bridge over Stony Brook
and block the road Cornwallis would come back on."

Standalone recap is house-required, but the carillon sections re-establish in two or
three strokes and get moving. Here the reader wades through five officers, two roads, a
bridge, and two parentheticals before the sun comes up.

**Fix:** cut the recap to two sentences (trapped at the creek → marched around him in the
dark, fires still burning behind them), and break the wing sentence in two: Sullivan and
the main body right toward the back of town; THEN a fresh sentence for Greene's wing and
its errand at the Stony Brook bridge. Mercer-leading-with-Cadwalader-behind can wait two
paragraphs, where they actually act — the collision ("each column spotted the other across
the fields") should arrive a beat sooner.

### S3. Section 1's pull quote is the memo, not the moment.

> QUOTE: "would avoid the appearance of a retreat … whilst we might by a fortunate stroke
> withdraw Genl Howe from Trenton and give some reputation to our Arms"

It is characterful (a gambler's sentence), but as the section's single elevated quote it
is administrative next to the soldier's line already sitting in the prose: the bridge
"looked red as blood, with their killed and wounded." Carillon's pull quotes are the
emotional registers of their sections ("the soul of General Abercromby's army seemed to
expire"). Red-as-blood is this section's.

**Fix (preferred):** elevate "looked red as blood, with their killed and wounded" to the
QUOTE block at the bridge-fight beat; fold Washington's rationale into prose at the
council ("Washington explained it to Hancock three days later: the move would 'avoid the
appearance of a retreat'…"). If the fact critic objects to elevating a MED participant
recollection to a pull quote, the fallback is to keep the current QUOTE but trim the
lead-in so the council paragraph ends ON it harder.

## TRIM

### T1. Bag-the-fox carries a double caveat. (Section 1)
> "No document from January 1777 records either line; the exchange first appears in
> accounts written generations later, and it should be enjoyed as the legend it is."
Two sourcing clauses where one does the work, and the second half already lands the
house move. Trim to one: *"No January 1777 document records either line — it should be
enjoyed as the legend it is."* (Rendered without the dash, per house: "No January 1777
document records either line; enjoy it as the legend it is.") The tradition still gets
its badge, and the paragraph's strong pivot ("What is certain is the decision itself.")
arrives a breath sooner.

### T2. Mistaken-for-Washington ends on a deflation. (Section 2, Mercer's fall)
> "some traditions hold that they took him for Washington himself, though that detail
> cannot be proven"
"Some traditions hold" IS the badge; "though that detail cannot be proven" is the
fact-check aside the brief says these beats must not be. Cut the trailing clause. The
sentence ends stronger on "Washington himself."

### T3. Section 3's arithmetic paragraph runs long. (¶2)
The whose-count-do-you-trust framing earns the dispute, and the Potter fog-of-war beat is
genuine storytelling — keep both. But Howe's return, Washington's claim, AND Stryker AND
the modern range is one ledger entry too many for prose (the dossier already carries the
full spread). Cut the Stryker sentence; "Howe admitted X, Washington claimed Y, and the
firmest number is the prisoners" is the story-shaped version of the dispute.

## NIT

### N1. "This is the moment the battle, and arguably the winter, turned." (Section 2)
The signpost is fine; "arguably" hedges the drum-hit. Either commit ("This is the moment
the winter turned.") or cut the clause and let Washington's ride make the claim.

### N2. "Set the two ends of the ten days side by side." (Section 3, closing ¶)
A mild instruction to the reader — the only sentence in the draft that stage-manages
instead of telling. The contrast paragraph that follows is excellent and needs no usher;
open it directly: "On December 24, the Continental Army was a dissolving force…"

### N3. The "enjoy" badge-verb repeats. (Sections 1 and 2)
"it should be enjoyed as the legend it is" (bag the fox) and "so enjoy the line, and hold
it loosely" (fox chase) are the same move twice. Both are good; vary one ("hold it
loosely" alone carries the fox chase, and is the better phrase).

### N4. "rode out in front of the wavering militia… reined up within about thirty yards" —
the rally sentence stacks three motions plus a unit list (Hitchcock, Hand) before the
thirty yards. Consider letting the reinforcements arrive in their own short sentence so
"reined up within thirty yards of the British line" stands nearer the end.

## WHAT'S WORKING (do not sand these down in revision)

- **Both-sides soldiering (criterion 6): fully met.** "Mawhood could have run for it.
  Instead he turned his column around and went straight at the strangers on his flank" —
  and the breakout: "even then Mawhood did not surrender it. He cut his way out down the
  Post Road… a performance admired on both sides of the field," sealed with Howe's and
  Washington's own words. The 17th Foot is an opponent, never an obstacle.
- **Tradition badges as pleasure beats (criterion 4): mostly exemplary.** The Fitzgerald
  hat, the fox-chase "enjoy the line, and hold it loosely," the 1905 cannonball legend
  resolving into "the college got its revenge with style" and the frame story — these are
  the badge-as-texture standard. Only T1/T2 above slip into deflation.
- **MEANWHILE placement (criterion 5):** Section 2's "Cornwallis wakes" is the best block
  in the draft — a true meanwhile, at the peak's far side, closing on the fox callback.
  S1 and S3 follow the carillon reflective-coda pattern correctly.
- **PILLs:** `/war-revolution/the-north` after the backstory ¶ and `/war-revolution/two-armies`
  at the forward-looking close are both where a reader would actually want them; correctly
  no pill on a non-live Trenton.
- **Pull-quote placement in S2 and S3:** Peale's "thousand different notes" at the volley
  and Cresswell's title-payoff "all liberty mad again" are exactly where the peaks are.
- **Section 3's restraint:** the lopsided-but-disputed bill, the shared grave, Leslie's
  burial, and a Trevelyan kept at arm's length — the ten days mean a great deal here
  without the prose ever cheering.
