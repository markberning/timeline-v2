# STORYTELLING CRITIC · Battle of Bennington (rev-bennington)

*Critic pass 2026-06-11. Draft: `rev-bennington-draft.md`. Bar: `fi-carillon-draft.md` (shipped, gated).
Storytelling lane only; accuracy is the fact-checker's lane.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is a strong draft with a real spine: the supply arithmetic reads as doom in slow
motion, Stark arrives as a character and not a name, both legend badges are pleasure
beats, and the aftermath lands its numbers without a victory lap. The voice
ship-blockers are clean (no em-dashes in our prose, no drama-bold, no chapter-about-itself
narration in the main prose). What keeps it from PASS outright: the second battle, which
the section's own title and blurb promise as a co-equal fight, is delivered as one
summary paragraph, and the climax never slows into a carillon-grade you-are-there beat.
Both are revision-sized, not rewrite-sized.

---

## BLOCKER

### B1. The Breymann fight arrives as an epilogue, not a second wave.
The section is titled for "The two fights" and the blurb promises "the battle had to be
won all over again before dark." The prose then settles the entire second battle in one
~190-word paragraph, while the first fight gets four paragraphs of patient build:

> "By about 4:30 the battle was won, and then it almost came undone. Stark's militia had
> scattered, guarding more than 600 prisoners and happily plundering the captured camp,
> when Breymann's relief column finally arrived from the west and marched straight into
> them."

The pivot sentence is excellent. But everything after it runs at summary speed: arrival,
reverse, jeopardy, Warner, the sunset quote, the escape, and the haul, all in one breath.
The jeopardy in particular is asserted rather than dramatized: "for a stretch of that
late afternoon the day hung in the balance" tells us the stakes the way a caption would.
And Breymann, who got a whole planted setup in the previous section (the notorious march,
the bogging ammunition carts), gets one clause of interiority on arrival ("wounded
himself"). Baum's half of the both-sides bargain is honored beautifully; Breymann's is
not.

**Fix (concrete):** break the paragraph into two or three, and spend the first on what
Breymann's men walked into. They marched 25 miles in a day and a half through mud, and
they arrived at a battlefield that had already been lost: the redoubt stormed, the road
full of prisoners, the camp being plundered. Let the reader stand on that road for two
or three sentences before the firing starts. Something like:

> "Breymann's grenadiers had been a day and a half in the mud, dragging their cannon out
> of one washout after another, and they arrived at about 4:30 to find the battle they
> had been marching to was already over. The hilltop was taken. The road ahead of them
> was full of American militia, scattered, loaded with plunder, herding 600 prisoners.
> Breymann did the only thing a column of regulars could do: he formed up and attacked
> into the wreckage, and for an hour it worked."

Then give Warner's arrival and counterattack its own paragraph (the muskets-drying
detail, the obstinate-till-sunset quote, dark, the abandoned cannon and wounded), and
keep the haul sentence as the closer. The material is all in the draft and the fact
ledger already; this is re-pacing, not new claims.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. No carillon-grade "imagine being one of them" passage at the climax.
The bar (carillon, the abatis): "Imagine being one of them. You hit a belt of felled
trees too thick to run through... close enough that you can hear the order to fire
before each one lands." Bennington's natural home for that beat is the dragoons' last
half hour on the hilltop, and the draft gets there in three very good but fast sentences:

> "Out of cartridges, surrounded on a hilltop in a country none of them had chosen, the
> heavy cavalrymen of the regiment Prinz Ludwig drew the long straight broadswords they
> had carried on foot from Canada and tried to cut their way out down the slope. The
> charge was shot apart at close range."

Every prop is already staged earlier in the page (the riding boots, the broadswords
carried from Canada, no English, the burning ammunition wagon, fire from every
direction at once). Slow down for 3–5 sentences of close-up before the charge:

> "Imagine the hilltop in that last half hour. The firing has come from every direction
> at once for two hours, from woods you were told were full of friends. Your cartridges
> are gone; the wagon holding the reserve has gone up in a column of smoke behind you.
> The orders, when they come, come in German, on a hill in New York. What was left was
> the weapon they had carried on foot all the way from Canada, and they drew it."

(Second-person is house-sanctioned by the carillon passage; if the reviser prefers
third person close, fine, but pick the hilltop. The militia side already has its texture
beat in "farmers stripped down for an afternoon's work" and doesn't need a second one.)

### S2. Section 2 opens with a recap ledger and stalls its own storm.
The first sentence is a keeper: "On August 15, 1777, it rained all day on two armies
that could not see each other." Then the paragraph re-litigates section 1 at census
detail: Baum re-introduced with his full intelligence backstory, the depot's mileage
re-given, Stark's force re-counted ("between 2,000 and 2,500 men across the day").
Re-grounding a deep-linkable section is right; doing it in ranges and clauses for
seven lines is not, and the paragraph's killer closer ("The rain kept everyone's
gunpowder wet and everyone's plans on hold") arrives late. **Fix:** compress the
re-ground to two sentences (who is on the hill, who is ten miles away, that Baum now
knows the depot is not weak) and keep both bookend sentences exactly as written.

### S3. Forward/meta narration in the Section 1 MEANWHILE.
> "They were within marching reach of the depot Baum was aiming at, and they were about
> to make the second half of this battle."

"About to make the second half of this battle" is the narrator describing the page's
plot structure, and it spoils the second fight's arrival as a surprise (which B1 is
trying to make land harder). **Fix:** end on the planted gun, not the spoiler: "They
were within marching reach of the depot Baum was aiming at." Lighter case of the same
instinct, optional trim: "Both were wrong, and the second one was wrong in a way that
would shape the battle itself" → "Both were wrong." (The follow-through in section 2
makes the point without the trailer.)

### S4. The Section 3 MEANWHILE retells the section's own prose.
The settled-in-America beat lands twice within four paragraphs, and the prose version
is the better telling:

> prose: "Men who had been marched across an ocean to fight farmers ended, in some
> numbers, as the neighbors of the men who had captured them."
> MEANWHILE: "a substantial number of them, in the end, made America their home instead."

A MEANWHILE should look sideways, not summarize the page it sits on. **Fix:** one home
per beat. Either re-aim the MEANWHILE (e.g., the regiment's story told from Brunswick's
end of the ocean: a cavalry regiment that sailed, never mounted, and never came back)
and keep the prose line, or move the whole settled-as-neighbors payoff into the
MEANWHILE and let the prose paragraph end on the march through Bennington.

## TRIM

### T1. The column-composition roster (section 1).
> "somewhere around 170 to 205 dismounted Brunswick dragoons... about 50 German light
> infantry and a handful of jägers...; some 48 British marksmen...; 150 or more
> Loyalists...; 50 or 60 Canadians; and about 100 Native warriors."

Six hedged ranges in one sentence chain. The dossier sides-note already carries the
roster; the story here is "one of the strangest forces of the war" and "half the column
did not speak English." Keep the dragoons' number (they're the protagonists of the
shortage), round the rest into prose ("a few hundred Loyalists, Canadians, and Native
warriors; a sprinkling of British marksmen and German gunners with two light cannon"),
and let "Orders moved through translation" stay as the punch.

### T2. Doubled pointer above the Bunker Hill PILL.
"(his fuller story lives on that page)" in the section prose sits directly above the
PILL that does that job. Cut the parenthetical from the prose (keep the dossier bio's
version, which has no pill adjacent). The pill then reads as the old-friend handshake
it's meant to be.

### T3. Pre-selling the thunder quote.
"Stark reported it to General Gates six days later in one of the great sentences of the
war." The carillon lead-ins are flat ("remembered it plainly") and the quotes do their
own selling. End at "six days later." (Do NOT apply the same trim to the Molly
paragraph's "one of the most famous things any American said in the whole war" — there
the hype is load-bearing; the joke turns on maximum fame vs. zero documentation.)

## NIT

- **N1. Hedge density.** 7 "roughly" + 23 "about" across three sections. Most are
  obligatory number hedges, but they cluster (S2's opening paragraph, T1's roster);
  the S2/T1 fixes will thin them. Vary the survivors per house rule.
- **N2. "And one last thing:"** (section 3) is table-of-contents voice. The bit lands
  fine opening cold on "The Battle of Bennington was not fought at Bennington, and not
  in Vermont." Judgment call; keep "Nobody tell them." — that closer is the house voice
  earning its keep, and this critic endorses it explicitly so nobody sands it off.
- **N3. Molly MEANWHILE placement.** As a section coda it works (battle, then human
  grace note). Adjacent to the legend paragraph, "the record makes her something better"
  would be a direct riposte. Either is defensible; flagging only so the choice is made
  on purpose.

## WHAT SINGS (do not break in revision)

- "It could fight anything in front of it and was slowly being defeated by its own
  wagon train." — the whole section-1 thesis in one line.
- "Baum could count." — best three words in the draft.
- The paper-in-hat badge as story: "...Baum left no record of any of it, because in two
  days he would be dead." The sourcing caveat is itself a gut-punch. This is exactly how
  an unrecoverable legend should be told.
- The Molly legend telling: "which is awkward, because nobody knows what he actually
  said" / "It is a legend, and a good one." — and the MEANWHILE payoff, "the legend made
  her a widow-in-waiting...; the record makes her something better." Pleasure beats,
  both. PASS on criterion 4.
- "What they did next belongs to them." — interpretive, not meta; it buys the last
  stand its dignity. Keep.
- Baum's both-sides arc lands: set up ("he never got to say"), paid off (the unmarked
  grave). PASS on the Baum half of criterion 6; the Breymann half is B1.
- "The storm broke on schedule." — perfect quote-to-consequence hinge.
- No victory-lap inflation: "Bennington did not cause that by itself, but it shortened
  the road" is exactly the right calibration. PASS on criterion 1's section-3 ask.
- Pull-quote placement: thunder quote at the attack's opening and gathering-storm quote
  at the section peak (and titling the section) are both right. The section-1 Burgoyne
  supply quote is administrative but on-theme; fine.
