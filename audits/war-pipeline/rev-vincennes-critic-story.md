## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

The march section earns its spine billing. The cold, hunger, and drowned lands arrive with
sensory precision — Bowman's terse quotes are placed perfectly ("No provisions yet. Lord help
us!" lands harder because the prose around it is just as terse), the physical stakes are
concrete (five-mile flood, water to the chest, ice at the edges, the weak carried in canoes),
and the decision to keep the Memoir theatrics as attributed Clark-telling rather than asserted
fact is disciplined. The executions are told with exactly the right weight: the pack's precision
(who the victims were, two audiences, Hamilton as labeled hostile witness, the unresolved
personal-participation question), placed in the story's middle rather than shunted to a
footnote. The ending closes on the Downes formulation and the "march made the legend /
negotiators made the map" beat, which is clean and strong.

What keeps it from a clean PASS: Clark as a character holds his audacity but loses his
ruthlessness the moment the page needs to show they are the same man. The executions are
documented and framed correctly, but the transit from Clark-who-waded-the-floods to
Clark-who-ordered-the-tomahawking is never dramatized as a single personality. Hamilton gets
his own logic and ordeal in the dossier but is largely absent from the narrative's interior —
the reader never feels the February decisions from his angle. Section 3 opens on Bowman's
death rather than on the irony its structure is clearly reaching for (the man the celebration
killed), and the Hair-Buyer payoff — the page's best complexity — is slightly buried under
the sequencing of the Virginia-irons material. These are fixable. The architecture is sound.

## BLOCKERS

### B1. Clark's audacity and ruthlessness are not shown as the same man

The brief demands that the bluff and the executions read as the same Clark — one personality
capable of both the theatrical counter-march and the staged killing. The draft documents both
events accurately and in close proximity, but never makes the psychological continuity visible.
The closest it comes is this transition:

> "And then, around midday, came the act that gave the bluff its teeth, and it is the part of
> this story that does not get softened."

The phrase "the act that gave the bluff its teeth" is doing the structural work, but "came the
act" distances the reader from the agent. Clark has just been marching flags behind a hill for
theatrical effect; now he is ordering men to kneel before a gate. Those are not separate
chapters in his career — they are the same operational logic applied at different scales (both
are about what the watching audience will believe). The prose announces the pivot but does not
dramatize it.

The executions section then renders Clark mostly through his stated rationale and Hamilton's
hostile account. Both are correctly sourced and placed. But between the flag-parade and the
tomahawk there is no sentence that lets us feel Clark's mind on the two acts as continuous.
He is audacious in paragraph one and ruthless in paragraph two, and the sentence connecting
them is structural narration ("the act that gave the bluff its teeth") rather than character.

**Fix:** In the paragraph opening "And then, around midday," add one sentence before the war
party walks into town that makes Clark's simultaneity explicit as a man rather than as a plot
function. Not an invented motive — the documented rationale ("a fair opportunity of making an
impression on the Indians") can arrive earlier, as the framing lens for the decision, not as
post-hoc explanation after the description. Something that reads: he had already shown the
watching town an army that wasn't there; now he would show the watching nations something the
fort's guns could not answer. The logic is the same logic. Let the reader see it as such before
the killing begins.

### B2. Hamilton's interior is in the dossier but missing from the narrative — his ordeal is
       told from the outside

The dossier bio is excellent: Hamilton as candid journalist, amateur artist, capable
administrator handed a brutal policy by London, whose February choices "handed Clark the
opening." His journal is used well as a sourced hostile witness. But the narrative never lets
the reader inside his position. For a story that hinges on his decision to winter with a
reduced garrison and trust the habitants' re-sworn oath, that decision is rendered as error
from the American perspective, never as logic from his own.

The closest the prose gets is:

> "He assembled the garrison: the regulars stood firm, but the Detroit French militia, in his
> own account, 'hung their heads,' and their sergeants muttered that it was hard they should
> fight against their own friends and relations in the town outside. Hamilton privately resolved
> to get honorable terms if he could."

This is Hamilton's ordeal told correctly, but from outside. The scene of the garrison assembly
— which is the moment Hamilton's entire February calculus collapses in front of him — arrives
as summary rather than as the felt moment the journal actually records. He counted only 35 or
36 men as reliably loyal; he already knew this; the wall was unfinished; the French-Canadian
militia would not fight. His "privately resolved to get honorable terms" is not resignation —
it is the moment a capable officer faces a situation where his arithmetic simply does not work,
and the page should let that land.

**Fix:** Expand the garrison-assembly sequence by two sentences — not invented dialogue, but
the felt arithmetic Hamilton's journal supplies. Something that conveys: the regulars were
seven, and wounded; the militia were unreliable before the firing started, and now the
fighting had confirmed what he already suspected. The "privately resolved" line can stay, but
it needs the failure of the arithmetic visible before it, not assumed. This also gives
Hamilton's surrender its dignity — not capitulation from weakness of character but from a
competent officer's honest accounting of what the numbers said.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. The section 3 opening misses its best hook — Bowman's death is buried inside sequencing

Section 3 opens:

> "The first man the victory killed was on the American side, and it killed him by accident."

This is a strong opening line. But it immediately retreats into tidy explanation:

> "During the 13-gun salute on February 25, about 26 captured 6-pounder cartridges exploded
> in the battery, terribly burning Maj. Joseph Bowman (American), Capt. Worthington, and four
> privates. Bowman, who had kept the journal that carried the whole march, died of the burns
> on August 14, 1779: the campaign's only American officer death, and he was killed by the
> celebration, not the fight. Clark's reports do not mention the accident."

The last sentence — "Clark's reports do not mention the accident" — is the knife in the story,
and it is thrown away as a kicker after a logistics paragraph. Bowman wrote the march. Clark's
triumph is inseparable from Bowman's record. Clark never mentioned the man who died for the
celebration. That irony is the thematic hinge of everything that follows (the legend growing
at the expense of the actual record), and it belongs in a position of emphasis, not at the end
of a catalog of burns.

**Fix:** Restructure the opening sequence so the Bowman beat lands in three beats, not one
continuous paragraph: (1) the strong opening line stands; (2) the explosion and Bowman's death
are compressed to one sentence; (3) "Clark's reports do not mention the accident" is its own
sentence, isolated — possibly as a one-sentence paragraph — so its weight registers before the
narrative moves to the Willing and the convoy capture.

### S2. The Hair-Buyer payoff arrives behind the irons sequence — the complexity is present
       but the irony lands late

The Hair-Buyer section is the page's best complexity: the epithet is propaganda doing real
work, and both the raids-organized claim and the per-scalp-payment refutation are documented
and placed correctly. But the sequencing buries the edge. The section runs: Hamilton to
Virginia (irons, jail, parole, exchange, death in office) → then the Hair-Buyer payoff
(what the record does and doesn't support, the Pennsylvania bounty, the dropped charge).

The problem is that the dropped charge — Jefferson's government jailed Hamilton for an
accusation it eventually could not prove and quietly abandoned — is the irony that reframes
everything before it. But it arrives after the narrative has already settled Hamilton's story
(paroled, exchanged, died in office). The Pennsylvania bounty comparison, which is the hardest
edge in the passage, is also the last item, and by the time it arrives the reader has been
through a lot of administrative history.

**Fix:** Move the Hair-Buyer analysis to immediately follow Hamilton's sentencing to irons,
before the parole and exchange sequence. The structure should be: Jefferson jails him as a
war criminal → here is what that accusation actually rests on (raids organized, yes; per-scalp
payment, no; Pennsylvania paid it in writing) → and the government that jailed him eventually
dropped the charge → he was exchanged, became a colonial governor, died in office. The
Pennsylvania bounty, placed right after the irons, makes the jailing's irony immediate rather
than retrospective.

---

### S3. The pacing of the three sections — section 2 is excellent; sections 1 and 3 have one
       structural looseness each

**Section 1:** The MEANWHILE ("The twice-switched town") is doing good contextual work, but it
arrives after the section's natural emotional peak (Clark's "I know the case is desperate"
quote) and before the cliffhanger of the march beginning. The quote and the MEANWHILE are
both strong, but the section ends on the MEANWHILE rather than on the moment Clark commits.
The reader's lean-forward energy is the march; the MEANWHILE is explanation. The section
would pace better with the MEANWHILE placed just before the Vigo intelligence beat (where it
provides the four-oath context the reader needs to understand why Gibault and the habitants
matter) rather than after the letter.

**Section 3:** The Willing's arrival and the convoy capture are accurate and the right material,
but they arrive between Bowman's death and the Hamilton irons narrative, which slightly breaks
the tone. The convoy capture is mop-up; the Hamilton material is the page's main complexity.
One sentence of transition to mark the register shift ("Before the reckoning, there was one
more prize") would ease the reader across.

## TRIM

### T1. The parenthetical on Memoir color — well handled, but compressible

The prose handles the Memoir-only color exactly right:

> "(Clark told some of this more cinematically a decade later, in a memoir written partly to
> salvage his reputation: blackening his own face with wet powder and raising a war whoop to
> drive the column into the last flood, the drummer boy floating across on his drum, an order
> to land the tallest men first. None of it is in Bowman's journal or in the letter Clark wrote
> months after the campaign, so it travels here only as Clark's later telling. The eighteen
> days in the flood need no embroidery; Bowman's terse journal carries the whole weight of them.)"

This is correct and the provenance handling is disciplined. The last sentence — "The eighteen
days in the flood need no embroidery; Bowman's terse journal carries the whole weight of them"
— is the best line in the parenthetical and the only one worth keeping. The middle sentence
("None of it is in Bowman's journal or in the letter Clark wrote months after the campaign")
is the structural reason the color is tagged, but it is interior scaffolding the reader does
not need to see. Compress to: "(Clark told some of this more cinematically a decade later, in
a memoir written partly to salvage his reputation — the drummer boy on his drum, the blackened
face and the war whoop. The eighteen days in the flood need no embroidery; Bowman's terse
journal carries the whole weight of them.)"

## NIT

### N1. "came the act that gave the bluff its teeth"

The word "act" is weak for the page's hardest moment. "Act" reads like a stage direction. The
fix from B1 will rewrite this sentence, but if the sentence remains in any form, "the thing
that gave the bluff its teeth" or "the killing that gave the bluff its teeth" is more direct.

### N2. "This was the thing Hamilton had thought impossible"

> "This was the thing Hamilton had thought impossible. He knew the floods; he had judged that
> no one could cross the drowned lands in February, and he was very nearly right."

"This was the thing Hamilton had thought impossible" is meta-narration pointing at significance
rather than trusting the reader to feel it. The next two sentences carry the real point
("he knew the floods / very nearly right"). Cut the first sentence; open with "Hamilton knew
the floods."

### N3. The MEANWHILE box in section 2 — "The riflemen and the gunports" — is competent
       but the payoff line is flat

> "A siege the textbooks would have called impossible was decided by marksmanship and an
> enemy's unfinished wall."

"The textbooks would have called impossible" is a rhetorical hedged construction — it
distances the verdict by invoking hypothetical textbooks. The marksmanship and the carpentry
are concrete; let them be the sentence. "The fort had field guns and the Americans had none.
By morning the guns were silent and the Americans had lost one man. Marksmanship and an
enemy's unfinished wall." Three sentences. No textbooks needed.

### N4. "It is worth seeing the 1936 building for what it is"

> "It is worth seeing the 1936 building for what it is: high-imperial commemoration of exactly
> the conquest narrative the record will not quite support."

"It is worth seeing X for what it is" is a mild meta-narrator construction — the prose is
instructing the reader how to feel rather than trusting the image. The observation about the
building's commemorative function and the contested claim is already in the sentence; cut the
preamble. "The 1936 building is high-imperial commemoration of exactly the conquest narrative
the record will not quite support" — or lead with the granite and marble (as the previous
sentence does) and let the next sentence land the irony directly.

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES (for the record)

- **Voice ship-blockers: clean.** No em-dashes in original prose. No drama-bold. No
  meta-narrator constructions except the two Nits flagged above. Parentheses for every inline
  definition (inhabitants, pirogue, 3- and 6-pounders, honors of war). Miles-first throughout.
- **Memoir blacklist: respected.** Drummer boy, blackened face, war whoop arrive only as
  Clark's later telling; the "170 looked like 1,000" hard number kept soft ("several times the
  real number"); Helm's cannon-and-whiskey surrender badged as tradition; "Pontiac's son" not
  used; per-scalp bounty not asserted; "won the Northwest" handled as legend-vs-record split.
- **The executions: not sanitized, not melodramatized.** The pack's precision is all there —
  who the victims were (Lakes warriors, not Wabash neighbors), why Clark did it in his own
  words, Hamilton as labeled hostile witness, Macutté Mong named, the unresolved personal-
  participation question held open with both accusers' hostility named. It is in the middle
  of the story, not a footnote. This is the draft's single strongest discipline.
- **The "won the Northwest" overclaim: never asserted.** The dossier outcome, section 3, and
  the Downes quote all hold the real-vs-claimed split explicitly. The Downes closer ("The
  march made the legend. The negotiators made the map.") is the right strong close rather than
  a deflation — it lands as a verdict, not an apology.
- **Habitants and Native nations: legible as story.** The four-oath MEANWHILE is the right
  structural choice (survival politics, not fickleness). The execution staging logic — Lakes
  men, not Wabash neighbors; two audiences — is explicit. Egushawa's survival and the
  worsening frontier war are present and honest.
- **Pacing within section 2: excellent.** The march chronology, the bluff, the riflemen, the
  executions, the surrender are sequenced without compression or padding. The side-by-side
  of Clark's stated rationale and Hamilton's hostile account for the executions, with the
  personal-participation question held open, is structurally the right choice.
- **Dossier surfaces: strong.** The commanders' bios hold the complexity the narrative cannot
  always carry (Clark's fall, Hamilton's artwork, the debt paid to Vigo's heirs 40 years late).
  The three-stream casualty breakdown is correct and the Wikipedia bundling warning is noted.
