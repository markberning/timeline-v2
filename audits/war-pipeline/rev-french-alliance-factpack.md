# FACT PACK — "The French Alliance & the Global War" (1778–1783)

*American Revolution vertical, war-years Off-the-Battlefield theme chapter (route slug `french-alliance`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against the cited sources; confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges and "flag" guidance for contested specifics. SCOPE FENCE: this is the ALLIANCE story — diplomacy, money, armies. The sea war's mechanics (privateers, John Paul Jones, fleet actions as naval history) belong to the sibling "War at Sea" chapter; the Battle of the Chesapeake gets ONE beat here as the alliance's payoff. The peace negotiations belong to the Treaty of Paris chapter — fuse out at Yorktown with the war continuing elsewhere.*

**Orientation.** France lost the Seven Years' War (the French & Indian War in America — this vertical has a full F&I war; reference tersely) and the 1763 peace stripped her of North America. From that humiliation forward, French foreign ministers — Choiseul first, then Vergennes — rebuilt the navy and watched Britain's colonies for the crack that would let France even the score. The American rebellion was that crack. From 1776 France secretly bankrolled the Revolution through a fake trading house run by a playwright; from 1778, after Saratoga proved the Americans could win, she allied openly — an absolute monarchy spending itself into ruin to midwife a republic. The alliance widened the war (Spain 1779, the Dutch 1780, an armed-neutrality league of the rest) until Britain stood alone against four navies with no continental ally — the exact inversion of the Seven Years' War. The alliance's middle years were rocky (d'Estaing's failures at Newport and Savannah nearly soured it), but in 1781 every part finally meshed: Rochambeau's army, de Grasse's fleet, Spanish silver raised in Havana, and Washington's Continentals converged on Yorktown. France got her revenge and a crushing debt that helped bring on 1789; America got independence it almost certainly could not have won alone.

## 1. WHY FRANCE CARED (1763–1775)

- **The 1763 verdict.** The Peace of Paris (10 February 1763) ended the Seven Years' War with France expelled from mainland North America (Canada to Britain, Louisiana to Spain) and her navy shattered: at the start of 1763 France had ~**47 ships of the line against Britain's ~111**. (HIGH — terse cross-ref to the F&I vertical; do not re-narrate.)
- **Choiseul's project.** The duc de **Choiseul** (foreign/war/navy minister, 1758–1770) reorganized the army and navy with a future war of revenge in view; the navy's strength roughly **doubled in a decade**. Louis XV's government set a target of ~**80 French ships of the line**, with Spain (the Bourbon "Family Compact" ally) expected to add ~60. (HIGH on direction; MED on the 80/60 targets — single-source figures, phrase softly.)
- **Choiseul saw the colonies coming.** He was among the first to argue that expelling France from America would free Britain's colonists to revolt — and from the mid-1760s he sent **secret observers to America, including Johann de Kalb** (1768), to gauge colonial discontent. (HIGH)
- **Vergennes = continuity.** **Charles Gravier, comte de Vergennes** (b. 1719; foreign minister 1774–his death in Feb 1787) inherited and executed Choiseul's logic: American independence would amputate Britain's empire and restore the European balance of power in France's favor. This was **balance-of-power calculation, not love of liberty**. (HIGH)
- **Vergennes to Louis XVI** (memorandum urging intervention): *"England is the natural enemy of France… All means to reduce the power and greatness of England… are just, legitimate and even necessary."* (MED — a standard published translation; the gist is solidly documented but the exact English wording varies by translation. Quote with attribution "Vergennes advised the king," not as a stone-carved epigraph.)
- **THE IRONY — state it plainly.** France was an **absolute monarchy** spending borrowed money to install a **republic** born of an anti-tax, anti-crown revolt. Vergennes knew the risk and ran it anyway because hurting Britain mattered more. (HIGH as framing; finance minister **Turgot** opposed intervention in 1776, warning France could not afford it — he lost the argument and office. HIGH.)

## 2. SECRET AID (1776–1777): THE PLAYWRIGHT'S COMPANY

- **Pierre-Augustin Caron de Beaumarchais** — playwright (*The Barber of Seville*, 1775; *The Marriage of Figaro* later, 1784), watchmaker's son, royal fixer/secret agent — became the conduit for covert French aid. His role is **real and central, not legend**: he lobbied Vergennes hard for intervention and then ran the supply operation. (HIGH)
- **Roderigue Hortalez & Co.** — a **fictitious Spanish-named trading house** set up in 1776 as the cutout. Seed money: **1 million livres from the French treasury (received 10 June 1776)**, **1 million from Spain**, plus roughly another million raised from private investors. (HIGH)
- **Scale.** By late 1776 Beaumarchais had assembled on the order of **200 field guns, tens of thousands of muskets (commonly cited ~30,000; one popular figure of "300,000" is an error), ~100 tons of gunpowder, thousands of tents, and clothing/equipment**, shipped through French ports and the West Indies. Historians credit Hortalez cargoes with **roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the gunpowder the Americans fired in the Saratoga campaign**. (MED on exact inventory counts — phrase as "shiploads of muskets, cannon, powder, tents and uniforms"; HIGH that the Saratoga-era army was largely armed from these cargoes.)
- **The 90% gunpowder figure — basis verified.** The oft-cited statistic traces to **Orlando W. Stephenson, "The Supply of Gunpowder in 1776," *American Historical Review* 30 (1925)**: roughly **90% of the gunpowder available to the Americans before 1778 came from overseas** (imported powder plus powder milled from imported saltpeter), overwhelmingly via France and her colonies; domestic production was ~a tenth. Safe phrasing: "about nine-tenths of American gunpowder in the war's first years came from abroad, most of it French." (HIGH on the figure's existence and basis; it counts imports *including* saltpeter-derived powder — don't sharpen it beyond that.)
- **Gift or loan? — the Beaumarchais tangle.** Whether Hortalez supplies were a royal gift or a commercial debt was disputed from the start (Arthur Lee insisted gift; Beaumarchais billed Congress). Beaumarchais was never fully paid in his lifetime (d. 1799); the U.S. settled with his heirs decades later for a fraction. (MED — fine as one wry beat; don't litigate the accounting.)
- **Silas Deane** — Connecticut merchant-congressman sent to Paris in 1776 as Congress's first agent; worked the Hortalez deals with Beaumarchais. He also signed contracts promising Continental commissions to **European officers by the boatload** — many self-promoting adventurers demanding general's rank. (HIGH)
  - **The officer flood as a real problem:** Congress, "desperate for volunteers earlier in the war," was overwhelmed, and the foreign-contract generals stirred resentment among American officers. The worst case: **Philippe du Coudray**, promised major-general rank and command of the artillery — **Knox, Greene, and Sullivan threatened to resign** over it; the problem solved itself when du Coudray **drowned crossing the Schuylkill (September 1777)**. (HIGH on the resignation threats and the drowning; tell it dryly.)
  - **The exceptions that worked:** **Lafayette** (arrived June 1777, age 19, served without pay — ONE beat; he's threaded elsewhere, no hagiography) and **Steuben** (below). (HIGH)
- **Steuben — the useful exaggeration.** Franklin and Deane's letter of introduction styled **Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben** a *"Lieutenant General in the King of Prussia's service."* He had actually left Prussian service as a **captain**. The inflation was deliberate — and the man delivered: he reached **Valley Forge 23 February 1778**, offered to serve initially without pay, and drilled the Continental Army into a disciplined force. (HIGH — the padded résumé is documented; a perfect house-voice beat.)

## 3. FRANKLIN IN PARIS

- **Arrival & base.** Franklin landed in France in **December 1776** and settled at **Passy**, outside Paris, for the duration. He was already world-famous as the tamer of lightning. (HIGH)
- **The fur cap — documented, and self-aware.** Franklin to **Emma Thompson, 8 February 1777**: he is *"very plainly dress'd, wearing my thin grey strait Hair, that peeks out under my only Coiffure, a fine Fur Cap, which comes down my Forehead almost to my Spectacles."* The republican-simplicity costume was a **calculated performance** for a Paris that expected a backwoods philosopher — and it worked. (HIGH — verified verbatim at Founders Online, Franklin Papers 23:0188.)
- **The celebrity industry — documented by Franklin himself.** Franklin to his daughter **Sarah Bache, 3 June 1779**: medallions of him were being made in every size, "some to be set in lids of snuff boxes, and some so small as to be worn in rings," and with the pictures, busts, and prints, *"have made your father's face as well known as that of the moon."* (HIGH — verified verbatim at Founders Online.)
- **The Voltaire embrace — documented (eyewitness: John Adams).** At the **Académie des Sciences, 29 April 1778**, the crowd refused to settle until Franklin and the dying Voltaire embraced *"à la françoise"* — hugging and kissing cheeks — to cries that it was **"Solon and Sophocles"** embracing. Adams recorded it in his diary; Condorcet also described it. (HIGH — one of the few celebrity anecdotes with first-rate documentation; the dinner-party legends around it are softer, stick to this one.)
- **The mission's actual work** (don't let the fur cap eat the chapter): Franklin (with Deane, then Arthur Lee; later as sole minister plenipotentiary from 1779) (HIGH on all):
  - **Money** — negotiated loan after loan and outright royal gifts, almost annually, as Continental paper collapsed (see §6).
  - **Supplies & convoys** — kept the materiel pipeline moving through French ports.
  - **Privateers & prizes** — handled commissions and prize disputes for American raiders using French harbors (mechanics belong to the War-at-Sea chapter; one clause here at most).
  - **Prisoners** — negotiated exchanges for American sailors rotting in English jails (a steady, unglamorous correspondence, 1779–1782).
  - **Managing Vergennes** — the indispensable relationship; Vergennes found Adams abrasive and dealt by strong preference with Franklin. (HIGH on the preference; phrase without bashing Adams.)

## 4. SARATOGA → THE TREATIES (DEC 1777 – JUNE 1778)

- **The news arrives, 4 December 1777.** Burgoyne's surrender at Saratoga (17 October 1777) reached Franklin at Passy on **4 December 1777** — the courier was the young Bostonian **Jonathan Loring Austin**. He also carried the news that Philadelphia had fallen; Saratoga was what mattered. **Two days later (6 December) Louis XVI assented to opening alliance negotiations.** (HIGH)
- **Why Saratoga moved France:** it proved the Americans could destroy a British field army — and it raised the specter of Britain offering conciliation that would end the war before France could profit. Vergennes moved to lock the Americans in. (HIGH as standard framing.)
- **6 February 1778 — the twin treaties**, signed in Paris by **Conrad-Alexandre Gérard** for France and **Franklin, Silas Deane, and Arthur Lee** for the United States (HIGH):
  - **Treaty of Amity and Commerce** — trade on most-favored-nation terms; in signing it France became the **first major power to formally recognize the United States**. (HIGH)
  - **Treaty of Alliance** — a *defensive* alliance triggered if Britain and France went to war (which everyone understood was now certain). The operative articles, verbatim from the engrossed text (HIGH — Avalon Project; preserve the 18th-c. spellings only if styled as quotation):
    - **Art. 2 — the point of it:** *"The essential and direct End of the present defensive alliance is to maintain effectually the liberty, Sovereignty, and independance absolute and unlimited of the said united States, as well in Matters of Gouvernement as of commerce."*
    - **Art. 8 — no separate peace:** *"Neither of the two Parties shall conclude either Truce or Peace with Great Britain, without the formal consent of the other first obtain'd; and they mutually engage not to lay down their arms, until the Independence of the united states shall have been formally or tacitly assured…"*
    - **Art. 11 — the mutual guarantee:** France guaranteed to the United States *"their liberty, Sovereignty, and Independence absolute, and unlimited"* plus their possessions; the United States reciprocally guaranteed **France's possessions in America** (the clause that would haunt U.S. politics in 1793 — at most one forward-glancing clause here, no detour).
  - A **separate secret article** held the door open for Spain to join. (HIGH)
- **From conditional to open war.** France informed London of the commercial treaty on **13 March 1778**; Britain recalled its ambassador. **Louis XVI formally received Franklin and the commissioners at Versailles on 20 March 1778.** Neither side issued a formal declaration of war; fighting began at sea on **17 June 1778**, when the British frigate *Arethusa* engaged the French frigate *Belle Poule* off Brittany. (HIGH; the fleet-scale Battle of Ushant, 27 July 1778, belongs to War-at-Sea.)
- **The Carlisle Commission — too late by design of events.** Panicked by Saratoga and the French threat, **Parliament in February–March 1778 repealed the tea duty (Taxation of Colonies Act 1778) and renounced taxing the colonies for revenue**; it sent commissioners (the **Earl of Carlisle, William Eden, George Johnstone**) empowered to offer, in effect, **everything but independence** — suspension of every objectionable act since 1763, no parliamentary taxation, home rule within the empire. (HIGH)
  - They reached Philadelphia in **June 1778** — just as the British army was ordered to evacuate the city (18 June), gutting their leverage. **Congress on 17 June 1778 unanimously refused** to treat unless Britain first recognized independence or withdrew its forces — conditions the commission could not grant. (HIGH)
  - Commissioner **Johnstone attempted bribery** (the famous reply attributed to Joseph Reed: he was not worth purchasing, "but such as I am, the King of Great Britain is not rich enough to do it"). (MED — the bribery attempt is documented; Reed's exact line is reported/attributed, flag as "Reed reportedly answered.")
  - **The chapter's bitter joke, verified:** the concessions of 1778 would have ended the crisis cold in 1775. After Saratoga and the French treaty, they were an offer for a war that no longer existed. (HIGH as framing.)

## 5. THE WIDENING WAR (1779–1780): BRITAIN ALONE

- **Spain enters — as FRANCE'S ally, not America's.** By the **Treaty of Aranjuez (12 April 1779)** Spain allied with France (not the U.S.) in exchange for French help recovering **Gibraltar, Minorca, and the Floridas**; Spain **declared war on Britain 21 June 1779**. Spain **never signed a treaty with the United States and did not recognize American independence until the general peace of 1783** — Madrid had its own empire of restive colonies and no appetite for blessing colonial revolts. State the distinction precisely. (HIGH)
  - **Gibraltar was Spain's price** — and France pledged to fight until it was recovered, which quietly chained the alliance's war (and thus, through the no-separate-peace clause, America's war) to a rock in the Mediterranean. The **Great Siege of Gibraltar (June 1779 – February 1783)** never succeeded. (HIGH on the siege and pledge; MED on pressing the "chained" framing — it's standard in diplomatic histories (Dull), keep to one sentence.)
  - **Bernardo de Gálvez — one verified beat.** The young governor of Spanish Louisiana had funneled supplies up the Mississippi to the rebels even before Spain entered; once at war he took **Baton Rouge (September 1779)**, **Mobile (March 1780)**, and — commanding ~**8,000 men** — **Pensacola (siege March 9 – May 10, 1781)**, sweeping Britain from the Gulf Coast and pinning British resources far from the thirteen states. (HIGH; resist expanding — it's a one-beat cameo.)
- **The Dutch war.** **Britain declared war on the Dutch Republic on 20 December 1780** — partly because the Dutch were about to join the League of Armed Neutrality (war made them a belligerent the League wouldn't protect), partly on the pretext of a **draft Dutch-American treaty found in the papers of Henry Laurens**, the American envoy captured at sea (September 1780). (HIGH)
- **The League of Armed Neutrality.** **Catherine the Great of Russia declared armed neutrality on 11 March 1780** (28 February Old Style); **Denmark-Norway and Sweden** joined her in 1780, others later — neutral navies asserting the right to trade with Britain's enemies and to escort their merchantmen by force. It never fired a shot at Britain, but it isolated her diplomatically and menaced her blockade rights. (HIGH)
- **The strategic inversion — the standard framing, verified.** In the Seven Years' War Britain had Prussia tying down France on the European continent and fought at sea with allies; by 1780 Britain faced **France, Spain, and the Dutch Republic at sea simultaneously, plus the American rebellion on land, with not one continental ally** — and an armed-neutral league of the remaining naval powers. The combined Bourbon fleets outnumbered the Royal Navy; in the summer of **1779 a Franco-Spanish armada cruised the Channel and southern England braced for invasion** (it came apart from sickness and muddle, not battle). This inversion is the standard scholarly framing (Mackesy, *The War for America*; Dull, *A Diplomatic History of the American Revolution*). (HIGH on the facts; the invasion-scare gets one sentence.)

## 6. THE ROCKY MIDDLE (1778–1780): D'ESTAING, FURY, AND FRENCH GOLD

- **d'Estaing arrives.** Vice Admiral **Charles Henri, comte d'Estaing** sailed from Toulon (April 1778) with **12 ships of the line**; the crossing took so long that the British had already evacuated Philadelphia. At New York his pilots judged the deepest ships couldn't cross the Sandy Hook bar, so the first allied stroke shifted to **Newport, Rhode Island** (August 1778). (HIGH)
- **Newport — the storm and the fury.** As d'Estaing prepared to engage Lord Howe's fleet, a **two-day nor'easter (from 11 August 1778)** mauled both fleets, dismasting French ships including the flagship. d'Estaing returned on **20 August** only to announce he was taking the fleet to **Boston for repairs** — abandoning the American army under **John Sullivan** mid-operation. (HIGH)
  - **American fury was open and official:** Sullivan and his generals signed a written **protest** at the departure; Sullivan's public criticism was so harsh it was rumored **Lafayette would challenge him to a duel**; in Boston that September a **mob brawl left a French officer (the chevalier de Saint-Sauveur) mortally wounded**. The brand-new alliance nearly curdled in its first joint operation; Washington and Lafayette worked to smooth it over. (HIGH on the protest and the Saint-Sauveur death; MED on the duel rumor — phrase as "it was rumored.")
- **Savannah, October 1779 — the bloody failure.** d'Estaing came up from the West Indies and joined American forces besieging British-held **Savannah**. The frontal **assault of 9 October 1779** was a slaughter: roughly **244 allied killed and ~580 wounded** (British losses a fraction of that); **d'Estaing himself was wounded twice** leading from the front; **Casimir Pulaski**, the Polish father of the American cavalry, was mortally wounded. The fleet sailed away; emboldened, Britain took Charleston the next spring. (HIGH — tell it straight: neither French-bashing nor excuse. d'Estaing was brave and unlucky at Newport, brave and wrong at Savannah; two years into the alliance France had delivered no victory in North America.)
- **French gold vs. Continental paper.** Through these same years the **Continental dollar collapsed** (Congress devalued it 40-to-1 in March 1780; "not worth a Continental"). What kept Congress's war solvent was the French pipeline: **repeated loans and outright royal gifts** negotiated by Franklin — including, in 1781 alone, a **gift of 6 million livres plus a French-guaranteed Dutch loan of 10 million**. (HIGH on the collapse and the 1781 package; MED on itemization — phrase softly.)
  - **Totals — give ranges.** Direct French financial aid to the United States (loans + subsidies/gifts, 1776–1783) is variously totaled in the **tens of millions of livres — commonly ~35–48 million, of which roughly 9–12 million were outright gifts**; by 1782 the U.S. already owed France ~17 million and borrowing continued into 1783. Against this stands France's **whole-war bill of roughly 1.0–1.3 billion livres** (see §9). **Never print a single precise total; the accounting was disputed even at the time.** (MED/DISPUTED — ranges only.)

## 7. ROCHAMBEAU (1780–1781): THE ARMY THAT PAID ITS WAY

- **The Expédition Particulière.** In 1780 France sent not just a fleet but an army: lieutenant general **Jean-Baptiste Donatien de Vimeur, comte de Rochambeau** (a 55-year-old veteran of two wars) landed at **Newport on 11 July 1780** with about **5,500 troops** (sources 5,300–6,000). The promised second division never came — blockaded in Brest — and a British squadron promptly corked the French fleet in Narragansett Bay, so the army waited at Newport for nearly a year. (HIGH; use ~5,500.)
  - **The court had learned from d'Estaing:** Rochambeau's orders **placed his corps under Washington's overall command** — a deliberate subordination of French regulars to a colonial general, nearly unthinkable by European protocol, and it worked. Rochambeau played the loyal lieutenant in public throughout. (HIGH)
- **The discipline story — verified.** Where armies traditionally took, the French **paid — in hard coin — for supplies, labor, and even rent on the land they camped on**, brought provisions from France, hired local tradesmen by the hundred, and kept iron discipline. Newport, initially cold to its Catholic occupiers (these were the heirs of New England's F&I-era bogeymen), warmed to them. (HIGH on pay-in-coin, rents, employment, discipline; avoid the over-polished "never took so much as an apple" formulations — keep to the documented practices.)
- **Washington and Rochambeau** met first at **Hartford (September 1780** — the conference interrupted by news of Arnold's treason**)**, then at **Wethersfield, Connecticut, 21–22 May 1781**, after word arrived that **de Grasse's great fleet** would come north for the summer. (HIGH)
- **New York vs. the Chesapeake — handle the historiography honestly.** At Wethersfield **Washington pressed for an attack on New York**, and the formal plan said New York; **Rochambeau preferred the Chesapeake** — and, while deferring publicly, **wrote privately to de Grasse** signaling the Chesapeake as the better target and asking him to bring troops and money. **The operative decision was de Grasse's**: the armies would have to go wherever the fleet went, and on **5 August 1781** he sailed for the Chesapeake. When the news reached Washington (**14 August**) he pivoted completely and drove the combined armies ~450 miles south, masking the move with fake camps and bread ovens opposite New York. (HIGH — the safe formulation: Washington wanted New York, Rochambeau steered toward Virginia, de Grasse decided, and Washington executed the pivot brilliantly. Do NOT write "Washington planned Yorktown all along" or "Rochambeau tricked Washington.")

## 8. THE PAYOFF (AUG–OCT 1781): THE MACHINE FINALLY MESHES

- **De Grasse goes all-in.** From **Cap-Français, Saint-Domingue** (now Cap-Haïtien, Haiti), Rear Admiral **François-Joseph Paul, comte de Grasse** stripped the Caribbean: he brought **all 28 ships of the line** north plus **~3,000–3,300 regulars under the marquis de Saint-Simon**, who joined Lafayette's force in Virginia in early September. (HIGH)
- **The money — and the Havana legend, handled carefully.** The expedition and the armies needed hard cash no one had. The Spanish royal agent **Francisco de Saavedra**, working with de Grasse, raised **~500,000 pesos in Havana in roughly 48 hours (15–17 August 1781)** from the treasury, merchants, and private lenders; the frigate *Aigrette* carried it to the fleet. **The famous "ladies of Havana pledged their diamonds" story is later legend** — it first appears in an anonymous postwar pamphlet by a non-eyewitness. Tell the verified version (Spanish silver, raised in Havana, in two days) and flag the jewels as the legend it is. (HIGH on Saavedra/500,000 pesos/48 hours; the jewels = DISPUTED/legend.) That September, at Head of Elk, many Continentals received **a month's pay in hard coin — borrowed from Rochambeau's war chest** — for some, the first real money of their service. (MED — well-attested in substance; amounts vary by source, phrase softly.)
- **The Battle of the Chesapeake — ONE beat.** On **5 September 1781**, de Grasse with **24 ships of the line** (the rest at anchor, crews ashore) stood out of the bay and fought Rear Admiral **Thomas Graves's 19** off the Virginia Capes: tactically a maul with no ships sunk, strategically absolute — Graves limped back to New York (**20 September**), **Barras's** Newport squadron slipped in with the **siege artillery**, and Cornwallis's army was sealed in Yorktown with the sea shut. The alliance's whole logic — Washington's "decisive naval superiority" — existed for exactly these weeks, and they were enough. (HIGH; full battle mechanics belong to War-at-Sea.)
- **Yorktown as a Franco-American operation — the numbers (ranges).** Siege of Yorktown, **28 September – 19 October 1781**: American Continentals ~**8,000** plus ~**3,100 militia**; French regulars ~**7,800–8,800** (Rochambeau's corps + Saint-Simon's) — i.e., the **French were roughly half the regular troops, and the fleet was entirely French** (28+ ships of the line offshore). French engineers laid out the formal siege; the parallel trenches and batteries were European siegecraft. On the night of **14 October** the allies stormed the two key redoubts side by side — **the French took Redoubt 9, the Americans (under Lafayette's command, led by Hamilton) Redoubt 10**. (HIGH on dates and the paired assault; troop figures MED — use ranges, never a fake-precise total.)
- **The surrender.** **Cornwallis surrendered ~7,000–8,000 men on 19 October 1781.** Pleading illness, he sent **Brigadier General Charles O'Hara**, who tried to surrender his sword to **Rochambeau — who redirected him to Washington**, who in turn had his own second, **Benjamin Lincoln**, receive it. (HIGH on the O'Hara redirect — a perfect closing image of the alliance's protocol; MED on the exact prisoner count — use the range.)
- **Fuse-out.** Yorktown ended the major fighting in America but **not the war**: the fleets fought on in the Caribbean and the Gibraltar siege ground on into 1783, and the peace took two more years to make — that story belongs to the Treaty of Paris chapter. (HIGH)

## 9. WHAT IT COST FRANCE

- **The bill.** France's total spending on the war is put at roughly **1.0–1.3 billion livres** (the 1.3 billion figure is the most commonly cited; scholarly estimates run from ~1.07 billion) — **more than double the crown's annual revenue**, with **1781 alone costing well over 200 million**. Nearly all of it was **borrowed** (Necker financed the war by loans, not new taxes). (MED — give the range; never one bare number.)
- **The road to 1789 — one honest beat, no overclaim.** Servicing that war debt was a major driver of the fiscal crisis of the 1780s — the crisis that forced the crown to the **Assembly of Notables (1787)** and then the **Estates-General (1789)**, the assembly that became the French Revolution. **Standard scholarship: the American war's debt was a major contributor to the monarchy's collapse, not its sole cause** (the tax system's structural failures were older and deeper). Safe phrasing: "the debt France took on to free America helped bankrupt the monarchy — one of the roads that led to 1789." (HIGH as the standard framing; do NOT write "the American Revolution caused the French Revolution.")
- **Vergennes didn't live to see it** — he died in **February 1787**, weeks before the Assembly of Notables convened. (HIGH)
- **The close — the standard judgment, citable.** The honest middle (see HANDLE-WITH-CARE #3) rests on a verdict no serious history disputes; a citable institutional formulation is the U.S. State Department Office of the Historian: *"French assistance was crucial in securing the British surrender at Yorktown in 1781."* French money armed the army, French gifts and loans kept Congress solvent, the French navy made Yorktown possible, and French regulars were half the besieging force — while American persistence, and an army that refused to die from 1775 to 1781, was what France's power was multiplied *by*. (HIGH)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **Franklin anecdote inflation.** DOCUMENTED: the fur-cap self-description (his own 1777 letter), the face-like-the-moon medallion craze (his own 1779 letter), the Voltaire embrace (Adams's diary, Condorcet). NOT reliably documented / inflated in retellings: most dinner-party bon mots, the swooning-ladies set pieces, and the **"it is not a truth, it is a Stormont" quip** (a contemporary jest reported at second hand — omit or flag as "a joke Parisians told"). Build the celebrity beat ONLY from the documented three.
2. **The "ladies of Havana's diamonds" is legend.** The verified story: Spanish agent Saavedra raised ~500,000 pesos in Havana in ~48 hours (15–17 Aug 1781) from treasury, merchants, and private lenders. The jewels-of-the-ladies version traces to an anonymous postwar pamphlet by a non-eyewitness. Tell the real one; if the legend is mentioned, name it as legend.
3. **"France won the war" vs "America won with French help" — hold the honest middle.** French navy + French money were *decisive* (no plausible Yorktown without them); the American army's survival and the population's persistence 1775–1781 were *necessary* (nothing for France to multiply otherwise). Refuse both nationalist framings; the chapter's thesis is that 1781 worked because BOTH halves finally meshed.
4. **Spain's position must be stated precisely.** Spain was **France's ally, never America's**; no Spanish-American treaty; **no Spanish recognition of U.S. independence until the 1783 peace**. Gibraltar — not American liberty — was Madrid's price. Getting this wrong is a real and common error.
5. **No Lafayette hagiography.** He gets one beat in §2 (the exception in the officer flood) and appears at Redoubt 10 / Virginia in passing; his full story is threaded elsewhere in the vertical.
6. **The d'Estaing failures: no French-bashing, no excuse-making.** Newport was a storm plus a hard naval call that abandoned an ally mid-siege; Savannah was a brave, bloody, mistaken assault. Both nearly broke the alliance's politics; both are why Rochambeau's subordination-to-Washington orders mattered. Tell all of it.
7. **Who chose the Chesapeake — historiography handled honestly.** Washington wanted New York (Wethersfield, May 1781); Rochambeau deferred publicly while privately steering de Grasse toward the Chesapeake; **de Grasse's 5 August decision settled it**; Washington pivoted and executed. Don't award sole authorship to anyone.
8. **Money numbers are ranges, always.** Direct aid to the U.S. ~35–48 million livres (gifts ~9–12M within it); France's total war cost ~1.0–1.3 billion livres; Yorktown troop and prisoner counts as ranges (§8). The accounting was contested in the 1780s and still is.
9. **The 90% gunpowder figure has a real basis but soft edges** (Stephenson, AHR 1925; includes powder milled from imported saltpeter; covers the early war, not all eight years). Say "about nine-tenths in the war's first years," never "90% of all the war's powder."
10. **The irony is the spine, not a sneer.** An absolute monarchy bankrolled a republican revolution out of balance-of-power revenge, and the debt helped destroy that monarchy. State it plainly once (§1) and let §9 close the loop; don't moralize it in between.
11. **Avoid inevitability.** As late as mid-1781 the alliance looked like a failure: three years, no joint victory, a bankrupt Congress, mutinies in the Continental line. Yorktown was a six-week window (de Grasse had promised to stay only to mid-October) that everyone hit at once.
12. **Steuben's padded résumé:** present the Franklin/Deane "Lieutenant General" inflation as the documented, knowing exaggeration it was — and Steuben as the rare adventurer who out-performed his fake billing. Don't launder the lie out of the story.
