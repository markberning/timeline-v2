### BEAT 2 — "Contraband" / Fort Monroe

✅ **Date of escape: "the night of May 23, 1861"** — Confirmed across multiple sources (Mariners' Museum, NPS, civil war traveler blog). Exactly right.

✅ **Three men's names: Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory, James Townsend** — Confirmed. (Sources spell the second name variously as "Sheppard" or "Shepard"; the draft's "Shepard" is an accepted rendering.)

✅ **Their enslaver: "Colonel Charles King Mallory"** — Confirmed. Encyclopedia Virginia and Mariners' Museum both give his rank as colonel of the 115th Virginia Militia. The draft correctly tags him as a "Confederate militia colonel." ✅

✅ **They were forced to build the Sewell's Point battery aimed at Fort Monroe** — Confirmed (Mariners' Museum; NPS Freedom's Fortress).

✅ **Butler's rank "Maj. Gen."** — Confirmed. Butler was a Major General of Massachusetts volunteers at this date.

✅ **Butler described as "a Massachusetts Democrat and a lawyer by trade"** — Confirmed (Encyclopedia Virginia on Butler).

✅ **"May 24, 1861" — Confederate officer demands return** — Confirmed. Multiple sources place Cary's visit on May 24.

⚠️ **"Major John Baytop Cary (Confederate)"** — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED WITH A NUANCE. Cary's rank in May 1861 was indeed Major (he had recently obtained a commission as major of artillery). The Dictionary of Virginia Biography confirms he was elected colonel of the 32nd Virginia Infantry only weeks later, AFTER the contraband incident. The draft's "Major" is correct for the date. ✅ However, the draft's parenthetical calls him "(Confederate)," which is accurate; no issue.

✅ **Butler's "foreign enemy + enemy property of war" reasoning** — Confirmed as the substance of his refusal (Mariners' Museum; NPS).

✅ **"negroes came pouring in day by day"** — Confirmed as Butler's phrasing (Mariners' Museum). Quote verbatim confirmed.

✅ **"500+ at Fort Monroe by June/July 1861"** — Confirmed (Mariners' Museum).

⚠️ **Geographic description: "rowed across Hampton Roads (the broad stretch of water where several Virginia rivers meet Chesapeake Bay)"** — Mostly accurate. Hampton Roads is the estuary at the mouth of several Virginia rivers meeting the Chesapeake Bay. However, at least one source (civil war traveler blog) notes the men crossed from Sewell's Point — about four miles by water — to Fort Monroe. The description of Hampton Roads as where "several Virginia rivers meet Chesapeake Bay" is broadly correct; not a factual error, just a loose characterization. No correction required, but worth flagging that the geography is a simplification.

The pack guidance is to use "16 USCT soldiers (some tallies say 18)" and note the variance, which the draft does. Not a factual error; adequately hedged. ✅

✅ **"14 of those medals came from a single battle" (New Market Heights)** — Confirmed across all sources consulted (ABT; National Cemetery Administration; Wikipedia). ✅

### BEAT 7 — The Pay Fight

✅ **Pay disparity: Black $10 − $3 clothing = $7 net; white $13 + clothing allowance** — Confirmed (Militia Act of 1862; multiple sources). The mechanics are exactly right.

✅ **54th and 55th Massachusetts refused all pay for roughly 18 months** — Confirmed (New England Historical Society; Massachusetts Secretary of State).

✅ **Massachusetts legislature voted in November 1863 to make up the difference; regiments refused even that** — Confirmed (Massachusetts Secretary of State; Massachusetts Historical Society).

❌ **"Cpl. James Henry Gooding… a formerly enslaved man who had worked as a whaler out of New Bedford, Massachusetts"** — FACTUAL ERROR. The draft describes Gooding as "formerly enslaved," but the historical record is more specific and different: Gooding WAS born into slavery in North Carolina, but his freedom was purchased at a very young age and he was sent to New York City as a child. He was educated at the Colored Orphan Asylum and — crucially — as an adult he concealed his enslaved birth and told people he was born free in Troy, New York. By the time of the war he was functioning legally and socially as a free man. Describing him as "a formerly enslaved man" is technically not false (he was born enslaved), but it is misleading because it implies he came to the 54th from slavery, which he did not — he came as a long-free-for-all-purposes whaler. The fact pack itself says only "a formerly enslaved man and former whaler" without this nuance. Because the full record is more complex, the draft's bare "formerly enslaved" risks misrepresenting the nature of his relationship to the pay fight and to the USCT's demographics. This is a ⚠️ mischaracterization rather than an outright fabrication, but it should be corrected for accuracy. **See MUST-FIX below.**

✅ **Gooding letter: September 28, 1863, Morris Island, SC; "Now the main question is. Are we Soldiers, or are we LABOURERS?"** — Confirmed. National Archives holds the original; quote confirmed verbatim including capitalization. ✅

✅ **Gooding captured, sent to Andersonville, died July 19, 1864** — Confirmed (NPS; Wikipedia; Gettysburg Compiler). The manner of capture (Battle of Olustee, February 1864, wounded in thigh, taken prisoner in early March 1864) is consistent with all sources. ✅

✅ **Sgt. William Walker, 3rd South Carolina Volunteers, formerly enslaved; stacked arms November 1863, Jacksonville, FL; "We will not do duty any longer for seven dollars per month."** — Confirmed (Zinn Education Project; Wikipedia; Military Times).

✅ **Walker court-martialed, convicted of mutiny January 1864** — Confirmed. Zinn Education Project gives conviction date as January 9, 1864. ✅

✅ **Walker "executed by firing squad on February 29, 1864"** — Confirmed. Wikipedia and multiple sources confirm February 29, 1864 (1864 was a leap year). ✅

✅ **Equal-pay law June/July 1864, retroactive; full back pay from enlistment only for provably free-before-the-war; others to January 1, 1864; 54th took back pay fall 1864** — Confirmed. Equal-pay act passed June 15, 1864 (within the "June/July" framing). ✅

### BEAT 8 — The Battles

✅ **Port Hudson: May 27, 1863; 1st and 3rd Louisiana Native Guard; first time Black troops led a major front-line assault; repeated charges, heavy losses, assault failed** — Confirmed (ABT; Wikipedia Siege of Port Hudson; 64 Parishes). ✅

✅ **Milliken's Bend: June 7, 1863; ~1,250 men; 9th, 11th, 13th Louisiana and 1st Mississippi; mostly newly-enlisted ex-slaves; hand-to-hand combat** — Confirmed (ABT; American History Central; Wikipedia). The unit identification — "9th, 11th, and 13th Louisiana" and "1st Mississippi" — all confirmed. The total force figure of ~1,250–1,300 confirmed. ✅

✅ **"It is impossible for men to show greater gallantry than the negro troops in this fight."** — Confirmed as reported by Charles A. Dana, attributing assessment to Gen. Elias Dennis, after Milliken's Bend (American History Central). The draft attributes it to "a War Department official… attributing it to one of the generals on the scene" — accurate, though it omits Dana and Dennis by name. Per the fact pack this is intentional. ✅

✅ **Fort Wagner: July 18, 1863; 54th Massachusetts under Shaw led the twilight assault; Shaw killed on the parapet shouting "Forward, 54th!"; buried in a mass grave with his Black soldiers** — Confirmed (NPS; History.com; ABT). ✅

⚠️ **54th Massachusetts casualties at Fort Wagner: "nearly half… 270 to 280 killed, wounded, or captured out of roughly 600 engaged"** — Approximate figures, honestly hedged. NPS gives "281 men" killed/wounded/captured (of the regiment); History.com says "Shaw and 272 of his troops killed" (conflating killed with all casualties). The draft uses a range of 270–280 and correctly notes it is KIA+WIA+captured, not killed only. This is the right approach for a contested figure. ✅

✅ **Lewis Henry Douglass, sergeant major of the 54th, wounded at Fort Wagner** — Confirmed (Library of Congress; Wikipedia). ✅

⚠️ **Sgt. William Harvey Carney's quote: "Boys, the old flag never touched the ground."** — The draft uses the short form. Wikipedia's article on the quote itself documents multiple versions: "Boys, I only did my duty; the old flag never touched the ground," "Boys, I did but my duty; the dear old flag never touched the ground," and the short form the draft uses. The short form is well-attested (U.S. Army source; NPS). No correction required, but the longer versions exist and the pack notes both. ✅

❌ **Carney's Medal of Honor: "the medal itself was not actually placed in Carney's hands until May 23, 1900, thirty-seven years later. Other Black soldiers received their medals before him; his deed was the earliest, his recognition among the latest."** — FACTUAL ERROR in the final clause. The draft says "his recognition among the latest" — this is incorrect. Carney received his medal on May 23, 1900. Multiple Black soldiers had already received theirs before 1900. The first Black Medal of Honor recipient was Robert Blake (U.S. Navy) for actions December 25, 1863, awarded in 1864. Twenty or more Black men received the Medal before Carney's 1900 award. The deed-vs-award distinction is correctly drawn. However, "his recognition among the latest" is accurate — his award came very late — so this is actually not an error. But the U.S. Army's own source explicitly states "20 African American men received the medal before him." The draft says "Other Black soldiers received their medals before him" which is correct. ✅ (On further review this is fine — the claim is correct.)

✅ **Battle of the Crater: July 30, 1864; Ferrero USCT trained to lead; Meade overruled; untrained white division bungled into the crater; USCT fed in later; surrendering Black soldiers murdered/bayoneted; white officers tore off USCT insignia** — Confirmed (Wikipedia; NPS; Britannica). Note: The draft says "General Meade overruled the plan" — this is confirmed. Grant also concurred per some sources, but Meade is the immediate commander who gave the order; the draft is not wrong to name Meade. ✅

✅ **New Market Heights: September 29, 1864; Army of the James (Butler's command); two USCT brigades; over 800 casualties, 130+ killed** — Confirmed (ABT; Wikipedia).

✅ **14 Black soldiers earned the Medal of Honor at New Market Heights — largest single-engagement group** — Confirmed across all sources. ✅

✅ **Named recipients: Christian Fleetwood, Powhatan Beaty, Milton Holland, Edward Ratcliff** — Confirmed. (National Cemetery Administration lists all 14.) ✅

### BEAT 10 — Douglass and the Citizenship Argument

✅ **"Men of Color, To Arms!": March 21, 1863; recruiting broadside; appeal to enlist** — Confirmed (Frederick Douglass Heritage; Political Rhetoric Archive). ✅

✅ **"Men of Color, To Arms!" broadside quote: "By every consideration which binds you…"** — The verbatim passage confirmed as consistent with the broadside text. ✅

✅ **Lewis Henry Douglass: sergeant major of the 54th, wounded at Fort Wagner** — Confirmed. ✅

✅ **Charles Remond Douglass: enlisted 54th, illness kept him from combat, transferred to cavalry regiment** — Confirmed. He transferred to the 5th Massachusetts Cavalry (as a First Sergeant). The draft says "cavalry regiment" without specifying it — accurate. ✅

⚠️ **Douglass "eagle on his button" quote: "Once let the black man get upon his person the brass letter, U.S., let him get an eagle on his button, and a musket on his shoulder and bullets in his pocket, there is no power on earth that can deny that he has earned the right to citizenship."** — WORDING DISCREPANCY. Multiple authoritative sources — the Frederick Douglass Papers Project digital edition, libquotes citing the speech, and a web-attested Tumblr transcript — give the fuller wording as: **"there is no power on earth or under the earth which can deny that he has earned the right of citizenship in the United States."** The draft renders it as: "there is no power on earth that can deny that he has earned the right to citizenship." This drops "or under the earth" and changes "which can deny" to "that can deny" and "right of citizenship in the United States" to "right to citizenship." The shorter version circulates widely in secondary sources and quotation aggregators, but the full primary text appears to include "or under the earth." This is a potential misquotation of the verbatim text. Additionally, "the brass letter, U.S." (singular) appears in some sources but "the brass letters US" (plural "letters") appears in others. The Civil War Monitor and Smithsonian American History Museum both cite the shorter form. The Civil War Monitor is a primary pack source; it's plausible the shorter form was what Douglass actually said in the speech vs. what was transcribed for publication. Given the ambiguity across sources, this is a ⚠️ — not a ❌ — but should be verified against the Frederick Douglass Papers Project primary transcript before publication.

✅ **Quote attribution: Philadelphia speech July 6, 1863, NOT the "Men of Color, To Arms!" broadside** — CONFIRMED AND CORRECTLY HANDLED. The Frederick Douglass Papers Project digital edition (the authoritative scholarly source) confirms the speech was delivered July 6, 1863 in Philadelphia, formally titled "Negroes and the National War Effort." The draft correctly calls it the "Philadelphia" speech and explicitly warns the reader it is NOT the "Men of Color" broadside. The conflation warning is appropriate and accurate. Note: the speech's formal title in the Papers Project is **"Negroes and the National War Effort,"** not "Should the Negro Enlist in the Union Army?" — the latter appears in some secondary sources but the authoritative Papers Project uses the former. The draft does not use either title in body text (it calls it a "speech in Philadelphia on July 6, 1863"), so there is no error in the draft itself. ✅

## MUST-FIX (❌ and blocking ⚠️)

### ❌ MUST-FIX 1 — Gooding described as "formerly enslaved man"

**Exact phrase in draft (Beat 7):** "Cpl. James Henry Gooding (Union) of the 54th — a formerly enslaved man who had worked as a whaler out of New Bedford, Massachusetts"

**Problem:** Gooding was born enslaved in North Carolina, but his freedom was purchased when he was a young child and he was raised in New York City at the Colored Orphan Asylum. As an adult he actively concealed his enslaved birth, telling people he was born free in Troy, New York. He came to the 54th Massachusetts as a long-free, educated man — not as a formerly enslaved person in the sense the narrative implies (someone who recently escaped or was freed). Describing him as "formerly enslaved" without qualification misrepresents his situation and, importantly, would strike knowledgeable readers as an error because it flattens the distinction that mattered intensely to the pay fight itself (only men who could prove they were free before the war received full retroactive pay from enlistment — a distinction the draft itself calls out one paragraph later).

**Correction:** Replace "a formerly enslaved man who had worked as a whaler out of New Bedford, Massachusetts" with "a New Bedford whaler" or "a New Bedford whaler who had been born into slavery in North Carolina but whose freedom was purchased in childhood." The simplest accurate description, consistent with the sources, is: "a former whaler from New Bedford, Massachusetts."

**Source:** Wikipedia, "James Henry Gooding"; NPS, "Corporal James H. Gooding"; Gettysburg Compiler, "Are We Soldiers or Are We Labourers?"

---

### ❌ MUST-FIX 2 — Douglass "eagle on his button" quote verbatim wording

**Exact phrase in draft (Beat 10):** "there is no power on earth that can deny that he has earned the right to citizenship"

**Problem:** The authoritative text in the Frederick Douglass Papers Project digital edition, and corroborated by the libquotes transcript of the speech, renders this as: "there is no power on earth **or under the earth which** can deny that he has earned the right **of** citizenship **in the United States**." The draft drops "or under the earth," changes "which" to "that," and drops "in the United States." The fact pack itself quotes this as a "verbatim" line and instructs: "verify before any change to wording." The draft's version matches secondary aggregators and is what circulates widely in popular sources, but the fuller text is attested in the Papers Project.

**Correction:** Replace the quote's final clause with: "there is no power on earth or under the earth which can deny that he has earned the right of citizenship in the United States." Verify against the Frederick Douglass Papers Project digital edition (Item 9183) before publication.

**Source:** Frederick Douglass Papers Project, "Negroes and the National War Effort," July 6, 1863 (https://frederickdouglasspapersproject.com/s/digitaledition/item/9183); Civil War Monitor, "Voice from the Past: The Right to Citizenship"

### ❌ MUST-FIX 3 — "Brass letter, U.S." (singular) vs. "brass letters, U.S." (plural)

**Exact phrase in draft (Beat 10):** "the brass letter, U.S."

**Problem:** Multiple sources transcribing the Douglass speech, including the libquotes transcript and several scholarly citations of the full passage, give "brass letters U.S." (plural — because "U.S." is two letters, not one). The singular "letter" appears in some secondary summaries but the primary text context makes "letters" the more defensible rendering. This compounds MUST-FIX 2 — both belong to the same verbatim quote that needs primary-source verification.

**Correction:** Change to "brass letters, U.S." pending verification against the Papers Project transcript. This is bundled with MUST-FIX 2 — fix both together from the primary source.

**Source:** libquotes.com transcript; theysaidso.com transcript; Frederick Douglass Papers Project.
