- Wounded: 3,107–3,114
- Captured/missing: 2,829–3,236 [CONTESTED — Wikipedia and ABT give different figures; see Section 10]

**Note on cost:** The Confederate total of ~7,993 is heavier in killed and wounded (1,483 KIA, 6,402 WIA) while the Union total includes ~2,800–3,236 captured, reflecting the collapse of Porter's line. Hood's Texas Brigade (Whiting's division) alone suffered 1,018 casualties — roughly a quarter of the men engaged — including all of the 4th Texas's field-level officers. [Sources: search results from multiple ABT articles; Warfare History Network]

**Nine Union regimental commanders were killed or mortally wounded.** The 9th Massachusetts lost 206 killed and wounded, the most casualties of any Northern regiment at the battle. The 20th North Carolina and 16th New York together suffered a combined 466 killed and wounded. [Source: Warfare History Network; Essential CW Curriculum]

## 3. What Came Before

### The Peninsula Campaign
McClellan landed his Army of the Potomac at Fort Monroe on the Virginia Peninsula in late March/early April 1862 and advanced northwest toward Richmond. [Source: Wikipedia "Peninsula Campaign"; Seven Days Battles Wikipedia]

By late May 1862, McClellan's forces had crept to within sight of Richmond's church spires — roughly 5–6 miles from the Confederate capital — after a slow advance up the Peninsula. [Source: Seven Days Battles Wikipedia; Essential CW Curriculum "Seven Days Battles"]

### Lee Takes Command (June 1, 1862)
At the Battle of Seven Pines / Fair Oaks (May 31–June 1, 1862), Confederate Gen. Joseph E. Johnston was seriously wounded. Confederate President Jefferson Davis replaced him with Robert E. Lee on June 1, 1862. Lee immediately renamed his force the Army of Northern Virginia. [Source: Wikipedia "Battle of Seven Pines"; ABT Seven Pines; LOC blog "June 1, 1862: Lee takes Command"]

Lee, knowing he could not win a defensive siege against the larger Union army, adopted an aggressive offensive strategy. [Source: Wikipedia "Seven Days Battles" — "Lee knew he could not win in a battle of attrition or siege"]

### Lee's Intelligence Problem and Strategic Plan
Lee's operational maps lacked Boatswain's Swamp — the key terrain feature of the coming battle was entirely absent from Confederate maps, an intelligence failure that contributed to the piecemeal, costly assaults on June 27. [Source: Essential CW Curriculum; Encyclopedia Virginia]

Lee's plan: cross the Chickahominy River with most of his army to attack Porter's isolated V Corps on the north bank while leaving minimal forces (under Magruder and Huger) to screen Richmond against McClellan's main body to the south. The plan relied on Jackson arriving from the Shenandoah Valley to turn Porter's right (northern) flank. [Source: Wikipedia; Essential CW Curriculum; Seven Days Battles Wikipedia]

### McClellan's Overestimate
Based on intelligence from Allan Pinkerton's detective agency, McClellan believed Lee commanded approximately 180,000–200,000 men (telegraphed Washington on June 28: "over 200,000 men"). Actual Confederate Army of Northern Virginia strength was approximately 92,000. This inflated estimate — possibly as much as double or triple the actual Confederate strength — drove McClellan's defensive mindset and his decision not to reinforce Porter aggressively. [Source: ABT "Peninsula Campaign: McClellan's Strategic Masterstroke and Tactical Blunder"; Seven Days search results — "McClellan...reported 200,000 when Lee had approximately 92,000"]

### Opening of the Seven Days: Beaver Dam Creek / Mechanicsville (June 26)
On June 26, Lee launched the Seven Days with an attack at Beaver Dam Creek near Mechanicsville. A.P. Hill's division attacked across Beaver Dam Creek and suffered approximately 1,000–1,500 casualties, gaining no ground. The assault was a tactical Confederate failure, yet it triggered McClellan's psychological shift: he began planning his "change of base" to the James River that very day. [Source: Seven Days Wikipedia; Essential CW Curriculum; americancivilwar.com narrative]

### McClellan's Pre-Battle Decision
In the pre-dawn hours of June 27 — before the Battle of Gaines' Mill even began — McClellan issued orders to "pull back to the James River" and establish a new supply base. He told Porter that once Lee's attack developed, Porter's corps should "bring your corps across the Grapevine Bridge." Porter was to fight a rearguard action, not an offensive. [Source: ABT Battle Facts summary; americancivilwar.com narrative citing direct Porter–McClellan exchange]

### Jackson's Slowness — Prior Day
Jackson's column, coming from the Shenandoah Valley, was already arriving days later than Lee expected. On June 26, Jackson departed late — reportedly at 8 a.m., six hours behind schedule — delayed by feverish exhaustion after his demanding Valley Campaign, destroyed bridges, muddy roads, enemy skirmishers, and supply wagon failures. Hundreds of stragglers left his column in search of food and water. His tardiness on June 26 at Beaver Dam Creek/Mechanicsville meant Lee's flanking plan never materialized there. [Source: historyofwar.org; warfarehistorynetwork.com; search results summarizing multiple accounts]

## 4. The Battle (June 27, 1862)

### Porter's Defensive Position
After withdrawing from Beaver Dam Creek, Porter established a new position behind Boatswain's Creek (also called Boatswain's Swamp) — a boggy ravine that ran in a semicircular arc roughly 1.5–2 miles long, protecting the plateau where the Watt House stood. [Source: Wikipedia; Warfare History Network; ABT Battle Facts]

The position was strong: Porter deployed in three defensive tiers.
- First line: at the creek bottom in the bottomland, with sharpshooters behind felled trees
- Second line: halfway up the hillside
- Third line: rifle pits along the plateau crest, ~50 feet above the stream
- Artillery: Porter arrayed 96 guns on the plateau, with additional batteries on the south bank of the Chickahominy providing supporting fire
[Source: Warfare History Network; Essential CW Curriculum]

Porter's corps dispositions: Morell's division on the left (Turkey Hill), Sykes's division on the right, McCall's division in reserve along the plateau crest. [Source: Wikipedia; Warfare History Network]

The key terrain: the plateau near the Watt House and Adams farm gave Porter's artillery commanding fields of fire. Any Confederate assault had to cross open ground, descend into the boggy creek, and charge up the steep hillside under concentrated fire. [Source: ABT Battle Facts; Essential CW Curriculum; NPS]

The historic Watt House — centerpiece of the plateau — still stands today within the Richmond National Battlefield Park's 60 acres of the battlefield. [Source: NPS Richmond NBP]

### Confederate Assaults — Piecemeal and Costly

**~Noon–1 p.m.:** D.H. Hill's division reached Old Cold Harbor and encountered unexpected Union resistance from Porter's line — not Porter's flank as Lee had planned, because Boatswain's Swamp (absent from Confederate maps) meant Porter's line extended further than anticipated. [Source: Wikipedia; Essential CW Curriculum]

**~2:30 p.m.:** A.P. Hill's ~12,000-man division launched the first major assault across Boatswain's Creek. His brigades under Gregg and Branch took the brunt. The assault gained no ground; Hill's division suffered over 2,000 casualties in roughly two hours. [Source: Wikipedia; Warfare History Network; Essential CW Curriculum]

**~4 p.m.:** Ewell's brigades entered the fight with three brigades in ferocious charges. Ewell personally commanded on foot after his horse was killed. His attacks also stalled with heavy loss. [Source: Wikipedia; Encyclopedia Virginia]

**~5 p.m.:** Porter telegraphed that he was "pressed hard, very hard." McClellan, directing by telegraph from HQ south of the river, dispatched two brigades from Sumner's II Corps (Meagher's Irish Brigade and French's brigade) as reinforcements — they arrived approximately three hours later due to distance, just after a failed Union cavalry charge. [Source: Warfare History Network; ABT Battle Facts; Essential CW Curriculum]

**Jackson's Tardiness on June 27:** Jackson's column was again delayed. He had been led down the wrong road, requiring an hour-long countermarch. Destroyed bridges, roadblocks, and sharpshooter fire further impeded his advance. He reached Old Cold Harbor expecting to find Porter's flank; instead he "found the Yankees in line facing him and not their flank, as Lee had thought." He arrived on the battlefield late in the afternoon but initially awaited further orders due to miscommunication, and his forces did not engage effectively until the final phase. [Source: Wikipedia; ABT Battle Facts (Jackson "led down the wrong road, which resulted in an hour-long countermarch"); Essential CW Curriculum; historyofwar.org; warfarehistorynetwork.com]

Artillery officer Edward Porter Alexander's post-war assessment: "Had Jackson attacked when he first arrived, or during A.P. Hill's attack, we would have had an easy victory." [Source: Warfare History Network citing Colonel E.P. Alexander; Wikipedia footnoted reference to Stephen Sears]

### The 7 p.m. Coordinated Assault and Hood's Breakthrough
**~7 p.m. (less than an hour before sunset):** Lee ordered a general coordinated assault across the full Confederate line — approximately 32,000 men in 16 brigades, the largest single assault Lee launched during the entire war, and likely the largest Confederate assault of the Civil War. (For comparison: Pickett's Charge at Gettysburg involved ~12,500 men; Hood's assault at Franklin involved ~20,000.) [Source: ABT 10 Facts; ABT Battle Facts; NPS Richmond NBP — "the largest single attack he ever launched during the war"]

**Hood's breakthrough:** Brig. Gen. John Bell Hood, commanding the Texas Brigade (part of Whiting's division), personally led the decisive charge. He took the 4th Texas (his old regiment) and companies of the 18th Georgia to the right of Whiting's other troops, targeting a spot across the swamp where the ground seemed slightly more favorable. [Source: ABT 10 Facts; search results; Warfare History Network; Essential CW Curriculum]

Hood's brigade advanced without firing — maintaining discipline, fixing bayonets, and rushing the creek and hillside at close range with the Rebel Yell. They stormed Martindale's Federal brigade, which had been fighting without reinforcement all day. The 4th Texas's charge was supported on Hood's right by Brig. Gen. George Pickett's brigade of Longstreet's division. [Source: ABT; Warfare History Network; search results]

The Texas Brigade broke through the first Union defensive line at the bottom of the ridge, then — with retreating Federal soldiers in front of them making it difficult for the second and third lines to fire — drove through the second and third lines as well, capturing 14 artillery pieces at the top of the ridge. Total Union artillery captured in the battle: ~22 pieces. [Source: search results; Encyclopedia Virginia; Warfare History Network]

Jackson, once ordered forward, contributed the final pressure: he reportedly told his men: "Tell them this affair must hang in suspense no longer. Sweep the field with the bayonet!" [Source: Warfare History Network — attributed as "Jackson's order via courier"; [VERIFY: primary source citation needed for this exact wording]]

Hood's division (Whiting's, including Hood's and Law's brigades) suffered 1,018 total casualties in the assault. The 4th Texas alone lost 570 casualties including all their field-level officers. [Source: search results from ABT/Civil War forums; Warfare History Network]

Jackson surveyed Hood's charge and declared: "The men who carried this position were soldiers indeed!" [Source: Essential CW Curriculum; [VERIFY: exact quote; cited in multiple secondary sources but primary source unclear]]

Lee reportedly said to Hood before the assault: "This must be done. Can you break his line?" [Source: Warfare History Network; americancivilwar.com narrative; [VERIFY: quoted across several secondary sources; primary source unclear]]

### Collapse of Porter's Line and Night Withdrawal
As the Confederate line broke through, Porter's forces scattered across the open fields and swampland toward the Chickahominy. Two entire Union regiments surrendered when surrounded. [Source: Encyclopedia Virginia]

A failed Union cavalry charge by Brig. Gen. Philip St. George Cooke — roughly 200 troopers from the 1st and 5th U.S. Cavalry — attempted to counter the Confederate breakthrough but was driven back with approximately one-quarter of the force cut down. [Source: Encyclopedia Virginia; search results]

During the night of June 27–28, Sykes's Regulars covered the retreat as Porter's corps withdrew across four narrow bridges over the Chickahominy River. Union engineers then destroyed the bridges behind them. [Source: Warfare History Network; Wikipedia; Essential CW Curriculum]

Lee had achieved his breakthrough, but the onset of darkness prevented a pursuit that might have destroyed Porter's corps entirely. [Source: search results citing multiple accounts — "onset of darkness saved his corps from total destruction"]

## 5. The Aftermath

**Lee's immediate report:** Lee reported the victory to Confederate President Jefferson Davis that night. [Source: Warfare History Network]

**McClellan's telegram to Stanton:** Shortly after midnight on June 28, McClellan sent Secretary of War Stanton a telegram stating in part: "I now know the full history of the day. On this side of the river (the right bank) we repulsed several strong attacks. On the left bank our men did all that men could do, all that soldiers could accomplish, but they were overwhelmed by vastly superior numbers, even after I brought my last reserves into action." The telegram ended with language so insubordinate that the chief censor of the Military Telegraph Service, Col. Edward S. Sanford, ordered it deleted before forwarding to Stanton: "If I save this army now I tell you plainly that I owe no thanks to you or any other persons in Washington — you have done your best to sacrifice this army." This suppression was not publicly known until 1907, when telegrapher David H. Bates revealed it in *Lincoln in the Telegraph Office*. [Source: Abraham Lincoln and the Civil War blog (abrahamlincolnandthecivilwar.wordpress.com); Seven Days Wikipedia; multiple search results]

**McClellan's "change of base" decision:** Convinced he faced a force vastly superior to his own (he telegraphed Washington on June 28 that he faced "over 200,000 men" — Lee's actual strength was ~92,000), McClellan ordered his entire army to withdraw southeast to Harrison's Landing on the James River, where U.S. Navy gunboats could protect a new supply base. [Source: Seven Days Wikipedia; search results on Pinkerton overestimates]

**The remaining Seven Days:** The Army of the Potomac's retreat across the Peninsula continued through:
- Savage's Station (June 29)
- Battle of Glendale / Nelson's Farm / Frayser's Farm (June 30)
- Battle of Malvern Hill (July 1) — Lee suffered a severe tactical defeat trying to assault the strong Union artillery position; McClellan nonetheless continued the retreat
The army reached Harrison's Landing on the James River. [Source: Seven Days Battles Wikipedia]

**McClellan was never removed immediately** but was effectively relieved after the campaign failed; Lincoln organized new forces under John Pope. Porter, McClellan's V Corps commander, was later court-martialed in November 1862 — convicted January 10, 1863, on charges related to Second Bull Run (not Gaines' Mill), cashiered January 21, 1863. After 23 years of effort, he was fully vindicated: a commission under Gen. John Schofield exonerated him in 1878; President Grover Cleveland commuted his sentence; Congress restored his commission as infantry colonel in 1886. Porter voluntarily retired on August 7, 1886. [Source: Wikipedia "Court-martial of Fitz John Porter"; NPS "Fitz John Porter"]

**Dual observation balloons:** Gaines' Mill was the first battle in which observation balloons were used simultaneously by both sides. [Source: ABT 10 Facts]

**Medal of Honor:** Seven Union soldiers earned Medals of Honor at Gaines' Mill, including Dan Butterfield (who later composed "Taps") and future Congressman John Moffitt. [Source: ABT 10 Facts]

**Family division:** Union Gen. Philip St. George Cooke, who led the failed cavalry counterattack, was the father-in-law of Confederate cavalry commander J.E.B. Stuart. [Source: ABT 10 Facts]

## 6. Key People (Side-Tagged)

**Gen. Robert E. Lee (CS)** — Commanding general, Army of Northern Virginia. Assumed command June 1, 1862, after Johnston's wounding at Seven Pines. Gaines' Mill was his first major victory of the war. His aggressive tactics in the Seven Days — despite heavy Confederate casualties and imperfect coordination — drove McClellan away from Richmond and established his reputation as an offensive commander. [Source: ABT 10 Facts; Lee Takes Command search results; Seven Days Wikipedia]

**Maj. Gen. A.P. Hill (CS)** — Commanded the Light Division (~12,000–13,200 men). Launched the first major Confederate assault of the battle ~2:30 p.m., suffering over 2,000 casualties in two hours while gaining no ground. His piecemeal assault, though costly, kept pressure on Porter's line. [Source: Wikipedia; Warfare History Network; Essential CW Curriculum]

**Maj. Gen. James Longstreet (CS)** — Division commander. Participated in the general 7 p.m. assault. Pickett's brigade (part of Longstreet's division) attacked on Hood's right during the breakthrough. [Source: Wikipedia; Essential CW Curriculum; Warfare History Network]

**Brig. Gen. John Bell Hood (CS)** — Commander, Texas Brigade (Whiting's division). Led the decisive breakthrough at dusk with the 4th Texas, companies of the 18th Georgia, and support from Hampton's Legion and 1st and 5th Texas. Advanced without firing, charged with bayonets, and broke all three of Porter's defensive lines, capturing 14 artillery pieces atop the Watt House plateau. His division suffered 1,018 casualties. The Texas Brigade's action at Gaines' Mill was "its first great feat of combat arms." [Source: ABT 10 Facts; Warfare History Network; search results]

**Maj. Gen. Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson (CS, arriving late)** — Commanded the flanking column from the Shenandoah Valley. Intended to turn Porter's right flank; instead arrived hours late due to a combination of exhaustion from the Valley Campaign, a civilian guide leading him down the wrong road (requiring ~two-hour countermarch), destroyed bridges, muddy roads, enemy sharpshooters, and supply failures. His tardiness arguably cost Lee an even larger victory or the potential destruction of Porter's corps. He arrived on the battlefield late afternoon, contributed to the final assault, and personally praised Hood's men afterward. [Source: Wikipedia; ABT; Warfare History Network; historyofwar.org; Essential CW Curriculum]

**Brig. Gen. [Maj. Gen.] Fitz John Porter (US)** — Commander, V Corps. Conducted a skilled, tenacious defensive battle against roughly 57,000 Confederates with approximately 34,000 men. His three-tiered line behind Boatswain's Creek held off piecemeal Confederate assaults for hours before Hood's breakthrough at dusk. Porter communicated with McClellan by telegraph throughout; McClellan never personally appeared on the field. Later court-martialed for actions at Second Bull Run (not Gaines' Mill) and vindicated after 23 years. [Source: Wikipedia; ABT; Warfare History Network; NPS "Fitz John Porter"; court-martial Wikipedia]

**Maj. Gen. George B. McClellan (US)** — Commander, Army of the Potomac. Directed operations by telegraph from HQ south of the Chickahominy River, never personally appearing at Gaines' Mill. Sent Porter only limited reinforcements (two brigades, arriving ~three hours late). Had already decided to abandon the advance on Richmond and "change base" to the James River before the battle even began. His Pinkerton-inflated overestimate of Confederate strength (reporting 200,000 vs. Lee's actual ~92,000) drove a defensive posture that surrendered the initiative to Lee. His famous insubordinate telegram to Stanton ("you have done your best to sacrifice this army") was suppressed by the telegraph censor. [Source: Multiple sources throughout; Warfare History Network; ABT; Seven Days Wikipedia]

## 7. What It Meant

**Lee seizes the initiative:** Gaines' Mill was Lee's first major victory of the war and "radically shifted the strategic initiative in the East to Robert E. Lee." [Source: ABT 10 Facts] The battle established Lee's reputation for aggressive, audacious offensive action — a reputation he would build on at Second Bull Run (August 1862) and Antietam (September 1862).

**Richmond saved for 1862:** The battle convinced McClellan — who already believed himself badly outnumbered — to abandon the campaign against Richmond entirely. McClellan's "change of base" to the James River meant the Confederate capital would not fall in 1862. Seven Days Wikipedia: "The campaign ended McClellan's effort to take Richmond, as well as the last chance to end the war under circumstances that might resemble the status quo of 1860." [Source: Seven Days Wikipedia; ABT 10 Facts]

**The cost of Lee's aggression:** The Seven Days were costly for both sides, but disproportionately for the Confederates — the army that could not afford it. Total Seven Days casualties: Union ~16,000 (1,734 killed, 8,066 wounded, 6,055 captured); Confederate ~20,000 (3,494 killed, 15,758 wounded, 952 captured). At Gaines' Mill specifically, the Confederates lost ~7,993 with far fewer captured (108) compared to Union losses of ~6,800–7,337. The pattern of frontal assault against fortified positions that produced enormous Confederate casualties was a feature of Lee's generalship throughout the war. [Source: Seven Days Wikipedia; ABT; Wikipedia Gaines' Mill]

**Second Bull Run and Antietam:** Lee's momentum from the Seven Days (especially Gaines' Mill and the broader campaign) set up the Second Manassas campaign (August 1862) and then the Maryland Campaign / Battle of Antietam (September 17, 1862) — the bloodiest single day in American history. [Source: Seven Days Wikipedia; standard campaign chronology]

**The "change of base" and Harrison's Landing:** The Union Army of the Potomac retreated to Harrison's Landing on the James River, where it sat for the remainder of the summer under naval protection. Lincoln visited the army there and received McClellan's "Harrison's Landing Letter" arguing against emancipation — a political document that helped drive the administration toward the Emancipation Proclamation despite McClellan, not because of him. [Source: Seven Days Wikipedia; Harrison's Landing search result]

## 9. Geography for a Map

Orientation: North up. The critical geographic axis is N–S: Confederate forces attacking from the north/northeast/east; Union position facing north/northeast; the Chickahominy River behind (south of) Porter's position.

**The Chickahominy River:** Runs roughly east–west (with curves) through the area. Lee's army was on both sides: the bulk of the Confederate attacking force (Hill, Longstreet, D.H. Hill, Jackson) north of the river; a screening force (Magruder, Huger) south of the river opposite McClellan's main body. Porter's V Corps was isolated on the NORTH bank, with its back to the river and the bridges. [Source: Wikipedia; ABT Battle Facts; Warfare History Network]

**Boatswain's Creek / Boatswain's Swamp:** The defining terrain feature of the battle. A boggy ravine running in a semicircular arc roughly 1.5–2 miles long. Porter deployed his three-tiered line immediately behind it. Confederates had to cross open ground, descend into the swamp, and charge up the steep hillside (~50-foot rise) under artillery and infantry fire. The creek runs roughly east–west / northeast–southwest before the plateau. [Source: Wikipedia; Warfare History Network; ABT; NPS ("swampy" ravine); Essential CW Curriculum]

**Watt House / Watt Farm:** The historic farmhouse on the plateau above Boatswain's Creek; the centerpiece of Porter's position and the focal point of the Confederate assault. Hood's Texas Brigade captured guns atop the Watt House plateau. The Watt House still stands within the Richmond National Battlefield Park's 60-acre core holding. [Source: NPS Richmond NBP; ABT Battle Facts]

**Adams Farm:** Another farm on the high ground near the Watt House, referenced in Porter's positioning. [Source: ABT Battle Facts]

**Old Cold Harbor (crossroads):** The road junction to the north/northeast of the battlefield where D.H. Hill's division arrived around noon–1 p.m. and where Jackson's column approached from. The name "Cold Harbor" here — predating the more famous 1864 battle — comes from an old English term for a roadside shelter offering no food or fire. [Source: Wikipedia — standard Civil War history]

**Gaines' Mill (the mill itself):** Located on Boatswain's Creek; the mill was owned by Dr. William F. Gaines; Porter's position was described as "on a plateau behind Boatswain's Swamp, just to the southeast of a mill owned by Dr. William F. Gaines." [Source: Wikipedia]

**The Chickahominy bridges:** Porter's V Corps retreated south across four narrow bridges spanning the Chickahominy during the night of June 27–28. Engineers destroyed the bridges behind them. The key bridge for Porter's planned withdrawal was the Grapevine Bridge (McClellan told Porter to "bring your corps across the Grapevine Bridge"). [Source: Warfare History Network; americancivilwar.com narrative; ABT Battle Facts]

**Confederate assault directions:**
- A.P. Hill and Longstreet: attacked from the west/northwest
- D.H. Hill: approached from the north via Old Cold Harbor Road
- Jackson: approached from the north/northeast, intending to turn Porter's right (eastern) flank
- Hood's breakthrough: occurred roughly in the center of the Union arc, in the sector facing west/northwest, near the junction with Longstreet's attack
[Source: Wikipedia; Warfare History Network; ABT battle maps referenced in search results]

**Preservation note:** The NPS owns 60 core acres including the Watt House. In 2011, 285 additional acres (the "Longstreet Attack" tract) were preserved. The American Battlefield Trust and partners have preserved 932 total acres of the ~2,000-acre battlefield. [Source: ABT 10 Facts; NPS Richmond NBP]

## 10. Contested / Uncertain Facts

### Casualty figures
The most consistently contested element. Multiple authoritative sources give slightly different numbers:

| Source | Union total | Union KIA | Union WIA | Union captured | CS total | CS KIA | CS WIA | CS captured |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wikipedia infobox | 7,337 | 1,005 | 3,107 | 3,236 | 7,993 | 1,483 | 6,402 | 108 |
| ABT Battle Facts summary | 6,800 | — | — | (~2,800 incl.) | 8,700 | — | — | — |
| Warfare History Network | 6,837 (KIA 894 + WIA 3,114 + capt. 2,829) | 894 | 3,114 | 2,829 | 7,993 | 1,483 | 6,402 | 108 |
| NPS Richmond NBP | 6,000 | — | — | — | ~9,000 KIA+WIA | — | — | — |
| Essential CW Curriculum | ~4,000 KIA+WIA + ~3,000 captured | — | — | — | ~8,700 | — | — | — |
| ABT 10 Facts (combined) | 15,500 total | — | — | — | — | — | — | — |

**Verdict:** The Wikipedia/Warfare History Network figure of ~6,837 Union / ~7,993 Confederate (total ~14,830) appears to be drawn from the Official Records or a careful tabulation. The commonly cited "15,500" total and "6,800 Union / 8,700 CS" are rounded figures widely used by ABT. The NPS figure of ~6,000 Union / ~9,000 CS KIA+WIA may count only killed and wounded (not captured). Use the ~6,837 / ~7,993 split with the note that rounded figures of 6,800 / 8,700 (total ~15,500) are also in wide circulation. Flag in narrative.

### Total Confederate forces engaged
Wikipedia infobox: 57,018. ABT narrative article: "60,000 to 65,000." NPS: "approximately 60,000." The 57,018 figure appears to come from the OR tabulation of forces engaged; the 60,000–65,000 figure may include forces available but not fully committed. Use 57,018 as the specific OR-derived figure; note the higher range exists.

### Porter's rank at Gaines' Mill
Wikipedia and ABT refer to Fitz John Porter as "Major General" in context. His commission as Major General of Volunteers was dated May 18, 1862, so he held that rank on June 27. [VERIFY: confirm commissioning date against official records]

### Naming: "Gaines' Mill" vs. "First Cold Harbor"
Both names are authentic. The battle is recorded in the OR under multiple designations. "Gaines' Mill" is the most common modern name (NPS, ABT, Wikipedia article title). "First Cold Harbor" was used by Confederates and appears in period documents and some official compilations; it distinguishes the site from the catastrophic Second Battle of Cold Harbor (June 1864). The crossroads of Cold Harbor is approximately 1 mile north of the Gaines' Mill position. The CWSAC designation uses "Gaines' Mill" as the primary name. [Source: Wikipedia; eHistory OSU ("Gaine's Mill (First Cold Harbor)"); ABT Facebook post; HMDB]

### Jackson's exact reasons for tardiness
Accounts give varying weight to: physical exhaustion/illness; civilian guide errors; wrong road requiring countermarch; destroyed bridges; muddy roads; enemy sharpshooters; supply/stragglers problems. The mix is consistent across sources but the relative weight of each factor is debated by historians. The countermarch delay is the most specifically cited (ABT: "led down the wrong road, which resulted in an hour-long countermarch"). No single authoritative source gives a definitive sequence. [VERIFY against Sears, *To the Gates of Richmond* (2001) — the leading secondary source on the Peninsula Campaign and Seven Days]

### McClellan's pre-battle decision to retreat
One source (ABT Battle Facts) states McClellan issued orders to retreat to the James River "in pre-dawn hours of June 27" — before the battle. Other accounts suggest the decision crystallized and hardened after hearing of Porter's difficulties on the 27th. The "change of base" idea was clearly forming before June 27, but the exact timing of the formal order matters for the narrative. [VERIFY against official orders and OR]

### Lee's quote to Hood ("This must be done")
Cited across multiple secondary sources but no primary-source citation confirmed in available research. Considered plausible and widely repeated; flag as [VERIFY primary source].

### Jackson's quote to Hood's men ("The men who carried this position were soldiers indeed!")
Similarly widely attributed in secondary literature; primary source unclear. [VERIFY]

### "Largest assault of the Civil War" claim
ABT 10 Facts states the 7 p.m. assault of ~32,000 men was "likely the largest of the Civil War," exceeding Pickett's Charge (~12,500) and Hood's Franklin attack (~20,000). This claim is consistently sourced to ABT and appears credible by comparison, but "largest assault" is a fraught metric (vs. largest battle, etc.). Note the qualifier "likely." [Source: ABT 10 Facts — reliable institutional source; flag the "likely" qualifier in narrative]

### "Second bloodiest battle" claim
NPS and ABT 10 Facts both call Gaines' Mill the "second bloodiest battle in American history" as of June 1862 (after Shiloh). This is a fair historical assessment for the period, though "bloodiest" rankings shift as the war progresses. Flag as historically accurate for the moment, not a permanent ranking.
