## FIRST-CHECK (ship-blocking)

### (a) Are Native nations first-class actors with their own aims, not props? — PASS / FAIR

The draft does this unusually well for a section nominally "about" the Acadians. The Mi'kmaq are introduced not as a sidebar but as the people whose land the whole quarrel sat on, and they get a dedicated section ("The Mi'kmaq were fighting their own war") whose entire thesis is their independent agency:

- "The land everyone was quarreling over was Mi'kma'ki, the homeland of the Mi'kmaq, who had lived there long before any European arrived and who had no intention of being settled over. They were allied to France, but the alliance served their own ends." — This is the textbook correct framing: alliance as leverage, not subordination.
- "Then they acted, on their own initiative and for their own reasons... These were not French operations; they were Mi'kmaq operations in defense of Mi'kmaq territory, led by their own chiefs." — Explicit anti-prop statement, naming Cope, Bâtard, and Laurent as their own leaders.
- The draft actively GUARDS against the most common erasure here: it refuses to let the French priest Le Loutre stand in for Mi'kmaq agency — "He was an instigator and an ally, but he was not Mi'kmaq, and the Mi'kmaq did not need him to tell them their land was being taken." This is precisely the failure mode the fact pack flagged (do not let Le Loutre absorb Mi'kmaq agency), and the draft beat it.
- The closing "Naming it" section returns to the Mi'kmaq as co-victims, refusing to let the Acadian tragedy crowd them out: "the people who lost the most were the ones who had been there longest: the Mi'kmaq, whose homeland was settled over and put under a bounty."

Web-verified: the Mi'kmaq land protest of 24 September 1749 through Father Maillard, and the framing of it as a declaration of Mi'kmaq ownership, are corroborated (Wikipedia, *Father Le Loutre's War*).

**FAIR.** Native nations are first-class actors with their own aims and leverage throughout.

### (b) Is the human cost / dispossession named in the section's OWN prose? — PASS / FAIR

Named plainly, repeatedly, and structurally — not buried in a number:

- Opening line frames it as atrocity: "The clearest atrocity of the French and Indian War was not a battle. It fell on farmers... Thousands died. The survivors never got their farms back."
- The mechanism of dispossession is concrete: burned houses/barns/crops to prevent return; families split across ships and "never found each other again."
- The death toll is carried in human terms (the Doiron family, ~120 people lost on the *Duke William*; the December 1758 wrecks) AND named honestly as uncertain: "thousands, and we cannot give the exact number."
- Mi'kmaq dispossession is named in its OWN prose: the scalp bounty ("a price on the heads of a people for living where they had always lived"), settlement on land "the British had promised not to settle."
- The land-contest thesis is stated outright: "The contest underneath all of it, the one the empires were really fighting, was over land that belonged to neither of them."
- The Acadian expulsion as the war's catastrophe for a civilian population is the spine of the whole section.

**FAIR.** The human cost and dispossession are named directly in the section's own voice.

**FIRST-CHECK RESULT: BOTH PASS.**

## SECONDARY FINDINGS (distortion hunt)

### F1 — "French and Indian War" naming / British-centric POV — FAIR
The directive asks that, where natural, the section acknowledge the war's name centers the British-colonial viewpoint and that "Indians" fought on all sides and for themselves. The draft does not contain an explicit "the colonists named the war for their enemies" line, but it does the substantive work: it foregrounds that the Mi'kmaq fought *for themselves*, names their own war (Father Le Loutre's War / Anglo-Mi'kmaq War) by its own name, and never reduces "Indians" to a French tool. The POV correction is carried in substance even though the etymological aside is absent. **FAIR — not a defect.** (See SHOULD-FIX S1 for an optional one-line strengthener.)

### F2 — False balance on the "ethnic cleansing / genocide" debate — FAIR
This is the highest-risk spot for false balance (using a scholarly label-debate to soften a documented atrocity). The draft handles it correctly:
- It states the fact FIRST and refuses to let the debate dilute it: "But it does not change what was done, or to whom, or how many never came home."
- It presents Faragher (ethnic cleansing) vs. Grenier/Basque/Johnston (objections) accurately. Web-verified: all four positions match the public record, including Basque's "does not apply at all" and Johnston's "you can't just transfer concepts between centuries."
- It includes the British military rationale (Acadians astride supply/intelligence lines) WITHOUT endorsing it as justification — it is presented as the cold logic the British used, immediately subordinated to "it does not change what was done."

This is the correct treatment: present the debate, do not adjudicate it, do not let it euphemize. **FAIR.**

### F3 — Romanticization of empire — FAIR
No glorification of British or French imperial projects. Britain is named as the actor that issued the bounty and ordered the burning; the orders are quoted in their own chilling plainness. France is not romanticized as protector either (the Acadians are shown wanting out of *all* the empires' wars). **FAIR.**

### F4 — Sanitized or gratuitous violence — FAIR
The violence (scalp bounty, burning, drownings, family separation) is named without euphemism AND without wallowing. The Doiron family set-piece is used to make numbers concrete, not for shock. Winslow's own discomfort is quoted but not used to launder him — the draft keeps him "acting under Lawrence's orders" and reading the sentence. **FAIR.**

### F5 — Presentism — FAIR
The draft explicitly flags the anachronism risk via Johnston's "you can't just transfer concepts between centuries" and does not impose modern legal categories as settled. It calls the event an "atrocity" (a moral, not a legal-anachronistic, term) which is defensible and matches the fact pack's "clearest atrocity" framing. **FAIR.**

### F6 — Acadian neutrality framed fairly — FAIR
The "neutral French" position is presented as a genuine bind, not naivety or treachery, and the draft is careful that the Acadians found at Beauséjour "had likely been pressed into service against their will" — refusing the British pretext that neutrality was "a fiction." This is the fair reading. **FAIR.**

## SHOULD-FIX (not ship-blocking)

**S1 (optional, naming-POV).** The directive's third prong — acknowledging that the war's *name* centers the British-colonial enemy's-eye view and that the "Indians" fought for themselves — is carried in substance but never said outright. One natural sentence (e.g. in the Mi'kmaq section or the closer) noting that the British colonists named this war for their enemies, while those "Indians" were fighting their own war for their own land, would close the loop the directive asks for. Substance is already present; this is a polish, not a gap.

**S2 (attribution care).** The draft uses the Lawrence Handfield "most vigorous measures... burning their houses" quote (well-sourced) and correctly AVOIDS the contested "Eye for an Eye" Commonwealth Museum quote the fact pack flagged. Good. No change needed — noting only that the draft made the right call here, so it should not be "improved" by adding the riskier quote later.

---

## VERDICT

**PASS.** Both first-check prongs pass: the Mi'kmaq are first-class actors with their own aims and leverage (and the draft explicitly defends that agency against the Le Loutre substitution trap), and the human cost / dispossession of both the Acadians and the Mi'kmaq is named plainly in the section's own prose. No DISTORTED findings; the highest-risk surface (the ethnic-cleansing/genocide label debate) is handled without false balance. Two SHOULD-FIX nudges, neither ship-blocking.
