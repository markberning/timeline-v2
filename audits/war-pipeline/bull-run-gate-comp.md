## MUST-ADD (Essential — blocking)

### §1 — Rose O'Neal Greenhow: the spy who gave Beauregard McDowell's plan

**Status in draft:** MISSING entirely.
**Status in fact pack:** Present (§6, Key People). Named with specific dates (July 9 and 16 dispatches), the courier (Betty Duvall), Davis's personal credit ("The Confederacy owes you a debt"), and Jordan's thank-you telegram.

**Why it's blocking:** The draft tells Bull Run as a story of armies, clocks, and railroads — all true. But it omits that Beauregard knew McDowell's plan *before the battle started*. That's not a footnote; it materially changes the shape of the Confederate response. Evans's speed in pulling from Stone Bridge and shifting to Matthews Hill, and Beauregard's ability to concentrate at the right moment, happened against a background of foreknowledge. The draft will mislead a reader who comes away thinking Confederate success was purely tactical improvisation.

This does NOT need to be a long passage — a single tight paragraph in the "Two armies and one railroad" or the "Flank March" section is enough. Something like: *Before either army took the field, a Washington socialite named Rose O'Neal Greenhow had already handed Beauregard the Union plan.* Then: what she passed, when, and what Davis later said. The draft's current framing — that Alexander's wig-wag signal was the key intelligence break — is incomplete without this.

**Fact needed not in pack:** The fact pack has everything required. No additional research needed.

---

### §2 — Beauregard's own attack plan against Centreville, never launched

**Status in draft:** MISSING.
**Status in fact pack:** Not in the fact pack either. This is a fact-pack blind spot (see below).

**Why it matters to the story:** One of the great ironies of Bull Run — acknowledged by Hennessey and by Wikipedia — is that Beauregard had his *own* flanking attack planned for the same morning, aimed at McDowell's left. Johnston arrived July 20 and approved it. Then McDowell attacked first, and Beauregard's plan was abandoned before it began. This is not just trivia: it underscores the "both armies green alike" theme at a command level. Two generals each planned to flank the other simultaneously, and the one who moved first held the initiative — and even McDowell only held it for a morning. It also explains why Beauregard's brigades on his right (Bonham, Longstreet, Jones) were slow to reinforce the left — they were positioned to attack, not defend.

This can be one sentence or a brief aside: the full irony of symmetry — both commanders planned the same move that morning.

**Fact needed not in pack:** Pack does not include Beauregard's offensive plan. Fact-pack needs this added. Sourced: Wikipedia ("First Battle of Bull Run"), Britannica, ABT.

---

### §3 — Jackson wounded in the hand during Henry House Hill fighting

**Status in draft:** MISSING.
**Status in fact pack:** Present (§6, Key People): "wounded in the hand; remained on field."

**Why it matters:** The draft's final line — "There stood Jackson like a stone wall — and Stonewall he would be until the day he died" — is a strong close. But it omits that Jackson built that legend while bleeding from a hand wound he sustained on the hill. This detail costs one sentence and adds significant texture to the character. A man who gave the order to "yell like furies" (or didn't — that ambiguity is already in the draft) and stood while others ran, while also bleeding, is a better story than the same man without the wound. It also foreshadows Chancellorsville 1863, where wounds on a battlefield will end Jackson's story — a connection readers will make themselves.

**Fact needed:** Already in the pack.

### §4 — The senator anecdotes during the rout

**Status in draft:** MISSING. (The senators are introduced in the "spectators" section, named, but their individual behavior during the rout is never revisited.)
**Status in fact pack:** Present (§5): Wade picked up a discarded rifle and threatened to shoot fleeing soldiers. Wilson had his buggy destroyed by a Confederate shell and escaped on a stray mule. Chandler tried to block the road to stop the retreat. Grimes narrowly avoided capture and vowed never to return to a battlefield.

**Why it's blocking:** The draft introduces four senators by name in the picnic section specifically to pay them off later — that's good setup. But it never pays off. The senators vanish from the narrative after the picnic paragraph. The reader has been primed to expect their fate, and the draft delivers only Alfred Ely's. The Wilson-on-a-mule detail alone is worth the paragraph: it is the comic/chaotic image that crystallizes what the battle did to Washington's self-image. These anecdotes also show the senators are not just passive witnesses — some tried to stop the rout, some barely escaped, one swore an oath of never again. That's the senators functioning as characters, not props.

This doesn't need all four stories; even two (Wade + Wilson) would discharge the setup made in the picnic section.

**Fact needed:** Already in the pack.

### §5 — Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War

**Status in draft:** MISSING.
**Status in fact pack:** Present (§7): "The Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War was established by Congress to investigate military affairs — partly a response to Bull Run."

**Why it matters:** The draft covers the operational aftermath (volunteers, McClellan, Army of the Potomac) well. The Joint Committee is the political/institutional aftermath — Congress responding to Bull Run by creating an oversight body that would interfere with military operations for the next four years. It's a legacy item, not just a footnote. One sentence in the "reckoning" section is sufficient. Without it the draft's picture of the long-war response is incomplete on the civilian-government side.

**Fact needed:** Already in the pack.

### §6 — Wilmer McLean: the man whose property bookended the war

**Status in draft:** MISSING entirely.
**Status in fact pack:** Present (§6, Key People) with the full story and the quote: "The war began in my front yard and ended in my front parlor."

**Why it's blocking:** This is one of the most compact, resonant ironies in American history, and it starts here. A Union cannonball drops through McLean's kitchen chimney during the Blackburn's Ford skirmish on July 18 — three days before the main battle. He moves his family to a quiet Virginia backwater called Appomattox Court House to escape the war. In April 1865, Lee surrenders to Grant in McLean's parlor. The story is made for a popular-history voice. It costs the draft one paragraph (or even just three sentences in the "what came before" section when Blackburn's Ford is mentioned). Leaving it out, when the fact pack has it in full, is the kind of omission that a sharp reader will notice and the draft will be worse for.

This is especially apt for this vertical's stated mission: "why we went to war" and "off-the-battlefield" are co-primary. A civilian who fled one battle and found the war had followed him to his new home is that theme made flesh.

**Fact needed:** Already in the pack. The quote wording varies slightly across sources — pack correctly notes this.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER (Enriching, not blocking)

### A — Wheat's Louisiana Tigers spoiling charge on Matthews Hill

The fact pack mentions Wheat was wounded; the story behind it (Tigers charged Burnside's brigade outnumbered ~6:1, briefly stopped them, buying Evans time) is a vivid small-unit story. Not essential — Evans's function in delaying is covered — but it adds texture to the Confederate improvised defense. One sentence.

### B — Beauregard promoted to full general by Davis on the day of the battle

Pack §6. Not in the draft. Odd detail — Davis hands out a promotion while the battle is being won. Low priority; could go in the "What It Meant" section if the author wants a grace note.

### C — Johnston was the ranking officer on the field — not Beauregard

The draft names both generals throughout as equal partners, which is functionally accurate for storytelling. But Johnston was technically in command once he arrived July 20, and this created lasting friction with Davis after the battle (the seniority dispute). This is more relevant to future chapters (Johnston vs. Davis is a recurring theme) than to the Bull Run story itself. Consider one sentence in the "What It Meant" section to seed the Johnston-Davis rift.

### D — Patterson's aftermath: relieved July 27, enlistment expired, never court-martialed

Pack §3. The draft notes Patterson "failed" but doesn't close the loop on what happened to him. A parenthetical note that he was simply let go — his enlistment expired, no court-martial — sharpens the "green armies, green commanders" theme. One clause.

### E — The name dispute itself: "Bull Run" (Union) vs. "Manassas" (First Manassas) (Confederate)

The draft uses "First Bull Run" throughout without acknowledging the Confederate naming. The tradition of North naming battles after water (Bull Run Creek), South after nearby town or station (Manassas), is a running motif across the whole war. Worth a parenthetical on first use: *(called First Manassas by the Confederacy — the South named battles after towns, the North after rivers, a split that would hold for four years)*. This is a low-cost detail that does real work for readers who will encounter both names going forward.

## Fact-Pack Blind Spots

These are items that a comprehensiveness review surface-checks as *potentially* significant but that the fact pack does not contain. The author should verify before adding any of these to the draft.

**1. Beauregard's pre-battle offensive plan (MUST-ADD §2 above)**
Pack entirely omits that Beauregard planned a flanking attack on McDowell's left for the same morning that McDowell attacked. This is a genuine gap in the pack. Source: Wikipedia "First Battle of Bull Run" (orders section); Hennessey; Britannica.

**2. The Blackburn's Ford skirmish (July 18) as a separate prior action**
The pack mentions Blackburn's Ford in the geography table and in the McLean entry, but the July 18 skirmish itself — Tyler's unauthorized probing attack that gave the Confederates three days of warning — is not analyzed as a prior action. It is the reason Johnston's last units were still arriving on July 21: the battle had been telegraphed days early. This is background context, not a MUST-ADD, but the pack should note its significance for any future section on McDowell's mistakes.

**3. Specific makeup of the spy dispatch — Betty Duvall as courier**
The Greenhow fact is in the pack, but the courier detail (Betty Duvall concealing a message in her hair, crossing Confederate lines on July 10) is not. This is the colorful hook that makes the Greenhow story vivid. Source: Warfare History Network; Wikipedia (Rose O'Neal Greenhow).

**4. The "yell like furies" order's post-war origin source**
Pack correctly flags this as post-war (1865 London *Times*, 1866 biography) with a [VERIFY]. But it doesn't provide what the *correct* contemporary/primary description of the rebel yell at Bull Run would be, if not Jackson's order. The pack should note whether any 1861-era account independently attests to the distinctive battle cry at this battle, or whether the whole "first-at-Bull-Run" attribution is itself secondary-source-dependent.

**5. The Beauregard promotion date — confirmation**
Pack §6 notes it as "day of battle" per Wikipedia with a flag to "confirm the exact date if precision matters." This is an unresolved loose end in the pack; a follow-up search against a primary Confederate military record would close it.

## Summary Assessment

The draft is strong on the structural spine: context → plan → morning success → Henry House Hill pivot → rout → reckoning. The voice is good. The coverage of the battles-within-the-battle (Matthews Hill, Henry House Hill, Cub Run) is solid and appropriately selective.

The six MUST-ADDs cluster in two areas:

**Area 1 — Intelligence and institutional context (§1 Greenhow, §5 Joint Committee, §6 McLean):** The draft underweights the pre-battle intelligence story and the full institutional aftermath. Greenhow is outright absent despite being credited by Davis himself. The McLean story is the most vivid "off-the-battlefield" detail in the whole battle and belongs here.

**Area 2 — Character payoffs (§3 Jackson's wound, §4 Senator anecdotes):** The draft sets up Jackson and the senators as characters, then doesn't fully close their arcs. Jackson's wound is a one-line addition; the senator anecdotes were clearly intended to be paid off (why else name all four in the picnic section?) and never are.

**Beauregard's offensive plan (§2)** is both a MUST-ADD and a fact-pack blind spot — neither the draft nor the pack covers it, and it's the deepest structural irony of the battle.
