# FACT PACK — Battle of Trenton, December 26, 1776

*American Revolution. ONLY source for the Trenton battle page. The story is the army's near-death and resurrection: enlistments expiring Dec 31 (the real clock), the storm crossing, the 45-minute (to ~2-hour) fight, and the myth-set to handle honestly (the Hessians were NOT drunk; the note-in-the-pocket story has a shaky chain; the read-Paine-to-the-troops tradition is unevidenced). Second Trenton/Assunpink kept brief — Princeton gets its own page.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- Battle of Trenton, morning of December 26, 1776, at Trenton, New Jersey (~40.22, -74.76), on the east bank of the Delaware River. Preceded by Washington's night crossing of the Delaware (Dec 25–26) at McConkey's Ferry, ~9 miles upstream/north of Trenton. [HIGH]
- Significance: the counterstroke that saved the Revolution's army-in-being. After the loss of New York and the long retreat across New Jersey, the Continental Army was dissolving — most enlistments expired December 31, 1776. Washington's surprise attack destroyed the Hessian garrison at Trenton at almost no battle cost, revived enlistments and morale, and set up Assunpink (Jan 2) and Princeton (Jan 3). [HIGH]
- Frame: a small battle with outsize consequences. Total engaged ~2,400 Americans vs ~1,400–1,500 Hessians; fighting in the town lasted roughly 45 minutes to two hours (see §10 uncertainties — "45 minutes" is the traditional figure for the decisive street fight; the full action ran longer). [HIGH on scale; duration MED — give as "about an hour and a half of fighting, the decisive phase under an hour"]

## 2. BACKGROUND — the December collapse
- 1776 had been a catastrophe since August: Long Island, the loss of New York City, Fort Washington (~2,800 captured, Nov 16), Fort Lee abandoned (Nov 20). Washington retreated across New Jersey through November–December with the army shrinking by expiring enlistments, desertion, and sickness; he crossed into Pennsylvania in early December, seizing/securing the boats on the Delaware so Howe could not follow at once. [HIGH]
- General Sir William Howe suspended campaigning for the winter (Dec 13–14), strung a chain of outposts across New Jersey (Perth Amboy–New Brunswick–Princeton–Trenton–Bordentown), and returned to New York. Trenton, the most exposed post, was garrisoned by a Hessian brigade under Col. Johann Rall. [HIGH]
- The same day Howe went into quarters, Gen. Charles Lee — Washington's senior subordinate, who had dawdled in joining him — was captured by British cavalry at Basking Ridge, NJ (Dec 13). Lee's division (under Sullivan) and Gates's regiments then joined Washington, giving him the strength to attempt something. [HIGH]
- **The real clock**: the bulk of the Continental Army's enlistments expired December 31, 1776. Washington wrote to Joseph Reed (Dec 23, from the camp above Trenton Falls): "Christmas day at Night, one hour before day is the time fixed upon for our Attempt on Trenton... necessity, dire necessity will — nay must justify any Attempt." Earlier (Dec 18, to his brother Samuel): "I think the game is pretty near up." (Quote chain in §8.) [HIGH]
- Thomas Paine, retreating with the army as a volunteer aide, wrote *The American Crisis* No. 1 ("THESE are the times that try men's souls"). **Publication chain (corrected by modern scholarship, per JAR/Smithsonian):** Paine said he "began it at Newark on the retreat and got it printed in Philadelphia the 19th of Dec." — i.e., it appeared as a Philadelphia printing ~Dec 19. The often-repeated claim that it ran first in the *Pennsylvania Journal* Dec 19 then as a pamphlet Dec 23 is WRONG: the *Journal* had suspended publication Dec 4, 1776–Jan 22, 1777. First confirmed newspaper appearance: *Pennsylvania Packet*, Dec 27, 1776 — AFTER the battle. [HIGH on the corrected chain]
- **The "read to the troops before the crossing" tradition**: traces to early-19th-century biography (Cheetham, 1809) and has NO contemporary documentary support. Possibly true, but historians can find nothing behind the assertion. [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — tell it as tradition, never as fact: "the story goes that officers read it to the ranks; no eyewitness says so."]
- New Jersey under occupation: plundering by British and Hessian troops (and the Dec 6 proclamation offering pardons for oaths of allegiance) first cowed, then enraged the population; by mid-December NJ militia bands were ambushing patrols around Trenton — this constant harassment is what exhausted Rall's garrison (see §4). [HIGH — Fischer's "rising of New Jersey" argument; standard]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS

### American — ~2,400 crossed with Washington, plus 18 guns [HIGH]
- **George Washington** (44), commander-in-chief. Six months of unbroken defeat behind him; he conceived the triple crossing and led the main column in person. He had told Reed "necessity, dire necessity" justified the gamble; his password for the operation was "Victory or Death" (chain in §8). After Trenton he personally appealed to the veterans to stay six weeks past their enlistments for a $10 bounty (~half stayed in the moment; the army held together into January). PORTRAIT: File:Brooklyn Museum - George Washington - Charles Willson Peale - overall.jpg (Peale, 1776) — verified. [HIGH; the bounty-appeal scene is well-sourced via Sgt. Nathaniel Root/contemporary accounts — MED on exact crowd response details]
- **Maj. Gen. Nathanael Greene** (34), Rhode Island Quaker-raised ex-ironmaster, self-taught soldier and Washington's most trusted subordinate despite bearing blame for the Fort Washington disaster a month earlier. Led the left/northern column down the Pennington Road to seal the town's upper end. Later the strategist who reconquered the South. PORTRAIT: File:Nathanael Greene by Charles Willson Peale.jpg (Peale, 1783) — verified. [HIGH]
- **Maj. Gen. John Sullivan** (36), New Hampshire lawyer's son, ex-delegate to the Continental Congress. Captured at Long Island and exchanged; took over Charles Lee's division when Lee was captured (Dec 13) and marched it to Washington. Led the right/southern column down the River Road to the lower town and the Assunpink bridge. PORTRAIT: File:Major General John Sullivan MET MM2147.jpg (1776 London print — an IMAGINED likeness, caption accordingly; see §9) — verified. [HIGH; portrait HANDLE-WITH-CARE]
- **Brig. Gen. Henry Knox** (26), the 280-pound Boston bookseller turned artillery chief who had hauled the Ticonderoga guns to Boston the winter before. Ran the embarkation/crossing (his booming voice ordering the boats) and massed an unusually heavy ratio of guns — 18 pieces for ~2,400 men — which decided the street fight: cannon at the heads of King and Queen Streets swept the Hessian formations. Promoted brigadier general for Trenton (commission dated Dec 27, 1776). PORTRAIT: File:Henry Knox by Peale.jpg (Peale, c. 1784) — verified; alt File:Henry Knox by Gilbert Stuart 1806.jpeg. [HIGH]
- **Col. John Glover** (44), Marblehead, Mass., fisherman-shipowner; his 14th Continental Regiment ("Marbleheaders," amphibious soldier-mariners who had already saved the army at Long Island in August) manned the Durham boats and ferries that put the army, horses, and 18 guns across the ice-choked river without a recorded loss of a man or gun in the crossing. PORTRAIT: NONE rights-safe verified (see §9) — fold into prose. [HIGH; portrait gap noted]
- **Lt. James Monroe** (18), future 5th US president, lieutenant in the 3rd Virginia: wounded at Trenton by a musket ball in the left shoulder that severed an artery; Dr. John Riker clamped it and saved his life. He and Capt. William Washington (the general's cousin, also wounded) had rushed the Hessian guns on King Street. PORTRAIT: File:James Monroe White House portrait 1819.jpg (Morse, 1819) — verified. [HIGH]

### Hessian — ~1,400–1,500 at Trenton, 6 guns [HIGH]
- **Col. Johann Gottlieb Rall** (~50, b. c. 1726 in Hesse-Kassel, a soldier's son), career soldier since 1740 — War of the Austrian Succession, the 1745 Jacobite campaign, the Seven Years' War, and a year with the Russians against the Turks (1771–72). Brave and battle-proven: at Long Island, White Plains, and Fort Washington (Nov 16), where he led the decisive assault (the captured fort was renamed Fort Knyphausen — for his superior, not for him). But he was contemptuous of the rebels ("nothing but a lot of farmers" — chain §8), spoke no English, and built no fortifications at Trenton despite his officers' urging and warnings from above, saying the bayonet would answer. Mortally wounded (shot twice in the side) leading his regiment's counterattack; died within a day at his headquarters, Stacy Potts's house; traditionally buried in an unmarked grave at Trenton's First Presbyterian Church. PORTRAIT: NONE — no authentic likeness survives (see §9). [HIGH on career/conduct; quote chains in §8; grave site MED]
- **Garrison**: three infantry regiments — **Rall's own (grenadier) regiment**, the **von Lossberg fusilier regiment**, and the **von Knyphausen fusilier regiment** — plus ~20 British light dragoons, some jägers, and 6 light field guns (2 per regiment). All three regimental commanders present were casualties or captured; Rall and Lossberg's Lt. Col. were mortally wounded (see §5). The brigade had fought well at White Plains and Fort Washington; it was an exposed post manned by tired, harassed troops, not a sleepy holiday garrison. [HIGH; minor-unit details MED]
- **Who the "Hessians" were**: ~30,000 German auxiliaries hired by Britain over the war from six principalities, more than half from Hesse-Kassel (hence the catch-all name). Not freelance mercenaries: their princes signed subsidy treaties with George III, renting out their standing regiments — Landgrave Friedrich II of Hesse-Kassel collected the money (plus "blood money" rates for the killed and wounded); the soldiers were conscripted/levied subjects serving for their ordinary pay. Trenton's captives were marched through Philadelphia (Dec 30–31) and later sent to the interior; a substantial number of Hessian POWs from the war ultimately stayed in America. [HIGH on system; "many stayed" keep general — MED on any number]

## 4. THE BATTLE

### The plan — three crossings, two failed [HIGH]
- Washington's plan for the night of Dec 25–26 was a triple crossing: (1) **Washington with the main body, ~2,400 + 18 guns, at McConkey's Ferry**, 9 miles above Trenton, to attack the town from the north; (2) **Brig. Gen. James Ewing with ~700 PA militia at Trenton Ferry**, directly opposite the town, to seize the Assunpink Creek bridge and cut the garrison's escape south; (3) **Col. John Cadwalader with ~1,500–1,900 (PA Associators + NE continentals) at Bristol/Dunk's Ferry downstream**, to demonstrate against/attack the Hessian-British post at Bordentown and pin reinforcements.
- **Both supporting crossings failed**: river ice (sheet ice along the Jersey shore downstream, where the tidal river behaved differently than at McConkey's) stopped Ewing entirely; Cadwalader got some infantry over but could not land his guns and recalled them. Only Washington's main force crossed — which left the Assunpink bridge open and let several hundred Hessians escape south (see §5). [HIGH]

### The crossing and the storm [HIGH]
- Christmas evening the weather broke into a nor'easter: rain turning to sleet, snow, and hail, with high wind and ice floes running in the 300-yard river. Glover's Marbleheaders and local watermen poled the army across in big Durham boats (40–60 ft cargo boats built for pig iron) and flat ferries for the horses and guns. Knox superintended the embarkation.
- The plan was to be across by midnight and attack before dawn; the storm put the army 3 hours late — last units and guns over ~3:00 a.m., march started ~4:00 a.m. Washington decided the delay made retreat more dangerous than attack and pressed on, accepting a daylight assault. [HIGH]
- Password: "Victory or Death." [HIGH — chain in §8]
- The famous human details with good sourcing: soldiers with broken shoes leaving blood on the snow (multiple contemporary mentions on the campaign; tag as reported by participants); two soldiers froze to death on the night march per the common account — Fischer treats the "frozen to death" deaths cautiously (see §5). [MED — phrase carefully]

### The march — two columns [HIGH]
- At Birmingham crossroads (~4.5 mi from Trenton) the army split: **Greene's division (with Washington) swung inland/left onto the upper Scotch Road → Pennington Road**, approaching Trenton from the northwest; **Sullivan's division took the right along the River Road**, hugging the Delaware to enter the lower town and grab the Assunpink bridge. They were to hit both ends of town simultaneously — and almost exactly did, ~8:00 a.m., within minutes of each other. [HIGH]
- Sleet/snow blew at the columns' backs (and into the defenders' faces). Some muskets were soaked; Sullivan sent word his powder was wet and Washington answered to use the bayonet ("...tell the General to use the bayonet, for the town must be taken" — popular wording; chain shaky, see §8). [Quote DISPUTED; the wet-powder/press-on exchange itself MED-HIGH]
- The accidental deception: a ~50-man Virginia party under Capt. Richard Clough Anderson (sent earlier by Gen. Adam Stephen WITHOUT Washington's knowledge) had raided a Hessian outpost on Christmas night — the very raid that made Rall think THE expected attack had already come and gone. Washington was furious at Stephen ("You, sir, may have ruined all my plans"— popular wording, chain MED). This accidental deception is why the garrison stood down on the worst possible morning. [Event HIGH (Fischer covers it); quote wording MED]

### The fight — ~8:00–9:30/10:00 a.m. [HIGH]
- ~8:00 a.m. Greene's vanguard struck the Hessian picket at the cooper shop (Richard Howell's) on the Pennington Road ~1 mile NW of town; Lt. Andreas Wiederholdt's outpost fired, fell back fighting ("Der Feind! Der Feind! Heraus!" — chain MED). Within minutes Sullivan hit the River Road picket; the two American columns rolled into both ends of town nearly simultaneously.
- Knox's guns deployed at the heads of **King and Queen Streets** (the two main streets, running downhill north→south to the Assunpink) and swept them; American infantry worked through houses and yards, firing from cover while the storm blew into the Hessians' faces.
- The three Hessian regiments tumbled out and tried to form: Rall's and Lossberg's regiments on lower King Street, Knyphausen's toward lower Queen Street. Rall — woken after the firing began — first ordered counterattack ("Forward! Advance! Advance!"), retook a pair of guns briefly; Capt. William Washington and Lt. James Monroe led the rush that took the Rall regiment's two cannon on King Street (both officers wounded there).
- Forced out of the streets by cannon and converging fire, Rall pulled the Rall + Lossberg regiments east into an apple orchard outside town and led a counterattack back toward King Street; in it he was hit twice in the side and fell from his horse, mortally wounded. His regiments, caught in the open in the orchard with artillery cutting them up and surrounded, struck their colors and surrendered. The Knyphausen regiment, trying to escape over the Assunpink, bogged in the marshy creek bank, lost time, and surrendered shortly after (~9:30–10:00 a.m.).
- Escapes: because Ewing never crossed, the Assunpink bridge was open early — the British dragoons, the jägers, and several hundred Hessians (mostly from the outposts and lower town, ~250–500; see §10) got away south toward Bordentown before Sullivan sealed the bridge. [HIGH on the escape happening; numbers MED]
- Duration: from first shots at the outposts to the last surrender, roughly 90 minutes–2 hours; the decisive fighting inside the town is traditionally compressed as "about 45 minutes." [MED — phrase as in §1]
- The mythic "drunk Hessians": **REFUTED.** No evidence of Christmas drunkenness; eyewitness/participant accounts describe no drunken or disorderly behavior, and the garrison turned out fast. What the evidence supports: the brigade was EXHAUSTED — weeks of NJ-militia ambushes and night alarms, sleeping in uniform with muskets at hand, a full-garrison turnout chasing the Christmas-night raid — and Rall, despite at least three credible warnings (a spy's report relayed by the British command, local Loyalist word, and American deserters), built no redoubts and dismissed fortification: "Let them come... We will go at them with the bayonet" (chain in §8). Grant's HQ even passed Rall a warning on Dec 25 that Washington might attack. [HIGH on the refutation and the exhaustion/no-fortifications picture]
- **The note in Rall's pocket**: the story — a Loyalist farmer brought Rall a written warning while he was at supper/cards at merchant Abraham Hunt's house on Christmas night; Rall pocketed it unread; it was found on him when he was dying. **Chain: this rests on later/secondhand Hessian accounts and local tradition, not on a surviving note; Fischer recounts it but its documentation is thin.** [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — tell as "the story the Hessians told afterward," not as established fact. See §10.]

## 5. CASUALTIES (ranges)
- **Hessian**: ~22 killed (incl. Rall mortally wounded; ranges 21–23), ~83–95 wounded, ~890–920 captured (ABT rounds 800–900+). Use: "about 22 killed, ~90 wounded, and roughly 900 captured." ~250–500 escaped over the Assunpink (number contested — see §10). All six guns, ~1,000 muskets, the regimental colors, wagons, supplies (and ~40 hogsheads of rum, which Washington ordered destroyed — mostly) taken. [HIGH with ranges]
- **American battle losses — famously tiny**: no one killed by enemy fire in the assault per the standard account; ~4–6 wounded (incl. Monroe and William Washington). Washington to Hancock (Dec 27): "Our loss is very trifling indeed, only two officers and one or two privates wounded." [HIGH]
- **But honestly**: the cold killed where the Hessians couldn't. The common account says two soldiers froze to death on the night march (some say more on the return); Fischer notes the evidence for the specific "frozen on the march" deaths is soft, while exhaustion/exposure/illness in the days after the double crossing genuinely raised American deaths — possibly above total Hessian dead. Phrase as: "almost no one fell to Hessian fire; the brutal weather and the double crossing cost lives in the days after — by some accounts more than the battle cost the Hessians." [MED — keep as ranges/qualified]

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- By noon Dec 26 Washington was recrossing to Pennsylvania with prisoners, guns, and stores — the army made the icy crossing a second (for many units third) time in 24+ hours, which is when much of the exposure toll hit. [HIGH]
- The ~900 prisoners were ferried over, marched to Newtown, then paraded through Philadelphia (Dec 30–31?) to enormous crowds — proof the invincible Hessians could be beaten; later moved inland (Lancaster etc.). [HIGH on the parade; exact date MED]
- Dec 30–31: Washington recrossed to Trenton AGAIN, and made his personal appeal to the expiring regiments — $10 bounty for six more weeks; enough stayed. Congress (Dec 27) had granted Washington emergency powers for six months. [HIGH]
- Jan 2, 1777: Cornwallis marched ~5,500 [check: ~8,000 total, ~5,500 effective at Trenton] down from Princeton; the day-long delaying fight on the road and the repulse at the **Assunpink Creek bridge** ("Second Trenton") held; that night Washington slipped around Cornwallis's flank by back roads and smashed his rearguard at **Princeton** (Jan 3) — KEEP BRIEF: Princeton gets its own page. The ten days Dec 25–Jan 3 ("the Ten Crucial Days") flipped the war's psychology; the British abandoned most of New Jersey, pulling in to New Brunswick/Amboy. [HIGH; Cornwallis numbers to verify on the Princeton pack]
- Frederick the Great allegedly called the campaign the most brilliant in military history — **APOCRYPHAL, do not use as fact.** [DISPUTED]
- British/Hessian reckoning: a Hessian court-martial (1778, ordered by the Landgrave) investigated the lost colors/conduct at Trenton; blame fixed on Rall (dead and unable to answer). Howe's outpost chain was condemned in London; Grant (who had dismissed the rebel threat) shared criticism. [HIGH on the court-martial existing; details MED]

## 7. THE GERMAN SOLDIERS' PERSPECTIVE (evidence level)
- See §3 "Who the Hessians were" for the subsidy system. Key honest points: they were subjects sent by their prince under treaty, not volunteers chasing loot contracts; Hesse-Kassel was one of Europe's most heavily militarized small states and soldier-subsidies were its main export earner; the soldiers themselves got ordinary army pay. [HIGH]
- They were good troops: the Trenton brigade had stormed Fort Washington six weeks earlier. American propaganda (and the Declaration's "large Armies of foreign Mercenaries") made "Hessian" a byword; Congress also ran a deliberate campaign offering land to Hessian deserters. [HIGH]
- Diaries/regimental journals (Wiederholdt's account, the Lossberg regiment journal, court-martial testimony) are the sources behind the modern not-drunk-but-exhausted picture. [HIGH]

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES (the honest-handling list)
1. **Hessian garrison size**: ~1,400–1,500 (ABT uses 1,400 in one place, 1,500 in another). Use "about 1,500."
2. **Hessian casualties**: killed 21–23 (22 standard), wounded 80–95 (83 common), captured ~890–920 (some say "nearly 900," ABT rounds 800–900). Escapees over the Assunpink: ~250–500 depending on whether outpost detachments are counted.
3. **American losses**: battle wounded ~4–6, none killed in action (standard); the "two frozen to death on the march" detail is in many accounts but Fischer treats the documentation as soft — the larger, well-supported point is the post-battle exposure/illness toll. Phrase qualified.
4. **Battle duration**: "45 minutes" (traditional, for the decisive street fight) vs ~90 min–2 hours first-shot-to-last-surrender. Don't print a single bare number.
5. **The note in Rall's pocket**: famous, possibly true, but rests on after-the-fact Hessian/local accounts (no surviving note); the Abraham Hunt card-party framing is tradition. Tell as "the story the Hessians told."
6. **The drunk-Hessian myth**: REFUTED — exhausted, not drunk; turned out fast and fought; multiple eyewitnesses note no drunkenness. (The 40 hogsheads of rum the Americans found — and Washington ordered destroyed — likely helped the myth along.)
7. **Paine chain**: pamphlet printed Philadelphia ~Dec 19; *Pennsylvania Journal* Dec 19 claim is WRONG (paper suspended); first newspaper run *Pennsylvania Packet* Dec 27 — after the battle. Read-aloud-to-the-troops = unevidenced tradition (Cheetham 1809).
8. **Rall's death timing**: night of Dec 26 vs Dec 27 (the Potts-house plaque and Wikipedia say he died the following day; ABT says that night). Use "died within a day."
9. **Rall's last words / humane-treatment request**: consistent tradition, no verbatim record — reported speech only. Washington's "glorious day" line = Wilkinson's memoir only.
10. **Quote translations from German** (Rall's "Let them come...", Wiederholdt's alarm): wording varies by translation; use as reported speech.
11. **Fort renamed for the Fort Washington victory**: the captured fort was renamed Fort KNYPHAUSEN (not for Rall), though Rall led the decisive assault — don't credit him with the renaming.
12. **Stephen-raid quote wording** ("You, sir, may have ruined...") — incident HIGH, sentence MED.
13. **Cornwallis's Jan 2 strength** (~5,500–8,000 depending on counting) — verify on the Princeton pack before printing.
14. **Frederick the Great's praise of the campaign** — APOCRYPHAL; never use as fact.
15. Knox letter dateline: transcribed both Dec 26 and Dec 28, and the "[not] unlike... last trump" line varies by transcription — quote exactly from one named transcription or paraphrase.
