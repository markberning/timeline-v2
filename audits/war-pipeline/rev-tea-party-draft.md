# The Tea Party & the Intolerable Acts
> eyebrow: Parliament tries to bail out a tea company and accidentally unites a continent

One small tax had been left standing in the law books, deliberately, as Parliament's flag planted in colonial ground (Chapter 3). The duty on tea was still there, three pence a pound, collected as the tea came into port. It collected almost nothing, because the colonies had largely stopped buying the legal, taxed tea and gone back to the smuggled kind. For three years that was where the matter sat, burning very low. Then, in 1773, Parliament did the one thing that could fan it back to life: it tried to make the colonists buy that taxed tea after all, by making it cheaper than the smuggled kind. It thought it was offering a bargain. The colonists saw a trap, and they were right.

To understand why a cheaper cup of tea could end with men throwing a fortune into the harbor, you have to start with the three quiet years before it, and with what was quietly being built during them.

## The lull breaks down
> eyebrow: A burned schooner, the judges' salaries, and a network wired between the towns

The calm of 1770 to 1773 was real, but it was not empty. Two things happened in it that put the question of colonial rights back on the table, and one thing got built in answer to them that would matter more than either.

The first was a fire in Rhode Island. The Royal Navy ran revenue schooners (small, fast warships used as customs patrol boats) up and down the American coast to catch smugglers, and one of them, HMS *Gaspee*, worked Narragansett Bay under a lieutenant named William Dudingston who was aggressive, thorough, and thoroughly hated by the locals. On 9 June 1772, chasing a packet boat called the *Hannah*, the *Gaspee* ran aground in the shallows off Gaspee Point near Warwick and stuck fast. Word raced up to Providence. In the small hours of the next morning a flotilla of longboats full of Providence men, led by the merchant John Brown and the ship captain Abraham Whipple, rowed out to the stranded schooner, boarded her, shot and wounded Dudingston, put his crew ashore, and burned the *Gaspee* to the waterline.

The burning was a serious crime, but it was not the part that frightened people. The frightening part was London's response. A royal commission of inquiry was set up to find the men who had done it, and it was empowered to ship suspects across the ocean to be tried in England, away from any Rhode Island jury that might know them and refuse to convict. This was the old vice-admiralty nightmare (Chapters 1 and 2) made flesh: an Englishman dragged from his own town and tried three thousand miles from any neighbor who could speak for him. As it happened, Rhode Islanders simply stonewalled the commission, which could not gather enough evidence and gave up. Nobody was ever prosecuted. But the threat had been made out loud, and it traveled.

The second thing was about money, and about a purse-string. Word reached Massachusetts in the fall of 1772 that the Crown would now pay the salaries of the colony's senior judges directly out of London funds, the same trick that had already been used on the governor (Chapter 3). For generations the assembly had voted those salaries itself, year by year, which gave it leverage over the men on the bench. A judge who held his commission "during pleasure" (removable whenever the Crown wished) and now drew his pay from London depended on London for, as John Adams later put it, "Bread as well as office." Chief Justice Peter Oliver and his four associate judges first took half-pay from the province while waiting on the Crown's share. Under public pressure through 1773 the associates backed away from the Crown salary, but Oliver refused to, and the Massachusetts House began moving toward impeaching him. First juries, with the *Gaspee*. Now judges. The lull was over in everything but name.

Here is the thing that got built in answer. At a Boston town meeting that November, Samuel Adams moved to create a standing Committee of Correspondence, a permanent body whose job was to state the rights of the colonists in writing and send that statement out to every other town. Joseph Warren was a key collaborator. The idea was deceptively plain. Instead of each town reacting to each crisis on its own, a few hours late and out of step with its neighbors, the towns would be wired together by a constant exchange of letters, so that the next outrage could be answered everywhere at once, in one voice. More than a hundred Massachusetts towns formed answering committees within months. Then the idea jumped the colony line. In March 1773 the Virginia House of Burgesses created the first standing intercolonial committee of correspondence, naming men whose names would soon be famous, among them Peyton Randolph, Richard Henry Lee, Patrick Henry, and Thomas Jefferson. By February 1774 eleven colonies had committees; with Pennsylvania's the following spring, every colony that would one day rebel had one. The resistance had grown a nervous system. When Parliament next reached out to touch the colonies, the colonies could now flinch as one body.

A private scandal added one more reason to trust nobody in the royal establishment. Benjamin Franklin, serving in London as agent for the Massachusetts House, had got hold of some old private letters that Thomas Hutchinson, by now the royal governor of Massachusetts, had written to a correspondent in England years before. In one of them Hutchinson had argued that the colonies, at their distance from Britain, could not keep every English liberty, that there must be "an abridgment of what are called English liberties." Franklin sent the letters to Boston meaning them to be shown only in private; Samuel Adams saw to it that they were published in full in June 1773. To patriot readers it was a confession that the governor himself had been quietly arguing in London for cutting their rights down, and the House petitioned the Crown to remove him. Boston was in no mood, that autumn, to take anything on faith from Thomas Hutchinson. It was about to have to deal with him over a cargo of tea.

## The trap baited as a bargain
> eyebrow: A bankrupt company, a mountain of tea, and the cheapest possible way to surrender a principle

To see why the Tea Act of 1773 was a trap, you have to start with a company that had nothing to do with America and everything to do with why the tea was coming.

The East India Company was the gigantic, half-governmental trading corporation that ran Britain's commerce with India and China, and in 1773 it was sliding toward collapse. It owed the British government £400,000 a year by agreement, it had been hammered by famine and war in Bengal and by a credit panic the year before, and it was sitting on something like seventeen or eighteen million pounds of tea, unsold, rotting in British warehouses, because cheap smuggled Dutch tea had eaten its American market alive. The company was too big and too entangled with the state to be allowed to fail. Parliament propped it up with a massive government loan and, crucially, with a plan to get that warehouse mountain of tea moving.

That plan was the Tea Act, which received royal assent on 10 May 1773, and its mechanics are worth getting exactly right, because the whole crisis turns on them. Before the Act, company tea took a long, expensive road to an American teacup: the company sold it at auction in London to British wholesalers, who sold it on to colonial merchants, who sold it to shopkeepers, with everyone taking a cut and a duty paid along the way. The Tea Act cut that road short. For the first time the company could ship its tea directly to America in its own name and sell it through a handful of hand-picked colonial agents called consignees (local merchants chosen by the company to receive and sell its tea on commission). The various duties the tea would have paid in Britain were refunded or waived. With the middlemen and the British duties stripped out, the price fell, and fell far enough that legal company tea would now undersell the smuggled Dutch tea Americans had been drinking.

And that is the trap. Nobody was being squeezed. Colonists were being offered cheaper tea than they had ever had. But there was a catch sewn into the price, and it was the one duty the Act deliberately left in place: the three-pence-a-pound Townshend tax, still collected in the colonial port as the tea landed. So every cheap, legal cup now carried that tax inside it, and to drink it was to pay the tax, and to pay the tax was to concede, with your own money, that Parliament had the right to tax the colonies whenever it pleased. This was the Declaratory Act's bare principle (Chapter 2) dressed up as a discount. Lord North had kept the tea duty precisely as a token of the right to tax (Chapter 3); now he had built a clever little machine to make Americans swallow it voluntarily, by making refusal cost them money. The colonists read the machine for exactly what it was.

The Act also united two groups who did not usually march together: men of principle and men of money. The Whig argument (the colonial constitutional case against being taxed without consent) objected to the duty outright. But the merchants had their own reason to hate the Act, and it had nothing to do with principle. By selling only through its chosen consignees, the company had cut every other colonial tea merchant out of the trade in one stroke, the honest importers and the smugglers alike, the whole world of men like John Hancock. The legal merchant feared a monopoly, the smuggler feared cheap legal tea drowning his business, the Whig feared the principle, and the Tea Act fused all three fears into one opposition. That autumn the company sent its tea to four ports, Boston, New York, Philadelphia, and Charleston, and named its consignees in each. In Boston the list included Richard Clarke and his sons, Benjamin Faneuil, Joshua Winslow, and, pointedly, the governor's own two sons, Thomas Jr. and Elisha Hutchinson. Of all the consignees in all the ports, Boston's were the ones who would refuse to back down, because the governor would not let them.

## The standoff at Griffin's Wharf
> eyebrow: A town that won't let the tea land, a governor who won't let the ships leave, and a clock running for both

The fight began before the tea arrived, with the consignees. In every other port the company's agents, faced with the town's anger, simply resigned, and that ended it. Boston's would not. Early in November the Sons of Liberty sent the Clarkes an anonymous summons to appear at the Liberty Tree and resign publicly; the consignees did not come. A crowd led by William Molineux went to Clarke's warehouse instead and tried to force the counting-room door, and on 17 November a crowd attacked Richard Clarke's house. Still they would not resign. Stiffened by Hutchinson, whose own sons were among them, the consignees instead took refuge at Castle William, the British fort on a harbor island, and waited. That refusal is why Boston, alone of the four ports, ended where it did.

Then the ships came in. There were three of them, and it matters that all three were ordinary colonial-owned merchant ships that happened to be carrying the company's tea as freight, not company vessels. The *Dartmouth*, owned by the Rotch family of Nantucket (Quaker whaling merchants), arrived on Sunday, 28 November 1773, carrying 114 chests of tea. The *Eleanor*, owned by the Boston merchant John Rowe, came in on 2 December with another 114. The *Beaver*, also Rotch-owned, reached the outer harbor about a week later but was held off at Rainsford Island under smallpox quarantine before she could come up to the wharf around 15 December, carrying 112 chests. All three ended up tied at Griffin's Wharf. A fourth tea ship, the *William*, never made it: she wrecked in a storm off Cape Cod on 10 December, and her chests were salvaged ashore.

Now the squeeze. British customs law worked on a clock. Once a ship formally entered port and reported her cargo, the law gave twenty days to land that cargo and pay the duty on it. If the twenty days ran out with the duty unpaid, customs officers could seize the cargo themselves, and seized tea could then be landed under the guns of the warships in the harbor, the duty effectively collected. For the *Dartmouth*, the first ship in, that clock ran out in the middle of December, with the deadline commonly reckoned at the 17th, which made the night of the 16th the last chance to do anything.

What made it a genuine trap was that the obvious escape was blocked at both ends. The town's demand was simple: send the ships back to England with the tea still aboard, duty unpaid, and let the whole thing return to sender. But a ship that had formally entered port could not legally clear outward (get the customs permission to depart) until her duty was paid, and the duty was the one thing the town would not allow. The one man who could grant a pass to let the ships slip past Castle William and the warships and go home unpaid was Governor Hutchinson, and Hutchinson, who had law and family and a long record of backing down on his side, flatly refused. So the tea could not land, because the town would not let it. The ships could not leave, because the governor would not let them. And the clock ran on regardless of what anyone wanted.

The town met the standoff with mass meetings unlike ordinary town meetings. Town meetings were for qualified voters; these were open to anyone, and they called themselves "the Body of the People." The day after the *Dartmouth* docked, a meeting called at Faneuil Hall overflowed, thousands came, and it moved to the Old South Meeting House, the largest building in Boston. The Body resolved that the tea must go back without a penny of duty paid, and posted an armed watch of twenty-five men on the ships to make sure not one chest was quietly landed in the night. The meetings went on through the first half of December as the clock wound down.

On 16 December the *Dartmouth*'s twenty days were up. Somewhere between five and seven thousand people, in a town of about sixteen thousand, packed into and around Old South in a cold rain for the last meeting. They sent the *Dartmouth*'s owner, young Francis Rotch, riding out one last time to Hutchinson's country house at Milton to beg for the pass that would let his ship go home. Rotch rode back after dark with the answer everyone expected: the governor had refused. By the famous account, Samuel Adams then rose and said this meeting could do nothing more to save the country, the words long retold as a prearranged signal to launch what came next. That signal story is doubted now (the line first surfaced in print a century later, and witnesses said people did not move until ten or fifteen minutes after the remark), so it is best taken as a thing Adams may have said rather than the starting gun of legend. However it began, war whoops sounded at the door, and the crowd spilled out of Old South into the dark and down toward Griffin's Wharf.

## The destruction of the tea
> eyebrow: Three hours, three ships, and a discipline that was the whole point

What happened next took about three hours and was, for a riot, astonishingly orderly.

Beginning around six or seven in the evening on Thursday, 16 December 1773, parties of men boarded all three ships at Griffin's Wharf, broke open the holds, hauled the chests up on deck, split them with hatchets, and tipped the tea and the broken chests into the harbor. It was low tide, so the wet tea piled up in the shallows rather than washing away. When it was done they had destroyed more than 340 chests (the traditional figure is 342; the three ships' manifests of 114, 114, and 112 add up to 340). That came to over 92,000 pounds of tea, around 46 tons of it, worth £9,659 in the money of the day, a fortune, the cargo of a small fleet poured into the sea. The tea was all Chinese: by the surviving breakdown, 240 chests of Bohea, 60 of Singlo, 15 of Congou, 15 of Hyson, and 10 of Souchong, black and green together.

The men who did it wore a disguise, and the disguise needs handling with some care, because it is so often gotten wrong. They wrapped themselves in blankets worn like coats, smeared their faces dark with soot and lampblack, and carried hatchets they called tomahawks, costuming themselves loosely as "Mohawks." This was not an attempt to fool anyone into thinking actual Mohawk people had done it, and no Mohawks were anywhere near Boston. Everyone present knew perfectly well who these men were, neighbors all. The disguise hid individual faces, because what they were about to do was a hanging-level crime against property the Crown protected, and it carried a meaning besides: to costume yourself as a person of this continent, an American, rather than a Briton. They were not many of them men of standing. A great number were young, apprentices and journeymen, sixteen of the documented participants still in their teens. Estimates of how many took part run from thirty or so core organizers up to a hundred and thirty or more once volunteers from the meeting joined in; about 116 are documented by name, though that count leans heavily on claims and family memories recorded long after.

The discipline is the part that lasted, and it was deliberate. Nothing but the tea was touched. There was no looting and no damage to the ships, and the men swept the decks clean when they finished. One padlock got broken, a ship captain's private property, and a replacement was sent to him the next day. When one man tried to stuff tea into the lining of his coat to keep, the others stripped it off him and roughed him up: this was destruction, not theft, and theft would have ruined the meaning of it. No one was killed. The only real injury was to a man named John Crane, knocked cold by a falling crate and at first taken for dead, hidden under wood shavings, who came around later. And every man there swore himself to secrecy, a vow most of them kept for decades and many to the grave. Only one participant was ever even arrested, and no one was ever convicted.

The strangest detail is what did not happen. Royal Navy warships lay close by in the harbor, well within range, and watched the whole thing without firing a shot. No order came to attack townspeople committing what was, on the surface, three hours of vandalism in a crowded port. One participant remembered it six decades later: "We were surrounded by British armed ships, but no attempt was made to resist us."

> quote: There was no other Alternative but to destroy it or let it be landed. — John Adams, diary, 17 December 1773

John Adams wrote that the next morning, and it is the clearest window we have into how even a cautious, lawyerly patriot squared this with himself. Landing the tea, he reasoned, meant "giving up the Principle of Taxation by Parliamentary Authority, against which the Continent have struggled for 10 years," and the ships could not get past the fort and the warships to take it home, so there was nothing else for it. He did not flinch from what had been done; he exulted in it. "This is the most magnificent Movement of all," he wrote. "There is a Dignity, a Majesty, a Sublimity, in this last Effort of the Patriots, that I greatly admire." He called it "an Epocha in History." That a man as careful as Adams reached for words like majesty and sublimity over the wholesale destruction of private property tells you how completely the principle had come to outweigh the property in patriot minds. It did not look that way to everyone. To Englishmen across the ocean, and to no few Americans, this was simply a town destroying a private company's goods and then refusing to pay for them.

One thing nobody called it that night was the Boston Tea Party. To the people who did it and the people who heard about it, it was "the destruction of the tea." The jaunty name we use would not show up in print for another fifty years or so, in the 1820s or 1830s, long after the men who waded through the wet tea were old or gone.

## The other ports
> eyebrow: The same tea, four cities, and only one of them ending in the harbor

Boston was not the rule. It was the exception, and the difference is the whole point: the same company tea went to four ports, and only in Boston was any of it destroyed, because only in Boston did the consignees refuse to resign and the governor refuse to bend. The other three cities show what the normal outcome looked like.

In Charleston the ship *London* arrived on 3 December 1773. The consignees were pressured into resigning, and when the twenty days ran out with nobody to claim the tea or pay its duty, the customs collector simply seized the 257 chests for non-payment and locked them in the cellars of the Exchange building. There they sat, harmlessly, until the revolutionary government later sold the tea off to help fund the cause. No harbor, no hatchets.

In Philadelphia the *Polly* came up the Delaware in late December carrying a much larger load, 697 chests. The river pilots and the captain had been warned off by handbills from a committee that promised to tar and feather anyone who helped land the tea. A mass meeting of perhaps eight thousand people on 27 December, one of the largest gatherings the colonies had yet seen, resolved that the tea would not land. The consignees had already quit. Captain Ayres took the hint, reprovisioned his ship, and sailed the *Polly* and all 697 chests straight back to England.

New York's turn came late, because its tea ship had been blown off course. The *Nancy* did not reach Sandy Hook until 18 April 1774, by which time her consignees had resigned months earlier; her captain was persuaded, under threat, to turn around and sail for England without unloading. A few days later another ship arrived claiming to carry no tea at all, but New Yorkers, tipped off from Philadelphia, found 18 chests the captain was smuggling in on his own account and dumped those in the harbor, a small New York tea party of its own. The pattern, in three cities out of four, was resignation and turn-back. Boston became the flashpoint not because Bostonians were braver but because Hutchinson and his consignee sons had made retreat impossible there, and the clock had done the rest.

## Parliament's answer
> eyebrow: The Coercive Acts, one by one, and the long-planned law the colonists folded in with them

News of the destruction reached London in January 1774, and it landed badly even with the friends of America. This was not a tax to argue over; it was the wanton destruction of a private company's property by a town that then declined to pay for it. Franklin himself thought Boston ought to make restitution. Lord North's government resolved to make an example, not of all the colonies, but of Boston and Massachusetts specifically, to cut the radicals off and isolate them.

It began with Franklin, who happened to be the most prominent American in London and had just been exposed as the man who leaked Hutchinson's letters. Two days after the news broke, on 29 January 1774, Franklin was made to stand for the better part of an hour in the Privy Council chamber known as the Cockpit while the Solicitor-General, Alexander Wedderburn, flayed him before a packed and jeering audience. Wedderburn called Franklin a "true incendiary" and the "first mover and prime conductor of this whole contrivance." Franklin stood through it in silence and said nothing in his own defense. The next day the government stripped him of his post as deputy postmaster-general for North America. He wrote later: "I made no justification of myself, but held a cool, sullen silence, reserving myself to some future opportunity." The man humiliated in the Cockpit had gone to London as one of the empire's great believers in itself. He did not stay one.

Then came the laws. Across the spring of 1774 Parliament passed a series of acts to punish Boston and bring Massachusetts to heel, what Americans would later come to call the Intolerable Acts (the men who passed them called them the Coercive Acts, and that is what they were in 1774). They came one at a time.

The **Boston Port Act**, given royal assent on 31 March 1774 and in force from 1 June, closed the port of Boston outright. No ship could load or unload there until the town paid the East India Company and the customs for the destroyed tea and the King was satisfied that order had returned. Customs business was moved up the coast to Salem and Marblehead. The Act passed the Commons without even a recorded vote against it and the Lords unanimously, a measure of how completely British opinion had turned against Boston.

The **Massachusetts Government Act**, on 20 May 1774, went after something far larger than a harbor: it rewrote the colony's charter, the 1691 document that had governed Massachusetts for over eighty years. The Council, the upper house of the legislature, had been elected; now the Crown would appoint it. Judges, sheriffs, and most officials would be appointed by the governor. Juries would be summoned by those appointed sheriffs. And town meetings, the bedrock of New England self-government, were limited to one a year unless the governor granted permission for more. This was the Act that frightened the other colonies most, and for an obvious reason. Boston could be told it had earned its punishment by destroying property. But if Parliament could simply abolish the elected government of one colony by a vote in Westminster, it could abolish any colony's, and no charter anywhere was safe.

The **Administration of Justice Act**, also of 20 May 1774, let the governor move the trial of a royal official charged with a capital crime, committed while putting down a riot or enforcing the revenue laws, to another colony or to Britain. The stated purpose was to ensure that such officials could get a fair trial where local feeling ran against them, and the Act even provided for witnesses' travel costs. Colonists read it the other way around and called it the "Murder Act," the charge being that a royal official could now kill a colonist and be whisked away to a friendly court where no local jury would ever judge him.

The **Quartering Act of 1774**, on 2 June, applied to all the colonies and let a governor take over unoccupied buildings to house troops when the barracks would not serve. It is worth being precise here, because the schoolbook image is wrong: this Act did not put soldiers into people's occupied homes. It reached empty buildings, not private parlors.

And then there was the **Quebec Act**, which received royal assent on 22 June 1774, and which was not actually part of the punishment at all. It was a long-planned settlement for the formerly French Canada that Britain had conquered, and on its own terms it was a tolerant one: it allowed the free practice of Catholicism, restored French civil law (which meant no jury in civil cases), set up government by an appointed council with no elected assembly, and extended Quebec's boundary south and west to the Ohio and Mississippi rivers, over land that Virginia and other colonies claimed for themselves. From Canada's side it was a generous accommodation of a conquered people. From the side of American Protestants it read as three insults stacked together: an established Catholic Church on their border, a government with no elected assembly, and a land-grab swallowing the west they wanted. Because it came out of the same session as the punishments, the colonists simply lumped it in with them as a fifth Intolerable Act, whatever Parliament had actually intended by it.

## Boston under the blockade, and the call that followed
> eyebrow: A general for a governor, relief by the shipload, and the colonies deciding to meet

To enforce all this, London changed who ran Massachusetts. On 13 May 1774 General Thomas Gage, the commander-in-chief of the British army in North America, landed in Boston as the colony's new royal governor. A general had replaced a civilian, which told the town exactly what kind of government it was now under. Thomas Hutchinson, who had refused the pass and held the line through the whole tea crisis, sailed for England on 1 June. He never saw America again.

That same 1 June the Boston Port Act took effect and the Royal Navy sealed the harbor. The day was marked across the colonies as a day of mourning, with fasts, tolling bells, and flags at half-mast, not just in Boston but in towns that had never seen a tea ship. Gage moved the seat of government up to Salem as the Act required, and Boston, a port city whose whole life ran through its wharves, was cut off from the sea.

And here the punishment did the opposite of what it was designed to do. It had been meant to isolate Boston and frighten the other colonies into keeping their distance. Instead the committees of correspondence (built two years earlier for exactly this moment) carried Boston's appeal out across the continent, and relief came pouring back in. It came overland and through Marblehead, the legal landing point, for Boston's Committee of Donations to distribute: grain and rye and corn by the thousands of bushels, rice shipped up from South Carolina, sheep and cattle driven down from Connecticut and across New England, flour, fish, and money. By the end of the year more than a hundred relief shipments had arrived. A town that London had tried to make a cautionary example of was instead being fed by every colony that watched it happen. The Coercive Acts had answered a Boston crime with a Massachusetts-wide demolition of self-government, and every colony had noticed that the demolition could just as easily come for them.

So the colonies began, separately and then together, to reach for the one answer big enough to meet a continental threat: a meeting of them all. In Virginia, when the royal governor dissolved the House of Burgesses on 27 May for daring to declare a day of fasting in sympathy with Boston, the burgesses walked down the street to the Raleigh Tavern in Williamsburg and proposed there an annual congress of all the colonies. The committees carried the idea from colony to colony through the early summer.

In Massachusetts it came to a head behind a locked door. On 17 June 1774 the House was meeting in Salem when it took up the question of sending men to such a congress. Gage got wind of it and sent the province secretary, Thomas Flucker, with a proclamation dissolving the assembly before it could act. The House had the door locked. Flucker stood on the stairs outside and read the dissolution to a door that would not open, while inside, the members went ahead and voted: five delegates, Thomas Cushing, Samuel Adams, John Adams, Robert Treat Paine, and James Bowdoin, to meet the other colonies at Philadelphia that September. The order to disband had arrived too late, and a closed door had kept it out just long enough.

The delegates would gather in Philadelphia in the fall. What they would do once they got there was, in the summer of 1774, still anyone's guess.

### Section: "The standoff at Griffin's Wharf" (pack §3)
- Consignees pressured first: anonymous Sons of Liberty summons to Liberty Tree to resign early November; they didn't appear; Molineux-led crowd at Clarke's warehouse forced counting-room door; 17 Nov crowd attacked Richard Clarke's house; consignees refused to resign, stiffened by Hutchinson, took refuge at Castle William. (§3 HIGH on spine; Castle date kept loose per pack MED)
- Three ships ordinary colonial-owned merchantmen carrying company tea as freight. (§3 HIGH)
- *Dartmouth* (Rotch family, Nantucket Quaker whaling merchants) arrived Sun 28 Nov 1773, 114 chests. *Eleanor* (John Rowe) 2 Dec, 114. *Beaver* (Rotch) held at Rainsford Island smallpox quarantine, up to wharf ~15 Dec, 112. All to Griffin's Wharf. (§3 HIGH)
- *William* wrecked off Cape Cod 10 Dec 1773; chests salvaged ashore (kept to one line, fate vague per pack MED). (§3)
- 20-day customs clock: 20 days after formal entry to land cargo + pay duty, then customs could seize; seized tea landed under the warships' guns. *Dartmouth*'s clock out mid-December, deadline commonly the 17th, night of the 16th the last chance. (§3 HIGH mechanism; exact day kept loose per CARE #12)
- [GLOSS-PACK] "clear outward (get the customs permission to depart)" — defines customs clearance, supported by §3 ("could not legally clear outward without paying").
- The three-way bind: tea can't land (town), ships can't leave (Hutchinson refused the pass past Castle William + warships), clock runs regardless. (§3 HIGH — the engine)
- Mass meetings "the Body of the People," open to anyone not just voters; day after *Dartmouth* docked Faneuil Hall overflowed (thousands) → moved to Old South (largest building); resolved tea must go back duty-free; posted 25-man armed watch; meetings continued. (§3; two-day structure compressed per pack MED)
- 16 Dec: 5,000–7,000 people (town ~16,000) at Old South in cold rain; sent owner Francis Rotch to Hutchinson at Milton for a pass; Rotch back after dark, refused. (§3 HIGH; 5:45 rendered "after dark" per pack MED)
- Samuel Adams "this meeting can do nothing further to save the country" handled as reported speech + signal-legend doubt flagged (printed a century later, people moved 10–15 min later). (DISPUTED, QUOTE #4 / CARE #4)
- War whoops at the door, crowd out toward Griffin's Wharf. (§3 HIGH)

### Section: "The destruction of the tea" (pack §4)
- Evening Thu 16 Dec 1773, ~6–7 p.m. start, ~3 hours; boarded all three ships, broke holds, hauled + split chests with hatchets, tipped tea + chests into harbor; low tide so tea piled in the shallows. (§4 HIGH)
- More than 340 chests (342 traditional; 114+114+112=340 manifests). Over 92,000 lbs, ~46 tons, £9,659 in period money; value kept in 1773 money, "a fortune… cargo of a small fleet," no modern-dollar figure (CARE #6). (§4 HIGH; chest count DISPUTED-range per pack)
- Tea all Chinese: 240 Bohea, 60 Singlo, 15 Congou, 15 Hyson, 10 Souchong (black + green). (§4 HIGH; sums to 340)
- "Mohawk" disguise: blankets matchcoat-style, soot/lampblack faces, hatchets ("tomahawks"); thin symbolic cover NOT impersonation, no Mohawks near, hid individual identity (hanging-level offense) + symbolized American identity. Handled per CARE #2 (not played for comedy, not "dressed as Indians" unframed, no implication Natives involved/blamed). (§4 HIGH)
- Many participants young, apprentices/journeymen, sixteen documented teenagers; counts 30–60 core to 130+, ~116 named via later claims/family memory (hedged per CARE #5/#12). (§4)
- Discipline: nothing but tea touched, no looting, decks swept; one padlock (captain's property) broken + replaced next day; coat-lining thief stripped/roughed up; no deaths; John Crane knocked cold by a falling crate, taken for dead, recovered (Crane MED, used as color); secrecy vow kept decades; only one ever arrested, none convicted. (§4 HIGH; Crane + arrest details per pack MED)
- Royal Navy watched, did not fire; Hewes recollection "We were surrounded by British armed ships, but no attempt was made to resist us." attributed + dated "six decades later" per CARE #5 / QUOTE #7. (§4 HIGH on no intervention)
- John Adams diary 17 Dec 1773: "no other Alternative but to destroy it or let it be landed" (pull quote); "giving up the Principle of Taxation by Parliamentary Authority, against which the Continent have struggled for 10 years"; "most magnificent Movement of all" (note: Movement not moment); "a Dignity, a Majesty, a Sublimity… that I greatly admire"; "an Epocha in History." (§4 / QUOTES #1–2, verbatim)
- Moderates/Englishmen saw destruction of private property + refusal to pay (Franklin urged repayment — stated here, expanded in §6). (CARE #10)
- "Boston Tea Party" a later name; contemporaries said "the destruction of the tea"; name in print ~50 years later, 1820s–30s. (§4 / CARE #1; exact year left as range)

### Section: "The other ports" (pack §5)
- Framing: same tea, four ports, only Boston destroyed, because only there consignees refused + governor refused; "avoid the colonies rose as one." (§5 HIGH framing / CARE #11)
- Charleston: *London* arrived 3 Dec 1773; consignees resigned; 20 days out (22 Dec) collector seized 257 chests for non-payment, locked in the Exchange cellars; later sold by the revolutionary government to fund the cause. (§5; later-sold a wry aside per pack MED)
- Philadelphia: *Polly* (Capt. Ayres), 697 chests, up the Delaware late Dec; tar-and-feather handbills warned pilots/captain; mass meeting ~8,000 on 27 Dec resolved no landing; consignees already resigned; Ayres reprovisioned and sailed *Polly* + tea back to England. (§5 HIGH)
- New York: storm-delayed *Nancy* (Capt. Lockyer) reached Sandy Hook 18 April 1774, consignees long resigned, turned back under threat; days later another ship claimed no tea, NYers (tipped from Philadelphia) found 18 chests smuggled on the captain's own account, dumped them — NY's own small tea party. (§5 HIGH)

### Section: "Parliament's answer" (pack §6)
- News reached London Jan 1774; even friends of America appalled at wanton destruction of private property + refusal to pay; North resolved to single out Boston/Massachusetts to isolate the radicals; Franklin urged restitution. (§6 HIGH; CARE #10)
- Cockpit 29 Jan 1774: Franklin stood ~an hour in the Privy Council chamber while Solicitor-General Alexander Wedderburn flayed him; "true incendiary," "first mover and prime conductor of this whole contrivance"; petition to remove Hutchinson dismissed; next day Franklin fired as deputy postmaster-general; Franklin "I made no justification of myself, but held a cool, sullen silence, reserving myself to some future opportunity." (§6 / QUOTES #5–6, verbatim)
- [GLOSS-PACK] the Cockpit = "the Privy Council chamber" — supported by §6.
- Name handling: "Intolerable Acts" a later American label; "Coercive Acts" the 1774 name; not put in 1774 mouths. (CARE #7)
- Boston Port Act: royal assent 31 Mar 1774, in force 1 June; closed the port until town paid for the tea (company + customs) and the King satisfied order restored; customs moved to Salem/Marblehead; passed Commons without a recorded vote against, Lords unanimous. (§6 HIGH)
- Massachusetts Government Act 20 May 1774: rewrote the 1691 charter; Council now Crown-appointed (was elected); judges/sheriffs/officials governor-appointed; juries summoned by appointed sheriffs; town meetings limited to one/year without permission; alarmed other colonies most (any charter shreddable). (§6 HIGH)
- [GLOSS-PACK] "the 1691 document that had governed Massachusetts for over eighty years" — derived from §6's "1691 charter" (1691→1774 = 83 yrs, stated as "over eighty"); bedrock arithmetic, not an invented fact.
- Administration of Justice Act 20 May 1774: move trial of a royal official charged with a capital crime (while suppressing a riot / enforcing revenue laws) to another colony or Britain; framed as fair-trial protection + witness travel costs; colonists called it the "Murder Act." (§6 HIGH; Washington attribution dropped, "colonists called it" safe per pack)
- Quartering Act of 1774, 2 June, all colonies: governor may requisition UNOCCUPIED buildings; explicitly NOT billeting in occupied private homes (schoolbook image killed per CARE #9). (§6 HIGH)
- Quebec Act 22 June 1774: NOT part of the punishment, long-planned; free Catholic practice, French civil law (no jury in civil cases), appointed council/no elected assembly, boundary extended SW to Ohio/Mississippi over claimed land; tolerant from Canada's side; American Protestant reading = popery + no assembly + land-grab; lumped in as the fifth "Intolerable Act." Handled per CARE #8 (separate purpose, same session, fused in perception). (§6 HIGH)

### Section: "Boston under the blockade, and the call that followed" (pack §7)
- Gage (commander-in-chief in North America) landed Boston 13 May 1774 as new royal governor; a general for a civilian = effective military government. Hutchinson sailed for England 1 June 1774, never returned. (§7 HIGH)
- 1 June: Port Act in force, harbor sealed by the navy; day observed with fasts, tolling bells, half-mast flags across colonies; Gage moved government to Salem. (§7 HIGH)
- Punishment backfired into solidarity: Boston Committee of Correspondence appeal (13 May); relief overland + through Marblehead to Boston's Committee of Donations: grain/rye/corn by the thousands of bushels, rice from South Carolina, sheep/cattle from Connecticut + New England, flour, fish, money; 100+ relief shipments by year's end. Itemized totals softened per pack MED. (§7 HIGH on the relief; CARE-soft on the table)
- The committees (§1) doing what they were built for; Coercive Acts answered a Boston crime with Massachusetts-wide constitutional demolition, every colony noticed. (§7 HIGH framing)
- Virginia 27 May 1774: Dunmore dissolved the Burgesses for declaring a fast for Boston; they reconvened at the Raleigh Tavern, Williamsburg, and proposed an annual congress of all colonies. (§7 HIGH)
- Massachusetts 17 June 1774 (Salem): House locked its doors; secretary Thomas Flucker read the dissolution to the locked door from the stairs; inside, House voted five delegates (Thomas Cushing, Samuel Adams, John Adams, Robert Treat Paine, James Bowdoin) to meet at Philadelphia in September. (§7 HIGH — closing image)
- Fuse-out: delegates would gather in Philadelphia in the fall; what they would do still anyone's guess. The Congress itself, Suffolk Resolves, and everything after are NOT narrated (ch.5). (§7 / pack STOP instruction)
