# FACT PACK — Battle of Cowpens, January 17, 1781

*American Revolution (southern campaign). ONLY source for the battle page. Morgan's tactical masterpiece: a backwoods grazing ground, a battle plan built around what militia actually could do instead of what they couldn't, and the cleanest double envelopment of the war — Tarleton's command destroyed in about an hour. The page's meaning-beat is the Camden inversion: five months earlier an American general demanded the impossible of militia and lost an army; Morgan asked them for two volleys and got everything. Confidence tags: HIGH / MED / DISPUTED; HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags inline.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **Battle of Cowpens, January 17, 1781** — "Hannah's Cowpens," a frontier cattle-grazing ground in upcountry South Carolina near the North Carolina line (today Cowpens National Battlefield, near Chesnee/Gaffney, ~35.137,-81.816), between the Pacolet River (south) and the flood-swollen Broad River (~6 miles behind Morgan's position). [HIGH]
- **Result: decisive American victory.** Tarleton's ~1,050–1,150-man force was roughly 80–90% destroyed or captured in about an hour; American losses were ~150 total. [HIGH on magnitude; figures as ranges, §5]
- **The signature [HIGH]:** the battle was *designed*, not improvised — a three-line defense in depth in which the militia's habitual flight was scripted in advance as bait. When the second line "broke" on cue and the British surged after it in disorder, they ran uphill into fresh Continentals, a point-blank about-face volley, a bayonet charge, cavalry in their right rear, and the reformed militia coming back around their left: a **double envelopment**, often called a small-scale Cannae (the Cannae framing is historians' shorthand, not a participant's — tag as such; and the envelopment was part plan, part improvisation on a misheard order — tell it honestly, see §4). [HIGH on the mechanism; MED on "Cannae" as framing]
- **Stakes [HIGH]:** Tarleton's command was the fast quarter of Cornwallis's field army — the British Legion, the light infantry, the best of his cavalry. Losing it in one morning crippled Cornwallis's ability to screen, pursue, and move, drove him to burn his own baggage at Ramsour's Mill days later, and set the Race to the Dan in motion. (Set up the race; do NOT tell it — Guilford Courthouse is the next page.)
- **Historiographical badge [HIGH as quotes, see §7]:** John Marshall: "Seldom has a battle, in which greater numbers were not engaged, been so important in its consequences as that of Cowpens." Buchanan: Morgan may have been "the only general in the American Revolution, on either side, to produce a significant original tactical thought."

## 2. BACKGROUND
### Greene takes the south, and commits the textbook sin [HIGH]
- After Charleston fell (May 1780, ~5,000 Americans surrendered) and Camden destroyed Gates's army (Aug 16, 1780), the Southern Department was on its third army. This time Congress let Washington pick the commander: **Maj. Gen. Nathanael Greene**, who arrived at Charlotte, NC on Dec 2, 1780 and took command from Gates on Dec 3. On paper ~2,300–2,400 men; barely 1,500–1,600 fit for duty. [HIGH on dates; strengths soften as "around 2,300 on paper, barely 1,500 fit to fight" — MED]
- In mid-December Greene **divided his inferior army in the face of a superior enemy** — the thing the textbooks forbid. Orders of **Dec 16, 1780** detached **Brig. Gen. Daniel Morgan** with the light troops (~600 men: ~300 Maryland/Delaware Continental light infantry under Lt. Col. John Eager Howard, Virginia riflemen, and ~80 light dragoons under Lt. Col. William Washington); Morgan marched Dec 21 west across the Catawba and Broad to operate toward the British post of **Ninety Six**, while Greene took the rest to a "camp of repose" at Cheraw, SC. The logic: a divided army could eat (two foraging districts), threatened the British posts from two directions, and forced Cornwallis to divide too — mass against either wing and the other was loose behind him. [HIGH — the Dec 16 orders are published; this framing is Greene's own]
- Morgan's detachment was called the **"flying army"** in the correspondence — he camped on the **Pacolet River** (Grindal Shoals area) from Dec 25, where **Brig. Gen. Andrew Pickens's** South Carolina militia joined him, with more militia riding in through January. [HIGH on the term and the Pacolet camp]
- **Cornwallis's answering split [HIGH]:** from Winnsboro, SC, Cornwallis (waiting on Maj. Gen. Alexander Leslie's ~1,500 reinforcements) sent **Lt. Col. Banastre Tarleton** with ~1,100–1,150 men (orders of Jan 1–2, 1781) to push Morgan away from Ninety Six and destroy him, while Cornwallis himself moved to cut off Morgan's retreat to the north-east. Tarleton's message to Cornwallis as he closed in: he would push Morgan toward Kings Mountain so Cornwallis could trap him against the Broad. [HIGH on the design; Tarleton's exact dispatch wording not pinned this session — paraphrase]
- **The pursuit [HIGH]:** Tarleton got firm intelligence of Morgan's position Jan 12 and pressed hard across the rain-swollen rivers. Morgan fell back from the Pacolet on Jan 16 — Tarleton moved so fast his troops ate the breakfast Morgan's men abandoned (the abandoned-breakfast detail is standard but anecdotal — MED, "the story goes"). By the evening of Jan 16 Morgan was at the Cowpens, with the flooded Broad ~6 miles at his back and Tarleton camped on Thicketty Creek a few hours behind. Babits's reconstruction of the British condition: in the 48 hours before the battle Tarleton's men had **less than four hours' sleep and ran out of food** — they reached the field exhausted and hungry after being roused at **2 a.m.** on the 17th for a five-hour night march. [HIGH via Babits]

### The Camden contrast — the page's meaning-beat [HIGH; carry it explicitly]
- At **Camden (Aug 16, 1780)**, Gates put ~2,500 Virginia and North Carolina militia — many of whom had never been in battle — in the line of battle opposite British regulars. When Webster's redcoats came on with the bayonet, nearly the whole militia wing threw down loaded muskets and ran, most without firing a shot; the Continentals' flank was bared, de Kalb went down, the army ceased to exist. The standing lesson most generals drew: militia were worthless in open battle. [HIGH — standard]
- **Morgan drew the opposite lesson:** the failure was the *ask*, not the men. Militia couldn't stand a bayonet charge — but they could shoot, and most of his were backcountry riflemen with personal scores. So he asked them for exactly what they could give — **two volleys at killing range — and gave them permission, in advance, to leave**, with a rally point named behind the hill. The retreat that at Camden was a rout became, at Cowpens, the working part of the machine. [HIGH on the design; this is the page's thesis and it is the consensus reading (NPS, Buchanan, Babits)]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
### Strengths (dossier stats — RANGES MANDATORY)
- **American: genuinely contested.** Morgan's own report says "we fought only 800 men, two thirds of which were Militia" [HIGH as his claim]; Buchanan estimates 800–1,000; **Babits's modern reconstruction is 1,887–1,912** (the militia kept arriving all night and Morgan's report understated them). NEVER print a single hard number — "somewhere between 800 and 1,900, depending on whose count of the militia you take" is the honest sentence. Core components [HIGH]: ~300 Maryland/Delaware Continentals + ~150 Virginia/Georgia ex-Continental state troops under **Howard**; ~150 picked riflemen as skirmishers; the militia line under **Pickens** (~300 in older accounts, 1,000+ in Babits); ~80–125 cavalry under **William Washington** (82 Continental dragoons + ~45 mounted SC militia under McCall).
- **British: ~1,050–1,150** [HIGH]: British Legion (cavalry ~250 + infantry), 7th Royal Fusiliers (many of them recruits), 1st Bn/71st Highlanders, a detachment of the 17th Light Dragoons, ~24 Royal Artillerymen with **two 3-pounder "grasshopper" guns**, ~50 Loyalist militia. Roughly half the force was American Loyalist provincials. [HIGH]
- **Duration:** about an hour — accounts run ~40 minutes to a little over an hour; first shots shortly after first light (~7 a.m.), over by ~8. [HIGH on "about an hour"; exact clock times MED]

### American commander bios (60–90 w material, verified)
- **Daniel Morgan (c. 1735/36–1802) [HIGH on career; lashes story = tradition, see care-note]:** New Jersey-born Virginia frontier teamster — "the Old Wagoner" — who drove wagons on Braddock's 1755 campaign. Carried a lifelong grudge against the British Army from a French and Indian War flogging: sentenced to 500 lashes for striking a British officer, and **by his own often-told tale the drummer miscounted at 499, so Britain still owed him one** (he showed the scars; no British record of the sentence survives — tell it as Morgan's own story, exactly as the Quebec and Saratoga pages already do). Led riflemen to Quebec (took command when Arnold fell, captured, exchanged); his rifle corps opened both Saratoga battles. **Resigned June 30, 1779** after being repeatedly passed over for brigadier (the light-infantry command he wanted went elsewhere); came back after Camden, took the promotion Congress finally voted him (**brigadier general, Oct 13, 1780**), and was handed the flying army in December. Cowpens was his masterpiece — and his last battle: sciatica broke his health within weeks (left the army Feb 10, 1781). In-repo portraits: `rev-saratoga-morgan.jpg` / `rev-quebec1775-morgan.jpg` — REUSE.
- **John Eager Howard (1752–1827) [HIGH]:** Maryland planter's son commanding the Maryland/Delaware Continentals — the third line. Veteran of White Plains, Germantown, Monmouth, and the Camden disaster itself. At Cowpens his misheard order nearly unraveled the battle and then won it: his face-about volley and bayonet charge broke the British center, and **by his own account British officers surrendered so fast he ended up holding seven swords at once**. Silver medal from Congress. Later five-term governor of Maryland and U.S. senator.
- **Andrew Pickens (1739–1817) [HIGH]:** dour South Carolina Presbyterian elder and militia leader — the "Wizard Owl." Had taken British protection after Charleston fell, then took the field again after Loyalists burned his plantation (HIGH on the arc; frame as "after his parole was, as he saw it, voided when his property was burned"). Commanded the militia line — the two-volley line — and brought it back around into the British left at the end. Congress voted him a sword for Cowpens [MED on the sword — commonly stated; confirm before printing as fact]. Escorted the prisoners north after the battle.
- **William Washington (1752–1810) [HIGH]:** Virginia-born cavalryman, second cousin of the commander-in-chief; wounded at Trenton leading the charge that took the Hessian guns. His dragoons were wrecked in the Charleston campaign (Monck's Corner, Lenud's Ferry — both Tarleton's work); Cowpens was the return match. He commanded the hidden cavalry reserve, repulsed the 17th Light Dragoons, delivered the right-rear hammer blow, and chased Tarleton 16 miles. Silver medal from Congress. Captured at Eutaw Springs later in 1781. (Commons portraits exist but are weak — see §9.)

### British commander bio
- **Banastre Tarleton (1754–1833) [HIGH]:** 26-year-old Liverpool merchant's son commanding the British Legion (Loyalist cavalry + light infantry) — the most feared man in the southern war. As a cornet he helped capture Charles Lee in 1776; in 1780 he destroyed the American cavalry around Charleston, and at the **Waxhaws (May 29, 1780)** his Legion cut down Virginia Continentals as they tried to surrender — "Tarleton's quarter" became backcountry slang for no quarter at all, and "the Butcher" his name in the Carolinas (contemporaries said "the Butcher"; "Bloody Ban" is a 1950s coinage — Bass's *The Green Dragoon* — do NOT use it as a period nickname [HIGH via JAR myth survey]). Whether Tarleton ordered the Waxhaws killing is genuinely disputed — his horse had been shot and pinned him at the surrender moment; what's undisputed is what his men did and what the backcountry believed. At Cowpens he commanded exactly the kind of force he'd always won with, drove it onto Morgan's anvil at the end of a sleepless, foodless 48 hours, and saw it destroyed. In-repo portrait: `rev-charleston-tarleton.jpg` (Reynolds, 1782) — REUSE.
- *(Optional, one clause each):* Maj. Archibald McArthur (71st Highlanders, captured — see his quote, §7); Maj. Timothy Newmarsh (7th Fusiliers, wounded). Cornwallis and Greene frame the page but were not on the field.

## 4. THE BATTLE
### The ground, and why Morgan wanted it [HIGH on the choice; the reasoning = Morgan's own later telling, MED-HIGH — attribute]
- The Cowpens was open pastureland: a long gentle rise (~350 yards of slope), scattered hardwoods with no underbrush (cattle grazing kept it open), no swamps at hand — and the flooded Broad River ~6 miles behind. To every conventional eye the position was a trap: wings in the air, cavalry country, no line of retreat. That was the point. [HIGH]
- **Morgan's own justification** — as printed in William Johnson's 1822 biography of Greene (Johnson himself hedges "if these were really Morgan's reasons"; quote as Morgan's *later self-justification*, never as battlefield speech): "I would not have had a swamp in the view of my militia on any consideration; they would have made for it... As to retreat, it was the very thing I wished to cut off all hope of... When men are forced to fight, they will sell their lives dearly... Had I crossed the river, one half of the militia would immediately have abandoned me." [Chain VERIFIED to Johnson vol. 1 (1822), p. 376, this session — full passage in §7]
- Practical pressures, also real [HIGH]: the Broad was high and hard to cross; Tarleton was hours behind; being caught mid-ford would have been fatal. Standing at the Cowpens was also the aggressive reading of a forced situation, not pure theory.

### The night before — the Old Wagoner among the campfires [MED — single-source memoir; the page's best texture, use with "Young remembered"]
- Militia companies rode in all evening; the camp butchered cattle and roasted beef. Morgan went from fire to fire: helped men fix their swords, joked with them about their sweethearts, and told them **"the old wagoner would crack his whip over Ben [Tarleton] in the morning, as sure as they lived"** — and: **"Just hold up your heads, boys, three fires, and you are free, and then when you return to your homes, how the old folks will bless you, and the girls kiss you, for your gallant conduct!"** Source: the memoir of **Thomas Young**, a 17-year-old militia dragoon that night, published 1843 (The Orion) — sixty-two years later. Vivid, probably true in spirit, single-source: frame as Young's memory. (Note Young says "three fires," counting the skirmish line's fire; the militia-line order proper was two volleys.) [MED]
- "On this ground I will defeat the British or lay my bones" — attributed to Morgan that night; 19th-century chain only. Use as "he is supposed to have said," or drop. [DISPUTED]

### The plan — three lines, each asked only for what it could give [HIGH]
1. **Skirmish line:** ~150 picked Georgia/North Carolina riflemen in loose order among the trees, to fire at ~50 yards (aimed, by everyone's understanding, at officers — Johnson 1822 has the men themselves exclaiming "Mark the epaulette men"; it was NOT a formal order — attribute exactly that way), then fall back firing into the militia line. [HIGH; the epaulette line MED as men's-own-cry via Johnson]
2. **Militia line (Pickens):** the main militia body ~150 yards behind, asked for **two deliberate volleys at fifty yards** and then *licensed in advance* to retire — around the American **left**, behind the hill, to reform as a reserve. Every man knew the plan; the line was told the cavalry would cover them. [HIGH]
3. **Main line (Howard):** the Continentals + veteran Virginia/Georgia companies on the crown of the rise, ~150 yards further back, to take the charge of an enemy already bled and disordered. **Washington's cavalry hidden behind a second rise to the rear** — close enough to strike, invisible from the front, safe from the guns. [HIGH]
- Morgan spent the night and dawn making sure **every line knew what the others would do** — so that when the militia "ran," nobody behind them panicked. This briefing discipline is as much the genius as the geometry. [HIGH as consensus interpretation]

### The battle (~7:00–8:00 a.m.) [HIGH on sequence]
- Tarleton's van came up the Green River Road at first light and he attacked straight off the march — no rest, no breakfast, minimal reconnaissance: light infantry right, Legion infantry center, 7th Fusiliers left, the two grasshoppers opening, 50-odd dragoons of the 17th on the flanks, the 71st and the Legion cavalry in reserve. [HIGH]
- **Line 1 & 2 worked as scripted:** the skirmishers bled the first dragoon probe (15 of the first 50 dragoons down is Babits's figure — MED) and fell back; the British line came on cheering into the militia's two volleys — aimed low, officer-heavy, devastating at fifty yards — and Pickens's men shouldered off around the left as planned. [HIGH]
- **The wobble:** Tarleton threw the 17th Light Dragoons after the "fleeing" militia; sabers fell on the hindmost (Young was slashed in this melee — his memoir scar-list is real color) — and **Washington's hidden cavalry burst over the rise and broke the dragoons off the militia's backs**. The militia made the rally point. [HIGH]
- **The misheard order [HIGH]:** Tarleton committed the 71st around the American right; Howard ordered his right-flank company to *refuse* (bend back) to face them; in the noise the order was heard as withdrawal, and company by company the whole Continental line faced about and marched — in step, muskets shouldered — to the rear. Morgan rode up demanding whether they were beaten; Howard pointed at the unbroken ranks and answered (his later account) **"Do men who march like that look as though they were beaten?"** Morgan accepted it, galloped ahead, and staked a new line. [Event HIGH; the dialogue is Howard's/19th-c telling — MED, frame as "Howard remembered"]
- **The trap closes [HIGH]:** the British, certain the day was won, broke ranks and surged uphill after them "like a mob" (the phrase is from the American account chain — a messenger from Washington to the rear line: **"They are coming on like a mob. Give them a fire and I will charge them"** — first printed in Johnson 1822; attribute as reported). At Howard's command the Continentals **faced about and fired a volley into them at ~30 yards**, then went in with the bayonet. Washington's cavalry hit the British right and rear; Pickens's reformed militia came back around onto the 71st on the British left. Double envelopment.
- **Collapse [HIGH]:** much of the British infantry — two days without food or sleep, officers down, surrounded — quit on the spot; Babits reads it as "combat shock" (tag as his interpretation). Men dropped to the ground or threw down arms wholesale. The artillerymen alone fought to the last around their two grasshoppers; both guns were taken. The 71st fought longest; Maj. McArthur surrendered to Howard's officers. The Legion cavalry — Tarleton's reserve and his last card — **refused his order to charge back in and rode off the field**. [HIGH]
- **The surrender and the quarter [HIGH — handle exactly]:** men in the American line shouted **"Tarleton's quarter!"** — the Waxhaws coming home — and for a moment the battle could have ended as a massacre. It did not: officers (Howard prominently, by his account and the standard tellings) physically protected surrendering men, and quarter was given. Morgan's report two days later: **"Not a man was killed, wounded or even insulted after he surrendered."** [Morgan's line is his own report — self-interested but corroborated by the arithmetic: ~600–830 prisoners taken alive against ~110 dead. The cries are well-attested; isolated bayoneting before officers intervened appears in some secondary tellings (britishbattles.com) but is NOT in the primary chain this session — do not stage it. The honest sentence: the cries went up, the officers stopped it, and the prisoners lived.]
- **Tarleton's exit and the saber clash [event MED-HIGH; details semi-legendary — keep to a clause or tell at evidence level]:** Tarleton, with the ~40–50 men who would still follow him (most of his fleeing Legion cavalry would not), made a last counter-charge, then broke for the road. Washington, riding ahead of his troopers, closed with a knot of British officers — **"believed to be Tarleton"** among them, which is Howard's own careful phrase and should set the page's confidence. In the family of accounts: Washington's saber broke at the hilt; a British officer's cut was parried by Washington's sergeant-major; **a teenage bugler/waiter of the 3rd Dragoons — Black, by the standard accounts, too small to wield a sword — shot another officer with his pistol, saving Washington's life**; the officer taken for Tarleton fired a pistol shot that wounded Washington's horse and escaped. Babits is openly skeptical the man was Tarleton at all (Tarleton's memoir never mentions it; his first documented wound came at Guilford Courthouse). The Ranney 1845 painting dramatizes exactly this scene with the Black trooper firing — a documented detail OF THE PAINTING'S SUBJECT (the in-repo caption already handles this correctly). The bugler's name is uncertain (one ABT account says "most likely named Collins"; older tellings leave him nameless) — do not print a confident name. [Tell the clash in one or two clauses with "believed to be Tarleton" doing the epistemic work]
- Washington pursued ~16 miles, lost Tarleton when a local planter (Adam Goudylock, pressed as a guide by Tarleton) led him away, and gathered ~100 stragglers instead. [HIGH]

## 5. CASUALTIES (dossier ranges — RANGES MANDATORY)
- **British [HIGH as ranges]:** ~**100–110 killed** (Morgan reported 10 officers + 100 men), **~200–230 wounded** (most also captured), **~500–630 captured unwounded** — total prisoners ~600–830; total loss ~**830–970 of ~1,050–1,150 (roughly 80–90%)**. Also lost: both grasshopper guns, the colors of the 7th Fusiliers, 60 baggage wagons, ~100 cavalry horses, the Legion's baggage and music. [HIGH on the magnitude and trophies; every specific number as a range]
- **American [HIGH as ranges]:** Morgan's report ~**12 killed, 60 wounded**; modern reconstruction (Babits, naming names from pension files) ~**25 killed, 124 wounded** (~150 total — Morgan undercounted the militia). Print "about 150 killed and wounded, perhaps fewer" or give both figures with their sources.
- Escaped British: Tarleton and roughly **50–250** — ~54 with him by one standard count, plus the ~200 Legion cavalry who had already quit the field. Give as "Tarleton got away with a remnant — a few dozen riders with him, a couple of hundred Legion cavalry who had refused his last order already gone." [MED — counts vary]

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **Morgan vanished before Cornwallis could move [HIGH]:** he crossed the Broad at Island Ford the same afternoon — eight hours after the battle ended he was camped beyond it — and was at Gilbert Town, NC by Jan 18. Pickens took the ~600 unwounded prisoners north out of Cornwallis's reach (they ended in Virginia). The wounded of both sides were left under flag at the field hospital. [HIGH; the wounded-disposition clause MED — keep general]
- **What Cornwallis lost [HIGH framing; arithmetic as ranges]:** the light troops — the Legion, the light infantry, the fast quarter of his field force (Tarleton's ~1,100 of a ~4,000-man army once Leslie joined; "roughly a quarter, and the quarter that made the rest of it fast" is the honest line). Every desperate thing Cornwallis did next — burning his own baggage train at Ramsour's Mill in late January to turn his whole army into light infantry, the failed dash to the Catawba fords — was an attempt to replace what died at the Cowpens. The Race to the Dan and Guilford Courthouse follow (NEXT page — end this one on the chase beginning, not its outcome).
- **The Cornwallis sword anecdote [HANDLE-WITH-CARE]:** an American prisoner later recounted that Cornwallis, hearing Tarleton's report, leaned on his sword until the blade snapped. One secondhand prisoner account — "the story goes," or drop. [MED-DISPUTED]
- **Tarleton's reckoning [HIGH]:** he offered his resignation and asked for an inquiry; Cornwallis refused it and publicly absolved him — his Jan 30 letter (printed in Tarleton's own 1787 *Campaigns*) told him "you have forfeited no part of my esteem" and that only "the total misbehaviour of the troops" had robbed him of the glory. Blaming the troops did not survive scrutiny: when Tarleton published that line in his 1787 memoir, Roderick Mackenzie — a 71st officer wounded at Cowpens — answered with a whole book of *Strictures* tearing the account apart. [HIGH on the sequence; the Cornwallis-letter quote is from Tarleton's own printing — MED-HIGH, quote with that provenance noted]
- **Morgan's exit [HIGH]:** sciatica and rheumatism, aggravated by the winter campaign, finished him. He guided the first leg of the retreat (Catawba crossings) and **left the army Feb 10, 1781**, days before the Dan. Cowpens was the last battle he ever fought.
- **The gold medal [HIGH]:** Congress resolved **March 9, 1781** to strike a **gold medal for Morgan** (silver for Howard and William Washington; a sword voted to Pickens [MED on the sword]). The medal — designed by Augustin Dupré in Paris, "Victoria Libertatis Vindex" — reached Morgan only in **1790**, presented by President Washington. The original gold medal survives (stolen 1818, replaced 1839; the 1839 replacement resurfaced at auction in the 2020s — trivia, use only if the medal image runs).
- **Reputation [HIGH]:** Cowpens became the model — Greene copied the three-line, two-volley design wholesale at Guilford Courthouse eight weeks later (next page's link). The Spartanburg Morgan statue (1881, centennial) and Cowpens National Battlefield carry the memory.

## 8. APOCRYPHA BLACKLIST / HANDLE-WITH-CARE
1. **The 499 lashes** — Morgan's OWN tale, told on himself, scars displayed; no British record survives. Always "by his own telling." Never narrate the flogging as documented fact. [TRADITION]
2. **The Washington–Tarleton "duel"** — a real cavalry scuffle happened at the pursuit's start, but the man Washington crossed swords with is "believed to be Tarleton" (Howard's phrase); Tarleton never mentioned it and was first verifiably wounded at Guilford. No Hollywood saber duel; one clause with the hedge built in. [DISPUTED in identity, MED in event]
3. **The dinner-party quips** — Tarleton sneering that Washington "is so ignorant a fellow that he can hardly write his own name," and Mrs. Willie Jones: "you ought to know better, for you bear on your person proof that he knows very well how to make his mark"; her sister Mrs. Ashe, when Tarleton said he'd like to see Colonel Washington: "If you had looked behind you, Colonel Tarleton, at the battle of the Cowpens, you would have had that pleasure." Both surface in 19th-century anecdote collections (Garden 1822 / Ellet 1848 chains), decades later, secondhand — AND the "mark" quip presumes Washington wounded Tarleton's hand at Cowpens, which is itself doubted (#2). If used at all: clearly flagged as the legend the new republic told itself, never as documented dialogue. Better: omit. [APOCRYPHAL/TRADITION]
4. **"Tarleton's quarter" as revenge story** — the cries are real and well-attested; an actual revenge massacre did NOT happen (Morgan's report + the prisoner arithmetic). Do not write Cowpens as payback-in-kind; the documented story is the discipline, not the bloodlust — that IS the meaning-beat. [HIGH]
5. **"Bloody Ban"** — 1950s coinage (Bass). Period words: "Tarleton's quarter," "the Butcher." Never put "Bloody Ban" in a period mouth. [HIGH via JAR]
6. **The Cornwallis sword-snap** — single secondhand prisoner anecdote. "The story goes" or omit. [DISPUTED]
7. **The "Mark the epaulette men" / "look for the epaulets" order** — Johnson 1822 has it as the skirmishers' OWN exclamation, explicitly noting no such order was given. Never render it as Morgan's command. [MED, attribute to the men]
8. **The "American Cannae"** — historians' label (and a self-conscious one); fine as "has been called," never as contemporary judgment. Also keep the honest mechanics: the envelopment was half plan (militia return was scripted), half improvisation (the Continental withdrawal was an accident Morgan and Howard converted). [FRAMING]
9. **Morgan's force = 800** — that's Morgan's own report; Babits's 1,887–1,912 reconstruction is the modern standard. State both or use the range; never print one number bare. [DISPUTED — structurally]
10. **"On this ground I will beat Benny Tarleton or lay my bones"** — 19th-c attribution; "he is supposed to have said." [TRADITION]

## 11. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)
- **Date:** January 17, 1781 · **Where:** Hannah's Cowpens, upcountry South Carolina, near the Broad River
- **Forces:** American 800 (Morgan's count) to ~1,900 (modern reconstruction) — print the range; British ~1,050–1,150
- **Duration:** about an hour, at the end of Tarleton's all-night march
- **Casualties:** British ~110 killed, ~200+ wounded, 500–630 captured (~80–90% of the force), both guns, the 7th's colors; American ~150 killed and wounded (as low as 12/60 in Morgan's report)
- **Winner line material:** American victory — the war's cleanest small-battle destruction of a British force. Morgan asked his militia for two volleys instead of miracles, scripted their retreat as bait, and folded Tarleton's exhausted command into a double envelopment that cost Cornwallis the fast quarter of his army and set the whole southern war running toward the Dan.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. **Morgan's strength** — irreducibly contested (800 his own report; 1,887–1,912 Babits). Range every time; this is also content (his report undercounted militia).
2. **Casualty figures** — give as ranges (§5); Morgan's 12/60 American loss vs Babits's ~25/124.
3. **Battle clock** — first light ~7 a.m., over by ~8; "about an hour" (some accounts say 40 minutes). Keep approximate.
4. **The swamp/retreat quote** — Johnson 1822 verbatim VERIFIED, but Johnson hedged whether they "were really Morgan's reasons"; quote as Morgan's later self-justification via Johnson.
5. **The Howard/Morgan and Washington-message dialogue** — 19th-century chains (Howard's recollections, Johnson); "Howard remembered," "word came back."
6. **The clash with Tarleton** — event MED, identity DISPUTED ("believed to be Tarleton"); the rescuing bugler's name uncertain (do not print "Collins" as fact); Tarleton's hand wound at Cowpens unsupported (first wound documented at Guilford).
7. **Tarleton's escape count** — ~54 with him / ~200 Legion cavalry already gone; "a remnant."
8. **The quarter** — cries HIGH, restraint HIGH (Morgan's report + prisoner arithmetic); any staged bayoneting-before-restraint scene is NOT in the verified chain.
9. **Pickens's sword from Congress** — commonly stated, not pinned to the March 9, 1781 resolution text this session; soften or verify.
10. **Greene's division-rationale quote and the Dec 16 order text** — paraphrase only until checked against the Papers of Nathanael Greene.
11. **The abandoned-breakfast pursuit detail and the 2 a.m. rouse** — rouse time HIGH (standard), breakfast anecdote MED ("the story goes").
12. **"Quarter of Cornwallis's army"** — sound as arithmetic (~1,100 of ~4,000 with Leslie) and standard as framing; give it as "roughly a quarter of his field force — the fast quarter," not a precise statistic.
