# Clarity Gate — Wilson's Creek Draft
_Newcomer critic · sharp 15-year-old, zero Civil War knowledge_
_Date: 2026-05-30_

## TERM-BY-TERM AUDIT

### fight-for-missouri section

---

**1. "border state" — intro eyebrow + first body paragraph**

> _"A border state — a slaveholding state that had not joined the Confederacy"_

STATUS: **CLEAR** — defined right there in the sentence. No problem.

---

**2. "arsenal"**

> _"the St. Louis Arsenal, a federal weapons depot (an arsenal being a government storehouse stuffed with guns, powder, and cannon)"_

STATUS: **CLEAR** — parenthetical definition is tight and good.

---

**3. "secessionist"**

> _"hostile secessionist crowds gathered along the route"_ (Camp Jackson section, first use)

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS**

"Secessionist" appears before any explanation that it means a person who wants the state to break away from the United States. The word "secession" appears later in the same section, but never gets a plain-English definition. A 15-year-old will probably parse it from context ("secessionist = people angry that the militiamen were captured") but that's an inference, not a definition.

Suggested gloss (inline, first use): _"secessionist (pro-breakaway) crowds"_ OR add one sentence the first time secession appears as a concept: _"secession being the act of a state leaving the United States altogether."_

---

**4. "Missouri State Guard" — Camp Jackson section**

> _"The lines were now drawn, and they were drawn in blood."_ — the Missouri State Guard is introduced in the previous sentence as if the reader knows what a "State Guard" is.

> _"a rattled legislature handed Governor Jackson near-dictatorial powers, and Jackson handed the reorganized Missouri State Guard — the South's homegrown Missouri army — to a Mexican War hero…"_

STATUS: **CLEAR** — the dash-gloss "the South's homegrown Missouri army" handles it well. No fix needed here.

---

**5. "Mexican War hero" — first mention of Sterling Price**

> _"Jackson handed the reorganized Missouri State Guard… to a Mexican War hero and former governor named Sterling Price (South)."_

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS** (minor)

Side tag (South) is present — good. But "Mexican War" is unexplained. A 15-year-old in 2026 will not know this refers to the 1846–1848 U.S.–Mexico War that preceded the Civil War by about 15 years. The phrase is used as a prestige marker ("proven fighter") but without context it's just a strange label.

Suggested gloss: _"a veteran of the 1846 war with Mexico"_ — drops the "hero" label (which is also a bit of an assertion) and gives the reader the timeline anchor.

---

**6. "Col." / "Brig. Gen." / "Maj." rank abbreviations — throughout**

> _"Col. Franz Sigel (North)"_ / _"Brig. Gen. Benjamin McCulloch (South)"_ / _"Maj. Samuel D. Sturgis (North)"_

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS** (first-use only)

Rank abbreviations are never decoded. "Col." and "Maj." especially look like noise. The draft is good about naming ranks in prose elsewhere ("promoted to brigadier general," "still only a captain") but the abbreviated prefixes in front of names are used cold. Suggest either spelling out on first use or adding a one-time bracketed note: _"Col. [Colonel] Franz Sigel."_ Does not need to happen for every instance — just the first time each abbreviated form appears.

---

**7. "flank" — bloody-hill section, The Plan**

> _"Col. Franz Sigel (North) would take a flanking column of roughly 1,100 to 1,200 men — a flank being the side of an enemy formation, the vulnerable spot away from where it's facing"_

STATUS: **CLEAR** — defined in the same sentence with a good parenthetical. No fix needed.

---

**8. "battery" — The Surprise section**

> _"Capt. James Totten's (North) artillery battery — a battery being a cluster of cannon worked as a single unit"_

STATUS: **CLEAR** — good inline gloss.

---

**9. "skirmishers"**

> _"he failed to post proper skirmishers out front to screen his line"_ (The gray-uniform catastrophe)

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS** (MUST-FIX — comprehension-critical at this passage)

"Skirmishers" is completely undefined. The whole point of this passage is that Sigel's carelessness cost him the battle — he failed to send scouts out front to check who was coming. But a reader who doesn't know what a skirmisher is will just see a military jargon word and skip over the sentence without grasping WHY Sigel was careless or what he failed to do.

Suggested gloss: _"skirmishers — scouts pushed out ahead of the main line to watch for enemy movement — out front to screen his line"_

---

**10. "brigade" — The gray-uniform catastrophe**

> _"Sigel's brigade shattered and ran."_

STATUS: **MUST-FIX**

"Brigade" is used without explanation. Earlier the draft used "column" and "flanking column" to describe Sigel's force. Then suddenly it's a "brigade." A 15-year-old has no idea whether a brigade is larger or smaller than the 1,100-man "column" they just read about, or whether the words mean the same thing. The shift also creates minor factual confusion: is the narrator now talking about a subset of Sigel's column, or all of it?

Suggested fix: either call it "Sigel's column" consistently, OR add a gloss on first use of "brigade": _"Sigel's brigade — the roughly 1,200-man force he had brought to the south end of the field."_

---

**11. "regiment" — bloody-hill section, various**

> _"the 1st Iowa Infantry, a Union regiment"_ / _"the 3rd Louisiana Infantry (South)"_ / _"the 2nd Kansas Infantry"_

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS** (first use only)

"Regiment" is used freely but never explained. The draft does say "a Union regiment that happened to wear gray" which at least signals it's a military unit, and a smart reader infers it. But the word never gets a size anchor. Hundreds of men? Thousands? The reader can't gauge scale. Suggest one gloss on the very first use: _"the 1st Iowa Infantry, a Union regiment (a regiment being roughly a thousand-man fighting unit)"_. Subsequent uses need no gloss.

---

**12. "Wire Road"**

> _"Sigel pushed up onto the Wire Road, the main road linking Springfield to Fort Smith, Arkansas"_

STATUS: **CLEAR** — defined immediately after in the same sentence. No fix needed.

---

**13. "orderly" (military sense)**

> _"Sigel hid in a cornfield and slipping back toward Springfield with a single orderly"_

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS** (minor)

"Orderly" in the military sense (an aide or personal soldier-servant assigned to an officer) is opaque to a 15-year-old who only knows the adjective form. Not a comprehension-breaker, but a quick note helps: _"a single orderly — a personal aide"_

---

**14. "Pulaski Arkansas battery" and "Woodruff" — The hill that earned its name**

> _"A Pulaski Arkansas battery had unlimbered near the creek ford at first light"_

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS** (minor)

"Unlimbered" is jargon for detaching the cannon from its horse-drawn carriage to set it up for firing. Used without explanation. Suggest: _"had set up its cannon near the creek ford"_ or add _"(unlimbered = unhooked from its horse teams and set up to fire)."_

---

**15. "bushwhackers and jayhawkers"**

> _"Bushwhackers and jayhawkers — homegrown partisan raiders on both sides"_

STATUS: **CLEAR** — dash-gloss handles it.

---

**16. "Trans-Mississippi"**

> _"the whole character of the Trans-Mississippi war to come"_ (Missouri's fate, final paragraph)

STATUS: **MUST-FIX**

"Trans-Mississippi" appears cold in the very last sentence without any gloss. The reader just encountered "west of the Mississippi" earlier in the same paragraph — the narrator could have used that phrase again. "Trans-Mississippi" sounds like an official title for a military theater but is never explained as such. A reader who doesn't know Latin prefixes ("trans" = across/beyond) is simply lost.

Suggested fix: replace with "war west of the Mississippi" (already used in the same paragraph), OR add a brief gloss: _"the Trans-Mississippi theater — the war fought west of the Mississippi River."_

---

**17. "jayhawkers"**

> _"Bands of bushwhackers and jayhawkers rode the back roads"_ (Meanwhile in Missouri, closing section)

STATUS: **CLEAR** — defined earlier in the section above this one. Carries over fine.

**25. Price — re-introductions across section breaks**

Suggested fix: add (South) to Price's first mention in bloody-hill and first mention in death-of-lyon.

## UNEXPLAINED LEAPS / OVERWHELMING PASSAGES

---

**27. The Planters' House section — "neutrality was to concede that Missouri's state government could dictate terms to the federal government"**

> _"To agree to neutrality was to concede that Missouri's state government could dictate terms to the federal government, and that was the one thing Lyon would never grant."_

STATUS: **SHOULD-FIX**

This is a logical leap that a reader without background won't follow. Why does accepting a ceasefire equal conceding that a state can dictate to the federal government? The gap is: neutrality = treating Missouri as a co-equal party in a negotiation = accepting that state governments can veto federal authority = the whole premise of the Confederacy's argument. That chain of reasoning is all in Lyon's head and the draft leaves it there.

Suggested addition: one bridging sentence — something like: _"In Lyon's reading, accepting neutrality meant treating Missouri's government as an equal party in a negotiation — and a state government that could negotiate with the federal government over whether to be at war had already won the argument the South was making."_

---

**28. "cartridges" — The withdrawal section**

> _"they were running out of cartridges"_

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS** (minor)

"Cartridges" is a specific ammunition term that a 15-year-old might know from video games, but in context (1861, muzzle-loading rifles) it means paper-wrapped powder-and-ball charges. It's not a comprehension-breaker, but "ammunition" or "ammunition (cartridges)" would be instantly clear to any reader.

---

**29. The Meanwhile sidebar — "admitted Missouri" (Nov 28, 1861)**

> _"on November 28, 1861, the Confederacy formally admitted Missouri"_

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS** (SHOULD-FIX)

"Admitted" in this context means accepted as a member state. A reader might wonder why you need to be "admitted" to a thing you're already technically a state of. A brief gloss: _"the Confederacy formally accepted Missouri as a member state"_ or just _"recognized Missouri as a Confederate state"_ avoids the confusion.

---

**30. "enlistments" / "ninety-day volunteers" — End of the tether**

> _"His short-term volunteers — men who had signed on for ninety days — were weeks from the end of their enlistments and entitled to go home."_

STATUS: **CLEAR** — the parenthetical does the job. No fix needed.

---

**31. "department commander" — End of the tether**

> _"his own superior, the department commander John C. Frémont (North)"_

STATUS: **NEEDS-GLOSS** (minor)

"Department commander" is a military bureaucracy term. A 15-year-old won't know what a "department" is in this context (it's a military administrative district, not a government department). Suggest: _"his superior, the general commanding the whole western region, John C. Frémont (North)"_ — gets the hierarchy across without jargon.

---

**32. "ordnance of secession" / "Neosho" passage**

> _"secessionist Missouri legislators met at Neosho and passed an ordinance of secession"_

STATUS: **MUST-FIX**

"Ordinance of secession" is legal/political jargon. A 15-year-old knows "ordinance" from city bylaws — a municipal rule — but "ordinance of secession" as a formal legal act of a state leaving the Union is not intuitive. Suggest: _"passed a formal declaration of secession"_ or _"voted to officially leave the United States."_

---

**33. "cockpit" (of vicious fighting)**

> _"A state with two governments and a population genuinely divided down the middle became the cockpit of the most vicious irregular fighting of the entire war."_

STATUS: **MUST-FIX**

"Cockpit" meaning "center" or "arena" (originally from cockfighting — the pit where the fight happens) is a period idiom that has largely left modern English. A 15-year-old will either not know it at all or picture an airplane cockpit. The meaning is entirely lost.

Suggested fix: _"became the center of"_ or _"became the arena for."_

---

**34. "Reconstruction"**

> _"well into Reconstruction"_ (Missouri's fate)

STATUS: **MUST-FIX**

"Reconstruction" (the post-Civil-War federal program to rebuild the South and integrate freedpeople) is used as a time anchor for how long Missouri's guerrilla war lasted, but it's undefined. A reader with zero Civil War knowledge won't know what Reconstruction is or when it happened.

Suggested gloss: _"Reconstruction (the federal program to rebuild the South after the war)"_ — or simply _"for years after the war ended."_

---

**35. "Pea Ridge" reference**

> _"The organized Confederate threat in the region was finally broken at Pea Ridge, Arkansas, in March 1862"_

STATUS: **CLEAR** — the sentence is self-contained. "At Pea Ridge, Arkansas" tells the reader it's another battle in another place. No fix needed.

## WORST OFFENDER

**M2 (skirmishers undefined)** is the single worst comprehension failure. The entire explanation of how Sigel lost his half of the battle turns on the sentence _"he failed to post proper skirmishers out front to screen his line."_ If the reader doesn't know what a skirmisher does, that sentence reads as military noise and the cause-and-effect of the battle's turning point evaporates. The gray-uniform catastrophe is one of the most dramatic moments in the draft — the reader should feel the horror of the mistake — but without knowing that skirmishers are the soldiers who would have spotted the approaching Confederates first, the moment is just: _Sigel did something wrong, got shot at, ran._ The dramatic stakes are invisible.

**M5 (cockpit)** is a close second — a completely dead idiom that will stop readers cold in a sentence that is supposed to deliver the thematic gut-punch of the whole story.
