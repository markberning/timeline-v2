# Nashville — STORYTELLING critic gate

> War content pipeline, step 4 (critic gate, STORYTELLING). Independent read.
> Judges: hook, momentum, stakes, scene-setting, the human core, arc, pacing.
> Never demands invented facts; where a fact would strengthen a beat, it is flagged
> as a fact-pack request, not asserted. GOOD/WEAK per beat; MUST-IMPROVE vs SHOULD-IMPROVE.

**VERDICT: PASS WITH REVISIONS.** This is a strong, structurally sound draft with a
genuine spine (the slow general everyone wanted to fire + the freedmen's charge as the
cause made flesh). The two tentpoles both land. The main risks are (1) the Overton Hill
centerpiece is slightly over-narrated — it tells the reader its own significance three or
four times instead of trusting the scene; and (2) the framing/thesis ("railroad is
terrain, slavery is the reason") is repeated almost verbatim across two sections, which
blunts it. One MUST-IMPROVE (Overton over-assertion), the rest SHOULD-IMPROVE.

---

## Section 1 — `the-standoff` ("An army too weak to attack, too proud to run")

**Hook — GOOD.** "The Confederacy was a building already on fire" is a strong, concrete
opening image and it earns the four-fact 1864-collapse drumbeat (Atlanta / Mobile / Lincoln
/ Sherman) that follows. The bolded staccato works.

**Stakes / scene-setting — GOOD.** "One last Confederate field army and what it would do"
is exactly the right altitude — it tells the reader why this one battle is the whole West.
The Franklin two-weeks-prior beat is well-placed: it gives Hood's army a wound the reader
can feel before Nashville opens, and the Eicher line is deployed cleanly with its
"modern historian, not a soldier" caveat doing real work rather than feeling like a
hedge.

**The "too weak / too proud" paradox — GOOD.** "An army parked in front of a fortress,
daring the fortress to come out" is the best single line in the section and it sets up the
hand-off to Thomas perfectly ("Inside the fortress was the man who would oblige him.").
This is a textbook section-to-section seam.

**Forrest MEANWHILE — GOOD.** Planting the great what-if here (best weapon sent away) and
paying it off in section 5 (the same unit that saved the remnant) is the draft's strongest
piece of long-range architecture. Keep it exactly where it is.

**WEAK — the Franklin numbers run a hair long.** "Six generals killed... 55 regimental
commanders" plus the Eicher line plus Cleburne is three pieces of Franklin freight in one
paragraph for a battle that isn't this one. The reader gets the point (Hood broke his own
army) by "killed by the thousand for nothing." SHOULD-IMPROVE: keep Cleburne (named human
cost) and the Eicher line; consider trimming "55 regimental commanders" — it's a stat the
reader can't feel and it's the third casualty figure in two sentences.

---

## Section 2 — `the-sledgehammer-telegrams` ("The general everyone wanted to fire")

**This is the best-built section in the draft.** The tension the brief asked for — the
general everyone wanted to fire — is constructed beautifully and escalates in clean stages.

**Character-first open — GOOD.** "His greatest strength is exactly what nearly got him
fired on the eve of his greatest day" is a thesis sentence that makes the reader want the
rest. The Virginian-who-stayed detail ("cost him his family, who never spoke to him again")
is the right kind of human cost — it makes Thomas a person, not a nickname rack.

**Momentum / escalation — GOOD.** The ladder is excellent: Grant's telegrams → the ice
storm freezes him in place → Grant doesn't threaten, he *acts* (Logan ordered Dec 13) →
Grant doesn't even trust Logan and boards a train himself. Each rung raises the stakes.
"The relief was, almost literally, knocking on the door" is a fair, earned line.

**The payoff — GOOD.** "It did not merely satisfy his critics; it erased them" and the
"without skates" grace note (handled as paraphrase) land the release after all that
pressure. The "slow man had been right" close is satisfying and seeds the section-5
callback.

**The plan-reveal MEANWHILE — GOOD.** Holding the feint/grand-right-wheel explanation for
the end of this section is the correct call — it answers "what were the horses *for*" right
before section 3 executes it. The swinging-door image is clear and gets reused well.

**WEAK — slight burying of the thaw beat.** The actual hinge ("And then the ice melted")
arrives after a long paragraph that already told us the relief was en route. It lands, but
the paragraph before it ("So on the morning the battle began...") restates the whole
relief-en-route situation a second time before the thaw. SHOULD-IMPROVE: this is one
restatement too many; the reader felt the vise close one paragraph earlier. Tighten the
"So on the morning the battle began" paragraph so the thaw lands faster.

## Section 3 — `the-right-wheel` ("The door swings shut on Hood's left")

**Open — GOOD.** "December 15 opened in fog, and it opened with the lie." is a crisp,
confident scene-open and "the lie" is the right word for a feint the reader was just taught.

**Momentum — GOOD.** The redoubt-collapse sequence (4, 5, 3, 2, 1 falling "like a line of
dominoes") has real physical momentum, and the regiments-racing-each-other detail is a
great human beat that conveys the lopsidedness without a casualty stat. "A flank meant to be
a barrier became a stepping path" earns its place.

**Scene-setting — GOOD.** The redoubts are defined in-line ("small standalone earthwork
forts, each mounting two to four cannon and held by roughly 150 men") so the collapse
*means* something. The Schofield-fought-Franklin aside ties his reserve role back to the
time he bought Thomas — good connective tissue.

**WEAK — actor traffic jam.** The middle paragraph routes five named generals (Wilson,
Smith, Schofield, Wood) plus three place-axes (Hillsboro Pike, Montgomery Hill) through one
block. It's accurate and side-tagged, but as *story* it's the closest the draft comes to an
order-of-battle recitation. SHOULD-IMPROVE: the reader needs the *shape* (cavalry on the
outer edge, infantry behind, wheel pivoting onto Hood's left) more than four corps
designations. Consider thinning the corps-by-corps detail and keeping the geometry — the
"slow, enormous hammer" image is doing more work than the unit list.

**Close — GOOD.** "One of them would become a slaughter pen, and the other would become the
place where the line finally broke." is a strong cliff-hang into the two-hill day, and it
correctly tees up *both* section 4 (Overton) and section 5 (Shy's Hill).

---

## Section 4 — `overton-hill` ("The hill the freedmen charged") — THE HUMAN CORE

**The charge lands — GOOD, this is the emotional center and it works.** The setup ("sent at
the strongest ground on the field to help destroy the army of the people who claimed to own
them"), the funnel/enfilade detail ("the deadliest fire there is. They went up into it
anyway"), the men reaching the parapet, the 13th's ~40% loss in its first action, and the
enemy's own testimony (Holtzclaw "never saw dead men lie thicker") form a complete, moving,
well-earned arc. The brief asked for this to land. It lands.

**The enemy-testimony beat — GOOD.** Pivoting from the Union verdict (Steedman, Thomas) to
the *Confederate* officer recording the valor of the men his cause existed to enslave is the
single most powerful move in the draft. "From a Confederate officer's own report" is the
right amount of underline.

**Tactical honesty — GOOD.** "Tactically, the Overton Hill assault *failed*... But failing
in the right place can still win a battle" is exactly the reframe the section needs so the
repulse doesn't read as the freedmen's charge being pointless. The drain-the-left mechanism
ties Overton to the real break at Shy's Hill — load-bearing.

**MUST-IMPROVE — the section tells its own meaning too many times.** The thesis ("railroad
is terrain / slavery is the reason / Overton is the proof") is stated at the top of the
section, embedded in the middle, and then delivered a *third* time as the closing paragraph
("That is the cause, made flesh... The railroad was the object. Slavery was the reason.
Overton Hill is the proof."). It is a genuinely great formulation — which is *why* saying it
three times costs it power. The scene (Black men dying on the parapet of the slaveholders'
army, praised by that army's own officer) already *is* the argument; the reader doesn't need
to be told three times what they just felt. **Recommendation:** keep the plain naming of
slavery up top (the gate requires it, and it's well-placed), and keep ONE ringing
"terrain vs. reason" statement — the closing one is the strongest. Cut or compress the
middle restatement. Trust the charge.

**WEAK — the opening "This is the heart of it" is a touch on-the-nose.** Telling the reader
"This is the heart of it — not just of the battle, but of what the whole war was about" is
the prose equivalent of a director shouting "this is the important part." The section proves
it within two paragraphs. SHOULD-IMPROVE: consider opening on the image (the slaveholders'
army watching the men it held in bondage come up the slope) and letting the significance be
self-evident.

**Two-brigades MEANWHILE — GOOD.** Necessary, accurate, and well-placed at the *end* so it
clarifies without interrupting the charge. Correct instinct to keep it out of the main beat.

## Section 5 — `the-army-destroyed` ("The line rolled up west to east")

**The real break — GOOD.** Shy's Hill is the section's engine and it's well-explained: the
geographical-crest-vs-military-crest flaw is defined in plain terms ("dead ground... where
attackers were sheltered from fire almost until the moment they reached the top") and the
salient-fired-from-all-sides detail makes the collapse feel inevitable rather than lucky.
"A trap waiting for the right shove" + McArthur's five-minutes-or-I-attack is a great
human-decision beat.

**The "zipper" image — GOOD.** "The collapse running down the army like a zipper pulled
open" is vivid and accurate to a west-to-east roll-up.

**The casualty-shape argument — GOOD, and this is sophisticated.** "The numbers tell the
story in their *shape* more than their size... You don't take 4,500 prisoners from an army
that's retreating in good order. You take them from one that no longer exists." This is the
right way to handle the casualty stats — it turns a number dump into the section's thesis
(this army didn't lose, it *dissolved*). Best handling of statistics in the draft.

**The Forrest payoff — GOOD.** Closing the long-range what-if (the unit sent away was the
only thing that saved the survivors) is satisfying and the "bitter irony of the campaign"
framing earns it. The named rearguard actions give it a factual spine.

**WEAK — the section has two endings and the final one re-litigates the whole draft.**
"Set it all in its place" (4th-to-last para) is a clean summary close. Then the Forrest
payoff. Then "Old Slow Trot kept the army" MEANWHILE. The final summary paragraph ("Atlanta
had fallen, Mobile Bay was sealed... The slow man had been right, the question of whether
Black soldiers would fight had been answered on Overton Hill...") recaps all three of the
draft's threads in one block. It's a strong paragraph, but it lands *after* the Beauregard/
Tupelo/Hood-resigns material already gave the battle its finality, so the section's
momentum sags in the back third before the recap re-gathers it. SHOULD-IMPROVE: pick ONE
closing summary movement. The "Set it all in its place... last offensive army in the West
was simply erased" paragraph is the strongest single close — consider letting it be the
end, with the Forrest payoff *before* it, so the draft ends on the army's erasure rather
than a thread-by-thread recap.

**WEAK — the Beauregard/effectives numbers (18,742 / <15,000) read as data after the
"shape" argument already won.** The draft already made the dissolution point with the
prisoner ratio; the post-battle effectives counts then make a second, quantitative version
of the same point. SHOULD-IMPROVE: keep Hood resigning and the Tupelo retreat (career
death = human stakes); the precise effectives figures could be trimmed to one.

## Cross-section notes

- **Thesis repetition across sections (SHOULD-IMPROVE).** "Slavery was the reason / terrain
  vs. reason" appears in §4 (three times) and again in §5's closing recap. Across the whole
  draft that's four-plus deliveries of one formulation. It's the draft's best idea; protect
  it by saying it fewer times. One ringing statement per relevant section is plenty.
- **Callback architecture (GOOD).** The two long-range setups — Forrest-sent-away (§1→§5)
  and the-slow-man-vindicated (§2→§5) — both pay off cleanly. This is the draft's structural
  strength; don't touch it.
- **No invented facts requested.** Every beat above is achievable by trimming/resequencing
  existing material; nothing here asks the author to add a fact the pack doesn't carry.
