## FIRST-CHECK (ship-blocking)

### (a) Are Native nations first-class actors with their own aims, not props? — PASS (FAIR)

This is the harder test for a section whose nominal subject is the *British-side* clash of military cultures (regulars vs. provincials). A weaker draft would treat Native nations only as the obstacle Braddock walked into or the scouts the British "needed." This draft does not. It makes their agency explicit and repeated:

- It devotes a full section ("The third army, the one that actually worked in the woods") to the proposition that the only reliably effective way of war "belonged first to the Native nations whose woods these were."
- It states the principle directly: "the Native nations were never simply the enemy, and never simply anyone's tool. They had their own country to defend, their own borders, their own calculations, and they used the rivalry between France and Britain as leverage to protect land and independence that both empires wanted. Native warriors fought on the French side, on the British side, and above all for themselves." This is exactly the first-class-actor framing the rule demands, and it explicitly covers BOTH sides.
- The Monongahela passage credits the French/Native force as the actor that *won* the fight ("His warriors fought from cover... while Braddock held his men in their tight road column"), names the specific nations (Ottawa, Ojibwa, Potawatomi, Abenaki, Lenape, Huron) and leaders (Shingas, Langlade), and correctly hedges Pontiac's contested presence — matching the fact pack.
- The Iroquois passage is the strongest single move: "the Confederacy was no one's instrument. It was divided, and it knew its own interests... weighing land, leverage, and survival, and bending toward neutrality whenever they could, because the one certainty was that both empires wanted what the Confederacy stood on. They were principals in this war, not auxiliaries to it." It also resists the trap of flattening "the Iroquois" into a pro-British bloc, distinguishing Mohawk leaning vs. Seneca/Onondaga Francophile lean.
- The Rogers' Saint-François raid is explicitly reframed away from scenery: "the people on the receiving end of this woodland war were communities of families, not scenery." Good — this is the spot most drafts would let slide into adventure-story register.

**No prop-reduction anywhere.** First-check (a) clears decisively.

### (b) Is the human cost / dispossession named in the section's OWN prose? — PASS (FAIR)

Both costs are named plainly, in the section's own voice, not euphemized or outsourced:

- **Native dispossession** (the war's central cost per the rule): "for the Native nations who had fought to hold a balance between two empires it was a catastrophe. The whole contest had been, at bottom, a war over their land, and now there was no second empire to play against the first... the dispossession that followed was the war's truest result." This is the required statement — the contest was over Native land, and the outcome was a catastrophe — stated flatly and given primacy ("truest result"), not buried.
- **Provincial human cost:** disease deaths are named without sanitizing — smallpox, dysentery, camp-fever/typhus, "Far more provincials were buried from sickness than ever fell to a French musket." Correctly written as a *pattern* rather than a fabricated F&I-specific ratio, exactly as the fact pack's §8/§13 caution requires.

First-check (b) clears.

### Naming the war from the British point of view — PASS (FAIR)

The rule asks that, where natural, the draft acknowledge that "French and Indian War" centers the British-colonial viewpoint. The draft does this twice and well: "They named it the French and Indian War, after the enemies they pictured, which tells you the war was being seen from one porch," and again in the close: "The British colonists who had named the war for their 'Indian' enemies pushed west..." It also explicitly says the "Indians" fought on all sides and for themselves. Fully satisfied.

## OTHER FRAMING FINDINGS

### 1. No romanticization of empire — FAIR
The draft does not lionize either British or colonial arms. British command is shown as contemptuous and wrong (Braddock's "what that contempt bought him is its own story"; Loudoun "watching a political culture he did not recognize and calling it insolence"; Wolfe's disdain). The colonists are not romanticized either — the contractual ethos is explained on its own terms (Puritan covenant), not flattered, and the close ("The contempt and the confidence went home together") is analytic, not triumphalist. The Revolution-foreshadowing ("rehearsing... a habit of mind their grandsons would not have to be taught") is a defensible historiographic thesis (Anderson's "schoolhouse"), not jingoism.

### 2. No false balance — FAIR
The regular-vs-provincial mutual contempt is presented symmetrically *as contempt*, but the draft does not false-balance the underlying rights question: it lands that "The same act meant rights to one side and crime to the other... They were arguing about what a man was." It takes the provincial grievance seriously rather than splitting the difference. The lash discipline is reported via Clough's witnessed sentences (30, 200, 500, 1,000), which is documented, not gratuitous.

### 3. Violence neither sanitized nor gratuitous — FAIR
The Monongahela is called "a slaughter" with real casualty figures (matching fact pack §9), not glamorized. The flogging detail is purposeful (it grounds the provincial grievance) and sourced to Clough, not lurid. The Saint-François raid is named as "destructive" and its victims named as families — the correct register.

### 4. Presentism — FAIR
The draft attributes the rights-language and contract ethos to its actual 18th-century roots (Puritan covenant, the elected-officer structure) rather than reading modern liberalism backward. The "grandsons" line is explicitly framed as something they did *not yet* understand ("without knowing it"), which avoids presentist over-claim.

### 5. Note (not a finding): unverified-quote flags are correctly deferred to FACT critic
The draft carries explicit `[VERIFY]` inline flags on the Braddock-via-Franklin line and the Wolfe "worst soldiers in the universe" line, and hedges Wolfe as "is remembered for" / "reportedly." These are sourcing questions for the FACT critic, not framing distortions; flagging them here only so they are not lost. From a *framing* standpoint, the Wolfe/Braddock contempt quotes are used to indict empire, not to romanticize it, so they do not create a framing problem even pending verification.

## SUMMARY

Both ship-blocking first-checks pass: Native nations are first-class actors with their own aims on both sides (not props), and both the Native dispossession ("the war's truest result") and the provincial human cost (camp disease) are named plainly in the section's own prose. The British-colonial naming of the war is acknowledged. No DISTORTED findings; no TILTED findings. The section is, if anything, a model for how a British-side theme section can keep Native agency and the human cost central without losing its own subject.

**Verdict: PASS** — no MUST-FIX, no SHOULD-FIX.
