# Storytelling Critic Report — "The Albany Congress & the Plan of Union"

**Section:** French and Indian War · theme (POLITICS) · no battle stat block
**Draft:** `audits/war-pipeline/fi-albany-plan-draft.md`
**Verdict: FAIL** (house-voice meta-narrator violations; one heading NEEDS WORK)

---

## Verdict at a glance

This is a strong, well-paced piece of historical storytelling. The big structural
moves are right: it leads with the Iroquois alliance as the congress's first business
(not a footnote), it holds the dual truth of diplomacy-and-dispossession in the same
breath, Hendrick's voice carries the middle, and the Franklin arc lands its
"failed-but-rehearsed-the-Revolution" payoff. The hook and stakes are clear; the human
dimension is real; the voice is the house voice.

It does NOT ship as written because the draft repeatedly steps out of the story to
lecture the reader on how to read it ("This is the part of the story that often gets
shrunk into a footnote," "It is worth being honest," "This is the part that has to be
held," "Notice what runs through that list," "There is one last thing the name itself
admits"). That is exactly the meta-narrator habit the locked war voice forbids
(`feedback_war_voice_restraint`: cut meta-narrator/drama). The facts and arrangement
already make these points; the narrator does not need to flag them. Excise the
editorializing scaffolding and the piece is a PASS.

## House-voice check

- **Em-dashes in prose:** PASS. Zero. The only em-dashes are in the `> quote:`
  attribution lines (`… — Theyanoguin (Hendrick) …`), which is the standard quote-block
  citation glyph, not prose punctuation. Clean.
- **Bold:** PASS. No `**bold**` anywhere in the prose.
- **Full-rank-first:** PASS. "Lieutenant Colonel George Washington" on first mention
  (line 4). Titles handled well throughout: "James DeLancey … Lieutenant Governor of
  New York," "President-General," "sachem (a civil leader)."
- **Cross-refs as standalone pill lines:** PASS. Three cross-refs, each on its own line
  with nothing else (lines 19, 80, 129). Correct.
- **Meta-narrator / drama:** **FAIL.** Multiple instances (see mustFix). This is the
  blocking violation.

## Heading-by-heading

### Opening (untitled lede) — STRONG
The hook is excellent. "a problem they could not solve one colony at a time" frames the
whole piece in one line; Washington's Jumonville Glen / Fort Necessity reverses give it
immediate war-stakes; "the first sparks of what would grow into a war fought on three
continents" sets scale without inflating. The kicker — "the meeting was not really their
idea, and the thing it is remembered for was not the first thing on its agenda" — is a
genuine, earned turn that sets up the Native-first reframing. No notes.

### Why London called the meeting — NEEDS WORK
The substance is right and clearly told: London ordered it, the Covenant Chain had gone
"badly rusty," gift-giving "was not a courtesy in this diplomacy, it was the diplomacy"
(a great line). But the closing paragraph breaks frame:

> "This is the part of the story that often gets shrunk into a footnote, and it should
> not be. The reason the colonies met at all was that the Native nations were drifting
> away, and the empire needed them back."

The *second* sentence is the real point and it lands. The *first* is the narrator telling
the reader how the story is usually told and instructing them to weight it correctly —
pure meta-narrator. Cut the first sentence; keep "The reason the colonies met at all was
that the Native nations were drifting away, and the empire needed them back." as the
section's clean close.

### The colonies that came — GOOD
Efficient roll-call that does real storytelling work: each name carries its future
("would help draft the union plan and one day become … a hated symbol of Crown
authority"; "would live to sign the Declaration of Independence"; Franklin "arrived with
an idea bigger than the meeting he had been sent to"). The "long list of absences"
eyebrow plus "did not bother to come" gives it attitude without overreaching. No
blocking notes. Minor: the parenthetical glosses ("the deputy to the colony's royal
governor") are fine for a zero-knowledge reader; leave them.

### The Iroquois come to the table — STRONG
The strongest stretch in the piece. The "they were not anyone's subjects … they
negotiated like one" framing is correct and forceful. Hendrick's quotes are sequenced
for maximum effect (the silence, the stick thrown behind the back, "you are like women,"
the land-quarrel grievance, the Onondaga close), and the staged-gesture beat is staged
well. The provenance honesty ("filtered through other men's pens, not as a recording")
is handled inside the story rather than as a disclaimer, and it sharpens rather than
weakens the moment: "He was not pleading. He was telling a partner it had failed to hold
up its end." The closing — "the alliance shored up and the land problem worse than
before" — sets up the next section perfectly. No notes.

### The land taken in the next room — NEEDS WORK
Best section title in the piece, and the content is the moral spine of the theme:
Wyoming Valley deed, the £2,000, the secrecy and "liquor and bribes," the excluded
Delaware (Lenape) and Shawnee, the Walking Purchase callback, Weiser's parallel grab,
the Pennamite-Yankee fuse. All gripping, all sourced. Two frame-breaks weaken it:

1. Opening line: "the difference between the two is the whole truth of the event." The
   narrator announcing the thesis. The juxtaposition itself ("one part … renewing a
   friendship, another part … quietly stealing land") already *is* the point.
2. Closing paragraph: "This is the part that has to be held alongside the diplomacy, not
   separated from it. … Both things are true, in the same town, in the same three weeks."
   This is the narrator instructing the reader to hold two truths — telling, not
   showing. The "same town … same three weeks" image is good and can stay if rephrased
   as narration of fact, but "This is the part that has to be held" is meta-commentary.

Fix: drop the "whole truth of the event" tag from the opener; rewrite the close so the
two facts sit side by side as narrated events without the narrator's instruction
("The same gathering that renewed the Covenant Chain stripped land from Native nations
who were not even in the room to object.").

### Franklin's bigger idea — GOOD
Solid. The "Join, or Die" timing is handled exactly as the fact pack demands ("the
cartoon ran before the Albany delegates ever sat down"), the folk-superstition detail
("a snake chopped to pieces could come back to life") earns its place, and the
mechanics of the plan (President-General / Grand Council, who appoints whom, whose assent
is required) are made legible for a lay reader. One meta-narrator beat to fix:

> "Notice what runs through that list: the three things the plan most wanted to take out
> of thirteen quarreling hands … were defense, Indian affairs, and western lands. The
> same things, in other words, that the colonies had just spent three weeks mishandling
> in person."

"Notice what runs through that list" is the narrator directing the reader's eye. The
observation underneath is sharp and worth keeping — just state it: "Three things ran
through that list: defense, Indian affairs, and the western lands — the same three the
colonies had just spent three weeks mishandling in person." (Note: no em-dash needed; a
period or comma works.)

### Adopted, then refused by everyone — STRONG
Clean, satisfying turn. "rejected from both directions for exactly opposite reasons"
structures it; the two Franklin autobiography quotes are well placed and the "true
medium" reflection is the right note to end on. The eyebrow ("Too much power, and not
enough, at the same time") nails the irony. No notes.

### What the failure rehearsed — NEEDS WORK
The legacy payoff is genuinely moving — "The union the colonies refused to build for
defense in 1754 was the one they would build, in anger, about twenty years later" is the
line the whole piece has been driving toward, and the Stamp Act Congress / First
Continental Congress beats earn it. The Franklin "might-have-been" quote is the right
capstone. Two issues:

1. "How the war was eventually paid for, and how that bill found its way back to the
   colonies as taxes, is its own story." This is the "that's a tale for another time"
   meta-move — a teaser for the cross-ref pill that follows it. The pill line
   ([War Finance & the Road to Taxation]) already does this job. Cut the sentence; let
   the pill carry the handoff.
2. The final paragraph (the "French and Indian War" naming critique, lines 133) is a
   strong and correct close in substance — Native nations "fought on all sides and, more
   than anything, for themselves." But it opens with "There is one last thing the name
   itself admits," which is the narrator clearing his throat one more time. Open instead
   on the fact: "The name itself admits something. The British colonists called this the
   French and Indian War …". Also reconsider whether this naming-critique paragraph
   belongs *after* the Franklin legacy capstone — it is a second ending. It is the more
   thematically resonant of the two, so consider moving the Franklin "might-have-been"
   quote to truly last, or accept the naming paragraph as the deliberate final word
   (defensible — it returns to the Native-first frame the piece opened on). Not blocking;
   author's call. The frame-break opener IS blocking.

## mustFix (each is a heading-level NEEDS WORK or a house-voice violation)

1. **Meta-narrator (line 17):** "This is the part of the story that often gets shrunk
   into a footnote, and it should not be." Delete the sentence; keep the factual sentence
   after it.
2. **Meta-narrator (line 70):** "the difference between the two is the whole truth of the
   event." Cut the thesis-announcement tag; let the juxtaposition stand.
3. **Meta-narrator (lines 77–78):** "This is the part that has to be held alongside the
   diplomacy, not separated from it. … Both things are true, in the same town, in the
   same three weeks." Rewrite as narrated fact, removing the instruction to the reader.
4. **Meta-narrator (line 99):** "Notice what runs through that list:" Remove the
   reader-direction; state the observation plainly.
5. **Meta-narrator (line 127):** "How the war was eventually paid for … is its own
   story." Delete; the War Finance cross-ref pill already carries the handoff.
6. **Meta-narrator (line 133):** "There is one last thing the name itself admits." Open
   on the fact instead ("The name itself admits something.").

## shouldFix

- Section "What the failure rehearsed" stacks two endings (Franklin's might-have-been
  quote, then the naming critique). Consider ordering so the strongest note is genuinely
  last; not required.
- "Notice the timing:" in the Franklin section (line 87) is a milder cousin of the same
  reader-direction habit. The sentence after it makes the point on its own. Optional cut.

## notes

cross-refs as standalone pills). The fixes are subtractive; none requires new facts.
