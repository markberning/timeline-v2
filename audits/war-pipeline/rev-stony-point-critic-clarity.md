# NEWCOMER-CLARITY CRITIC · Stony Point draft

*Critic pass on `audits/war-pipeline/rev-stony-point-draft.md`, 2026-06-12. Reader model:
zero prior knowledge — does not know the Revolution's geography, cannot place the Hudson or
the Highlands on a mental map, cannot picture a barbed-wire equivalent made of trees, has
not read any other article in this vertical, and has no military vocabulary beyond
"soldier" and "cannon."*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

A remarkably clean draft for its length. Geography and geometry are consistently excellent:
the peninsula-marsh-causeway picture, the three-column plan, the distance scale, the
strategic context of West Point and the Hudson seam — all clear and followable without a
diagram. The "why unloaded muskets" logic is stated explicitly and early. The "war of
posts" (why Washington abandoned what he bled for) is fully legible by Section 3. One
BLOCKER: "forlorn hope" is introduced but not defined at a moment when the newcomer needs
to feel the weight of it. A cluster of SHOULD-FIX terms are undefined or arrive glossed
too late. No structural blockers; the plan followability and geography checks all pass.

---

## BLOCKER

**B1. "Forlorn hope" introduced, never defined — and the omission costs the assault's
emotional peak.**
Section 2, first use: "ahead of each van went a forlorn hope, a twenty-man volunteer party
whose axemen had to hack lanes through the two belts of abatis under fire, knowing the men
in front are the ones who fall."

The phrase is used and then described by its function, but "forlorn hope" is never
explained as a fixed military term with a specific meaning (a volunteer advance party
accepted as likely to die — from the Dutch *verloren hoop*, "lost troop"). A newcomer
reads this and gets the functional description but misses that these men volunteered for an
assignment widely understood to be a death lottery. The casualty line immediately after —
"one of the twenty-man parties lost most of its men cutting the lanes" — is the draft's
most affecting sentence, and it lands harder if the reader knows "forlorn hope" was
already a term for near-certain sacrifice, not just a vivid description the author invented.

The fact ledger notes "forlorn hope, van, abatis (reused), escalade glossed" — but the
draft text does NOT gloss "forlorn hope" in the prose. The function is described, but the
term's meaning (and the weight it carried to every soldier who heard it) is absent.

- FIX: Change "ahead of each van went a forlorn hope, a twenty-man volunteer party whose
  axemen had to hack lanes through the two belts of abatis under fire, knowing the men in
  front are the ones who fall" to "ahead of each van went a forlorn hope — the term for a
  volunteer advance party the army regarded as almost certainly lost — twenty men whose
  axemen had to hack lanes through the two belts of abatis under fire before the column
  could follow."

## SHOULD-FIX

**S1. "Light infantry" defined only in the dossier sides note, never in the prose sections
where newcomers need it.**
The dossier sides note calls the corps "the army's elite, re-formed for the 1779 campaign
from the picked light companies of the Continental line (the full-time regulars): young,
agile, vetted men drilled to Baron von Steuben's new standard." That is a reasonable gloss,
but most phone readers tap directly into Section 1 or Section 2 prose and will not read
the dossier before entering. "Light infantry" in Section 2 prose arrives cold: "The Corps
of Light Infantry was the instrument the plan needed, and it had been built for exactly
this." The newcomer does not know what distinguishes light infantry from regular infantry
(fast, agile skirmishers who operate in open order rather than the tight shoulder-to-
shoulder line — the agility doctrine that made a night flanking assault possible at all).

- FIX (Section 2, at the first prose appearance): "The Corps of Light Infantry — fast,
  agile soldiers trained to fight in open formation and move at speed, rather than in the
  tight shoulder-to-shoulder line — was the instrument the plan needed, and it had been
  built for exactly this."

**S2. "Grenadier company" introduced in the dossier sides note without a definition.**
The dossier sides note for the British: "a grenadier company of a Highland regiment." This
is the newcomer's first encounter with grenadiers — the term is used at historical face
value without explanation. In the Saratoga draft this was a SHOULD-FIX; it is no easier
here. Grenadiers were historically the army's biggest, most experienced heavy infantry,
retained as elite shock troops long after grenades went out of fashion. The newcomer has
no frame for "grenadier" as distinct from any other soldier.

- FIX (dossier sides note, British, first use): "a grenadier company (the British army's
  elite heavy-infantry soldiers, the biggest and most experienced men in the regiment) of
  a Highland regiment."

**S3. "Abatis" defined in Section 1 but the definition arrives nine lines after the
first use in the dossier sides note.**
The dossier sides note (British commander note) reads: "two belts of abatis on a rock
surrounded by water on three sides." This is the newcomer's first encounter with "abatis"
and it is bare. The gloss — "felled trees with their sharpened branches turned outward,
the period's barbed wire" — arrives correctly in Section 1 prose on the first prose use.
But a newcomer who reads the dossier before the sections (which the app presents first)
hits the word cold once.

- FIX (dossier sides note, British, at first use): "two belts of abatis (felled trees
  turned sharp-end out, the period's barbed wire) on a rock surrounded by water on three
  sides." One word swap; the Section 1 gloss can then drop the parenthetical or retain it
  as a reinforcement.

**S4. "Van" is listed in the fact ledger as glossed, but it is NOT glossed in the prose.**
Section 2: "Two columns would make the real attack...Ahead of each assault column went a
van, a leading party, with Lieutenant Colonel François-Louis de Fleury (American), the
French volunteer engineer, leading the south column's." The word "van" is used and
immediately re-described as "a leading party" — which works — but the structure is
reversed (term first, plain-English second) and a newcomer reading fast may absorb
"a van" as a vehicle. A short swap fixes it.

- FIX: "Ahead of each assault column went a leading party — the van — with Lieutenant
  Colonel François-Louis de Fleury (American), the French volunteer engineer, at the head
  of the south column's."

**S5. "Watchword" introduced but never explained.**
Section 2: "the watchword, to be shouted only once the works were taken, was 'The fort's
our own!'" A watchword is a pre-agreed challenge-and-response or recognition phrase —
in this context, the signal-shout that told men in the dark that the fort had fallen and
to stop shooting their own side. A newcomer gets no frame for why shouting a phrase in
the dark was tactically meaningful (the fear of fratricide in a night assault).

- FIX: "the watchword — the prearranged shout that would tell every man in the dark that
  the fort was taken and the killing should stop — was 'The fort's our own!'"

**S6. "West Point's significance" is explained, but the Hudson chain is mentioned without
clarification of what it actually was.**
Section 1: "the fortress complex being built at West Point, a bend in the river about 50
miles above the city, with a great iron chain stretched across the water to stop ships
from passing." This is mostly clear, but the newcomer may wonder: how does an iron chain
stop a ship? The answer — the chain floated on log booms across the river's full width,
physically blocking passage — is the detail that makes it legible as a military obstacle
rather than a symbolic gesture.

- FIX: "with a great iron chain — a massive linked boom stretched across the river's full
  width on floating logs — to physically block British ships from passing upriver." One
  clause addition; everything else in that paragraph stands.

**S7. "Henry Clinton" is introduced in Section 1 as "General Sir Henry Clinton (British),
the British commander in chief in America" — but this is the newcomer's first encounter
with Clinton and the double "British" tag in the same sentence is awkward and may read
as a copy error.**
The sentence: "General Sir Henry Clinton (British), the British commander in chief in
America, held New York City." The parenthetical side-tag plus the immediately following
"the British commander" are redundant in a distracting way.

- FIX (light): "General Sir Henry Clinton, the British commander in chief in America, held
  New York City." Drop the parenthetical; the "British commander in chief" phrase carries
  the identification fully on its own. (If the side-tag system is mandatory in the opening
  paragraph, keep it; but "General Sir Henry Clinton (British), the British commander"
  reads oddly.)

## NIT

**N1. "War of posts" logic — why Washington abandoned a fort they just bled for — is
legible but slightly back-loaded.**
The answer appears fully and clearly in Section 3: "holding Stony Point would tie down
more than fifteen hundred men under the guns of the Royal Navy, sitting exposed on the
same rock where Johnson had just been taken." This is excellent. But the dossier stats
Winner line already contains: "the trophy was the proof, not the ground, and Washington
abandoned the point three days later" — which a newcomer reads without any explanation of
why. The newcomer carrying that dossier into Section 1 may be mildly confused for two full
sections about why you'd assault something you plan to give back. A single short
foreshadowing clause in the Section 1 intro or Section 1 note would land the logic earlier.

- FIX (optional, Section 1, after introducing the plan): "The attack, if it succeeded, was
  never meant to hold the ground — only to take it, haul off the guns, and walk away,
  giving Clinton nothing to besiege." Or leave as is; Section 3 handles it completely.

**N2. "King's Ferry" is named in the dossier `place` field before it is explained.**
The dossier place field: "Stony Point, New York (west bank of the Hudson at King's Ferry,
about 12 miles below West Point)." A newcomer reading the dossier first hits "King's Ferry"
with no gloss; it is fully explained in Section 1 prose. Since the dossier place field is
a structural label read before any prose, a brief appositive would help.

- FIX (place field): "Stony Point, New York (west bank of the Hudson at King's Ferry — the
  main river crossing between New England and the mid-Atlantic states — about 12 miles
  below West Point)." Or accept that dossier field is short-form metadata and leave it.

**N3. "Escalade" (Section 3) is glossed correctly but arrives late.**
Section 3: "a silent night escalade (an assault by scaling a fortification's defenses)."
The gloss is present and correct. POLISH only: "escalade" is a single technical term; the
phrasing "an assault by scaling a fortification's defenses" is slightly abstract.
"an assault that goes over the walls instead of through them" is plainer.

**N4. "A piece of clockwork" metaphor (Section 2) — a minor readability note.**
"The plan that the officers learned there was a piece of clockwork." This is a fine
idiom — but it is immediately followed by a sentence structure that lists three
simultaneous things, which IS the clockwork. No fix required; flag only if the reviser
wants to tighten.

**N5. "Scalp" wound — the prose says Wayne was "grazed across the scalp by a musket ball."**
"Scalp" here means the top of the head/skull, not a scalping action — but the word
"scalp" in an American Revolutionary War context can briefly trigger the wrong association
for a newcomer (Native American warfare). Given the Paoli revenge-and-restraint theme
running through the article, a newcomer momentarily misreading "scalped" would be briefly
disorienting. "Grazed across the head" or "grazed at the top of the skull" removes the
false read at no cost to accuracy.

**N6. The sentence "Both sides said so" appears twice — once in the dossier note and
once in Section 3 — without the Collier quote yet in view.**
Dossier note: "officers on both sides remarked on it." Section 3 intro: "contemporaries
on both sides said so plainly." The Collier quote that substantiates "both sides" arrives
later in Section 3. The structure works — the quotes follow the claim — but the
double-assert before the evidence is a faint redundancy. POLISH only.

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES CLEANLY

- **Peninsula-marsh-causeway geography: PASS.** Section 1 builds the picture in sequence:
  rock rising 150 feet, water on three sides, tidal marsh + causeway on the landward
  side, flats flooding at high tide. A newcomer can draw it.

- **Stony Point relative to NYC and West Point: PASS.** The dossier gives "about 12 miles
  below West Point." Section 1 gives "about 50 miles above the city" for West Point and
  the clear implication that the ferry is the southernmost crossing below it. The strategic
  map locates this well.

- **Three-column plan followable without a diagram: PASS.** Section 2 names the three
  pieces in sequence — south column (Wayne, unloaded, across the flats), north column
  (Butler, unloaded, along the river), center diversion (Murfree, loaded, at the
  causeway) — with each piece's purpose stated. The sequence of events follows the plan
  in order: Murfree fires, Johnson moves to the noise, both flanks go over. A newcomer
  can follow it.

- **Why unloaded muskets — logic stated: PASS.** The logic is explicit and appears twice:
  in Section 1 ("a single nervous shot in the dark would wake the garrison and throw away
  the surprise") and in Section 2 ("they could not stop to fire"). The hero image caption
  also states it. Clear.

- **Why Washington abandoned the fort — war-of-posts logic: PASS.** Section 3 gives the
  full reasoning explicitly: holding the point would tie down 1,500 men under the Royal
  Navy's guns on the same exposed rock. The "trophy not the ground" frame is in the
  dossier Winner line and the outcome block. Legible.

- **Timeline of the night: PASS.** The sequence is clear: departure from Sandy Beach
  ~noon July 15 → assembly at Springsteel's farm in the evening → columns out ~11:30 p.m.
  → the south column hits deep water and loses time → pickets fire the alarm → Murfree's
  companies open → Johnson moves to the noise → the forlorn hopes go into the abatis →
  columns over the works → Wayne wounded → Fleury first in → the summit met ~25 minutes
  after first contact → Wayne's dispatch at 2 a.m. The night is fully traceable.

- **Number scale: PASS.** ~1,200–1,350 Americans assault ~600–700 British. The force ratio
  is clear; the casualty figures are precise and honest about their uncertainty. The
  fort's ~15 guns and the garrison's composition are stated.

- **Antecedents: PASS.** The only mild risk is "the watchword was being shouted behind him"
  (referring to Johnson) in the dossier commander bio — "him" is unambiguous in context.
  No floating antecedent found in prose sections.

- **Miles-first rule: PASS.** No metric measurements appear in the prose. Distances are
  in miles (13-mile march, 12 miles below West Point, 50 miles above the city). Heights
  and depths in feet. Fully compliant.

- **Anthony Wayne introduced: PASS.** Wayne is introduced in Section 1 with full context:
  "Brigadier General Anthony Wayne (American), a Pennsylvania tanner's son turned
  brigadier, who had taken command of the army's new Corps of Light Infantry on the first
  of July." His Paoli connection is carried from the commander bio into the prose
  correctly.

- **Henry Clinton introduced: CONDITIONAL PASS.** Identified as "General Sir Henry Clinton
  (British), the British commander in chief in America" at first use. The double-tagging
  issue is flagged as a NIT above but does not block comprehension.

- **King's Ferry explained: PASS in prose.** Section 1 defines it completely: "where the
  river narrowed between two points of land: Stony Point on the west bank and Verplanck's
  Point on the east... the shortcut for men, mail, and supplies moving between New England
  and the mid-Atlantic states." The dossier `place` field is the only cold use (NIT N2).

- **Paoli context: PASS.** Introduced in Wayne's commander bio as "Wayne's own division had
  been bayoneted in the dark at Paoli in September 1777" and carried correctly into
  Section 2's restraint beat: "The same corps had grievances to settle. Two years before,
  Wayne's own division had been surprised in the dark and bayoneted at Paoli, in
  Pennsylvania, an attack Americans called a massacre." The connection to the quarter given
  is explicit.

- **"Blockhouse" glossed: PASS.** Section 1: "the unfinished American blockhouse (a small
  log fort)."

- **"Loyalists" glossed: PASS.** Section 1, dossier sides note: "a company of Loyalists
  (Americans who stayed loyal to the king)."

- **"Field fortification" vs. masonry fort distinction: PASS.** Section 1: "The British had
  not built a stone fort there but a field fortification, which for an attacker is bad
  enough." The difference is implied (masonry = permanent; field = earthworks and timber),
  and the newcomer gets the point that it was hard without needing the technical
  distinction.
