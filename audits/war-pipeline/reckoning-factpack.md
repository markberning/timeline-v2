5. **Reconstruction — the heart** — Johnson vs. the Radicals; Black Codes; Freedmen's Bureau; the 14th & 15th Amendments; the brief flowering of Black political power (Revels, Bruce, ~16 congressmen, ~2,000 officeholders); the violent backlash (KKK, Colfax 1873); Johnson's impeachment (1868).

7. **What the war settled vs. what it didn't** — the Union is permanent and indivisible, chattel slavery is dead (settled); racial equality and the meaning of Black freedom (NOT settled — lost for a century).
8. **The Lost Cause** — how the defeated South rewrote the war's memory (states' rights, the noble Confederacy, the faithful slave, Reconstruction-as-tragedy); the monuments; the UDC. The final irony.
9. **The closing meaning** — 750,000 dead, 4 million freed, a nation remade — and a reckoning deferred, not resolved.

## BEAT 1: The Human Toll

### The traditional figure (~620,000)
- For over a century the standard total was **~618,000–620,000 military dead**, conventionally split **~360,000 Union** and **~258,000 Confederate** (the often-cited precise figure is 618,222: 360,222 Union, 258,000 Confederate).
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Casualties" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/civil-war-casualties); History.com, "How Many Died in the American Civil War?" (https://www.history.com/articles/american-civil-war-deaths)
- **About two-thirds of those deaths were from disease, not combat** — this is the Medicine section's story; cross-reference, do not re-derive. (The traditional figure: ~2 of every 3 deaths from disease.)
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Casualties" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/civil-war-casualties)
- The 620,000 figure was always understood to be soft: the Confederate 258,000 in particular rested on incomplete battle reports and a crude estimate of disease deaths.
  — Source: History.com, "How Many Died in the American Civil War?" (https://www.history.com/articles/american-civil-war-deaths)

### The revised figure (J. David Hacker, 2011) — USE AS THE HEADLINE NUMBER, WITH RANGE
- In **December 2011**, **J. David Hacker** (then a demographic historian at SUNY-Binghamton; now University of Minnesota) published a study in the journal *Civil War History* arguing the death toll was substantially higher: **about 750,000**, with a plausible range of **650,000 to 850,000**.
  — Source: Binghamton University Research News, "Historian revises estimate of Civil War dead" (https://discovere.binghamton.edu/news/civilwar-3826.html); ScienceDaily, "U.S. Civil War took bigger toll than previously estimated" (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/09/110921120124.htm)
- **Method:** Hacker used newly digitized U.S. census micro-data (1850–1880) to measure the "missing" young men — comparing the surviving male population after the war against the demographically expected population had there been no war. The shortfall implied ~750,000 excess male deaths attributable to the war, capturing disease and war-related deaths that were never officially tallied.
  — Source: Binghamton University Research News (https://discovere.binghamton.edu/news/civilwar-3826.html); ScienceDaily (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/09/110921120124.htm)
- **The revision is ~20% higher** than the traditional 620,000.
  — Source: Binghamton University Research News (https://discovere.binghamton.edu/news/civilwar-3826.html)
- **Scholarly acceptance:** Pulitzer Prize-winning historian **James McPherson** endorsed the revision, saying the new figure "should gain acceptance among most Civil War scholars" and that an accurate tally matters "to gauge the huge impact of the war on American society."
  — Source: ScienceDaily (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/09/110921120124.htm); Binghamton University Research News (https://discovere.binghamton.edu/news/civilwar-3826.html)
- **AUTHOR GUIDANCE:** Lead with "about 750,000 — and possibly as many as 850,000," note it as a *revised, census-based estimate*, and contrast it with the old 620,000. Do not present 750,000 as a hard count; it is the best modern estimate of a range.

### Scale comparisons
- **~2% of the U.S. population** (population ~31 million in 1860) died — the equivalent share of a modern U.S. population would be in the **millions**.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Casualties" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/civil-war-casualties)
- The Civil War's military dead **exceed American military deaths in all other U.S. wars COMBINED** — a standard, widely repeated comparison (the Civil War remains the deadliest war in American history; its dead outnumber the combined U.S. military deaths of the Revolution, War of 1812, Mexican War, Spanish-American War, WWI, WWII, Korea, and Vietnam).
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Casualties" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/civil-war-casualties); History.com, "How Many Died in the American Civil War?" (https://www.history.com/articles/american-civil-war-deaths) [NOTE: the "more than all other wars combined" line is true against the *traditional* 620,000 figure and remains true under Hacker's; framed as such it is safe. The margin is smaller if Iraq/Afghanistan and a high WWII count are included, but the standard claim holds — see CONTESTED.]

### The wounded and the maimed
- An estimated **~476,000 soldiers were wounded** (bullets, shrapnel, sabers, bayonets) over the course of the war.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Amputations and the Civil War" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/amputations-and-civil-war)
- **~70,000 amputees** is the high-end estimate of men who lost a limb; about **three of every four operations** during the war were amputations. (Union surgeons alone performed just under 30,000 amputations, with a ~26% mortality; ~75% of amputees survived.)
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Amputations and the Civil War" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/amputations-and-civil-war)
- These men came home permanently disabled; states like Mississippi spent a fifth of their postwar budget on artificial limbs. The widows and orphans number in the hundreds of thousands (a generation of fatherless households North and South).
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Amputations and the Civil War" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/amputations-and-civil-war) [the Mississippi prosthetics budget figure is widely cited; flag for verification if quoted precisely — see CONTESTED]

### The death rate of USCT and the enslaved (the most disproportionate toll)
- Of roughly **180,000 United States Colored Troops (USCT)**, **over 36,000 died — a ~20.5% mortality rate**, about **35% higher** than the death rate among white Union troops.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "United States Colored Troops" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Colored_Troops)
- Most of those USCT deaths were from **disease**, which struck Black soldiers far harder: the USCT disease-mortality rate was **148 per 1,000** vs. **88 per 1,000** for white troops (smallpox was 6–7× more prevalent among Black soldiers; scurvy ~5×). Causes were structural — worse camps, worse rations, worse medical care, and assignment to unhealthy labor/garrison duty.
  — Source: PMC, "Why Did Black Soldiers Historically Have More Pneumonia Than White Soldiers" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10540094/); Wikipedia, "United States Colored Troops" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Colored_Troops)
- Beyond soldiers, **enslaved and freed people died in enormous numbers** in wartime "contraband" camps from disease, exposure, and starvation — a toll historically undercounted and not captured in the soldier tallies. [VERIFY for a precise figure — estimates of freedpeople's wartime deaths vary widely and are not firmly pinned; present as "tens of thousands, undercounted" rather than a hard number.]

## BEAT 2: The Actual End of the Confederacy (off the battlefield)

- **Appomattox (April 9, 1865)** — Lee surrenders the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant. **OWNED BY THE BATTLE LAYER.** Reference only as the symbolic end; this section does not narrate the surrender scene.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Appomattox Court House" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/civil-war/battles/appomattox-court-house)
- **Bennett Place (April 26, 1865)** — Gen. **Joseph E. Johnston** surrendered to Gen. **William T. Sherman** at the Bennett farm near Durham Station, North Carolina. This was the **largest surrender of the war — ~89,270 Confederate troops** across the Carolinas, Georgia, and Florida. (Negotiations began Apr 17–18; the first lenient terms were rejected by Washington; final terms signed Apr 26. Johnston disobeyed Davis's order to keep fighting a guerrilla war.)
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Bennett Place Surrender" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/bennett-place-surrender); NC Historic Sites, "April 26, 1865 Agreement" (https://historicsites.nc.gov/all-sites/bennett-place/history/april-26-1865-agreement)
- **Jefferson Davis captured (May 10, 1865)** — the fleeing Confederate president was caught by Union cavalry (Gen. James H. Wilson's command) near **Irwinville, Georgia**. Imprisoned at Fort Monroe, Virginia (placed briefly in leg-irons); indicted for treason but **never tried**; released on bail in 1867.
  — Source: History.com, "Confederate President Jefferson Davis captured" (https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/may-10/jefferson-davis-captured); Britannica, "Jefferson Davis: Capture and imprisonment" (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jefferson-Davis/Capture-and-imprisonment)
- **Kirby Smith (May 26 / June 2, 1865)** — Gen. **Edmund Kirby Smith**, commanding the Trans-Mississippi, was among the last; his forces' surrender was agreed May 26 and he formally signed terms in Galveston on **June 2, 1865**, ending Confederate land resistance.
  — Source: History.com, "One of the last Confederate generals surrenders" (https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/May-26/one-of-the-last-confederate-generals-surrenders); Wikipedia, "Conclusion of the American Civil War" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conclusion_of_the_American_Civil_War)
- **CSS *Shenandoah* (November 6, 1865)** — the Confederate commerce raider, still hunting Union whaling ships in the Pacific/Bering Sea after the war ended, learned of the collapse late; rather than surrender to U.S. authorities its captain sailed around the world and **surrendered to British authorities at Liverpool, England, on November 6, 1865** — the last organized Confederate force to lower its flag, seven months after Appomattox.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "CSS Shenandoah" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSS_Shenandoah); Wikipedia, "Conclusion of the American Civil War" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conclusion_of_the_American_Civil_War)

## BEAT 3: The Devastation — Especially the South

### Burned cities and ruined land
- **Richmond** — the Confederate capital burned on April 2–3, 1865, when retreating Confederate forces set fire to tobacco warehouses, bridges, and military stores; the "Burnt District" consumed a large part of the commercial downtown. The iconic photographs of Richmond's ruins (April 1865) became the visual shorthand for the South's defeat.
  — Source: Library of Congress, "Ruins of Richmond, Va., April 1865" (https://www.loc.gov/item/2014645782/); American Battlefield Trust (Richmond materials)
- **Atlanta** — burned in November 1864 as Sherman left for the March to the Sea; much of the city's military and industrial core was destroyed. (Owned more by the battle/March layer; reference as devastation.)
- **Columbia, South Carolina** — much of the city burned on the night of February 17, 1865, as Sherman's army occupied it; responsibility (retreating Confederates' burning cotton vs. Union troops) is historically disputed, but the city's destruction was near-total in its center.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Economic history of the American Civil War" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_the_American_Civil_War) [Columbia fire responsibility is contested — present as "burned, amid still-disputed blame"]
- The countryside was wrecked: fences down, fields overrun, livestock gone. Concrete illustration — South Carolina had ~965,000 hogs before the war and ~150,000 after.
  — Source: EH.net, "The Economics of the Civil War" (https://eh.net/encyclopedia/the-economics-of-the-civil-war/)

### The economic collapse
- **Emancipation destroyed the South's single largest form of capital.** Enslaved people had represented the lion's share of Southern capital investment — collectively worth more than all the region's land. The ~$3 billion (1860 dollars) "value" assigned to ~4 million enslaved human beings — the South's wealth on paper — was wiped out overnight (a destruction of property in the eyes of slaveholders; a liberation of people in fact).
  — Source: EH.net, "The Economics of the Civil War" (https://eh.net/encyclopedia/the-economics-of-the-civil-war/); Wikipedia, "Economic history of the American Civil War" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_the_American_Civil_War) [the ~$3 billion 1860 enslaved-property valuation is a standard figure but varies by source — present as "roughly $3 billion in 1860 dollars," flag as estimate]
- The Confederate economy was in ruins by 1865; the 11 ex-Confederate states **remained poor for roughly a century**, their land values depreciated, planters in debt, the regional economy reorganized around sharecropping and tenancy rather than recovery.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Economic history of the American Civil War" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_the_American_Civil_War)

### The dollar cost
- Direct wartime expenditures are conventionally estimated at well over **$6.7 billion (1860s dollars)** combined for both sides (figures vary; Union spending far exceeded Confederate). Modern inflation-adjusted "total cost" estimates that bundle military spending, property destruction, and the economic value of emancipation run into the **trillions** (one widely cited modern estimate puts the all-in cost at ~$5+ trillion in 2024 dollars).
  — Source: WarCosts.org, "Civil War" (https://www.warcosts.org/civil-war); EH.net, "The Economics of the Civil War" (https://eh.net/encyclopedia/the-economics-of-the-civil-war/) [AUTHOR GUIDANCE: the trillions figure is an advocacy-group modeling estimate — use cautiously, attribute it, prefer the contemporaneous billions figure as the firm number and the "trillions in today's dollars" only as a loose modern reframing.]

## BEAT 4: Emancipation Made Permanent — The 13th Amendment

- The Emancipation Proclamation (Jan 1, 1863) was a *war measure* — it freed enslaved people only in the rebelling states and rested on the president's war powers; it did not, by itself, abolish slavery as an institution or reach the loyal border states. (This is earlier-section territory; reference it as the reason a constitutional amendment was needed.)
- **The 13th Amendment** abolished slavery permanently and everywhere in the United States: *"Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction."*
  — Source: National Archives, "13th Amendment" (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment)
- **Passed the House January 31, 1865**, by a vote of **119–56** — just over the required two-thirds (the Senate had already passed it in April 1864). **Ratified December 6, 1865**, when the necessary three-fourths of states had approved it.
  — Source: National Archives, "13th Amendment" (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment); History.com, "House passes the 13th Amendment" (https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/january-31/house-passes-the-13th-amendment)
- The amendment made **~4 million** formerly enslaved people legally free.
  — Source: National Museum of African American History and Culture, "13th Amendment" (https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/stories/13th-amendment-constitution-united-states) [the 4 million figure is the standard 1860-census-based enslaved population total; widely used]
- **The crucial framing — legal freedom ≠ real freedom.** The 13th Amendment ended ownership of human beings, but it answered none of the questions that mattered next: Would freedpeople be citizens? Could they vote, own land, testify in court, move freely, keep their wages? The "except as a punishment for crime" clause itself became a loophole later exploited through convict leasing. The amendment opened the question of what Black freedom would mean — it did not settle it.
  — Source: National Museum of African American History and Culture, "13th Amendment" (https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/stories/13th-amendment-constitution-united-states)

## BEAT 5: Reconstruction — The Heart (be Foner-accurate, NOT Lost Cause)

### The collision: Andrew Johnson vs. the Radical Republicans
- **Andrew Johnson** (Tennessee Unionist, Lincoln's second-term VP, president after the assassination) favored a lenient, fast restoration of the white South. He pardoned ex-Confederates generously, allowed Southern states to reorganize under white-dominated governments, and opposed federal protection of freedpeople's rights.
  — Source: American Yawp, "Reconstruction" (http://www.americanyawp.com/text/15-reconstruction/)
- The **Radical Republicans** in Congress (Thaddeus Stevens in the House, Charles Sumner in the Senate as leading figures) wanted a thorough remaking of the South: civil and political rights for freedpeople, federal enforcement, and a real reckoning with the slaveholding order. The struggle between Johnson and Congress defined "Presidential" Reconstruction (1865–66) giving way to "Radical"/Congressional Reconstruction (1867 onward).
  — Source: American Yawp, "Reconstruction" (http://www.americanyawp.com/text/15-reconstruction/)

### The Black Codes (1865–66)
- The white-led Southern legislatures of 1865–66 passed **Black Codes** — laws designed to recreate slavery's social and economic order without the name. They restricted Black mobility and employment (vagrancy laws that forced freedpeople into labor contracts), barred them from many trades, restricted property and testimony rights, and bound Black children to white "apprenticeships."
  — Source: Britannica, "Black Codes" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Black-Codes); American Battlefield Trust, "Black Codes" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/black-codes)
- The Black Codes outraged the North and convinced Congress that the white South intended to nullify emancipation in practice — driving the turn to harder, Congressionally-enforced Reconstruction.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Black Codes" (https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/black-codes)

### The Freedmen's Bureau (created March 1865)
- Congress created the **Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands** (the **Freedmen's Bureau**) in **March 1865**. It distributed food and medical aid, helped reunite families separated by slavery, negotiated labor contracts, set up courts, and — most enduringly — **founded schools** across the South, the seedbed of Black education and several historically Black colleges.
  — Source: Lumen/U.S. History I, "The Freedmen's Bureau and Black Codes" (https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-ushistory1/chapter/the-freedmens-bureau-and-the-black-codes/)
- It was always underfunded and understaffed for the scale of the task; Johnson vetoed its 1866 extension (Congress overrode him). The promise of land redistribution ("forty acres and a mule," from Sherman's Field Order No. 15) was largely reversed when Johnson restored confiscated land to pardoned ex-Confederates.
  — Source: Lumen/U.S. History I (https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-ushistory1/chapter/the-freedmens-bureau-and-the-black-codes/)

### The Reconstruction Amendments
- **Civil Rights Act of 1866** — established Black citizenship and barred states from curtailing citizens' "fundamental rights"; passed over Johnson's veto. Its principles were then constitutionalized in the 14th Amendment.
  — Source: Lumen/U.S. History I (https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-ushistory1/chapter/the-freedmens-bureau-and-the-black-codes/)
- **14th Amendment — ratified July 9, 1868.** Granted **citizenship** to "all persons born or naturalized in the United States" (overturning *Dred Scott*) and guaranteed **equal protection of the laws** and **due process** against the states. The most consequential addition to the Constitution since the Bill of Rights.
  — Source: U.S. House of Representatives History / National Archives, "14th Amendment" (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents) ; ratification date per National Archives milestone documents
- **15th Amendment — ratified February 3, 1870** (passed Congress Feb 26, 1869). Barred denying the vote "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude" — establishing **Black male suffrage**. (Notably it did NOT bar literacy tests, poll taxes, or other race-neutral-on-paper devices later weaponized to disenfranchise.)
  — Source: National Archives, "15th Amendment" (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/15th-amendment); Wikipedia, "Fifteenth Amendment" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution)

### The brief flowering of Black political power
- For the first time, Black men voted and held office across the South. Historian **Eric Foner** documents **about 2,000 African Americans holding public office during Reconstruction** — from local positions to Congress.
  — Source: American Yawp, "Reconstruction" (http://www.americanyawp.com/text/15-reconstruction/), drawing on Eric Foner, *Freedom's Lawmakers* (1996)
- **16 Black men served in Congress between 1870 and 1877** — 14 in the House and 2 in the Senate.
  — Source: American Yawp, "Reconstruction" (http://www.americanyawp.com/text/15-reconstruction/)
- **Hiram Rhodes Revels** (Mississippi) became the **first Black U.S. Senator**, sworn in **February 25, 1870**, filling — pointedly — a seat once held by Jefferson Davis. He served a partial term (to 1871).
  — Source: U.S. Senate, "Hiram Revels: First African American Senator" (https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/senate-stories/First-African-American-Senator.htm)
- **Blanche Kelso Bruce** (Mississippi) became the **first Black senator to serve a full term** (1875–1881) — and, having been born enslaved, the first formerly enslaved person to preside over the Senate (Feb 14, 1879). After Bruce, no Black senator would serve again until **Edward Brooke in 1967**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Blanche Bruce" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanche_Bruce); Emerging Civil War, "Hiram Revels and Blanche Bruce" (https://emergingcivilwar.com/2019/02/28/hiram-revels-and-blanche-bruce-americas-first-black-senators/)
- The 1872 Currier & Ives lithograph "The First Colored Senator and Representatives" memorialized seven of them (Revels plus Reps. Turner, DeLarge, Walls, Long, Rainey, and Elliott). South Carolina's legislature briefly had a Black majority — a Foner point that directly refutes Lost Cause claims that Reconstruction governments were illegitimate.
  — Source: Library of Congress, "The first colored senator and representatives" (https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/98501907/)

### The violent backlash
- White-supremacist terror organized to overthrow Reconstruction by force. The **Ku Klux Klan** (founded 1866 in Tennessee) and successor groups (the White League, Red Shirts) waged a campaign of assassination, whipping, arson, and election-day massacre against Black voters, officeholders, and their white Republican allies.
  — Source: Bill of Rights Institute, "The Ku Klux Klan and Violence at the Polls" (https://billofrightsinstitute.org/essays/the-ku-klux-klan-and-violence-at-the-polls/)
- The federal **Enforcement Acts (1870–71)** and Grant's prosecutions broke the first Klan, but the terror persisted under new names.
  — Source: NPS, "President Grant Takes on the Ku Klux Klan" (https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/president-grant-takes-on-the-ku-klux-klan.htm)
- **The Colfax Massacre — Easter Sunday, April 13, 1873**, Colfax, Grant Parish, Louisiana. After a disputed election, a white mob of ex-Confederates, Klansmen, and White League members overwhelmed Black freedmen and a Black militia defending the parish courthouse. **Between ~62 and ~153 Black men were killed** — most of them murdered after surrendering. Eric Foner calls it **the worst single instance of racial violence in the Reconstruction era**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Colfax massacre" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colfax_massacre); Britannica, "Colfax massacre" (https://www.britannica.com/event/Colfax-Massacre)
- The legal aftermath gutted federal protection: in ***United States v. Cruikshank* (1876)** the Supreme Court overturned the convictions of the Colfax perpetrators, ruling the federal Enforcement Acts could not reach violence by private individuals — only state action. This stripped the federal government of much of its power to protect Black citizens from terror.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Colfax massacre" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colfax_massacre); Britannica, "Colfax massacre" (https://www.britannica.com/event/Colfax-Massacre)

### Johnson's impeachment (1868)
- The House **impeached President Andrew Johnson on February 24, 1868**, by **126–47** — the first impeachment of a U.S. president. The trigger: Johnson defied the **Tenure of Office Act** (passed 1867 over his veto) by removing Secretary of War **Edwin Stanton**, the Radicals' ally controlling military enforcement of Reconstruction. Eleven articles followed.
  — Source: U.S. Senate, "Impeachment Trial of President Andrew Johnson, 1868" (https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/impeachment/impeachment-johnson.htm); Wikipedia, "Impeachment of Andrew Johnson" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impeachment_of_Andrew_Johnson)
- The Senate trial (Chief Justice Salmon Chase presiding) ended in **acquittal on May 16, 1868**: **35 to convict, 19 to acquit — one vote short** of the two-thirds needed. Seven Republican "recusants" crossed party lines to acquit. Johnson finished his term but was politically broken.
  — Source: U.S. Senate, "Impeachment Trial of President Andrew Johnson" (https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/impeachment/impeachment-johnson.htm); History.com, "President Johnson acquitted" (https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/may-26/president-johnson-acquitted)

## BEAT 6: The End of Reconstruction → Jim Crow

### The disputed election of 1876 and the Compromise of 1877
- The **1876 presidential election** pitted Republican **Rutherford B. Hayes** against Democrat **Samuel Tilden**. Tilden won the popular vote (by ~250,000) and led the electoral count, but **20 electoral votes from Florida, Louisiana, South Carolina, and Oregon** were disputed amid fraud and intimidation in the still-occupied South.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Compromise of 1877" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compromise_of_1877); History.com, "Compromise of 1877" (https://www.history.com/articles/compromise-of-1877)
- A 15-member **Electoral Commission** (created Jan 1877) awarded **all 20 disputed votes to Hayes** on party-line votes, making him president **185–184**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Compromise of 1877" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compromise_of_1877)
- The **Compromise of 1877** — an informal bargain — let Democrats accept Hayes in exchange for the **withdrawal of the remaining federal troops from the South**. Hayes withdrew them after his March 1877 inauguration. This **ended Reconstruction**: with federal protection gone, white "Redeemer" Democrats took control of every Southern state.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Compromise of 1877" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compromise_of_1877); History.com, "How the 1876 Election... Ended Reconstruction" (https://www.history.com/articles/reconstruction-1876-election-rutherford-hayes)

### The descent into Jim Crow
- With Reconstruction dead, Southern states systematically **disenfranchised** Black men through poll taxes, literacy tests, understanding clauses, and grandfather clauses — race-neutral on paper, devastating in effect — and built a regime of **Jim Crow** segregation laws covering schools, transit, public accommodations, and nearly all of public life.
  — Source: Plessy/Jim Crow materials, Library of Congress (https://guides.loc.gov/chronicling-america-plessy-ferguson); History.com, "Plessy v. Ferguson" (https://www.history.com/articles/plessy-v-ferguson)
- The plantation labor system reorganized into **sharecropping and tenant farming** — formally free, but a cycle of debt and dependence that kept many freedpeople and their descendants tied to white-owned land in conditions of profound economic coercion.
  — Source: PBS, "Jim Crow & Plessy v. Ferguson / Slavery by Another Name" (https://www.pbs.org/tpt/slavery-by-another-name/themes/jim-crow/)
- **Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)** — the Supreme Court upheld a Louisiana train-segregation law, establishing the **"separate but equal"** doctrine that gave constitutional cover to Jim Crow for the next ~58 years (until *Brown v. Board of Education*, 1954). Justice John Marshall Harlan's lone dissent ("Our Constitution is color-blind") was the era's great rebuke.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Plessy v. Ferguson" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plessy_v._Ferguson); National Archives, "Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)" (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/plessy-v-ferguson)

## BEAT 7: What the War Settled — and What It Did Not

**SETTLED (permanently, at the cost of 750,000 lives):**
- **The Union is permanent and indivisible.** Secession was tested and defeated by force of arms; no state has seriously attempted to leave since. "These United States" became "the United States" — a singular nation.
- **Chattel slavery is dead.** The 13th Amendment ended the legal ownership of human beings in the United States, forever. ~4 million people were freed.

**NOT SETTLED (lost for a century):**
- **Racial equality and the meaning of Black freedom.** The Constitution now *promised* citizenship (14th) and the vote (15th) — but the country abandoned the will to enforce those promises in 1877. The question of whether Black Americans would be free in fact, not just in law, was answered, for nearly a century, with disenfranchisement, segregation, peonage, and lynching. The war ended slavery; the fight over what freedom would mean was lost, and would have to be fought again in the civil-rights movement of the 20th century — sometimes called the "Second Reconstruction."
  — Source: framing per Eric Foner, *Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution* (the standard scholarly synthesis); American Yawp, "Reconstruction" (http://www.americanyawp.com/text/15-reconstruction/)

**AUTHOR GUIDANCE:** This is the section's thesis. The clean, quotable structure is: *the war settled two questions and reopened a third.* Slavery: dead. The Union: permanent. Racial justice: deferred. Resist the temptation to soften the third with progress-narrative comfort — the honest claim is that the reckoning was *deferred, not resolved*.

## BEAT 8: The Lost Cause — the Final Irony

- After losing the war, the white South **won the argument over its memory** for generations. The **"Lost Cause"** is a revisionist mythology, built by ex-Confederates and their descendants, that recast the war: the Confederacy fought nobly for **states' rights** (not slavery); secession was constitutional and honorable; enslaved people were content and "faithful"; Reconstruction was a "tragedy" of corrupt carpetbaggers and unfit Black officeholders rather than a democratic experiment violently overthrown.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lost Cause of the Confederacy" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Cause_of_the_Confederacy); Britannica, "Lost Cause" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Lost-Cause)
- The mythology is contradicted by the Confederates' own words — the secession declarations and Alexander Stephens's "Cornerstone Speech" named slavery as the cause explicitly (earlier-section material; reference it here as the rebuttal).
- **The United Daughters of the Confederacy (UDC)**, founded **1894** (by Caroline Meriwether Goodlett and Anna Davenport Raines), became the chief institutional engine of the myth: it raised monuments, lobbied legislatures, ran essay contests for schoolchildren, and worked to control the content of history textbooks — shaping how generations of Americans learned the war.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lost Cause of the Confederacy" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Cause_of_the_Confederacy); Saturday Evening Post, "How the Lost Cause Myth Led to Confederate 'Monument Fever'" (https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2020/06/how-the-lost-cause-myth-led-to-confederate-monument-fever/)
- **The monuments tell on themselves.** The biggest wave of Confederate monument-building came **not right after the war but in the early 1900s** — exactly as Southern states were legally enshrining Jim Crow and disenfranchisement — and again during the 1950s–60s civil-rights backlash. The monuments were less about mourning the dead than about asserting white supremacy in the present.
  — Source: Southern Poverty Law Center, "Correcting History: Confederate Monuments, Rituals and the Lost Cause" (https://www.splcenter.org/resources/stories/correcting-history-confederate-monuments-rituals-and-the-lost-cause/); Saturday Evening Post (https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2020/06/how-the-lost-cause-myth-led-to-confederate-monument-fever/)
- **The final irony for this vertical:** the side that lost the war on the battlefield largely won the war over its meaning — for a century, the Lost Cause was taught as history in much of the country. This whole "off the battlefield" arc is structured to refute it. The closing should name that directly: the war's *facts* (slavery as cause, emancipation as result) were buried under a myth almost as soon as the guns fell silent, and recovering them is part of the reckoning still owed.

---

## BEAT 9: The Closing Meaning

- **~750,000 dead. ~4 million freed. A nation remade — and a reckoning deferred.**
- The arithmetic of the capstone: the deadliest war in American history killed roughly one in fifty Americans to settle that the Union would hold and slavery would end. It did both. It did not — because the country chose not to — secure the freedom it had won for the people it had freed. The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments wrote the promise into the Constitution; 1877 abandoned the enforcement; *Plessy* sealed the betrayal; and the bill came due again only in the 20th century.
- The honest closing note: this is a reckoning **deferred, not resolved.** (Frederick Douglass's framing — the revolution was *unfinished* — is the truest epigraph for the whole section.)

## CONTESTED / FLAGGED

### 1. The death toll — USE A RANGE, NOT A POINT (HIGH PRIORITY)
- Traditional: ~620,000 (often 618,222). Hacker (2011): ~750,000, range 650k–850k.
- **Author guidance:** Lead with "about 750,000, perhaps as many as 850,000 — a modern, census-based revision of the long-standard 620,000." Never present either as an exact count. Note that even Hacker's is an estimate of a range.

### 2. "More than all other American wars combined"
- True against the traditional 620,000 figure and the standard comparison (Revolution through Vietnam). It remains the deadliest U.S. war by a wide margin. The margin narrows if you add post-2001 conflicts and use a high WWII figure (~405,000), but the Civil War total still exceeds the *sum* of all others in the standard accounting.
- **Author guidance:** "More American soldiers died in the Civil War than in all the nation's other wars combined" is a safe, standard claim. Keep the comparison at that level of generality.

### 3. Confederate death figure (258,000) is soft
- The traditional Confederate number rested on incomplete records; Hacker's revision raises the Southern toll proportionally more. **Author guidance:** don't cite 258,000 as firm; use "roughly a quarter-million Confederate dead, likely undercounted."

### 4. The ~$3 billion enslaved-property valuation and the "$5 trillion" total cost
- The $3 billion (1860 dollars) figure for the market "value" of ~4 million enslaved people is standard but varies by source. The "$5+ trillion in today's dollars" all-in figure is from an advocacy-group model (WarCosts.org) and bundles disputed categories. **Author guidance:** use the contemporaneous billions figures as firm; treat the modern-trillions reframing as illustrative and attributed, not authoritative.

### 5. Columbia (SC) fire responsibility
- Whether Sherman's troops or retreating Confederates (burning cotton) caused the February 1865 Columbia fire is still genuinely disputed. **Author guidance:** "Columbia burned as Sherman's army occupied it, amid blame that historians still argue over."

### 6. Colfax death toll
- Range given as ~62 to ~153 Black men killed; the exact number is unknown because bodies were thrown in the Red River or buried in mass graves. **Author guidance:** "between roughly 60 and 150 Black men — most murdered after surrendering."

### 7. Freedpeople's wartime death toll
- Deaths of enslaved/freed people in contraband camps from disease and exposure were large and historically undercounted, but no firm consensus figure exists. Marked `[VERIFY]` above. **Author guidance:** "tens of thousands, never properly counted" — do not assign a hard number.

### 8. The "three boxes" quote
- "The ballot box, the jury box, and the cartridge box" is widely attributed to Douglass but the formulation predates him (Stephen Decatur Miller, 1830; others). **Author guidance:** if used, attribute as "a saying Douglass made his own," not as originally his.

### 9. "Forty acres and a mule"
- Derives from Sherman's Special Field Order No. 15 (Jan 1865) and subsequent Bureau practice; the phrase is a later popular distillation, and the promised land was largely reclaimed by Johnson's pardons. **Author guidance:** present as a promise made and broken, not a formal nationwide policy.

### 10. Mississippi prosthetics-budget figure
- The claim that postwar Mississippi spent ~20% of its budget on artificial limbs is widely repeated but cite carefully. **Author guidance:** safe as illustration; soften to "a fifth of one state's postwar budget, by one common account" if quoted precisely.
