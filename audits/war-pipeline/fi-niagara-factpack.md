## A. THE STAKES / STRATEGIC SETTING

1. Fort Niagara stood on the east bank of the Niagara River where it meets Lake Ontario (near present-day Youngstown, New York). [S1][S3]
2. The fort controlled the Niagara Portage, the overland carrying route around Niagara Falls between Lake Ontario and Lake Erie. This was the single chokepoint linking the Lower and Upper Great Lakes. [S5]
3. Old Fort Niagara states the site controlled "a strategic waterway that linked French settlements along the St. Lawrence River with Detroit, the upper Great Lakes, the Ohio Valley and Louisiana." [S5]
4. Taking Niagara would sever the supply line to every French post to the west and south (the Ohio Country and the Great Lakes interior). Its fall triggered the French to abandon western forts including Fort Machault, Fort Présentation/Rouillé, and Fort St. Frédéric, pulling back toward the populated Canadian core. [S1][S3]
5. The 1759 campaign was the British "year of victories": Niagara in the west, Wolfe's expedition against Quebec in the east, and the Lake Champlain advance, all aimed at converging on the heart of New France. [S1][S3]
6. The land around Niagara was Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) territory, specifically Seneca country (the Seneca being the westernmost of the Six Nations). [S1][S5]

## B. COMMANDERS

7. **Brigadier General John Prideaux (British)** commanded the expedition and opened the siege. Killed during the siege (see Section D). [S1][S3][S5]
8. **Sir William Johnson (British)** — British Superintendent of Indian Affairs for the Northern colonies / Indian agent. Led the expedition's Haudenosaunee contingent and took command of the entire siege after Prideaux's death. [S1][S3][S5]
9. **Captain Pierre Pouchot (French)** — French military engineer and regular-army officer (commission as captain Sept 1748). He had enlarged Fort Niagara and given it new earthwork defenses; reappointed its commander in March 1759, months before the siege. He directed the defense and surrendered the fort. [S6][S1]
10. **Lieutenant Colonel Eyre Massey (British)** — commanded the British/Iroquois force that fought the relief column at La Belle-Famille; wounded in the action. [S2][S4]
11. **Captain François-Marie Le Marchand de Lignery (French)** — led the French relief column; mortally wounded at La Belle-Famille. [S1][S2][S4]
12. **Charles Philippe Aubry (French)** — co-led the relief column; captured. [S2][S4]
13. **Michel Maray de La Chauvignerie (French)** — relief-column officer, captured at La Belle-Famille. [S2][S4]
14. **Sayenqueraghta** — named as an Iroquois (Seneca) leader with the British-allied force. [S1]
15. **Kaendae** — French-allied Native leader who, per Old Fort Niagara, initiated negotiations on July 11 that led to British-allied Natives' temporary withdrawal the next day. [S5] *(Note: this detail differs in framing from the La Belle-Famille parley account; see Section E.)*

## C. FORCES / NUMBERS — DISPUTED, USE RANGES

16. **British besieging force — DISPUTED.**
    - S5: ~2,500 British + New York provincials, plus ~945 Native allies recruited by Johnson.
    - S1: ~2,000 regulars (44th and 46th Foot plus 60th companies) + ~1,000 New York/Rhode Island militia + 945 Iroquois auxiliaries.
    - S3: gives a single figure of 6,520 (likely a paper/total-establishment count). **USE: roughly 2,000–2,500 regulars and provincials plus about 945 Haudenosaunee allies. Do not assert 6,520.**
17. **French garrison — DISPUTED but converging.**
    - S5: "fewer than 500."
    - S1: ~200 regulars (drawn from four regiments) + 20 artillerymen + ~300 provincial troops/militia + ~100 Iroquois allies. That sums to ~500–520 plus the Native allies.
    - S3: garrison reduced to ~500 by July (down from a notional 2,500 establishment earlier in 1759, after redeployments). **USE: roughly 500 defenders.**
18. French had about 100 Iroquois allies at the fort who withdrew to parley with the British-allied Iroquois. [S1]

## D. THE SIEGE AND PRIDEAUX'S DEATH

19. Prideaux's force arrived at Fort Niagara on July 6 (S1/S5); ABT dates the engagement July 2–26 (S3). **USE: siege opens early July (Prideaux arrives July 6).** [S1][S5][S3]
20. The British began formal siege operations immediately, digging approaches and emplacing batteries against Pouchot's earthworks. [S1][S5]
21. **Prideaux's death — DATE AND MECHANISM.**
    - Date: July 20 (S1/S5).
    - Mechanism (DISPUTED in detail, agreed in essence — it was accidental, by his own ordnance): S1 says struck by "a shell fragment thrown from one of his own guns"; S5 says "killed by friendly fire"; S3 says "decapitated after accidentally stepping in front of a mortar." **USE: killed accidentally on July 20 by one of his own guns/a bursting cohorn-type mortar. The "decapitated/stepped in front of a mortar" specific is one account; do not assert the gore as fact. Frame as accidental death by his own artillery.** [S1][S3][S5]
22. Sir William Johnson took command of the siege after Prideaux was killed and pressed it to conclusion. [S1][S3][S5]
23. Pouchot mounted a vigorous, skilled defense (he was the engineer who had built the works). [S6][S5]

## E. NATIVE DIPLOMACY / THE PARLEY (CENTRAL — HANDLE FAIRLY)

24. Johnson's ~945 Haudenosaunee were independent allies, not auxiliaries under command; their participation was a matter of their own diplomacy and interest. [S1][S3]
25. **The parley (La Belle-Famille).** Per S2: "Before the action, the British native allies told their French counterparts that they intended to remain neutral in the upcoming clash. They wished that the French native allies would do likewise. This induced many of the French-allied Indians to desert." [S2]
26. The ~100 French-allied Iroquois at the fort withdrew to meet/parley with the Iroquois who had come with the British. [S1]
27. Old Fort Niagara frames an earlier, related diplomatic moment: French-allied leader Kaendae initiated negotiations July 11 that led British-allied Natives to withdraw the following day. [S5] *(The siege-level and battle-level diplomatic episodes should be told as Haudenosaunee on both sides choosing not to fight one another; the cleanest single documented statement is the La Belle-Famille parley, fact 25.)*

## F. THE BATTLE OF LA BELLE-FAMILLE (July 24, 1759)

28. A French relief column marched to break the siege, coming up the Niagara portage trail from the western posts. Organized from Fort Machault (in the Ohio Country); Lignery had been preparing to move against Fort Pitt when Pouchot's distress call reached him (~July 12). [S2][S4]
29. **Relief-column size — DISPUTED.**
    - S2: "800 regulars and militia" plus "500 Native Americans."
    - S7: "About 1,300 men" responded; "About 800 French soldiers attempted to break through."
    - **USE: roughly 800 French regulars and militia plus several hundred Native allies (~500); about 1,300 in all before desertions.** [S2][S4][S7]
30. **British/allied blocking force under Massey — DISPUTED in detail.**
    - S4: 464 British regulars of the 46th Foot + 100 New York militia + 450 Iroquois (~900 total); ~130 of the 46th held the La Belle-Famille clearing with grenadiers and light companies behind a breastwork across the road, about two miles south of the fort. **USE these figures, hedged.** [S4]
31. The British built/occupied a prepared breastwork (abatis/log barricade) across the portage road in the clearing. The French emerged from the woods into the clearing and began deploying from marching column into firing line. [S4]
32. S4: "Lignery and Aubry, in spite of their knowledge of Indian ways, apparently advanced without taking normal precautions against ambush, and marched right into a prepared trap." [S4][S2]
33. Tactical sequence (S4): British troops lay down and fixed bayonets, then rose and volleyed; "The 46th fired seven volleys, then advanced, firing at will." [S4]
34. **Casualties at La Belle-Famille — DISPUTED, USE RANGES.**
    - S4: French "at least 334 killed" + "at least 96 captured"; British "12 dead" and "40 wounded."
    - S7: "some 250 French soldiers were killed or wounded."
    - S1 (siege-level totals, which fold in La Belle-Famille): French 109 killed/wounded + 377 captured; British/allied 239 killed/wounded.
    - **USE: the French relief column was destroyed — hundreds killed or wounded and hundreds captured (the column ceased to exist as a fighting force). British/allied losses at the ambush itself were light (on the order of dozens). Give ranges, no false precision.** [S4][S7][S1]
35. Lignery mortally wounded; Aubry captured; La Chauvignerie captured. [S2][S4]
36. Many French-allied warriors did not fight, having stood aside after the parley (fact 25). [S2]

## G. SURRENDER AND AFTERMATH

37. **Surrender date — DISPUTED (July 25 vs 26).** S5/S6: July 25. S1/S2: July 26. **USE: "the next day" (i.e., July 25, with some sources July 26). Pouchot surrendered the day after La Belle-Famille once the relief was destroyed.** [S5][S6][S1][S2]
38. With the relief column annihilated, Pouchot recognized the position was hopeless and capitulated to Johnson. [S5][S6]
39. **Surrender terms (honorable):** per S5, officers and men kept personal belongings and marched out under arms with drums beating, hauling a single light cannon. The garrison became prisoners of war. [S5]
40. The fall of Niagara cut the French west off from Canada; the French abandoned their western and Lake Ontario posts (Fort Machault, Fort Présentation/Rouillé, Fort St. Frédéric) and pulled back to defend the populated St. Lawrence core. [S1][S3]
41. The victory, paired with the fall of Quebec (Sept 1759) and the Champlain advance, doomed New France. [S1][S3]

## UNVERIFIED QUOTES — DO NOT USE
- No verbatim first-person quotation from Prideaux, Johnson, Pouchot, Lignery, or Aubry was located in clean, attributable form during this research pass. Do NOT fabricate dialogue or last words.
- Old Fort Niagara paraphrases Pouchot recognizing "the situation is hopeless" (S5) but this is a paraphrase, not confirmed verbatim — do NOT present it as a quotation.
- The S5 surrender-terms wording ("Officers and men can keep their personal belongings and march out under arms with drums beating and hauling a single light cannon") may be a museum paraphrase of the capitulation articles; use as factual content, not as a quoted document, unless verified against the articles of capitulation.

## DISPUTED-FIGURE SUMMARY (for the author)
- British siege force: ~2,000–2,500 + ~945 Haudenosaunee (NOT 6,520).
- French garrison: ~500.
- Relief column: ~800 French + ~500 Native (~1,300 total before desertions).
- La Belle-Famille casualties: French relief column destroyed (hundreds killed/wounded, hundreds captured); British losses light (dozens). Give ranges.
- Prideaux death: July 20, accidental, his own ordnance (don't assert "decapitated").
- Surrender: July 25 (some sources July 26) — "the next day."
