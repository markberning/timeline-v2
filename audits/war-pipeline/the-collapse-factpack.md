## §1 — RECOMMENDED SECTION: "A republic running out of everything" (the state of the Confederacy, winter 1864–65)

The strategic setup: by the winter of 1864–65 the Confederacy was losing the war it could
measure — men, food, rail, money — before it lost a battle that ended it.

- **The Western army was already gone.** Hood's (South) Army of Tennessee had wrecked itself
  in two battles in late 1864 — at **Franklin (Nov 30 1864)**, a frontal assault that cost
  the Confederates **over 6,000 casualties including ~1,750 killed**, and the loss of **14 generals**
  (6 killed, 7 wounded, 1 captured) plus 55 regimental commanders — and then was destroyed
  outright at **Nashville (Dec 15–16 1864)**, where Hood lost ~6,000 more (many captured) against
  ~3,000 Union casualties. After Nashville the Army of Tennessee "ceased to exist as an effective
  fighting force"; Hood retreated to Mississippi with under 20,000 men, bleeding deserters the whole
  way, and resigned. [American Battlefield Trust: Franklin; Britannica: Battle of Nashville; Wikipedia: Franklin–Nashville campaign]
  - **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/western/franklin` and `/war-civil-war/western/nashville`
    (these are full dossiers — reference the outcome, don't re-narrate the tactics).
- **Sherman (North) turning north.** Having taken Atlanta (Sep 1864) and marched to the sea
  (Savannah, Dec 1864), Sherman turned his army NORTH into the Carolinas in January 1865 — into
  South Carolina, the cradle of secession — cutting a path of destruction and aiming to come up
  behind Lee. [Wikipedia: Carolinas campaign] (Columbia, SC burned Feb 17 1865 — cause debated;
  see §6 and contested flags.)
- **The blockade now near-total.** "By 1864, the Union blockade was intercepting the majority of
  blockade runners," choking imports of food, medicine, and military supplies. [Gilder Lehrman:
  The Confederacy Begins to Collapse] Wilmington, NC — the last major open port — fell after the
  capture of Fort Fisher (Jan 1865).
- **Runaway inflation + starvation.** Confederate currency that was worth ~95 cents to the U.S.
  dollar in 1861 had collapsed; food became unaffordable; there had already been a **bread riot in
  Richmond (1863)**, and by the winter of 1864–65 soldiers' families were going hungry. [Gilder
  Lehrman; Encyclopedia Virginia: Poverty and Poor Relief] [VERIFY the precise late-1864 exchange
  rate before quoting a hard number — sources give the 1861 figure firmly, the late-war figure varies wildly.]
- **Desertion as a system failure.** "Desertions rose as soldiers worried about starving families
  back home … by early 1865, desertion had become a crisis." This is the manpower side of the
  collapse — men voting with their feet. [Gilder Lehrman]
- **Rail collapse.** The Southern rail net was disintegrating: "lesser lines are being torn up to
  provide rail for more important lines," rolling stock dwindling, gauges mismatched — the logistics
  that move an army were failing. [search summary; corroborate against Wikipedia: American Civil War
  logistics before quoting verbatim — VERIFY]

**STRATEGIC FRAME for the section:** Grant's (North) 1864 strategy was *coordinated, simultaneous
attrition* — apply pressure everywhere at once (Grant on Lee in Virginia, Sherman through Georgia
and the Carolinas, Thomas in Tennessee) so the Confederacy could no longer shuttle troops to plug
holes. By winter 1864–65 that strategy had done its work below the surface; 1865 is the year the
structure finally fell in. [frame; supported by Wikipedia: American Civil War + the campaign articles]

People: Hood (South), Sherman (North), Grant (North), Thomas (North, the victor at Nashville).

## §2 — RECOMMENDED SECTION: "The contradiction that broke the cause" (the arm-the-slaves debate, Feb–Mar 1865)  ← NAME THE CAUSE anchor

This is the chapter's clearest place to state, in its own voice, what the war was over — because
the Confederacy itself stated it, by what it could not bring itself to do.

- **The proposal.** Facing collapse, the Confederate government debated **arming enslaved men as
  soldiers** — a slaveholding republic considering whether to put muskets in the hands of the people
  it was fighting the war to keep enslaved. (The idea traced back to Gen. **Patrick Cleburne's (South)**
  Jan 1864 proposal, which had been suppressed.) [NPS Richmond: Arming the Enslaved; Encyclopedia
  Virginia: Black Confederates]
- **Lee backed it — on military necessity, not principle.** **Gen. Robert E. Lee (South)** endorsed
  arming (and, he urged, freeing) enslaved soldiers. In his Jan 1865 letter to Andrew Hunter, Lee
  framed the choice as: either "slavery shall be extinguished by our enemies and the slaves used
  against us, or use them ourselves," and recommended "giving immediate freedom to all who enlist."
  Critically, Lee's support was driven by military necessity, not moral conviction — in the same
  breath he called "the relation of master and slave … the best that can exist between the white and
  black races." [search summary citing Lee's Hunter letter; NPS Arlington House: Lee and Slavery]
  [VERIFY exact wording of any direct Lee quote against the primary letter before using verbatim —
  paraphrase is safe.]
- **The law.** Congressman **Ethelbert Barksdale (South, MS)** introduced the bill (Feb 10 1865) with
  Davis's backing. The House passed it Feb 20; the **Senate approved it by a one-vote margin on Mar 8**;
  **President Jefferson Davis (South) signed it into law March 13, 1865.** The law authorized Davis to
  "ask for and accept from the owners of slaves" able-bodied Black men as soldiers — but, crucially,
  **it did NOT stipulate freedom** for those who served (Lee's freedom condition was stripped out).
  [HISTORY: Confederacy approves Black soldiers, Mar 13 1865; freedmen.umd.edu: CS enlistment law;
  Encyclopedia Virginia: Black Confederates]
- **General Orders No. 14 (Mar 23 1865)** implemented it — enlistment of free Blacks and of enslaved
  men whose enslavers manumitted them first. [Civil War on the Western Border: General Orders No. 14]
- **It came to nothing.** A few companies drilled in Richmond in the war's final weeks; "several
  thousand" at most were ever enlisted, and they never fought a real battle before the surrender —
  against the **nearly 200,000 Black soldiers who fought for the Union.** [HISTORY: Mar 13 1865;
  Encyclopedia Virginia: Black Confederates]

**THE NAMING (chapter's own voice).** This is the material to state plainly that the war was over
slavery: a government founded to defend human bondage, with its armies starving, debated freeing and
arming the very people it had seceded to keep enslaved — and even then could not bring itself to
promise them freedom. The contradiction *is* the cause, made visible. [framing — supported by the
above facts]

- **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/freedom-struggle` (the Black-led abolition
  arc), `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/emancipation` (the war's changed aim), and
  `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/usct` (the ~200,000 who fought for the Union).

People: Cleburne (South), Lee (South), Barksdale (South), Davis (South).

## §3 — RECOMMENDED SECTION: "The line breaks at Petersburg" (Fort Stedman → Five Forks → Apr 2 breakthrough)

The military hinge: the ten-month Petersburg siege — pure attrition, Grant (North) extending his
lines west to cut Lee's (South) last supply railroads — finally cracks.

- **Lee's last gamble: Fort Stedman (Mar 25 1865).** Lee tried one desperate offensive — a pre-dawn
  assault on the Union Fort Stedman to break Grant's siege grip and open an escape route. It failed
  within hours with heavy Confederate losses; it spent men Lee could not replace and left him weaker.
  [Wikipedia: Battle of Fort Stedman — VERIFY casualty figures before citing specific numbers; ~4,000
  Confederate / ~1,000 Union are the commonly cited ranges, confirm.] **(No dossier — narrate here.)**
- **Five Forks (Apr 1 1865) — "the Waterloo of the Confederacy."** Grant sent **Maj. Gen. Philip
  Sheridan (North)** west around Lee's right to seize the **Five Forks** road junction — the key to
  the **South Side Railroad**, Lee's last open supply line and evacuation route. Sheridan, with the
  Fifth Corps (and the controversial relief of **Maj. Gen. Gouverneur K. Warren (North)** mid-battle),
  shattered the Confederate force under **Maj. Gen. George Pickett (South)**. Confederate casualties
  ~2,400–2,950 (the great majority **captured** — over 2,400 prisoners, per Sheridan); Union losses
  ~830. [Wikipedia: Battle of Five Forks; Britannica; Battlefields.org: Five Forks]
  - **(No dossier — this is the chapter's set-piece; narrate it in full.)**
- **The breakthrough — Third Petersburg (Apr 2 1865).** With the South Side Railroad lost, Grant
  ordered a general assault on the Petersburg lines at dawn April 2. The lines broke. **Lt. Gen. A.P.
  Hill (South)** was killed that morning. Lee telegraphed Davis that Petersburg and Richmond must be
  evacuated. The siege of attrition had finally cracked Lee's army. [search summary; Wikipedia: Third
  Battle of Petersburg — VERIFY A.P. Hill death date Apr 2 1865 and casualty figures.] **(No dossier.)**

**STRATEGIC POINT:** Five Forks isn't important for its body count — it's important because it cut
the last railroad. The whole 1865 collapse is a logistics story: cut the supply, and the army cannot
stay in the field. That is attrition finishing its work. [frame]

People: Grant (North), Lee (South), Sheridan (North), Warren (North), Pickett (South), A.P. Hill (South).

## §4 — RECOMMENDED SECTION: "The capital falls" (Richmond, Apr 3–4 1865)

The symbolic and strategic fall — and the moment the war's meaning becomes visible in the streets.

- **Evacuation + fire (Apr 2–3).** As Lee's (South) lines broke, the Confederate government fled
  Richmond on the night of April 2. Retreating Confederates set fire to military stores, tobacco
  warehouses, and bridges; the fire spread and consumed **as many as ~1,000 buildings** in the
  business district. Union troops entered the next morning, **April 3, 1865**, to find the capital
  evacuated and burning. [Emerging Civil War: Fall of Richmond; NPS Richmond] [VERIFY "~1,000 buildings"
  range.]
- **USCT among the first in.** **U.S. Colored Troops of the XXV Corps** were among the first Union
  regiments into Richmond (the 36th USCT is reported as first; there was a competing claim by ~40 men
  of the 4th Massachusetts Cavalry). Black soldiers entered the former capital of the slaveholders'
  republic as liberators; the city's enslaved population welcomed them. [mrlincolnandfreedom.org:
  Entering Richmond — Black correspondent **Thomas Morris Chester**'s reporting; NPS Richmond] [VERIFY
  "36th USCT first" — sources note the honor was disputed; present it as such.]
  - **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/usct`.
- **Lincoln walks the captured capital (Apr 4 1865).** **President Abraham Lincoln (North)** toured the
  fallen Confederate capital on April 4 — walking its streets, sitting at one point (by tradition) in
  Davis's executive office, surrounded by crowds of newly freed Black residents. To **Maj. Gen.
  Godfrey Weitzel (North)**, the city's occupation commander, Lincoln reportedly gave his guidance for
  the defeated: **"let 'em up easy."** [eyewitnesstohistory.com: Lincoln Enters Richmond; Soldiers of
  the Cross: "let them up easy"] [VERIFY exact wording of the "let 'em up easy" quote and that it was
  said to Weitzel — widely reported, confirm phrasing.]

**STRATEGIC/MORAL POINT:** The image is the argument — a Black army and a freed population in the
streets of the capital that seceded to preserve their enslavement, with the President of the United
States walking among them. The cause of the war is legible in the scene. (NAME-THE-CAUSE reinforcement.)

People: Lincoln (North), Weitzel (North), Davis (South, fleeing), Thomas Morris Chester (North, correspondent).

## §5 — RECOMMENDED SECTION: "Appomattox" (the campaign + surrender, Apr 6–9 1865)

The chapter's emotional and strategic climax: how the war was ENDED, and the logic of a soft peace.

- **The campaign (Mar 29 – Apr 9).** With Petersburg and Richmond gone, Lee (South) tried to march his
  starving army west to link up with Johnston (South) in North Carolina. Grant (North) pursued and
  paralleled him, cutting him off. [Wikipedia: Appomattox campaign]
- **Sailor's Creek (Apr 6 1865) — the death blow.** Union forces caught up with Lee's strung-out,
  exhausted column at Sailor's (Saylor's) Creek and **destroyed roughly a quarter of the remaining
  army — ~7,700 Confederates killed or captured**, including several generals (Lee, watching, is said
  to have exclaimed, "My God, has the army been dissolved?"). [Wikipedia: Appomattox campaign /
  Battle of Sailor's Creek] [VERIFY the "My God, has the army dissolved?" quote — traditionally
  attributed to Lee; flag as traditional attribution.] **(No dossier — narrate here.)**
- **The surrender (Apr 9 1865).** Cornered, out of food, and outnumbered, Lee surrendered the **Army
  of Northern Virginia (~28,000 men paroled)** to Grant at the **McLean House** at Appomattox Court
  House, Virginia. (Owner **Wilmer McLean** had fled the First Bull Run battlefield in 1861 for the
  quiet of Appomattox — the war began near his door and ended in his parlor.) [Wikipedia: Battle of
  Appomattox Court House; National Archives: Ending the Bloodshed] **(No dossier — this is the chapter's
  capstone scene; narrate in full.)**
- **Grant's deliberately generous terms — and the strategy behind them.** Grant paroled the entire
  army; officers kept their **side-arms, private horses, and baggage**; men who owned horses kept them
  for spring plowing; all could **go home undisturbed** so long as they honored their parole. Grant
  issued **25,000 rations** to the starving Confederates (much of it from Confederate stores Sheridan
  had captured). [Battlefields.org: Grant's Terms of Agreement; National Archives] The terms were a
  conscious POLITICAL act: a soft peace meant to **avoid treason trials, prevent the army dissolving
  into guerrilla bands, and begin reconciliation** — they became the template for every surrender that
  followed. [National Archives: Ending the Bloodshed]
- **The wider logic:** Lincoln (North) had set the tone ("let 'em up easy"); Grant executed it. The
  strategic insight is that *how* you end a war shapes the peace — magnanimity at Appomattox was a
  deliberate instrument, not mere sentiment.

People: Lee (South), Grant (North), Sheridan (North), Johnston (South), Wilmer McLean (civilian).

## §6 — RECOMMENDED SECTION: "The rest of the armies lay down" (the surrenders cascade, Apr–Jun 1865)

Appomattox ended Lee's army, not the war — the chapter should show the war *winding down* as a system,
army by army.

- **Bentonville (Mar 19–21 1865).** Before the cascade, Johnston (South) made one last attempt to stop
  Sherman (North) in North Carolina, at Bentonville — the largest battle ever fought in NC and the last
  Confederate offensive of the war. He was driven off. [HISTORY: Battle of Bentonville; Wikipedia:
  Carolinas campaign] **(No dossier — narrate briefly here as the prelude to Johnston's surrender.)**
  (Columbia, SC had burned Feb 17 1865 during the same campaign — cause genuinely disputed; see flags.)
- **Johnston surrenders (Bennett Place, near Durham, NC).** Johnston (South) signed an initial armistice
  with Sherman (North) on **Apr 18** (three days after Lincoln's assassination); Sherman's first,
  over-generous political terms were rejected in Washington, and Johnston formally surrendered on purely
  military terms on **Apr 26, 1865** — his army plus all Confederate forces in the Carolinas, Georgia,
  and Florida. [HISTORY: Bentonville/Johnston; northcarolinahistory.org: Carolinas Campaign]
- **The last surrenders.** **Gen. Edmund Kirby Smith (South)**, commanding the Trans-Mississippi
  Department (~43,000 men on paper), surrendered **May 26, 1865** (terms signed at Galveston **June 2**)
  — the last full general to surrender. **Brig. Gen. Stand Watie (South)**, a Cherokee and the only
  Native American to reach brigadier general in the Confederate army, was the **last Confederate general
  to surrender, June 23, 1865**, at Doaksville. [HISTORY: One of the last Confederate generals surrenders,
  May 26; Wikipedia: Edmund Kirby Smith; HISTORY: Stand Watie]

**STRATEGIC POINT:** No single Appomattox ended the war — it ended in a *cascade*, because the
Confederacy was a system that had run out of the means to continue, not an army beaten in one field.
This is the thesis in its final form.

People: Johnston (South), Sherman (North), Kirby Smith (South), Stand Watie (South).

## §7 — RECOMMENDED SECTION: "Lincoln is killed" (Apr 14 1865) — KEEP BRIEF, links out

The war ends and the victorious commander-in-chief is murdered at the moment of victory. This has its
OWN full page; the chapter touches it for one strategic/human beat and links down.

- **The act.** **John Wilkes Booth (South sympathizer)**, an actor and Confederate sympathizer, shot
  **President Abraham Lincoln (North)** at **Ford's Theatre** in Washington on the night of **April 14,
  1865** — **five days after Appomattox.** Lincoln died the next morning, **April 15, 1865.** Booth was
  cornered and killed (gunshot) on April 26 after a 12-day manhunt. [Wikipedia: Assassination of Abraham
  Lincoln; HISTORY: Apr 14 1865; Ford's Theatre]
- **The strategic/human point for THIS chapter:** the war's architect of a soft, reconciling peace was
  killed at the instant of victory — removing the one man most committed to "let 'em up easy" and handing
  Reconstruction to others. Keep this to a beat or two; do NOT retell the assassination.
- **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/assassination`.

People: Lincoln (North), Booth (South sympathizer).

## §8 — RECOMMENDED SECTION: "What it cost, and what it secured" (the reckoning + the 13th Amendment) ← chapter close + NAME THE CAUSE payoff

The close: tally the cost, then state what the victory MEANT — emancipation made permanent and
constitutional.

- **The death toll — give the dispute.** The traditional figure, accepted for over a century, is
  **~620,000 dead** (both sides). The modern revision by demographer **J. David Hacker (2011)**, using
  census survival ratios, puts it at **~750,000** (with a stated range of ~650,000–850,000). A still
  more recent full-count census analysis lands around ~698,000. Present it as a genuine scholarly
  dispute, not a single number: the war killed *at least* ~620,000 and likely far more — roughly
  three-quarters of a million Americans. [NPR: Civil War Death Toll; Hacker, *Journal of the Civil War
  Era* / *Civil War History* 2011; ScienceDaily/Binghamton] [The reckoning theme uses
  "three-quarters of a million" — keep consistent.]
- **The maimed and the ruined South.** Hundreds of thousands more were wounded and maimed; the South's
  farms, cities, and rail were devastated; an entire labor and social system was gone. (Keep light here —
  the Reckoning theme carries the detail.)
- **~4 million freed.** The war's outcome freed roughly **four million** enslaved people. [Reckoning
  theme; standard 1860 census figure ~3.95 million enslaved.]
- **The 13th Amendment — emancipation made permanent.** The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) had been a
  war measure of limited legal reach; permanence required a constitutional amendment. The **House passed
  the 13th Amendment on January 31, 1865** (vote **119–56**, just clearing the two-thirds bar; the Senate
  had passed it April 8, 1864). It was **ratified December 6, 1865.** Its text: *"Neither slavery nor
  involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly
  convicted, shall exist within the United States…"* [National Archives: 13th Amendment; Wikipedia:
  Thirteenth Amendment; HISTORY]
  - **LINK DOWN TO:** `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/emancipation` and
    `/war-civil-war/off-the-battlefield/reckoning`.

**THE CLOSE (chapter's own voice — NAME THE CAUSE payoff).** The Confederacy went to war to keep four
million people in slavery; it lost; and the price of its defeat was written into the Constitution itself.
The strategic and moral meaning of the victory was one and the same: slavery — the thing the war was
fought over — was abolished, permanently, by the supreme law of the land. That is what the men, food,
railroads, and will of a slaveholding republic ran out *against.*
