# FACT PACK — "The Tea Party & the Intolerable Acts" (1773–1774)

*American Revolution vertical, CAUSES arc, chapter 4 (route slug `tea-party`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against the cited sources (Wikipedia, Massachusetts Historical Society / Colonial Society of Massachusetts, Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum scholarship, founders.archives.gov, Library of Congress); confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges and "flag" guidance for contested specifics.*

**Orientation.** Ch.3 ended with the partial repeal of the Townshend duties (April 1770) — every duty gone except the one on tea, deliberately kept as a token of Parliament's right to tax — the boycotts collapsing colony by colony, and a genuine lull settling over the dispute (1770–1773). Ch.4 opens with that lull quietly breaking down: a burned revenue schooner in Rhode Island (the *Gaspee*, June 1772), a royal plan to pay Massachusetts judges from London (cutting the last purse-string the assembly held), and Samuel Adams's answer — standing Committees of Correspondence that wired the towns, then the colonies, together. Then Parliament hands the network its cause. The Tea Act of 1773 is a bailout of the nearly bankrupt East India Company that makes legal, taxed tea *cheaper* than smuggled tea — a trap: drink the cheap tea and you concede Parliament's right to tax. Boston's three tea ships get caught between a town that won't let the tea land and a governor who won't let the ships leave; when the customs clock runs out on December 16, 1773, men in thin "Mohawk" disguise destroy 340-odd chests of tea — about £9,659 worth — with almost theatrical discipline, and harm nothing else. Parliament's answer is the Coercive Acts of 1774: Boston's port sealed, Massachusetts's charter government gutted, a general (Gage) installed as governor. Intended to isolate Boston, the punishment instead pulls the colonies together — relief pours in by the shipload, and colony after colony calls for a continental congress. The chapter ends with the Massachusetts House voting behind a locked door (June 17, 1774) to send delegates to Philadelphia. Do NOT narrate the Congress itself — that opens ch.5.

## 1. THE LULL BREAKS DOWN (1772–1773)

### The Gaspee affair (June 1772)
- **HMS *Gaspee*** — a Royal Navy **revenue schooner** enforcing the Navigation Acts (customs/anti-smuggling patrol) in Narragansett Bay, Rhode Island, commanded by **Lieutenant William Dudingston**. Aggressive and locally hated. (HIGH)
- **9 June 1772** — chasing the packet boat ***Hannah***, *Gaspee* **ran aground in shallow water** off **Gaspee Point (Namquid Point), Warwick, Rhode Island**. (HIGH)
- **~1 a.m., 10 June 1772** — a band of **Providence men in eight boats** (sources commonly say eight; one account says 18 — keep loose as "a flotilla of longboats"), led by merchant **John Brown** and ship captain **Abraham Whipple**, boarded the stranded schooner. **Dudingston was shot and wounded** (in the groin); the crew was put ashore; **the *Gaspee* was burned to the waterline.** (HIGH on the spine; MED on the boat count — use "a flotilla of boats")
- **The royal response was the alarming part:** a **royal commission of inquiry** was empowered to identify suspects for possible **transport to England for trial** — bypassing local juries entirely. Rhode Islanders stonewalled; the commission **could not obtain sufficient evidence and gave up; no one was ever prosecuted.** (HIGH)
- The threat of overseas trials, more than the burning itself, is what radiated outward — it confirmed the old vice-admiralty fear (ch.2–3): Englishmen tried without a jury of their neighbors. (HIGH — framing well attested)

### The judges'-salary controversy (1772–1773)
- **Fall 1772** — news reached Massachusetts that the Crown would henceforth **pay the salaries of the Superior Court judges directly** (as it had already done for the governor under the Townshend scheme), ending the assembly's traditional power to vote — or withhold — their pay. Since the judges held their commissions **"during pleasure"** (removable at the Crown's will), Crown pay made them **entirely dependent on the Crown "for Bread as well as office"** (John Adams's later characterization). (HIGH)
- **March 1773** — Chief Justice **Peter Oliver** and the four associate judges (Edmund Trowbridge, Foster Hutchinson, Nathaniel Ropes, William Cushing) initially accepted half-pay from the province expecting the Crown to supply the rest; under public pressure through 1773 the associate judges renounced the Crown salary — **Oliver alone refused**, and the Massachusetts House moved toward **impeaching him** (articles drafted with John Adams's help, early 1774). (HIGH on the controversy and Oliver's holdout; MED on fine sequencing — keep the impeachment as a one-line beat, it climaxes in 1774)
- Use this as the second drip that re-politicized the lull: first juries (Gaspee), now judges. (HIGH framing)

### Committees of Correspondence
- **Boston, November 1772** — at a Boston **town meeting**, **Samuel Adams** moved the creation of a standing **Committee of Correspondence** (Joseph Warren a key collaborator) to state the rights of the colonists and communicate them to the other towns — provoked by the judges'-salary news and the Gaspee commission. (HIGH; the commonly cited specifics — 2 November 1772 vote, a 21-member committee — are MED: present as "that November" + "a standing committee" unless re-verified)
- Its first product, the so-called **"Boston Pamphlet"** (*Votes and Proceedings* — a statement of colonial rights + list of grievances), went out to the towns. (MED — fine as a clause, not a set piece)
- **More than one hundred Massachusetts towns** formed answering committees within months. (HIGH)
- **Virginia, 12 March 1773** — the House of Burgesses created the first **standing intercolonial committee of correspondence**; members included **Peyton Randolph, Richard Henry Lee, Patrick Henry, Thomas Jefferson, Dabney Carr** (and others — Bland, Pendleton, Harrison, Nicholas, Digges, Cary). (HIGH)
- **By February 1774, 11 colonies** had committees; with Pennsylvania (May 1774), every colony that later rebelled had one. (HIGH)
- **Function:** a permanent nervous system for the resistance — moving news, coordinating positions, so the next crisis would be answered continentally, not town by town. (HIGH framing)

### The Hutchinson letters affair (1773) — optional but strong connective tissue
- **Benjamin Franklin**, in London as agent for the Massachusetts House, obtained private letters **Thomas Hutchinson** had written (1767–1769) to Thomas Whately in England, and sent them to Boston (to Speaker Thomas Cushing, December 1772) — meant to be shown privately; **Samuel Adams engineered their publication in June 1773.** (HIGH)
- The incendiary line: Hutchinson had written that the colonies could not retain all English rights at that distance — there must be an **"abridgment of what are called English liberties."** (HIGH — phrase verified; quote exactly)
- The Massachusetts House petitioned the Crown for **Hutchinson's removal**. The petition's fate — and Franklin's punishment — lands in §6 (the Cockpit). (HIGH)
- Effect: by late 1773 Hutchinson was, in patriot eyes, a proven conspirator against their liberties — context for why Boston would not trust him in the tea standoff. (HIGH framing)

## 2. THE TEA ACT (1773)

- **The East India Company was near collapse**: obligated to pay the British government **£400,000 a year**, hammered by famine and war costs in Bengal and by the credit crisis of 1772; it sat on roughly **17–18 million pounds of unsold tea** in British warehouses (smuggled Dutch tea had gutted its market). Parliament propped it up with a loan (commonly given as **£1.4–1.5 million**) and a rescue plan for the tea mountain. (HIGH on near-collapse + £400,000 + the 17–18M lbs surplus as a range; MED on the precise loan figure — round to "a massive government loan")
- **Tea Act — royal assent 10 May 1773.** Mechanics: (HIGH)
  - The Company could for the first time **ship tea directly to America** in its own right, selling through hand-picked colonial agents (**consignees**), cutting out the London auctions and colonial wholesale merchants.
  - Duties payable in **Britain** were refunded or not imposed on tea re-exported to America.
  - The **3d-per-pound Townshend duty, collected in colonial ports, was deliberately retained.**
- **THE TRAP — the chapter's central mechanism.** The Act **lowered the price of legal, taxed tea below the smugglers' price**. Nobody was being squeezed; colonists were being offered cheaper tea. But every cheap cup **paid the Townshend duty — and conceded Parliament's right to tax**. The colonists read it (correctly, by North's own logic from ch.3) as the principle of the Declaratory Act dressed as a bargain. (HIGH — this framing is the heart of the chapter)
- Scale of the smuggling economy: colonists drank some **900,000 lbs/year of smuggled foreign tea** against ~**562,000 lbs/year of legal tea**. (HIGH — Wikipedia figures; round freely)
- **Why merchants and principle aligned:** legal tea merchants and smugglers alike (Hancock's world) faced ruin from a **Company monopoly sold through favored consignees**; Whig principle objected to the duty. The Tea Act fused the self-interested and the principled into one opposition. (HIGH)
- **Autumn 1773 shipments** went to four ports: **Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Charleston**. Boston's consignees included **Richard Clarke & Sons, Benjamin Faneuil (Jr.), Joshua Winslow — and the governor's own sons, Thomas Jr. and Elisha Hutchinson.** (HIGH on the names; the count "seven consignees" appears in sources — MED, say "a handful")

## 3. THE STANDOFF IN BOSTON (November–December 1773)

- **Pressure on the consignees came first.** Early on **2 November 1773** the Clarkes received an anonymous summons from the Sons of Liberty to appear at **Liberty Tree** the next day and resign; on **3 November** the consignees did not appear, and a crowd led by **William Molineux** confronted them at Clarke's warehouse instead, trying to force the counting-room door. On **17 November** a crowd attacked **Richard Clarke's house**. The consignees — unlike those in every other port — **refused to resign**, stiffened by Hutchinson, and took refuge at **Castle William** (the harbor fort) in late November. (HIGH on the spine and dates; MED on the exact date they fled to the Castle)
- **The ships** (all three were ordinary colonial-owned merchantmen carrying the Company's tea as freight): (HIGH)
  - ***Dartmouth*** — arrived **Sunday, 28 November 1773**; Rotch family (Nantucket Quaker whaling merchants); had carried whale oil to London, tea back. **114 chests.**
  - ***Eleanor*** — arrived **2 December**; owned by Boston merchant **John Rowe**. **114 chests.**
  - ***Beaver*** — reached the outer harbor about **7–8 December** but was held at **Rainsford Island in smallpox quarantine**; came up to the wharf about **15 December**. Rotch-owned. **112 chests.**
  - All three ended up at **Griffin's Wharf**. A fourth tea ship, the ***William***, **wrecked in a storm off Cape Cod on 10 December 1773**; its ~58 chests were salvaged ashore (most of that tea, too, was eventually destroyed or fought over — keep to a one-line aside). (HIGH on the wreck; MED on the salvaged tea's fate — keep vague)
- **The 20-day customs clock.** Once a ship entered and its cargo was formally reported, **British law gave 20 days to land the cargo and pay the duty — after which customs officials could seize it** (and seized tea could be landed under the guns of the fleet, duty effectively secured). For *Dartmouth* the clock ran out in **mid-December — the deadline is commonly given as 17 December, making the night of the 16th the last chance.** (HIGH on the mechanism; MED on pinning the exact expiry day — phrase as "the twenty days ran out on December 16th/17th")
- **The squeeze:** the town demanded the ships **return to England with the tea, duty unpaid**; but a ship that had formally entered **could not legally clear outward without paying** — and **Governor Hutchinson refused the pass** that would let the ships by Castle William and the warships in the harbor. The tea could not land (the town); the ships could not leave (the governor); the clock ran regardless. (HIGH — this three-way bind is the engine of the section)
- **The mass meetings — "the Body of the People."** On **29 November 1773** (the day after *Dartmouth* docked) a meeting called at Faneuil Hall **overflowed — thousands attended — and moved to Old South Meeting House**, the biggest building in town. It resolved (echoing Philadelphia's resolutions) that the tea **must go back without payment of any duty**, and posted a **25-man armed watch on the ships** to make sure no tea was landed. Meetings continued through mid-December. These were no longer legal town meetings — anyone could take part (a body "of the people," not just voters). (HIGH; the Nov 29–30 two-day structure is MED — compress to "a two-day mass meeting" or just "a mass meeting")
- **16 December 1773** — the last day. **5,000–7,000 people** (Boston's whole population was ~16,000) packed Old South in a cold rain. The meeting sent ***Dartmouth*'s owner Francis Rotch** on horseback to Hutchinson's country house at **Milton** to beg one last time for a pass. Rotch returned around **5:45 p.m. (after dark): the governor had refused.** (HIGH; the 5:45 time is MED — "after dark" is safe)
- **Samuel Adams then said words to the effect that "this meeting can do nothing further to save the country."** Long retold as the prearranged signal that launched the Tea Party — **the signal story is doubted by modern scholarship** (it first appeared in print ~a century later via Adams's great-grandson, and eyewitnesses say people did not move until 10–15 minutes after the remark). War whoops were heard at the door; the crowd poured out toward Griffin's Wharf. **Use the quote only as reported speech + flag the signal legend.** (DISPUTED — see HANDLE-WITH-CARE #4)

## 4. DECEMBER 16, 1773 — THE DESTRUCTION OF THE TEA

- **Evening of Thursday, 16 December 1773**, roughly **6–7 p.m. start, about three hours' work** — parties boarded all three ships at Griffin's Wharf, hoisted the chests on deck, split them open with hatchets, and shoveled tea and broken chests into the harbor (low tide; the tea heaped in the shallows). (HIGH)
- **The numbers:** **340 or 342 chests** (sources conflict; 342 is the traditional figure; the per-ship manifests — 114 + 114 + 112 — sum to 340. Use "more than 340 chests" or give both). **92,616 lbs of tea** ("over 92,000 pounds," ~46 tons). Value: **£9,659** in 1773 money. **AVOID precise modern-dollar conversions** — say "a fortune; the cargo of a small fleet" or similar. (HIGH on weight + £ value; DISPUTED-range on the chest count)
- **The tea itself was all Chinese**: by the surviving breakdown, **240 chests Bohea, 60 Singlo, 15 Congou, 15 Hyson, 10 Souchong** (Bohea/Congou/Souchong black, Hyson/Singlo green). (HIGH — nice concrete detail; note the breakdown sums to 340)
- **Participants:** estimates run **from ~30–60 core men up to 130 or more**, swelled by volunteers from the meeting; **~116 men are documented by name** (mostly via later claims and family tradition — see CARE #5). Many were **young**: apprentices and journeymen, sixteen of the documented participants teenagers. Organized in three boarding parties, one per ship. (HIGH on the range; MED on "three parties" framing — well attested but keep light)
- **The "Mohawk" disguise** — blankets worn matchcoat-style, faces darkened with soot/lampblack, hatchets ("tomahawks"). It was **thin, symbolic cover, not an attempt to pass as (or blame) actual Mohawk people**: everyone present knew who these men were socially; the disguise hid *individual* identities (the act was a hanging-level property crime against the Crown's protected company) and, symbolically, costumed the actors as **Americans — people of this continent, not Britain**. Handle with the care spelled out in CARE #2. (HIGH on the description; the symbolism reading is the standard scholarly one)
- **The discipline is the story:** (HIGH unless noted)
  - **Nothing but tea was touched.** No looting, no damage to the ships; the decks were swept after.
  - **One padlock was broken — a ship captain's private property — and a replacement was sent the next day.** (HIGH — museum scholarship)
  - A participant who **stuffed tea into his coat lining was stripped and roughed up/stopped** — destruction, not theft, was the point. (HIGH on the incident; name variants exist — leave unnamed)
  - **No one was hurt** beyond one man (John Crane) knocked unconscious by a falling crate — at first thought dead, hidden under wood shavings, recovered. (HIGH on no deaths; the Crane anecdote MED — optional color)
  - **Sworn to secrecy.** Participants kept names secret for decades — many to the grave; **only one man, Francis Akeley (Eckley), was ever arrested**, and no one was ever convicted. (HIGH; spelling of Akeley varies — MED on the name's spelling)
  - In the days after, tea that washed up in the shallows was **beaten into the water with paddles and oars** so none could be salvaged. (MED — museum detail, good color)
- **The Royal Navy watched and did not fire** — warships lay close by, but no order came to attack townsmen committing what was, on its face, vandalism in a crowded port. Participant George Hewes (late recollection — CARE #5): "We were surrounded by British armed ships, but no attempt was made to resist us." (HIGH that the navy did not intervene)
- **John Adams's verdict, next morning** — diary, **17 December 1773** (verbatim, see QUOTES): "the most magnificent Movement of all… a Dignity, a Majesty, a Sublimity… an Epocha in History." Adams also reasoned there had been **"no other Alternative but to destroy it or let it be landed"** — landing it meant surrendering the ten-year principle. Use Adams to mark that even cautious, lawyerly patriots embraced this destruction of property. (HIGH — quotes verified)
- **"Boston Tea Party" is a later name.** Contemporaries called it **"the destruction of the tea."** The name's first appearance in print is given variously as **1825 or 1834** (Alfred Young's careful claim: not until the 1830s, popularized with the Hewes biographies). Safe phrasing: "Nobody at the time called it the Boston Tea Party — that name shows up in print only some fifty years later." (HIGH that the name is much later; the exact first-print year DISPUTED 1825 vs 1834)
- **A second, smaller tea party — 7 March 1774**: ~60 men boarded the ***Fortune*** and destroyed **~30 more chests**. One line at most. (HIGH it happened; MED on the chest count ~28–30)

## 5. THE OTHER TEA PORTS — THE COLONIES WERE NOT ONE MIND

*The same Company tea went to four ports; only Boston ended in destruction — because only in Boston did the consignees refuse to resign and the governor refuse to bend.* (HIGH framing)

- **Charleston** — the ***London*** (Capt. Alexander Curling) arrived **3 December 1773**. The consignees were forced to resign; when the twenty days ran out (**22 December**) the customs collector **seized the 257 chests for non-payment and stored them — locked in the Exchange building's cellars**. The tea sat there; it was later **sold by the revolutionary government to fund the cause**. (HIGH on arrival/seizure/storage; MED on the later-sold detail — fine as a wry aside)
- **Philadelphia** — the ***Polly*** (Capt. Ayres), carrying **697 chests**, came up the Delaware in late December; broadsides had warned the river pilots and the captain (the "Committee for Tarring and Feathering" handbills). A mass meeting of **~8,000** on **27 December** — among the largest gatherings yet in the colonies — resolved the tea must not land; the consignees had already resigned; **Ayres reprovisioned and sailed the *Polly* back to England, tea and all.** (HIGH)
- **New York** — the storm-delayed ***Nancy*** (Capt. Lockyer) finally reached Sandy Hook **18 April 1774**; the consignees had resigned months before; Lockyer was persuaded, under threat, to **turn straight around for England**. On **22 April 1774** the ***London*** (Capt. Chambers) arrived claiming no tea; New Yorkers, tipped off from Philadelphia, found **18 chests Chambers was smuggling on his own account and dumped them in the harbor** — New York's own small tea party. (HIGH)
- **Narrative use:** resignation + turn-back was the *normal* outcome. Boston became the flashpoint because Hutchinson and his consignee sons made retreat impossible. Avoid "the colonies rose as one." (HIGH framing)

## 6. THE BRITISH REACTION — THE COERCIVE ACTS (1774)

### The mood turns in London
- News of the destruction reached London in January 1774. Even friends of America were appalled: this was the **wanton destruction of private property** by a town that then refused to pay for it. North's government resolved to single out Boston and Massachusetts for exemplary punishment. (HIGH)
- **Franklin in the Cockpit — 29 January 1774.** Two days after the news broke, Benjamin Franklin (agent for Massachusetts, exposed as the man who obtained the Hutchinson letters) stood for roughly an hour in the Privy Council chamber called the **Cockpit** while Solicitor-General **Alexander Wedderburn** flayed him before a packed, jeering audience — Franklin the "true incendiary," the **"first mover and prime conductor of this whole contrivance."** Franklin stood in silence; the Massachusetts petition to remove Hutchinson was dismissed as "groundless" and "vexatious"; **the next day Franklin was fired as deputy postmaster-general for North America.** He later wrote: "I made no justification of myself, but held a cool, sullen silence, reserving myself to some future opportunity." (HIGH — all verified; powerful beat for the radicalization of a famous moderate)

### The four Coercive Acts (colonists: "the Intolerable Acts" — but see CARE #7 on that name too)
- **Boston Port Act** — royal assent **31 March 1774**; in force **1 June 1774**. **Closed the port of Boston** — no loading or unloading — **until the town paid for the destroyed tea** (restitution to the East India Company and the customs) *and* the King was satisfied order was restored. Customs operations moved to **Salem/Marblehead**. It passed **without even a division (recorded vote) in the Commons and unanimously in the Lords** — British opinion was that united against Boston. (HIGH)
- **Massachusetts Government Act** — **20 May 1774**. **Rewrote the province's 1691 charter**: the Council (upper house), previously *elected*, now **appointed by the Crown**; judges, sheriffs and most officials appointed by the governor; **juries summoned by the (appointed) sheriffs**; and **town meetings limited to one a year** without the governor's permission. This — abolishing self-government by fiat — alarmed the other colonies most of all: if Parliament could shred one charter, it could shred any. (HIGH)
- **Administration of Justice Act** — **20 May 1774**. A royal official charged with a capital crime committed while suppressing a riot or enforcing revenue laws could have his **trial moved to another colony or to Britain**. Colonists called it the **"Murder Act"** — the charge (attributed to George Washington among others) being that an official could kill and escape local justice. Note honestly: it provided for witnesses' travel costs and was framed as ensuring fair trials for officials who could not get one locally. (HIGH on terms/date; MED on the Washington attribution — phrase as "colonists including Washington called it…" only if kept; "colonists called it the Murder Act" is safe)
- **Quartering Act of 1774** — **2 June 1774**. Let the governor requisition **unoccupied buildings** to lodge troops when barracks were unsuitable. **PRECISION: it did NOT authorize billeting soldiers in occupied private homes** — the schoolbook image is wrong (historian David Ammerman's point, now standard). Applied to all colonies. (HIGH on date; HIGH on the unoccupied-buildings precision — state it)
- **The Quebec Act** — **22 June 1774**. **Not part of the punishment package** — a long-planned settlement for conquered French Canada: free practice of **Catholicism**, restoration of **French civil law** (no jury in civil cases), government by appointed council (**no elected assembly**), and Quebec's boundary extended **south-west to the Ohio and Mississippi** — over lands Virginia and others claimed. American Protestants read it as popery, autocracy and a land-grab rolled into one, and **lumped it in with the Coercive Acts as the fifth "Intolerable Act."** Handle precisely: separate purpose, same session, fused in colonial perception. (HIGH)

## 7. BOSTON UNDER THE BLOCKADE — AND THE CALL FOR A CONGRESS

- **Gage replaces Hutchinson.** General **Thomas Gage**, commander-in-chief of the army in North America, landed in Boston **13 May 1774** as the new **royal governor of Massachusetts** — a general where a civilian had been; Massachusetts now effectively under military government. **Hutchinson sailed for England on 1 June 1774** — he never saw America again (d. 1780, in England). (HIGH)
- **1 June 1774** — the Port Act took effect; the harbor sealed by the navy. The day was observed in many colonies with **fasting, tolling bells, flags at half-mast**. Gage moved the seat of government to **Salem** (per the Act). (HIGH)
- **The punishment backfired into solidarity.** The Boston Committee of Correspondence circulated an appeal (13 May 1774); donations flowed overland and through **Marblehead** (the legal landing point) for distribution by Boston's **Committee of Donations**: grain, rye and corn by the thousands of bushels, **rice from South Carolina** (hundreds of casks), **sheep and cattle driven from Connecticut and across New England**, flour, fish, money — by year's end **over a hundred relief shipments** had come in. Exact totals are a single compiled source — use "rice from Carolina, sheep from Connecticut, grain, flour, money" and round phrases like "by the hundred barrels," not the precise table. (HIGH that massive intercolonial relief arrived incl. SC rice; MED on the itemized totals — soften)
- **The Coercive Acts answered a Boston crime with a Massachusetts-wide constitutional demolition — and every colony noticed.** The committees of correspondence (§1) now did exactly what they were built for. (HIGH framing)
- **The call for a congress:**
  - **Virginia, 27 May 1774** — Governor Dunmore dissolved the House of Burgesses for declaring a day of fasting in solidarity with Boston; the burgesses walked down the street and reconvened at the **Raleigh Tavern** in Williamsburg, where they **proposed an annual congress of all the colonies**; a further meeting (30 May) called a Virginia convention. (HIGH)
  - **Massachusetts, 17 June 1774** — the House, meeting in Salem, **locked its doors** while Gage's messenger (province secretary Thomas Flucker) read the dissolution proclamation **to the locked door from the stairs outside**; inside, the House voted **five delegates** to meet the other colonies at **Philadelphia on 1 September 1774**: Thomas Cushing, Samuel Adams, John Adams, Robert Treat Paine, James Bowdoin. (HIGH — the locked-door scene is verified and is the chapter's closing image)
  - Other colonies chose delegates through the summer; the Congress actually convened **5 September 1774** at Carpenters' Hall. **STOP THE CHAPTER AT THE CALL/THE LOCKED DOOR** — name that a congress is coming (it may note "they would meet in September"), but the Congress itself, the Suffolk Resolves, and everything after belong to ch.5. (HIGH)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **"Boston Tea Party" is a later coinage.** Contemporaries said **"the destruction of the tea."** First print appearance is cited as **1825 by some sources, 1834 by Alfred Young**; it became standard only with the 1830s Hewes celebrity. The app may use the familiar name (readers expect it) but should *tell* the reader nobody in 1773 called it that.
2. **The "Mohawk" disguise.** State plainly: blankets, soot-darkened faces, hatchets — a **thin, symbolic disguise, not an impersonation meant to deceive or to shift blame onto actual Native people** (no one was fooled, and no Mohawks were within 200 miles). The symbolism most scholars read: identifying as *American* — of this continent — rather than British, plus simple concealment of identity for a hanging-level offense. Do not play it for comedy; do not call them "dressed as Indians" without the framing; never imply Native people were involved or blamed.
3. **The Tea Act was a price CUT.** Resist the lazy "Britain taxed tea so colonists rebelled" frame — ch.3 already taxed tea. The 1773 Act made legal tea *cheaper*; the objection was the **principle + the monopoly**, not the price. This inversion is the chapter's teaching moment; get it exactly right.
4. **The Samuel Adams "signal" legend.** The quote's wording surfaced ~a century later (via his great-grandson); eyewitness timing (people left 10–15 minutes after the remark) undercuts the prearranged-signal story. Tell the dramatic version *as the legend* and the doubt in one clause, or skip the signal claim entirely.
5. **George Hewes's recollections are 60+ years late.** The two biographies (Hawkes, *A Retrospect of the Boston Tea-Party*, 1834; Thatcher, *Traits of the Tea Party*, 1835) recorded a man in his 90s and absorbed later folklore (Alfred Young's *The Shoemaker and the Tea Party* is the standard study). Likewise the "116 documented participants" rests heavily on later claims and family tradition. Use Hewes color sparingly, always dated ("remembered decades later"); give participant counts as a range.
6. **The £9,659 value: keep it in 1773 money.** Avoid precise modern-dollar conversions (the "$1.7 million today" figure floats around but is methodology-dependent). "About £10,000 — a fortune" or a period-anchored comparison is safer.
7. **"Intolerable Acts" is also a later label.** Contemporaries (and Parliament) said **Coercive Acts**; even "Intolerable Acts" as a fixed name is **19th-century**. Colonists in 1774 spoke of "the late acts." Fine to use "what Americans came to call the Intolerable Acts" — don't put the phrase in 1774 mouths.
8. **The Quebec Act is NOT one of the Coercive Acts.** Separate, long-planned legislation lumped in by colonial perception. Present its terms neutrally (it was, from Canada's side, a tolerant settlement — Catholic rights, French law) and the colonial reading (popery + no assembly + land-grab) as *their* reading.
9. **The Quartering Act did not put redcoats in private homes.** Unoccupied buildings only. Kill the schoolbook image.
10. **Both sides reason; no cartoon villains.** North's logic: a state that cannot protect a lawful company's property in a colonial port has lost authority; punish one town, isolate the radicals. Hutchinson's logic: he was upholding the law as written, and retreat had cost the government every previous round. The miscalculation — punishing all Massachusetts, which united the colonies instead of isolating Boston — is the story; tell it as miscalculation, not malice. Equally: the destruction of £9,659 of private property *was* radical and shocked moderates (Franklin urged repayment) — let that land too.
11. **AVOID inevitability.** In 1774 almost nobody — including the men dumping tea — sought independence; the demand was restored rights within the empire. The committees, donations, and the congress call are colonists trying to make Britain *back down*, not break away. Independence talk belongs to ch.5.
12. **Numbers to keep loose:** chest count 340 vs 342; participants 30–130+ (116 named later); the Gaspee boats (8 vs 18); the consignee count ("seven"); the Dec 16 attendance (5,000–7,000); the donation table (single compiled source); the *Fortune* chests (~28–30); the Dec 17 vs Dec 16 deadline day.
