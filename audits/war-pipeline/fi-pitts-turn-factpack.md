## 1. William Pitt and the Strategic Reversal

### Timeline of Power
- William Pitt (the Elder) first became Secretary of State for the Southern Department in **November/December 1756**, but his first ministry fell within months.
- He returned in a coalition with the Duke of Newcastle in **June 1757**, forming the **Pitt-Newcastle ministry**: Newcastle managed the House of Lords and Treasury; Pitt held effective control of military strategy and the House of Commons.
  - Source: Wikipedia, William Pitt, 1st Earl of Chatham; Britannica biography
- Famous quote on assuming the coalition: Pitt told the Duke of Devonshire: **"My Lord, I am sure I can save this country, and no one else can."** [Source: Britannica biography of Pitt]
- Note for author: Pitt held the title **Secretary of State for the Southern Department**, NOT Prime Minister — Newcastle was the nominal head. Pitt was the war minister in all but name.

### Why British Strategy Had Failed Before Pitt
- Prior commander in North America, the **Earl of Loudoun** (Lieutenant General John Campbell, 4th Earl of Loudoun), had antagonized the colonial assemblies with high-handed demands — press gangs, billeting, rank disputes — producing friction and "outright mutiny." Pitt recalled Loudoun in late December 1757.
  - Source: WebSearch results; Britannica
- British regulars outranked provincial officers by a 1754 order-in-council that placed all provincial officers below the lowest-ranking regular officer holding the king's commission. This infuriated colonial leaders.
  - Source: Colonial Society of Massachusetts / Pitt circular context

### Pitt's Core Strategic Innovations
1. **Subsidize Prussia in Europe:** Pitt committed an **annual subsidy of £670,000–£700,000** (⚠️ sources vary slightly) to Frederick the Great to keep France tied down on the Continent.
   - Source: Wikipedia (Pitt article); Britannica confirm "£670,000" in one phrasing, "$700,000" in another
   - ⚠️ Exact figure contested between sources — use range: "roughly £670,000–£700,000 per year"
2. **Colonial assemblies as partners:** Parliament granted Pitt "almost unlimited funds." He promised colonial assemblies **liberal reimbursement of their war expenses** in exchange for raising provincial troops. A **1757 warrant** elevated provincial officers of colonel rank and above to parity with British regulars (though still junior to same-rank regulars).
   - Source: Britannica (French and Indian War — British advantages); WebSearch results
3. **Naval supremacy as the lever:** Rather than land campaigns in Europe, Pitt prioritized the **Royal Navy** — blocking French ports, cutting reinforcement routes to Canada, launching amphibious expeditions globally.
   - Source: Wikipedia (Pitt); Britannica
4. **Taking Canada as the primary objective:** Pitt targeted New France directly — Louisbourg first, then Quebec. His strategy recognized that holding Canada would make any European peace terms irrelevant. He famously boasted of having "won Canada on the banks of the Rhine."
   - ⚠️ **"Won Canada on the banks of the Rhine"** — widely attributed to Pitt but the exact wording varies across sources; treat as [UNVERIFIED verbatim] pending primary-source confirmation.
   - Source: Wikipedia (Pitt article) cites the formulation; Britannica echoes the idea
5. **Provincial troop expansion:** By summer 1758, Britain had **approximately 50,000 men in uniform in North America** — regulars plus colonial provincial regiments — roughly equal to the entire white population of New France.
   - Source: Britannica (French and Indian War); WebSearch results

### The Pitt-Newcastle Partnership
- Political arrangement: Newcastle controlled patronage and the Lords; Pitt directed the war. "An unlikely but productive political partnership."
- Source: Wikipedia (Pitt); Britannica

## 2. The Three-Pronged 1758 Offensive

Pitt's design for 1758 was three simultaneous thrusts to overwhelm French capacity to respond:

| Prong | Target | Commander | Outcome |
|-------|--------|-----------|---------|
| 1 | Louisbourg (Atlantic gateway) | Amherst / Wolfe / Boscawen | British victory, Jul 26 |
| 2 | Fort Carillon / Ticonderoga (Lake Champlain corridor) | Abercromby | French victory, catastrophic British defeat |
| 3 | Fort Duquesne (Ohio Country) | Forbes | French abandon fort, Nov 24 |

Plus: Fort Frontenac (Aug 1758) — not originally the primary third prong, but strategically decisive.

## 3. Siege of Louisbourg (June 8 – July 26, 1758)

### Location
Fortress of Louisbourg, **Île-Royale** (modern Cape Breton Island, Nova Scotia). Guarded the mouth of the St. Lawrence River — the key to any strike at Quebec.

### British Command
- **General Jeffery Amherst** — overall British commander; ~11,000–14,000 regulars plus ~200 American rangers (⚠️ sources give 11,000 regulars in one place, "nearly 14,000 troops" in another; Wikipedia wikitext says "11,000 regulars plus 200 rangers"; another source says "approximately 14,000 soldiers mostly regulars")
  - ⚠️ Use range: approximately 11,000–14,000 British troops total
- **Brigadier General James Wolfe** — commanded the Red Division; led the critical landing
- **Brigadier General Charles Lawrence** — Blue Division
- **Brigadier General Edward Whitmore** — White Division
- **Admiral Edward Boscawen** — naval command; **40 warships** and **150 transport vessels**
- Sources: Wikipedia (Siege of Louisbourg 1758); Britannica; Warfare History Network

### French Command and Defense
- **Chevalier Augustin de Boschenry de Drucour** — governor and fortress commander
- French garrison: approximately **7,000 troops** (⚠️ one source says 3,500 regulars + 3,500 sailors/marines)
- French naval force: **7 ships of the line, 4 frigates, 1 fluyt**
- **Mi'kmaq** warriors fought alongside French defenders throughout the siege
  - Note for author: Mi'kmaq alliance with France was longstanding; their role at Louisbourg should be named explicitly — first-class participants, not a footnote.
  - Source: Wikipedia wikitext (Siege of Louisbourg 1758)

### The Landing — June 8, 1758
- Wolfe's Red Division made the **landing at Kennington Cove** (French name: Anse de la Cormorandière) on **June 8, 1758** — a rocky beach initially judged too dangerous
- During a repulse, some of Wolfe's light infantry boats drifted east and spotted an undefended rocky landing spot; taking the initiative, they went ashore and held it; the rest of Wolfe's force followed
- The French, unsure of Wolfe's full strength, pulled back toward the fortress, surrendering the outer defenses
- Source: HistoricPlaces.ca; Parks Canada; WebSearch results on Kennington Cove

### The Siege Unfolds (June–July)
- British artillery systematically dismantled the fortress walls over seven weeks
- **July 21:** British mortar fire struck the 64-gun French ship **_Célèbre_**, igniting fires that spread to **_Entreprenant_** and **_Capricieux_**. Three French ships destroyed or burned.
- **July 23:** The King's Bastion burned
- **July 25:** British forces captured **_Bienfaisant_** and burned **_Prudent_** (which had run aground), clearing the harbor for Royal Navy entry
- Source: Wikipedia wikitext (Siege of Louisbourg 1758)

### Surrender Terms — July 26, 1758
- Articles of capitulation signed at **midnight, July 26, 1758**
- Drucour requested **honors of war** (allowing garrison to march out with arms); Amherst refused
- Amherst's reply (direct quote, verified): **"We give your Excellency an hour to determine on the only capitulation we are willing to grant, which is, you surrender yourselves prisoners of war."**
  - Source: WebSearch results citing British-battles.com; WorldHistoryEdu.com
- Drucour accepted to protect civilian inhabitants; all French military personnel surrendered arms, flags, and equipment and were shipped to France as prisoners of war
- Source: Wikipedia; WebSearch

### Casualties
| Side | Killed | Wounded | Captured |
|------|--------|---------|---------|
| British | 172 | 355 | — |
| French | 102 | 303 | 6,600 |

- Plus: 3 French ships destroyed in harbor fire, 2 captured, 2 scuttled
- Source: Wikipedia wikitext

### Strategic Significance
- "The loss of Louisbourg deprived New France of naval protection, opening the Saint Lawrence to attack" — direct path to Quebec now open
- Enabled the **1759 siege of Quebec** (Battle of the Plains of Abraham)
- Source: Wikipedia (Siege of Louisbourg); Britannica

## 4. Battle of Carillon / Fort Ticonderoga (July 8, 1758)

### Location
Fort Carillon, on the narrows between Lake Champlain and Lake George, in present-day **Ticonderoga, New York**. The defensive key to the Lake Champlain–Richelieu River corridor into the heart of New France.

### The Preliminary: Death of Lord Howe — July 6, 1758
- **George Augustus Howe, 3rd Viscount Howe** (Brigadier General; British regular) — second-in-command, universally beloved by both British regulars and colonial provincials
  - Howe had learned irregular warfare from **Major Robert Rogers** and his Rangers; he insisted on fighting the way the wilderness demanded — no powdered wigs, trimmed coats, shortened muskets
  - Described by colonials as a man who "love and fear him" — the only high-ranking British officer they trusted
- On **July 6**, during a skirmish at **Trout Brook** (also called Bernetz Brook), Howe led a column with the 55th Regiment and Connecticut militia when his force collided with a separated French detachment. A musket ball struck him in the chest; he died instantly.
- Famous quote: British officer's dispatch: **"In Lord Howe, the soul of General Abercromby's army seemed to expire. From the unhappy moment the General was deprived of his advice, neither order nor discipline was observed, and a strange kind of infatuation usurped the place of resolution."** [Source: emergingrevolutionarywar.org; confirmed by Fort Ticonderoga historical account]
  - ⚠️ The full exact attribution of this quote is unclear — often cited as "a British officer," sometimes attributed to Captain James Abercrombie (different man). Treat as period-attributed but author-unverifiable without further primary sources.
- James Wolfe later called Howe's loss "irreparable"
- Source: emergingrevolutionarywar.org; Fort Ticonderoga Facebook post; WebSearch

### French Command and Preparation
- **Lieutenant General Louis-Joseph de Montcalm, Marquis de Montcalm** — French commander-in-chief in North America; brilliant defensive tactician
- **Chevalier de Lévis** (François-Gaston de Lévis) — second-in-command; commanded the right wing at Carillon
- **Chevalier de Bourlamaque** (François-Charles de Bourlamaque) — commanded the left wing
- French force at Carillon: **approximately 3,500–3,600 men**
  - Bougainville's count: 3,526; Vaudreuil's count: 4,760 including laborers (⚠️ contested)
  - Use: "approximately 3,500 French regulars and militia"
  - Regiment breakdown: Royal-Roussillon and Berry (center, Montcalm); Béarn, Guyenne, La Reine (right, Lévis); La Sarre, Languedoc (left, Bourlamaque); plus ~250 Indigenous warriors
  - Source: Project Seven Years War (kronoskaf.com); Warfare History Network; WebSearch

### The Breastwork — Built July 6–7, 1758
- Starting the evening of **July 6**, the French began constructing defenses on a ridge **three-quarters of a mile from Fort Carillon**, at a spot called the **Heights of Carillon**
- By the evening of **July 7** — in roughly **24 hours** — they had completed:
  - A **log breastwork at least 8 feet high**, zigzagging for approximately **one half mile** across the heights to enable enfilading fire
  - An **abatis**: felled trees with branches sharpened and pointed outward, stretching ~30 yards in front of the logs — functioning like barbed wire
- Source: Warfare History Network; WebSearch (crossroads of a continent; emerging rev war)

### British Command and Force
- **Major General James Abercromby** (also spelled Abercrombie) — overall British commander; 52 years old, politically connected, limited combat experience
- ⚠️ Note for author: Abercromby is widely described as incompetent in the secondary literature; balanced framing should note he had successfully managed logistics for the enormous force
- British force: **6,367 regulars + 9,034 provincial troops = approximately 15,000–16,000 total** (sources range from "over 12,000" to "some 15,000"; the exact Wikipedia figure is 6,367 + 9,034)
  - ⚠️ Use: "approximately 15,000 men"
  - Source: American Battlefield Trust; Warfare History Network; kronoskaf.com

### Why No Artillery?
- British engineer **Lieutenant Matthew Clerk** climbed Rattlesnake Mountain and reconnoitered the French position; he **reported the breastworks could be stormed by infantry assault** without siege artillery
- Abercromby, confident of numbers, accepted this assessment and ordered a frontal assault without bringing up his cannon
- ⚠️ Modern historians debate how much blame belongs to Clerk vs. Abercromby; Abercromby later attempted to blame Clerk. Clerk was wounded in the battle and died shortly after.
- Source: Project MUSE scholarly article citation in WebSearch; Warfare History Network

### The Assault — July 8, 1758
- Abercromby launched repeated frontal infantry assaults through the abatis directly into the breastwork; attacks began mid-afternoon
- British formations were broken up by the sharpened branches; French poured withering fire from behind the 8-foot log wall
- "Coordination broke down quickly" — Abercromby had planned a simultaneous assault along the full French line but the assault dissolved into isolated charges
- British troops were "cut down like grass"; one observer compared the killing ground to a slaughterhouse
- Abercromby never deployed his artillery; he never flanked the position; he never attempted to cut the line or wait for a siege
- After several hours and catastrophic losses, Abercromby ordered retreat the evening of July 8; by July 9 the British force was pulling back to the southern end of Lake George

### Casualties
| Side | Killed | Wounded | Missing/Total |
|------|--------|---------|------|
| French | ~104 | ~273 | ~377 combined |
| British | 500+ killed | 1,300+ wounded | ~1,600–2,000 total |

⚠️ British casualties are contested:
- Abercromby reported "just under 2,000 casualties, more than 500 dead"
- Warfare History Network (citing Edward P. Hamilton): "some 1,610 killed, wounded, and missing"
- One French source: "over 3,000 British casualties" (likely inflated)
- Use range: approximately 1,600–2,000 British casualties (500+ dead)
- French: "42 officers and nearly 400 men killed or wounded" (Warfare History Network)

### Aftermath
- Described as **"the bloodiest battle in the North American theater of the French and Indian War"** — and "the bloodiest action fought on the North American continent in modern history" until the Civil War
  - Source: emergingrevolutionarywar.org; American Battlefield Trust
- Abercromby recalled to Britain in September 1758; replaced by General Jeffery Amherst
- Despite the humiliation, Abercromby was promoted to lieutenant general (1760) and general (1772) by seniority — political connections protected him from formal censure. He never held field command again. He died 1781 in retirement in Banffshire, Scotland.
- Source: Wikipedia (James Abercrombie); WebSearch
- French morale soared; Montcalm was celebrated across New France and France
- Fort Carillon was finally taken by the British in **1759**, renamed **Fort Ticonderoga**

## 5. Capture of Fort Frontenac (August 25–27, 1758)

### Location
Fort Frontenac, where **Lake Ontario drains into the St. Lawrence River**, at present-day **Kingston, Ontario, Canada**. The hub of French supply to every western post — Fort Duquesne, Fort Niagara, the Great Lakes forts.

### British Commander
- **Lieutenant Colonel John Bradstreet** — led the operation; energetic, experienced in Great Lakes logistics; had helped plan Braddock's re-supply years earlier
  - Note for author: Bradstreet was half-Acadian; he understood the waterways. He was not one of Pitt's marquee generals but proved the most efficient officer of the 1758 campaign.

### Forces
- British: approximately **2,635 men** (135 regulars, remainder provincial troops from New York, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Rhode Island)
  - ⚠️ Another source says "a little over 3,000 men" — desertion apparently reduced effective strength to ~2,035 by Aug 21
  - Source: Wikipedia (Battle of Fort Frontenac); WebSearch Fort Frontenac results
- French garrison: **approximately 100–150 men** (some sources say 110 persons including 48 enlisted regulars, officers, employees, women, children, 8 Indigenous people; others say ~150)
  - French commander: **Pierre-Jacques Payen de Noyan et de Chavoy**, elderly King George's War veteran
  - Source: Wikipedia; WebSearch

### Action and Surrender — August 25–27, 1758
- Bradstreet landed August 25; emplaced impromptu siege lines with a few small cannon
- French garrison of ~100–150 could not resist; surrendered **August 27, 1758**
- Source: WebSearch (historyofwar.org gives "Fall of Fort Frontenac, 26 August 1758"; Wikipedia gives 26–28 August; most sources cite August 27 as surrender)
  - ⚠️ Exact surrender date: sources vary between August 26 and August 27. Use: "surrendered August 26–27, 1758."

### What Was Taken or Destroyed
- **60+ cannon** (including British guns captured at Fort Oswego in 1756)
- **Hundreds of barrels of provisions**
- Furs and trade goods worth approximately **800,000 French livres** — the entire surplus of the pays d'en haut (western fur trade region)
- **2 French warships** captured (ran aground); remaining French naval presence on Lake Ontario destroyed
- Much of the fort itself demolished
- Source: Wikipedia (Battle of Fort Frontenac)

### Casualties
- British: **11 wounded**; no killed
- French: **2 killed**; entire garrison captured
- Source: Wikipedia

### Strategic Significance
- Cut the **main French supply line from Montreal/Quebec to all western posts** — Fort Duquesne, Fort Niagara, the Great Lakes forts
- "Fort Duquesne was now totally isolated" — Bradstreet's raid made it strategically untenable even before Forbes reached it
- French "lost command of Lake Ontario and, with it, their lines of communication with the other French settlements on the Great Lakes and along the Ohio and Mississippi"
- Boosted British morale after the Carillon catastrophe
- Source: WebSearch (encyclopedia.com; historyofwar.org; newyorkalmanack.com)

## 6. The Forbes Expedition and Fall of Fort Duquesne (Nov 24, 1758)

### Background and Commander
- **Brigadier General John Forbes** — Scottish officer, Pitt's personal choice; methodical, respected, not a glory-seeker
- Forbes suffered from **dysentery throughout the entire campaign** and was too ill to ride; he was carried in a litter. He reached Fort Duquesne's ruins briefly, then was transported back to Philadelphia. He died in Philadelphia in **March 1759**, months after his triumph.
  - Note for author: Forbes won his greatest victory while slowly dying. He never saw the fort he captured in any operational sense.
- Source: Wikipedia (Forbes Expedition); WebSearch

### Forces
Total force: approximately **6,000 men**
- 77th Foot (1st Highland Battalion, Grant's regiment): ~998
- Royal Americans (1st Bn): ~363
- Virginia Regiment (1st): ~782 (including George Washington's contingent)
- Virginia Regiment (2nd): ~702
- Pennsylvania Regiment battalions: ~755, ~664, ~771
- North Carolina, Maryland, Delaware provincials: ~202, ~270, ~263
- Source: Wikipedia (Forbes Expedition) with regiment-level breakdown

**Lieutenant Colonel Henry Bouquet** — Swiss mercenary officer commanding the Royal Americans; served as Forbes' effective operational commander since Forbes was largely incapacitated. Bouquet made most day-to-day decisions.

**Colonel George Washington** — commanded Virginia contingent; personally led three scouting parties into the ruins of Fort Duquesne on November 24. ⚠️ Note: Washington and Forbes clashed over route — Washington wanted to use Braddock's Road (south), Forbes insisted on a new road (Pennsylvania, central). Forbes won the argument.

### Forbes Road
- Forbes ordered construction of a **new road** across southern Pennsylvania's Appalachians (through modern-day Bedford and Ligonier, PA) rather than reopening Braddock's Road through Virginia
- The **Forbes Road** was built methodically westward, establishing supply depots (Fort Bedford, Fort Ligonier) at each stage — a contrast to Braddock's dangerously fast advance
- Source: Wikipedia (Forbes Expedition); WebSearch

### Grant's Defeat — September 14, 1758
- **Major James Grant** (of the 77th Highlanders, the "Black Watch" regiment) was sent with approximately **850 men** to reconnoiter Fort Duquesne
- Grant **divided his force** into three components — a decoy under Captain McDonald, an ambush party of 400, and a concealed force under Major Andrew Lewis — and the divided units could not support each other
- French garrison (~600) plus Native allies (~500 warriors) sallied out and defeated Grant's fragmented force
- British/American casualties: **342 total (104 killed, 220 wounded, ~18 captured including Grant himself)**
- French/Native losses: approximately **8 killed, 8 wounded**
- Battle date: ⚠️ sources disagree slightly — reconnaissance began September 11; main battle September 13–14. Use: **September 14, 1758**
- Grant was captured, held as a prisoner of war
- Source: Wikipedia (Battle of Fort Duquesne); WebSearch

### Treaty of Easton — October 26, 1758
*(Full details in Section 7 below)*
- Strategic effect on Forbes: once the Ohio nations and Delawares formally withdrew support from France (after the treaty, signed October 26), Fort Duquesne's garrison was left without its Native alliance network
- The French found themselves commanding a 600-man garrison in a fort with no local support and Forbes' 6,000 men bearing down from the east
- Source: Wikipedia (Forbes Expedition); Wikipedia (Treaty of Easton)

### Fort Duquesne Abandoned — November 24, 1758
- As Forbes' force approached within striking range in mid-November, the French **destroyed Fort Duquesne on November 24, 1758** — blowing up the magazine, burning the buildings, and retreating
- French garrison commander recognized the position was indefensible: Bradstreet had cut the supply line from the north, the Treaty of Easton had removed Native allies, Forbes' road had bypassed the wilderness defenses
- **George Washington personally led three scouting parties into the ruins on November 24**
- Source: Wikipedia (Forbes Expedition); WebSearch

### Fort Pitt and Pittsburgh
- The day after the fall, **November 27, 1758**, General Forbes wrote to William Pitt the Elder:
  - **Direct quote:** *"I have used the freedom of giving your name to Fort Duquesne, as I hope it was in some measure the being actuated by your spirits that now makes us masters of the place."*
  - Source: WPXI historical article; Visit Pittsburgh historical sources; HMDB historical marker
- The British constructed **Fort Pitt** on the site — designed by Captain Harry Gordon in a **pentagonal (star-shaped) bastioned** plan; the largest and most elaborate British fortress in America at 20 acres; construction 1759–1761
- The city of Pittsburgh grew around Fort Pitt — named directly after the fort, and thus after William Pitt the Elder
- Source: Wikipedia (Fort Pitt, Pennsylvania); Visit Pittsburgh; WebSearch

## 7. Treaty of Easton — October 26, 1758 (Native Diplomacy Turning Point)

### The Event
- Treaty signed on **October 26, 1758** in **Easton, Pennsylvania**
- Over **500 Native Americans** attended in an outdoor ceremony
- Source: Wikipedia (Treaty of Easton); WebSearch

### British Signatories
- **William Denny**, Lieutenant Governor of Pennsylvania — principal British negotiator
- Governor of **New Jersey** (name not identified in accessible sources — ⚠️ gap)
- Source: Wikipedia (Treaty of Easton)

### Native Nations at Easton
The treaty involved representatives of **13 Native nations**, including:
- **Iroquois Confederacy nations** (Six Nations: Mohawks, Onondagas, Oneidas, Cayugas, Senecas, Tuscaroras)
- **Tuteloes, Nanticokes and Conoys, Chugnuts** (smaller tributary nations)
- **Eastern and Western Lenape (Delaware)**
- **Minisinks and Wapings**
- **Mohickons (Mahicans)**
- **Mingo** (Iroquois in the Ohio Country)
- **Shawnee**
- Full list from the published treaty title: "Mohawks, Oneydos, Onondagas, Cayugas, Senecas, Tuscaroras, Tuteloes, Nanticokes and Conoys, Chugnuts, Delawares, Unamies, Mohickons, Minisinks, and Wapings"
- Source: University of Michigan Library Digital Collections (Evans Early American Imprint Collection — the original published treaty minutes)

### Key Figures
- **Teedyuscung** — "King of the Delawares" (Eastern Lenape leader); principal Native negotiator; had been engaged in a series of peace talks at Easton since 1756
- **Charles Thomson** — secretary and advisor to Teedyuscung; later became Secretary of the Continental Congress
- The **Friendly Association for Regaining and Preserving Peace with the Indians by Pacific Measures** (Quaker-organized) played a significant role in creating conditions for the treaty
- Source: Wikipedia (Treaty of Easton); WebSearch (beavercountyindians.com)

### What the Nations Were Promised
1. **Recognition of hunting rights** in the Ohio River valley — the British committed to acknowledging Iroquois and other tribes' rights to their traditional hunting grounds
2. **Settlement restriction west of the Allegheny Mountains** — Britain promised "to refrain from establishing colonial settlements west of the Allegheny Mountains after the conclusion of the war"
3. **Return of previously ceded lands** — Pennsylvania returned "large blocks of land which the Iroquois had ceded a few years before" (relating to disputed earlier treaties)
4. **Payment**: approximately **1,000 Spanish dollars** as partial consideration
- Source: Wikipedia (Treaty of Easton); beavercountyindians.com

### The Military Effect (Why It Won the War in the West)
- The central military provision: the Native nations **"would not fight on the side of the French against the British in the current war"** — i.e., withdrawal of the alliance network that had sustained French power in the Ohio Country since 1754
- Without Native warriors, Fort Duquesne was operationally dead — the French could not defend the Ohio Country against Forbes' methodical advance
- This was the diplomatic key that unlocked the Forbes Expedition. Forbes's road was the hammer; Easton was what cleared the path.
- Source: Wikipedia (Forbes Expedition) states the treaty caused "a loss of Native American support for the French"; WebSearch

### The Betrayal
- The settlement restriction was enshrined conceptually in the **Royal Proclamation of 1763**, which reserved territory west of the Appalachians for Native peoples — but this was not enforced
- After the war ended, British colonists flooded westward; the promises made at Easton were systematically broken within five years
- The betrayal of Easton fueled **Pontiac's War (1763–1766)** — the Lenape, Shawnee, and other Ohio nations who had withdrawn from France based on these promises took up arms when they saw the British were not honoring the terms
- Note for author: The treaty is a study in imperial promises made to win a war and discarded once the war was won. It deserves a full beat in the narrative — not just as a military maneuver, but as a moment of real Native agency that was then deliberately betrayed. Pontiac's War is the direct aftermath.
- Source: Wikipedia (Treaty of Easton); beavercountyindians.com; WebSearch

## 8. The Strategic Shift: 1758 as the Turning Year

### The Balance Sheet at Year's End
**British gains:**
- Louisbourg: the gateway to the St. Lawrence, now open
- Fort Frontenac: French supply network to the west severed
- Fort Duquesne: French expelled from the Ohio Country; Fort Pitt established
- Treaty of Easton: Native nations detached from the French alliance in the west

**French gains:**
- Carillon: one spectacular defensive victory — but it required Montcalm to commit nearly his entire effective field force in a tactical gamble, and it could not reverse the strategic losses

### Why the Tide Turned
- **Naval supremacy**: Britain's Royal Navy strangled New France's reinforcement pipeline. By 1758 the French could barely resupply their garrisons, let alone reinforce them. Britain had the resources of an empire; France had the debts of a bankrupt monarchy.
- **Numbers**: The reimbursement policy and rank reforms brought colonial assemblies on board. Roughly 50,000 men in North America vs. the entire white population of New France (~75,000 total; male military-age population far smaller).
- **Diplomatic genius at Easton**: The Ohio nations did not have to fight the British at all. They chose not to. Easton was as important as any battle.
- **French strategic dilemma**: Montcalm could win battles (Carillon) but could not be everywhere. Every British objective he successfully defended left two others undefended.

### Setting Up 1759
- With Louisbourg taken, Wolfe could sail up the St. Lawrence to Quebec
- With Fort Frontenac gone, Fort Niagara was the next domino
- With Fort Duquesne gone and Easton signed, the entire Ohio Country was British
- 1759 would become the **"Year of Victories"** — Quebec (September 13), Fort Niagara (July 25), Fort Ticonderoga (July 26), Crown Point (August 4)
- Source: Britannica (French and Indian War); WebSearch

## Revision additions (verified) — round 2

### A. The Walking Purchase (1737)

**Verified facts:**
- The fraudulent deed cited by the Penn proprietors was an incomplete 1686 document — described in sources variously as "an unsigned draft," "an unratified treaty," or "an outright forgery." Experts examining it more than a century later concluded it was likely a forgery.
- The principal architects: **Thomas Penn** and **John Penn** (sons of William Penn), with **James Logan** (provincial secretary of Pennsylvania). Their agent, **Andrew Hamilton**, showed the Lenape sachems a map deliberately misrepresenting the Lehigh River as the closer Tohickon Creek, so the chiefs believed the purchase covered only territory south of Tohickon Creek.
- The walk itself took place **September 19, 1737** (one source extends it to a second day, but September 19 is the date most sources give). Thomas Penn hired three of the fastest runners in the colony — **Edward Marshall**, **Solomon Jennings**, and **James Yeates** — supervised by **Sheriff Timothy Smith** of Bucks County. Only Marshall finished the full distance.
- Distance covered: **~70 miles** (113 km) north, reaching the vicinity of present-day Jim Thorpe, Pennsylvania.
- Land claimed: approximately **1,200,932 acres** (~4,860 km²; "only slightly smaller than the size of Rhode Island"). The boundary was then angled northeast to the confluence of the Delaware and Lackawaxen Rivers, gaining still more territory.
- Aftermath: most Delawares in eastern Pennsylvania relocated west to the Ohio River valley; many sided with France when Braddock's expedition rejected their overtures in 1755.
- **For the revision:** The ~1.2 million acres figure is solid; the "65 miles" sometimes cited in popular accounts is lower than the 70 miles Wikipedia and the Philadelphia Encyclopedia give — use ~70 miles. Do not assert a two-day walk as confirmed; September 19 is the established date.
- Sources: Wikipedia (Walking Purchase); Philadelphia Encyclopedia (philadelphiaencyclopedia.org); PHMC Pennsylvania Documentary Heritage

### B. Teedyuscung at the Easton Conferences (1756–1758)

**Verified facts:**
- **Teedyuscung** (c. 1700–1763), known as "King of the Delawares," was an Eastern Lenape leader who became the principal Native spokesman in the Easton treaty negotiations that ran from **1756 to 1762**, with the pivotal session in **October 1758**.
- His core grievance — raised persistently across the Easton conferences — was the Walking Purchase of 1737. He accused the proprietors of fraud in both that purchase and a 1749 land sale in which the Iroquois (Six Nations) had sold Lenape lands above the Kittatinny Hills without Lenape consent.
- Famous statement attributed to Teedyuscung at the Easton negotiations: **"This very Ground that is under me (striking it with his Foot) was my Land and Inheritance, and is taken from me by Fraud."** [Source: Wikipedia (Teedyuscung); widely reproduced; treat as a period-recorded quote confirmed in secondary literature, though the original treaty minutes are the primary verification.]
- He was supported by **Quaker allies** organized as the **Friendly Association for Regaining and Preserving Peace with the Indians by Pacific Measures**, and by his secretary **Charles Thomson** (later Secretary of the Continental Congress).
- The **Treaty of Easton (October 26, 1758)** committed the British to two specific land-related promises:
  1. Recognition of Iroquois and other tribal **hunting rights in the Ohio River valley**
  2. A pledge to **"refrain from establishing colonial settlements west of the Allegheny Mountains after the conclusion of the war"**
- Pennsylvania also returned "large blocks of land which the Iroquois had ceded a few years before."
- The western-settlement pledge was the conceptual precursor to the **Royal Proclamation of 1763**, which attempted to formalize it — but was not enforced as settlers poured into the Ohio Country beginning in 1759, directly fueling **Pontiac's War (1763–1766)**.
- **Note on the Walking Purchase resolution:** The 1758 treaty did NOT formally void or compensate the Walking Purchase — the grievance remained unresolved. What Easton secured was the western-lands pledge and cessation of Delaware hostilities, not restitution for 1737. The Walking Purchase was not formally investigated until a Royal Commission ordered by the Crown in 1758 (at Benjamin Franklin's instigation in London), though that investigation was inconclusive and the land was never restored.
- Sources: Wikipedia (Teedyuscung); Wikipedia (Treaty of Easton); beavercountyindians.com; Penn State Pabook (Teedyuscung biography); colonialwarspa.org (Treaty of Easton, October 1758)

### C. Indigenous Warriors at Carillon/Ticonderoga (July 8, 1758) — VERDICT: Largely absent

**Verified finding: Native warriors played essentially NO significant role in the French defense at Carillon on July 8, 1758. The revision should NOT describe a substantial Indigenous presence at this battle.**

**The evidence:**
- Wikipedia's Battle of Carillon infobox lists "600 Indians" in the total French strength, but the article body immediately qualifies: **"Although the French depended on Indian support to increase their comparatively small numbers throughout the war, Indian forces were quite low in this battle, and Montcalm generally disliked them and their practices."**
- The Warfare History Network account confirms that the Indigenous allies present "hovered along both flanks to cover the broken ground" but played no meaningful role in the abatis defense. The Warriors on Rattlesnake Mountain (some ~450, fighting on the British side under William Johnson) **"refused to take an active part in the affair, preferring to watch the British and French kill each other"** and fired only a few ineffectual shots.
- A search drawing on the Canadian Encyclopedia and militaryhistory.ca explicitly states that **"Native American scouts' numbers had dwindled to fewer than 100 by the battle due to prior disillusionment over plunder restrictions."**
- Context: Montcalm's 1757 campaign (Fort William Henry) had depended heavily on Native allies — then numbered in the hundreds to over a thousand — and ended with a massacre of British prisoners that Montcalm could not control. He was deeply averse to repeating that reliance. By 1758, most Native warriors had departed or were absent.
- **The existing fact pack (Section 4, line 140) lists "~250 Indigenous warriors" in the French order of battle.** This figure, drawn from kronoskaf.com, likely represents the number who were nominally present at or near Carillon, not those who actively defended the breastwork. The more accurate characterization for the revision is: **some Native warriors were listed in Montcalm's force (~250 or fewer), but by the time of the July 8 assault they had largely dispersed, and their role in the battle was negligible.** The active fighting was done by French regulars and militia behind the log breastwork.
- **Contrast with 1757:** At Fort William Henry (August 1757), Montcalm had approximately 1,800 Native warriors from 40+ nations alongside his regulars. At Carillon, that alliance had effectively dissolved for this engagement.
- Sources: Wikipedia (Battle of Carillon); Warfare History Network (1758 Battle of Ticonderoga); militaryhistory.ca (7 Years War — Battle of Fort Carillon)

### D. Why Louisbourg "Opens the St. Lawrence" — Geography Confirmed

**Verified facts:**
- The Fortress of Louisbourg was located at **Louisbourg, Île-Royale** (modern Cape Breton Island, Nova Scotia) on the **Atlantic coast**, positioned to control the approach to the **Gulf of St. Lawrence** — the only practical deep-water sea route into the interior of New France.
- Parks Canada and Wikipedia (Fortress of Louisbourg) state it directly: the location was chosen **"because it was easy to defend against British ships attempting to either block or attack the St. Lawrence River, at the time the only way to get goods to Canada and its cities of Quebec and Montreal."**
- Wikipedia (Fortress of Louisbourg): **"The Fortress of Louisbourg on the Atlantic coast of Cape Breton Island was the bastion guarding the entrance to the Saint Lawrence River and access to French Canada."**
- With Louisbourg taken in 1758, the British had both the sea route open and a logistical staging base on the doorstep of the Gulf. Wikipedia (Siege of Louisbourg 1758): **"The loss of Louisbourg deprived New France of naval protection, opening the Saint Lawrence to attack."**
- The 1759 operational chain followed directly: **"Louisbourg was used in 1759 as the staging point for General Wolfe's famous siege of Quebec."** Wolfe's fleet sailed from Louisbourg up the St. Lawrence to the Plains of Abraham.
- **For the revision:** The geography is confirmed and the causal chain is tight — Louisbourg guards the Gulf of St. Lawrence mouth; the Gulf/River is the only deep-water highway to Quebec; taking Louisbourg in July 1758 gave Wolfe a clear route and a ready base for the 1759 Quebec campaign. This can be stated directly without qualification.
- Sources: Wikipedia (Fortress of Louisbourg); Wikipedia (Siege of Louisbourg 1758); Parks Canada (Fortress of Louisbourg National Historic Site)
