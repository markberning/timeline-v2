## Methodology

Every date, name, number, quote, and key claim was verified independently against primary and authoritative secondary sources: the *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln* (Basler ed., University of Michigan digital), Abraham Lincoln Online, NPS Lincoln Home, the American Presidency Project (UCSB), National Archives Electoral College records, Illinois State Archives, Wikipedia cross-checked against primary sources, and contemporaneous source texts (Teaching American History, Miller Center, Voices of Democracy/UMD). The author's fact pack was read **after** independent verification was complete; it is used here only for cross-reference.

### BEAT 1: Lincoln's Biography

**Claim:** "He was born on February 12, 1809, in a one-room log cabin on a Kentucky farm."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Born February 12, 1809, on Sinking Spring Farm, Hardin County (now LaRue County), Kentucky. The structure is consistently described as a one-room log cabin.
*Source: NPS Abraham Lincoln Birthplace NHP; Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"*

**Claim:** "his formal schooling, added all together, came to less than a year"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Standard biographical record: less than 12 months total formal schooling, accumulated by approximately age 15.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"; Library of Congress*

**Claim:** "working through William Blackstone's *Commentaries on the Laws of England* (the standard four-volume English legal treatise that generations of American lawyers learned from)"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Self-taught law via Blackstone's *Commentaries* is well-documented. The characterization as a four-volume standard treatise is accurate.

**Claim:** "He served four terms in the Illinois state legislature"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Four consecutive terms, 1834–1842, representing Sangamon County.
*Source: NPS, "Lincoln in the Illinois State Legislature"; Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"*

**Claim:** "then a single term in the U.S. House of Representatives, from 1847 to 1849, as a member of the Whig Party. Lincoln had promised back home to serve only one term, and he kept the promise."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. One term, March 4, 1847 – March 3, 1849. The pledge to serve one term (made to win the 1846 nomination over rivals) is confirmed.
*Source: US House of Representatives History, Art & Archives; Miller Center*

**Claim:** "The 'Spot Resolutions' were principled and politically poisonous."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Introduced December 22, 1847. Lincoln challenged Polk to name the exact "spot" where American blood was shed. Politically unpopular; earned Lincoln the derisive nickname "Spotty Lincoln" in some Illinois press.
*Source: National Archives, "Lincoln's Spot Resolutions"; Wikipedia, "Spot Resolutions"*

**Claim:** "Lincoln... turned down a government post out in the Oregon Territory because it would have wrecked his Illinois career"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Lincoln declined appointment as Secretary of the Oregon Territory (not a governorship, as sometimes confused), fearing it would derail his Illinois political career.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Abraham Lincoln"*

**Claim:** "Its two great leaders, Henry Clay and Daniel Webster, had both died in 1852, and the party fell apart in the years after"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Henry Clay died June 29, 1852; Daniel Webster died October 24, 1852. Both deaths in the same year. The Whig Party disintegrated in the years immediately following.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Henry Clay"; Wikipedia, "Daniel Webster"; History.com, "Why the Whig Party Collapsed"*

**Claim (Rail-Splitter):** "At the Illinois Republican convention in Decatur in May 1860, a supporter arranged for Lincoln's cousin to march into the hall carrying two fence rails"
**Verdict:** ⚠️ PARTIALLY CONFIRMED WITH NUANCE. The detail is substantively correct but imprecise on two points: (1) The organizer was Richard J. Oglesby, not an unnamed "supporter," and (2) the person who carried the rails into the hall was John Hanks — who was Lincoln's cousin, confirmed — but some accounts also name Isaac Jennings as carrying them alongside Hanks. The date is confirmed as May 9, 1860, and it was the Illinois State Republican Convention at Decatur. These nuances do not rise to an error in the draft's telling.
*Source: Smithsonian Magazine, "The Legend of Lincoln's Fence Rail"; Wikipedia, "John Hanks"*

**Claim (Rail-Splitter quote):** `"mauled many and many better ones since he had grown to manhood"`
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. This is the standard rendering of Lincoln's response. Confirmed in the New York Tribune (May 22, 1860) and aligns with *Collected Works* vol. 4.
*Source: Baltimore Sun (1995); Smithsonian Magazine; Collected Works vol. 4, University of Michigan*

**Claim (Honest Abe):** "no one can point to the moment it was coined or who coined it, so it is best understood as a reputation he grew into rather than a slogan someone minted for him"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED as appropriately hedged. Correct. No documented first use in available sources. The draft handles this properly.

### BEAT 2: Stephen Douglas

**Claim:** "Stephen Arnold Douglas (Northern Democrat) was a senator from Illinois... barely five feet four"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Douglas's height is consistently given as 5 feet 4 inches across all sources.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Stephen A. Douglas"; U.S. Senate Featured Biography*

**Claim:** "He had been in the Senate since 1847"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. First elected to the Senate by the Illinois legislature in 1846, took his seat in 1847.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Stephen A. Douglas"; U.S. Senate*

**Claim:** "he was the author of the very Kansas-Nebraska Act"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED.

**Claim:** "Douglas came into 1858 wounded inside his own party. He had broken openly with the Democratic president, James Buchanan, over Kansas: Buchanan wanted to admit Kansas as a slave state under a pro-slavery constitution that most Kansas settlers plainly rejected"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. This refers to the Lecompton Constitution dispute, confirmed across sources.

**Claim:** "Douglas was the front-runner... seeking a third Senate term in 1858"
**Verdict:** ⚠️ MINOR NOTE. The fact pack states Douglas was seeking a third Senate term. His terms: first elected 1847, re-elected 1852, seeking re-election in 1858 — that would be his third term. ✅ CONFIRMED.

### BEAT 3: The "House Divided" Speech

**Claim:** "Illinois Republicans nominated Lincoln for Douglas's Senate seat on June 16, 1858, and that same evening Lincoln gave the speech"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. June 16, 1858, at the Republican State Convention, Illinois State Capitol (now Old State Capitol), Springfield.
*Source: Miller Center; Abraham Lincoln Online; Wikipedia, "Lincoln's House Divided Speech"*

**Claim:** "Before about a thousand convention delegates in Springfield"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED WITH APPROPRIATE HEDGE. The draft says "about a thousand," which aligns with the standard estimate of approximately 1,000. The fact pack correctly flags this as imprecise; the draft's hedge is appropriate.

**Claim:** "he reached for a line from the Gospel of Mark"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Mark 3:25 (KJV: "And if a house be divided against itself, that house cannot stand."). Lincoln quoted the biblical phrase, then used it as political metaphor.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln's House Divided Speech"*

**Claim (House Divided verbatim quote):**
> "'A house divided against itself cannot stand.' I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free. I do not expect the Union to be dissolved — I do not expect the house to fall — but I do expect it will cease to be divided. It will become all one thing, or all the other. Either the opponents of slavery will arrest the further spread of it, and place it where the public mind shall rest in the belief that it is in the course of ultimate extinction; or its advocates will push it forward, till it shall become alike lawful in all the States, old as well as new — North as well as South."

**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM. Cross-checked against Abraham Lincoln Online (text sourced from *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln*, Basler ed., vol. 2, pp. 461–69) and the Miller Center. The passage matches exactly.
*Source: Abraham Lincoln Online; Miller Center; Voices of Democracy/UMD*

### BEAT 4: The Seven Debates

**Claim:** "seven times, all over Illinois: Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy, Alton, from August 21 to October 15"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. All seven towns, all dates: Ottawa 8/21; Freeport 8/27; Jonesboro 9/15; Charleston 9/18; Galesburg 10/7; Quincy 10/13; Alton 10/15. Exact match.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"; Britannica*

**Claim:** "Each debate ran about three hours in the open air, with no moderator and no time for sound bites. One man opened for a full hour, the other answered for an hour and a half, and then the first man came back for a half-hour rejoinder."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The 60/90/30 format is confirmed universally.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"*

**Claim:** "As the incumbent, Douglas got to open four of the seven."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Douglas opened four of the seven debates; Lincoln opened three.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"; NPS Lincoln Home*

**Claim (crowds):** "twelve thousand at Ottawa, fifteen thousand at Freeport, somewhere around sixteen to eighteen thousand at Galesburg"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. All three crowd estimates match the standard sourced figures (Ottawa ~12,000; Freeport ~15,000; Galesburg 16,000–18,000).
*Source: Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"*

**Claim:** "The second debate, in Freeport, on August 27"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Freeport was the second debate, August 27, 1858.

**Claim (Freeport Doctrine):** "Slavery, he argued, could not actually survive anywhere unless local laws actively protected it... what he called 'unfriendly legislation.'"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The phrase "unfriendly legislation" is confirmed in primary debate text. Douglas's Freeport Doctrine is accurately summarized.
*Source: NPS Lincoln Home, "Freeport Doctrine"; Wikipedia, "Freeport Doctrine"; Teaching American History (debate transcripts)*

### BEAT 5: Lincoln Loses — Wins National Profile

**Claim:** "the Illinois General Assembly met and re-elected Douglas to the Senate, 54 to 46. ... On January 5, 1859"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The joint session vote was 54–46 for Douglas, on January 5, 1859.
*Source: Illinois State Archives; Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"*

**Claim:** "Republican legislative candidates statewide actually outpolled the Democrats by a few thousand votes. But the legislative districts had been drawn in a way that favored the Democrats"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Republicans received approximately 50.6% of the statewide legislative vote; outpolled Democratic candidates by ~3,402 votes (or 24,094, depending on how counted). Despite this, Democrats won more seats due to district apportionment.
*Source: Illinois State Archives; Wikipedia, "Lincoln–Douglas debates"*

**Claim:** "It sold tens of thousands of copies (the exact figure is uncertain, with estimates running across a wide range)"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED as appropriately hedged. Sales figures vary from ~30,000 to ~50,000 across sources. The draft properly hedges. No error.

**Claim:** "Seventeenth Amendment in 1913"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The 17th Amendment, providing for direct election of senators, was ratified April 8, 1913.

### BEAT 6: Cooper Union

**Claim:** "On February 27, 1860, Lincoln spoke at the Cooper Union... to an audience of about fifteen hundred"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Date and venue confirmed. Crowd size of approximately 1,500 confirmed by NPS Lincoln Home.
*Source: NPS Lincoln Home, "The Cooper Union Address: The Making of a Candidate"*

**Claim:** "he had prepared for months, burying himself in founding-era records back in Springfield to build a single, lawyerly, devastating argument"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Lincoln spent months researching founding-era documents (specifically Jonathan Elliott's *Debates* and similar records) before the speech.

**Claim:** "His tally: a clear majority of the framers had, in their own careers, treated the federal government as having the power to restrict slavery's spread."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The draft wisely keeps this general ("clear majority") rather than asserting the specific 21-of-39 or 36-to-3 counts. The framing is accurate: Lincoln's research found 21 of 23 signers who had later opportunities to vote on the question had supported federal power to restrict slavery; he argued 15 of the remaining 16 would have concurred. The draft's conservative framing avoids the risk of mis-stating the tally's precise logic.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"; NPS Lincoln Home*

**Claim (Cooper Union verbatim closing):**
> "Neither let us be slandered from our duty by false accusations against us, nor frightened from it by menaces of destruction to the Government nor of dungeons to ourselves. LET US HAVE FAITH THAT RIGHT MAKES MIGHT, AND IN THAT FAITH, LET US, TO THE END, DARE TO DO OUR DUTY AS WE UNDERSTAND IT."

**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED VERBATIM. Cross-checked against Abraham Lincoln Online (text from *Collected Works*, Basler ed.) and Voices of Democracy/UMD.
*Source: Abraham Lincoln Online; Voices of Democracy, University of Maryland*

**Claim (Greeley review):** "the editor Horace Greeley, whose powerful *New-York Tribune* reached Republicans across the North, called it one of the happiest and most convincing political arguments ever made in the city and judged that no man had ever made such an impression on a first appearance before a New York audience"
**Verdict:** ⚠️ MINOR INACCURACY IN GREELEY PARAPHRASE. The draft paraphrases Greeley as calling it "one of the happiest and most convincing." The actual *Tribune* text is: **"One of the most happiest and most convincing political arguments ever made in this City."** The draft omits "most" before "happiest" — it reads "one of the happiest" rather than "one of the most happiest." This is a minor paraphrase discrepancy, not a fabrication, and the second clause ("no man ever made such an impression on his first appeal to a New-York audience") is accurate. However, for a fact-check, the paraphrase should reflect the source. SHOULD-FIX (the draft is paraphrasing, not quoting, so this is low severity — but "most happiest" is the actual Greeley phrase).

**Claim:** "Several New York papers printed the speech in full, and others reprinted it in Chicago, Detroit, and Albany."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The *New York Times*, *Evening Post*, and other New York papers printed the full text; Chicago, Detroit, and Albany papers reprinted it.
*Source: NPS Lincoln Home; Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"*

**Claim:** "Lincoln went on a tear, speaking in eleven cities in twelve days"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Confirmed by NPS Lincoln Home and multiple sources about the New England tour following Cooper Union.
*Source: NPS Lincoln Home; New England Historical Society*

**Claim (Holzer):** "The historian Harold Holzer, who wrote the standard book on the speech, calls Cooper Union the event that turned Lincoln from a regional figure into a national phenomenon"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Holzer's 2004 book *Lincoln at Cooper Union: The Speech That Made Abraham Lincoln President* is the standard work; his characterization is confirmed.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech"; University of Michigan/JALA review of Holzer*

**Claim (Brady quote):** "Lincoln is often quoted as crediting 'Brady and the Cooper Institute' with making him president. It's a good line, but it surfaces secondhand and can't be firmly verified, so it's best treated as something he may have said rather than something he certainly did."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED as appropriately handled. Multiple sources confirm this quote is apocryphal — not found in *Collected Works*, no corroborating contemporary evidence. The draft correctly hedges it and marks it [VERIFY]. This is the right treatment.
*Source: mrlincolnandnewyork.org; Wikipedia, "Cooper Union speech" (absence of the quote); research consensus*

### BEAT 7: The 1860 Republican Convention

**Claim:** "They met in Chicago, in a vast temporary wooden hall thrown up specifically for the convention and nicknamed 'the Wigwam,' big enough to pack in something like ten thousand people."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The Wigwam was a temporary wooden structure built for the convention. Capacity is given as approximately 10,000 in the Wikipedia article. (Some sources say up to 12,000 or even 15,000, but 10,000 is confirmed.)
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"; Wikipedia, "Wigwam (Chicago)"*

**Claim:** "The obvious choice was Senator William H. Seward of New York, a former governor"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Seward was a former governor of New York and the front-runner.

**Claim (Seward's phrases):** "he had spoken of a 'higher law' than the Constitution and of an 'irrepressible conflict' over slavery"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The "higher law" speech was delivered in the Senate in 1850; the "irrepressible conflict" speech was delivered in Rochester, October 25, 1858.
*Source: Road to the Civil War; EBSCO Research Starters; Wikipedia*

**Claim:** "There were other serious men in the field too: Governor Salmon Chase of Ohio, the former Missouri congressman and judge Edward Bates, Senator Simon Cameron of Pennsylvania."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. All four names and affiliations confirmed.
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"*

**Claim (ballots):** "On the first ballot Seward led with 173½ votes to Lincoln's 102. On the second, Lincoln surged to a near-tie. On the third, the anti-Seward delegates broke decisively his way, and Lincoln took 350 of the 466 votes"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Exact figures: 1st ballot — Seward 173.5, Lincoln 102; 2nd ballot — Seward 184.5, Lincoln 181 (near-tie); 3rd ballot — Lincoln 350 of 466 votes cast. All confirmed.
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"*

**Claim:** "The convention added Senator Hannibal Hamlin of Maine as his running mate"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Hannibal Hamlin of Maine was nominated for VP.
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"*

**Claim:** Lincoln stayed home in Springfield
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Lincoln did not attend the convention.

**Claim (counterfeit tickets):** "reportedly printed up counterfeit tickets to pack the Wigwam's galleries with roaring Lincoln supporters"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The counterfeit gallery tickets strategy is reported by multiple sources (Wikipedia, American Battlefield Trust).
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Republican National Convention"; American Battlefield Trust, "Inside the Wigwam"*

### BEAT 8: The Four-Way Election

**Claim:** "The Democrats first met in Charleston, South Carolina, in the spring of 1860. ... Fifty-one Southern delegates walked out, whole delegations from the Deep South marching off the floor, and the convention broke up without naming anyone."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Charleston convention April 23 – May 3, 1860. Fifty-one delegates walked out (Alabama, Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina, Texas, plus partial delegations from Arkansas and Delaware). Convention adjourned without a nominee.
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Democratic National Conventions"*

**Claim:** "The party reconvened weeks later in Baltimore, and the Southerners walked out again."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Baltimore reconvened June 18–23, 1860; ~110 Southern delegates walked out again.

**Claim:** "the sitting Vice President of the United States, John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky (Southern Democrat)"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Breckinridge was the incumbent Vice President, from Kentucky.
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 Democratic National Conventions"*

**Claim:** "John Bell of Tennessee, running for the brand-new Constitutional Union Party, a refuge for old Whigs and others who wanted to dodge the slavery question entirely and simply stand for 'the Constitution, the Union, and the laws.'"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Bell's platform language and party affiliation are accurately described.
*Source: Britannica, "United States presidential election of 1860"*

**Claim:** "in ten Southern states the Republican Party hadn't organized at all and Lincoln's name did not appear on the ballot"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Lincoln received no votes in ten Southern states: Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, and Texas.
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 United States presidential election"; Emerging Civil War*

### BEAT 9: The Election Result

**Claim:** "On November 6, 1860, Americans voted in numbers never seen before, better than eight in ten eligible voters turned out, the highest share in the nation's history to that point."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED WITH ONE NUANCE. Turnout was 81.2%, confirmed as the highest in U.S. history to that point. "Better than eight in ten" is accurate (81.2% > 80%). The phrase "to that point" is important — the 1876 election (81.8%) subsequently surpassed it, but the draft correctly limits the claim historically. No error.
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 United States presidential election"; Washington Post/Star Tribune, "1876 election holds record"*

**Claim:** "Lincoln won roughly 1.86 to 1.87 million votes, just under forty percent of the national total."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. UCSB American Presidency Project: 1,867,198 (39.8%). The range "1.86 to 1.87 million" and "just under forty percent" are accurate and appropriately hedged.
*Source: American Presidency Project, UCSB*

**Claim (Douglas popular vote):** "(The exact popular-vote totals from 1860 are genuinely disputed... especially for Douglas, whose share is often put around 1.38 million, or roughly 29 percent, in the most-cited modern tabulation.)"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED as appropriately hedged. UCSB gives Douglas 1,379,434 (29.4%); Wikipedia gives widely divergent figures (~1.004 million / 21.47%). The draft correctly flags the dispute and uses the UCSB figure as the "most-cited modern tabulation." This is accurate. The [VERIFY] flag is the right call.
*Source: American Presidency Project, UCSB; Wikipedia (for discrepancy)*

**Claim:** "Lincoln carried 180 electoral votes, sweeping nearly the entire free North, when he needed only 152 to win."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Lincoln: 180 electoral votes. Majority threshold: 152 of 303. Exact match.
*Source: National Archives, "1860 Electoral College Results"; American Presidency Project, UCSB*

**Claim:** "Breckinridge, the Southern Democrat, took the Deep South."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Breckinridge: 72 electoral votes, from 11 slave states.

**Claim:** "Bell took three border states."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Bell won KY, TN, VA — three border/upper-South states, 39 electoral votes.

**Claim:** "Douglas... winning almost nothing in the electoral count, carrying only Missouri and a sliver of New Jersey"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Douglas won Missouri (9 electoral votes) and 3 of New Jersey's 7 electoral votes, for 12 total. "A sliver of New Jersey" is accurate — he won 3 of 7 NJ electors via fusion ticket.
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 United States presidential election in New Jersey"; National Archives*

**Claim:** "he won without a single electoral vote from any slave state, the first time a winning presidential ticket had ever been built purely on the North, with no Southerner and no Southern support anywhere on it"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Lincoln carried zero slave-state electoral votes. Wikipedia characterizes him as "the first successful major-party presidential candidate who drew no electoral support from the South."
*Source: Wikipedia, "1860 United States presidential election"*

### BEAT 10: Secession Winter

**Claim:** "South Carolina went first. On December 20, 1860, barely six weeks after the election and months before Lincoln could so much as take the oath, a state convention voted, 169 to nothing, to dissolve South Carolina's bond to the United States."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. December 20, 1860. Vote: 169–0, unanimous. Confirmed across multiple sources.
*Source: Wikipedia, "South Carolina Declaration of Secession"; Gilder Lehrman; NPS*

**Claim:** "The declaration the state issued a few days later did not bother to disguise the reason. It named the 'increasing hostility' of the free states to slavery and the election of a president 'whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery.'"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The South Carolina "Declaration of the Immediate Causes" (December 24, 1860) uses this language. The quotes are paraphrases of the document, not direct quotations in scare quotes — this is handled correctly.
*Source: Teaching American History, "Declaration of the Causes of Secession"*

**Claim (secession dates):** "Mississippi went on January 9, 1861, then Florida on the 10th, Alabama on the 11th, Georgia on the 19th, Louisiana on the 26th, and Texas on February 1."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. All six dates exactly match the historical record.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Secession in the United States"; AHA Chronology; NPS*

**Claim (Buchanan):** "Buchanan's response... 'the sword,' he said, had not been placed in the government's hand to hold the Union together by force."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Buchanan's Fourth Annual Message (December 3, 1860) contains this passage: "the sword was not placed in their [Congress's] hand to preserve it by force." The draft paraphrases accurately.
*Source: Miller Center, "December 3, 1860: Fourth Annual Message"; American Presidency Project*

**Claim (Star of the West):** "In early January, Buchanan authorized a single, half-hearted move: he sent an unarmed civilian merchant steamer, the *Star of the West*... to slip supplies to the U.S. soldiers still holding Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor. On January 9, 1861, as it tried to enter the harbor, South Carolina shore batteries opened fire on it."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Date correct (January 9, 1861). The ship was the civilian merchant steamer *Star of the West*. South Carolina batteries on Morris Island fired on it; it was hit and retreated.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Star of the West"; History.com; American Battlefield Trust*

**Claim:** "The ship took a few hits, did an about-face, and steamed back to New York with its supplies undelivered."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The ship was hit (three times, no major damage), turned around, returned to New York. Mission failed.

**Claim (Crittenden):** "In December, Senator John Crittenden of Kentucky proposed a sweeping compromise, a set of constitutional amendments that would, among other things, revive the old line dividing free from slave territory and extend it clear to the Pacific, guaranteeing slavery federal protection south of it, in lands 'now held, or hereafter acquired,' and forever barring Congress from abolishing slavery in the slave states."
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Crittenden was a Constitutional Unionist from Kentucky. He proposed the compromise December 18, 1860. The three key provisions described are accurately stated, including the "now held, or hereafter acquired" language.
*Source: Wikipedia, "Crittenden Compromise"*

**Claim (Lincoln's rejection wording):** "[VERIFY: Lincoln's exact phrasing on the Crittenden Compromise (e.g. 'Entertain no proposition for a compromise in regard to the extension of slavery') — fact pack flags wording for confirmation against Collected Works vol. 4.]"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED — QUOTE IS REAL AND ACCURATELY ATTRIBUTED. The phrase "Entertain no proposition for a compromise in regard to the extension of slavery" is confirmed in *Collected Works*, vol. 4 — it appears in Lincoln's letter to Congressman William Kellogg, December 11, 1860. The draft correctly notes the [VERIFY] flag; the quote is real. The draft's protective hedge ("The substance of his position is well documented, though the exact wording of his private instructions should be confirmed against his collected papers before being quoted directly") is appropriately cautious, but the quote IS in Collected Works. The fact-checker clears it.
*Source: Collected Works, vol. 4 (quod.lib.umich.edu); History.com; Letter to William Kellogg, December 11, 1860*

**Claim (Crittenden vote):** "the Crittenden Compromise was voted down in the Senate on January 16, 1861, 25 against to 23 in favor, every one of the 25 'no' votes a Republican"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. The vote on January 16, 1861 was 25–23, with all 25 nay votes cast by Republicans. Six senators from seceding states abstained.
*Source: History.com, "Crittenden Compromise is killed in Senate"; U.S. Senate history*

**Claim:** "Lincoln had been elected in November, but under the rules then in force he would not be inaugurated until March 4, 1861"
**Verdict:** ✅ CONFIRMED. Lincoln inaugurated March 4, 1861.
*Source: NPS; White House Historical Association; History.com*

### SF-1: Greeley paraphrase — "happiest" vs. "most happiest"

**Draft phrase:** "called it one of the happiest and most convincing political arguments ever made in the city"

**Actual Greeley text (*New-York Tribune*, February 28, 1860):** "One of the most happiest and most convincing political arguments ever made in this City."

**Issue:** The draft drops "most" before "happiest," rendering the paraphrase slightly off. "Most happiest" (grammatically awkward but Greeley's own phrasing) is the actual text. The draft's paraphrase is close but not exact. Since the draft is paraphrasing rather than quoting Greeley in quotation marks, this is low severity — but the record should reflect Greeley's actual words.

**Recommended fix:** Change to "called it 'one of the most happiest and most convincing political arguments ever made in this City'" (quoted), or revise the paraphrase to "called it among the happiest and most convincing political arguments ever made in the city."

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX (low; paraphrase, not a factual error in substance).
