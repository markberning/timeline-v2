# Second Battle of Petersburg — NEWCOMER / CLARITY gate

> War pipeline critic gate (clarity). Cold read of `second-petersburg-draft.md` as a
> zero-knowledge but sharp 15-year-old: no prior Civil War knowledge, no military
> vocabulary, but pays attention and will notice when something is named without being
> explained. CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST per term or passage; MUST-FIX (a real reader
> would stall or misread) vs SHOULD-FIX (a bump, not a wall).

## Verdict: PASS (with 3 MUST-FIX)

The draft is unusually good about inline-defining its hard terms — *entrenchments*,
*steal a march*, *pontoon bridge*, *battery*, *corps*, *redan*, *enfilade* are all
glossed on first use, in-line, in plain language. That is most of the clarity battle
won. The remaining MUST-FIX items are a handful of terms that get *used* before (or
without) their gloss, and one structural leap (the corps/army/division hierarchy) that
a newcomer can't reconstruct from the pieces given. The named-actor side-tagging is
consistent and helpful.

## Structural / leap audit

- **The corps → division → brigade → regiment hierarchy is never assembled.**
  The draft glosses *corps* ("several divisions") and uses *division*, *brigade*,
  *regiment* throughout, but a newcomer can't place them relative to each other. By §4
  the reader is tracking "Potter's IX Corps brigades," "Ledlie's division," "Hartranft's
  brigade," "the 1st Maine Heavy Artillery (regiment)" with no scale anchor. They'll feel
  the units are different *sizes* but won't know the nesting. **MUST-FIX 2** below proposes
  a single sentence at the first cluster that establishes the ladder (army > corps >
  division > brigade > regiment) — after which every later unit name is self-locating.

- **"Stealing a march" / the head-start clock — CLEAR.** The single most important leap
  (why a maneuver, not a battle, decides this) is taught explicitly and the "two days Lee
  didn't know where Grant was" clock is restated at the end of §1 as the question the whole
  battle answers. A newcomer can follow this. Excellent.

- **Why the city was "open" — CLEAR.** The thin-defenders math (2,200 men, ten feet apart,
  "a bluff") is set up in §1's MEANWHILE and paid off in §3. The reader understands *why*
  one hard push would have won. This is the emotional engine and it's legible.

- **The June 18 paragraph (§4) is the one genuinely overwhelming passage.** In a single
  stretch the reader meets Birney, Kershaw, Field, Willcox, Warren, Chamberlain, plus the
  II/XVIII/IX/V Corps, the Dimmock works, Taylor's Branch, and Rives's Salient. Even with
  side-tags, that's a lot of new proper nouns at once. A newcomer won't be *lost* (the
  through-line — "they attacked, the line held, they were slaughtered" — survives) but they
  will skim. This overlaps the storytelling gate's §4 overload finding; flagged here as a
  comprehension cost, not just a pacing one. SHOULD-FIX (de-densify), tracked primarily on
  the storytelling side.

- **Side-tags — CLEAR and consistent.** Every named person carries (North)/(South) on
  first mention per section and tags reset per section, exactly as specified. A newcomer
  always knows which side a name is on. No untagged combatant found. This is done well.

- **Named actors are scene-set, not just named.** Smith ("commanding the XVIII Corps"),
  Hinks ("commanded the United States Colored Troops"), Beauregard ("the job of defending
  Petersburg fell to"), Hancock ("came up that evening — fresh troops"), Lee, Grant all get
  a role on entry. Chamberlain gets "the hero of Little Round Top" — which is a *reference
  a newcomer won't have* (Little Round Top is Gettysburg, never explained here). It reads
  as "this man was already famous," which mostly works, but see SHOULD-FIX.

## MUST-FIX (a real newcomer stalls or misreads)

1. **Cold Harbor's §4 callback needs to stand on its own.** §1 introduces Cold Harbor as
   the trauma that changed Grant's strategy; §4 leans on it again as the *proof* that
   attacking entrenchments is suicide ("exactly the situation Cold Harbor had proved was a
   slaughter to attack"). 80+ lines apart, a zero-knowledge reader may not carry it. Add a
   three-word reminder at the §4 use — e.g., "exactly the situation Cold Harbor — the
   massacre that started all this — had proved was a slaughter." One clause, no new fact.

2. **Establish the unit ladder once, at the first cluster (§2).** When *corps* is glossed,
   extend it by one sentence to nest the smaller units the reader will meet for the next
   three sections: a corps is several *divisions*, a division several *brigades*, a brigade
   several *regiments* (a regiment ≈ several hundred to ~1,000 men). After this, every later
   "Ledlie's division" / "Hartranft's brigade" / "the 1st Maine… regiment" is self-locating.
   Without it the reader feels the units differ in size but can't order them. (This also
   makes the 1st Maine's "632 men in minutes" hit harder — the reader will know a regiment
   is roughly that size, i.e. it was *annihilated*.)

3. **Gloss *salient* at Rives's Salient (§4).** The term carries real weight (stops Warren's
   corps, nearly kills Chamberlain) but the reader is only told *where* it is, not *what* it
   is. Add the concept inline: a salient is an outward bulge in the line, which is exactly
   why it could pour fire onto attackers from the front *and* both sides at once. One clause;
   it also retroactively explains why this single strongpoint was so deadly.

## SHOULD-FIX (a bump, not a wall)

- **breastworks** — used twice undefined (§3, §4). Add a three-word gloss on first use
  ("breastworks — chest-high dirt walls thrown up fast"). Recoverable from context, but
  cheap to fix.
- **USCT abbreviation** — defined in full on first use, then the bare initials "USCT" run
  through §2–§4. A 15-year-old will hold it (it's introduced clearly), but the first
  switch to bare "USCT" could carry a "(United States Colored Troops)" reminder, or the
  draft could lean on "the freedmen"/"Hinks's men" which it already does well.
- **siege** — first use could carry a half-clause gloss ("a siege — surround the city and
  starve it out rather than storm it"); the meaning is recoverable from the §4 ending but a
  reader would prefer it up front, since the word is in the section title.
- **"the hero of Little Round Top"** (Chamberlain) — references a battle (Gettysburg) the
  newcomer doesn't know. It mostly reads as "already famous," which is fine, but consider
  "already famous from Gettysburg" so the reference isn't a dead end. Minor.
- **June 18 proper-noun pileup (§4)** — comprehension cost from density; primary fix tracked
  on the storytelling gate (de-crowd the run-up). Flagged here for the record.
