# FACT PACK — Fall of Fort Ticonderoga (July 1777)

*American Revolution vertical. ONLY source for the author. Burgoyne's 1777 campaign forces the evacuation of the most famous fort in America — almost without a shot at the fort itself; the real fighting is the rearguard action at Hubbardton. This is the fort's THIRD famous moment; keep the three cleanly distinct:*

- **1758** — Battle of Carillon: Abercromby's huge British army wrecked against Montcalm's abatis. FULL PAGE EXISTS at `/war-french-indian/battles/carillon` (pill target).
- **1775** — May 10: Ethan Allen + Benedict Arnold seize the fort from a tiny British caretaker garrison; Knox hauls its guns to Boston that winter. Told in the rev outbreak chapter — terse cross-ref, do not retell.
- **1777 (THIS PAGE)** — July 2-6: Burgoyne's invasion army envelops the fort, hauls guns up the undefended Mount Defiance, and St. Clair evacuates by night July 5-6; rearguard battle at Hubbardton July 7; flotilla destroyed at Skenesborough July 6; rearguard fight at Fort Anne July 8. A British "victory" that ends up saving the American army for Saratoga.

Sections follow the fi-carillon-factpack template. Confidence tags: [HIGH] / [MED] / [DISPUTED]; HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags inline.

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- Episode dates: **July 2–8, 1777** [HIGH]. Investment + skirmishing at the fort July 2–5; night evacuation July 5–6; British occupy fort + Mount Independence without a fight July 6; naval chase + destruction of the American flotilla at Skenesborough July 6; rearguard battle at **Hubbardton July 7** (the only pitched battle of the episode); rearguard fight at **Fort Anne July 8**. "Siege of Fort Ticonderoga (1777)" is the standard label, but there was **no formal siege and no battle at the fort itself** [HIGH] — frame it as a fall/evacuation, not a siege.
- Place: Fort Ticonderoga, present-day Ticonderoga, NY (~43.84, -73.39), on the neck between Lake George (south) and Lake Champlain (north), with the La Chute River/portage between them — the same ground as 1758 and 1775. American works spanned the lake to **Mount Independence** on the Vermont shore, linked by a floating bridge [HIGH].
- Significance: the opening blow of Burgoyne's 1777 invasion. The "Gibraltar of the North" — the most famous fortress in America, freighted with the 1758 and 1775 legends — fell in four days **essentially because nobody had fortified the hill that overlooked it** (Mount Defiance / Sugar Loaf — the same hill, then Rattlesnake Hill, that Abercromby's engineer urged him to use in 1758) [HIGH]. A political earthquake for the Americans and a propaganda triumph for Britain — and yet St. Clair's decision to run saved ~2,500–3,000 Continentals who became part of the army that trapped Burgoyne at Saratoga three months later [HIGH].
- Result line: **British victory** (fort taken intact, flotilla destroyed, garrison scattered) — but the evacuation preserved the army, and the "victory" stretched Burgoyne's supply line toward the disaster at Saratoga. Both halves belong in the winner line.

## 2. BACKGROUND
- **Burgoyne's plan (the Hudson corridor strategy)** [HIGH]: approved in London for 1777 — Burgoyne leads ~8,000 from Canada up (southward along) the Lake Champlain corridor toward Albany; a smaller diversionary force under Barry St. Leger comes east down the Mohawk Valley; the intent is to control the Champlain–Hudson line and split New England from the rest of the colonies.
- **The "Howe was supposed to meet him" question** [DISPUTED — HANDLE WITH CARE]: the popular version says Gen. William Howe was ordered to march north from New York City to meet Burgoyne at Albany and the orders went astray. The evidence is messier: Howe had told Germain (Nov 1776–Apr 1777 correspondence) that he intended to campaign against Philadelphia, and Germain/the King approved, while also expecting Howe could cooperate on the Hudson; no positive order compelled Howe north, and Burgoyne knew before leaving Canada that Howe's main army was likely headed to Pennsylvania. Safest phrasing: the plan ASSUMED cooperation on the Hudson that London never explicitly ordered and Howe never intended to give; "the lost order" single-villain version is myth-adjacent. (See §10.)
- **The fort's reputation vs. condition** [HIGH]: politically the fort was untouchable — "the Gibraltar of the North" — but the works were decaying, sprawling (old French lines + new redoubts + Mount Independence across the lake), and needed ~10,000 men to man properly. St. Clair had under 2,500 effectives (a June 20 council of war counted "under 2,500 effectives, rank and file"; ~3,000 total with militia arrivals) [HIGH].
- **Why the garrison was so thin** [HIGH]: Washington and Congress expected the main 1777 British blow to come from New York City northward, not from Canada; the Northern Department (Schuyler) was starved accordingly. Schuyler commanded the department from Albany; St. Clair had taken command at the fort only ~3 weeks before the British appeared [HIGH].
- **The 1758 echo** [HIGH — verified, see §4]: in 1758 Abercromby's engineer Lt. Matthew Clerk urged hauling guns up Rattlesnake Hill (the later Mount Defiance) to command the French line; Abercromby ignored it. In 1776–77 the Americans repeated the neglect from the other side: John Trumbull (then Gates's deputy adjutant) argued the hill commanded both forts and climbed it with Anthony Wayne and a convalescent Benedict Arnold, concluding guns "could probably be dragged up"; the warning was dismissed for lack of men. Burgoyne's engineers proved both of them right in two days.
- **1775 in one beat** [HIGH]: Ethan Allen and Benedict Arnold took the fort by surprise on May 10, 1775, from a ~45-man British caretaker garrison; Knox sledged its cannon to Boston that winter. Terse cross-ref only — the outbreak chapter tells it.

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
### British / German (~7,800–8,000 regulars; ~10,000+ with auxiliaries and followers)
- **Lt. Gen. John Burgoyne** — army commander. Strength returns: ~3,981 British regulars + ~3,116 Brunswick/Hesse-Hanau Germans (~7,100 regulars) [HIGH]; with artillerymen, ~250 Canadians/loyalists, and Native allies the fighting force is commonly given as **~7,800–8,000** [HIGH]; 10,000+ counting camp followers (incl. Baroness Riedesel and her three small daughters) [MED]. British regiments included the 9th, 20th, 21st, 24th, 47th, 53rd, 62nd Foot [HIGH].
- **Maj. Gen. William Phillips** — artillery commander, Burgoyne's senior subordinate; drove the Mount Defiance gun placement. The famous "where a goat can go..." line is attributed to him (see §8 — attribution traditional, not documented) [MED].
- **Brig. Gen. Simon Fraser** — commanded the Advanced Corps (~2,000: grenadier + light infantry battalions, 24th Foot, marksmen), the army's spearhead on the west shore; led the pursuit to Hubbardton [HIGH].
- **Maj. Gen. Friedrich Adolph, Baron Riedesel** — commanded the German division (Brunswick + Hesse-Hanau, ~3,100), advancing on the east (Vermont) shore toward Mount Independence; marched to Fraser's support at Hubbardton [HIGH].
- **Native allies**: ~400–800 warriors (commonly "about 500" at the outset, reinforced mid-July) scouting and screening [MED — ranges vary by source]. Their fires on Sugar Loaf the night of July 4 helped tip the Americans off [MED]. NOTE BOUNDARY: the Jane McCrea killing (July 27, near Fort Edward) and its propaganda storm belong to the SARATOGA telling, not this page — at most one forward-pointing clause.
### American (~2,500 effectives; ~3,000–3,500 souls in the works)
- **Maj. Gen. Arthur St. Clair** — garrison commander, in post only ~3 weeks (arrived June 12) [HIGH]. Return of effectives: **2,089 rank and file fit + 124 unarmed artificers + ~900 short-term militia** [HIGH — St. Clair's own figures]; the June 20 council of war counted "under 2,500 effectives, rank and file" against works needing ~10,000 [HIGH].
- **Maj. Gen. Philip Schuyler** — Northern Department commander at Albany (not present at the fort); held a small reserve (~700) downstate; shared the political blame [HIGH].
- Brigade commanders at the post: Brig. Gens. **Matthias Alexis de Roche Fermoy** (Mount Independence — the man whose burning house lit up the evacuation), **John Paterson**, **Enoch Poor**; Col. **Pierse Long** (led the water column to Skenesborough, fought Fort Anne) [HIGH].
- Rearguard at Hubbardton: Col. **Seth Warner** (Green Mountain Boys / Warner's Continental Regiment), Col. **Ebenezer Francis** (11th Massachusetts), Col. **Nathan Hale** (2nd New Hampshire — NOT the executed spy; flag for the author) [HIGH].

## 4. THE BATTLE (the four days, day by day)
- **Setup** [HIGH]: Burgoyne's army staged at Crown Point (~10 mi north) by June 30 and came on in two wings: Fraser's Advanced Corps + the British division (with Phillips) on the WEST shore against the old fort and the French lines; Riedesel's Germans on the EAST shore toward Mount Independence, aiming to cut the Hubbardton road. St. Clair, short of men, had already conceded the outer ground mentally; the works were the old fort, the 1758 French lines, the Mount Hope outpost (covering the La Chute/Lake George portage and the water supply), the star fort atop Mount Independence, and the quarter-mile floating bridge (with a log-and-chain boom) linking the two shores [HIGH].
- **July 2** [HIGH]: St. Clair pulled in the Mount Hope garrison at dawn (it was nearly cut off; the withdrawing troops burned the works); Fraser's advance occupied the height. Afternoon skirmish on the west lines — an American picket line fired on British/Native skirmishers; James Wilkinson's premature-fire anecdote attaches here [MED]. A drunk British soldier left behind was pumped for intelligence [MED].
- **July 3–4** [HIGH]: the lines drew "harassingly close"; British engineers (Lt. William Twiss) reconnoitered **Sugar Loaf / Mount Defiance** — the ~850-ft hill across the La Chute mouth, ~1,400 yards from the fort, ~1,500 from Mount Independence, commanding BOTH — and reported it could take guns. Fraser + Phillips put ~400 men and most of the army's draft cattle to cutting a road up the back slope; two 12-pounders were hauled to the summit by midday July 5 [HIGH — gun count "two twelve-pounders" per British accounts; some popular accounts inflate; use "a battery of heavy guns, the first two 12-pounders"]. The work was meant to stay hidden until the battery could fire.
- **July 5 (discovery + council)** [HIGH]: the Americans spotted activity on the summit (Native campfires the night of July 4 and then visible movement/scarlet on the rock). St. Clair convened his four brigade commanders ~noon; unanimous: "it is impossible with our force to defend Ticonderoga and Mount Independence" and "a retreat ought to be undertaken as soon as possible." The guns on Defiance had not yet fired — the THREAT, plus Riedesel closing on the only land escape road, decided it [HIGH]. St. Clair's reported reflection — that by holding he could "save his character and lose the army," by retreating "save the army and lose his character" — see §8 [MED].
- **The night evacuation, July 5–6** [HIGH]: after dark, everything that could float — 200+ bateaux and the small armed vessels — was loaded with the sick, stores, and what guns could be moved, and sent up (south on) the lake to **Skenesborough** under Col. Pierse Long. The main body crossed the floating bridge to Mount Independence and took the military road southeast toward **Hubbardton** and Castleton in the Grants (Vermont). Left behind: the heaviest cannon (dozens of pieces), flour, meat, tents, ammunition — a windfall for Burgoyne [HIGH]. The retreat was supposed to be silent; **Gen. Fermoy, against orders, set fire to his quarters on Mount Independence about 3 a.m.**, lighting the bay and the columns [HIGH that he burned it against orders; whether the blaze actually betrayed the retreat is doubted — British accounts credit deserters with the news, and dawn was an hour off; tag the "betrayal" framing MED/DISPUTED]. A four-man rearguard left at the bridge with a loaded cannon was found drunk (or asleep) by the British in the morning [MED — colorful, widely repeated from British accounts; HANDLE WITH CARE].
- **July 6 — occupation + Skenesborough** [HIGH]: Fraser's men were across the bridge by early morning; British colors went up over both works without a shot fired AT the fort. Burgoyne's gunboats broke the "impassable" boom and bridge in about half an hour and ran the fleet south; by late afternoon they caught the American flotilla at Skenesborough — galleys taken or blown up, stores burned, Long's men escaping overland to Fort Anne. The main American column made Castleton (~30 mi) by evening.
- **July 7 — HUBBARDTON (the only real battle)** [HIGH]: St. Clair's rearguard — Warner, Francis, Hale, ~1,000–1,200 men plus hundreds of stragglers/sick (figures to ~2,000 in some counts) — halted at Hubbardton against orders to march on to Castleton. Fraser, pursuing with ~750–850 (grenadiers, light infantry, 24th Foot, loyalist + Native scouts; ~1,030 counting Riedesel's arriving column), attacked at dawn. Hale's encampment by Sucker Brook was overrun first; Francis's and Warner's line on Monument Hill fought Fraser to a standstill for ~45 min–2 hrs, wounding/killing ~1 in 5 of his force, and was bending his left back when **Riedesel's Brunswickers came up the Castleton road — jägers and grenadiers sent in singing a hymn to a military band, on Riedesel's order, to seem more numerous** [HIGH that the Germans advanced singing with music; the "to appear more numerous" motive is from German accounts — keep] — and broke the American right. **Col. Ebenezer Francis was killed by a volley**; Warner ordered the survivors to scatter and "meet me at Manchester" [MED — traditional wording, see §8]; Hale and ~230–270 (with stragglers ~300) were captured. It was the only Revolutionary War battle fought on Vermont soil (Bennington was fought just over the NY line) [HIGH].
- **July 8 — FORT ANNE** [HIGH]: Long's ~600 (joined by ~400 NY militia under Henry van Rensselaer) turned on the pursuing British 9th Foot under Lt. Col. John Hill (~190–200) at Fort Anne, nearly enveloped it in a 2-hour firefight up Battle Hill, and fell back only when ammunition ran out and a "war whoop" — actually one British officer, Capt. John Money, whooping ahead of his stalled Native scouts — convinced them reinforcements had arrived [HIGH — the Money ruse is well documented]. The Americans burned Fort Anne and withdrew to Fort Edward.
- **What Burgoyne chose next** [HIGH]: instead of backtracking to the Lake George water route, Burgoyne pushed his main army overland from Skenesborough toward Fort Edward — 23 miles of creek, ravine, and forest that Schuyler's axemen turned into an obstacle course (trees felled, bridges broken, Wood Creek choked). It took ~3 weeks and helped doom the campaign; only his guns went via Lake George [HIGH].

## 5. CASUALTIES (mostly Hubbardton's — give as ranges)
- **At the fort itself (July 2–6)**: trivial — American ~7 killed, ~11 wounded in the skirmishing; British ~5 killed (never formally tallied) [MED — small-number precision is false; say "a handful on each side"].
- **HUBBARDTON, July 7** [HIGH as ranges]: American ~41 killed (some counts to 150 by folding in later deaths/stragglers), ~96 wounded, ~230–270 captured (with sick stragglers, prisoner hauls reported to ~300) — roughly 40% of the rearguard. British/German ~50–60 killed, ~140–170 wounded (official: 39 British + 1 Canadian killed, 127 British + 2 Canadians wounded; Germans 10 killed, 14 wounded) — over 20% of Fraser's engaged force. Use Ketchum's low American figures as the floor.
- **FORT ANNE, July 8** [MED]: British 9th Foot ~13 killed, ~22 wounded, 3 captured; American losses uncertain (sources spread 50–200 total incl. captured) — flag the spread.
- **Materiel** [HIGH]: the real American loss — scores of cannon (commonly "more than 70 pieces" / "upwards of 100" in period claims; use "dozens of guns, perhaps 70+"), the Lake Champlain flotilla, and mountains of provisions. Flag period inflation of the gun count [DISPUTED on exact number].

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **The political earthquake** [HIGH]: Congress was stunned; rumor said St. Clair and Schuyler had been bribed (one absurd story: paid in silver balls fired into the fort). John Adams (to Abigail, Aug 19, 1777): "I think We shall never defend a Post, untill We shoot a General." [HIGH — byte-verified; see §8]. Washington called it "an event of chagrin and surprise, not apprehended, nor within the compass of my reasoning" [HIGH — letter to Schuyler, July 15, 1777].
- **Heads rolled upward** [HIGH]: Congress recalled both Schuyler and St. Clair from the Northern Department; Horatio Gates replaced Schuyler (Aug 1777) — which mattered enormously at Saratoga's politics. **Both men demanded courts-martial and both were acquitted**: St. Clair tried Aug–Sept 1778, acquitted "with the highest honor" of all charges [HIGH]; Schuyler acquitted (Oct 1778) of neglect of duty [HIGH]. Congress confirmed both verdicts; neither man got a major field command again [HIGH for St. Clair; Schuyler resigned his commission 1779].
- **London's celebration** [HIGH that there was one; the SCENE is anecdote]: the news reached England as proof the rebellion was collapsing. The famous story — George III bursting into the Queen's chamber crying **"I have beat them! beat all the Americans!"** — is a single-thread anecdote of uncertain origin (commonly tied to Walpole-era gossip; no contemporary document) [DISPUTED — usable ONLY as "the story goes," never as fact; see §8].
- **The trap disguised as a triumph** [HIGH]: Burgoyne now owned the Gibraltar of the North — and had to garrison it (~900+ men, mostly the Prinz Friedrich regiment + the 62nd, subtracted from his field army). The garrison the Americans "lost" reassembled at Fort Edward; Long's and Warner's survivors fought at Bennington and Saratoga. Every mile south stretched a supply line that ran back over Champlain to Canada. In September an American raid (Brown's raid) even retook the Defiance/portage outworks briefly and freed prisoners. After Saratoga (Oct 17), the British burned and abandoned Ticonderoga in November 1777 [HIGH].
- **The fort's story ends** [HIGH]: it never mattered militarily again — a ruin by the 1780s, quarried by locals; restored as a museum from 1909. Good closing-beat material.

## 7. COMMANDER BIO MATERIAL (60–90 words each; portraits verified live against the Commons API 2026-06-11)
### American
- **Arthur St. Clair (1737–1818)** [HIGH]: Scottish-born (Thurso), he fought FOR Britain in the French and Indian War — at Louisbourg and Quebec — then settled in western Pennsylvania and became one of its biggest landholders. A Continental major general from February 1777, he had commanded Ticonderoga barely three weeks when Burgoyne arrived. The retreat made him a national villain; a court-martial acquitted him "with the highest honor" (1778). Later: president of the Confederation Congress, first governor of the Northwest Territory — and commander at St. Clair's Defeat (1791), the worst rout Native forces ever inflicted on a US army. Died poor, 1818.
  - Portrait: **File:Arthur St. Clair MET ap32.110.jpg** (Charles Willson Peale miniature, 1780, Met, CC0) — VERIFIED. Alt: **File:ArthurStClairOfficialPortrait-restored.jpg** (Peale, 1782–84, PD) — VERIFIED.
- **Seth Warner (1743–1784)** [HIGH]: Connecticut-born leader of the Green Mountain Boys in the Vermont land wars against New York. He took Crown Point two days after Ethan Allen took Ticonderoga (May 1775), fought in the Canada invasion, and was elected colonel of his own Continental regiment. At Hubbardton he chose the ground and ran the fight; a month later his regiment helped win Bennington. Health broken by the war, he died at 41.
  - Portrait: **NONE — no authentic life portrait of Warner is known** [HIGH]; the Bennington statue is a 20th-c. invention. Statue photo if needed: **File:Seth Warner statue at Bennington Vermont.JPG** (PD, photographer-released) — VERIFIED.
- **Philip Schuyler (1733–1804), context figure** [HIGH]: Albany's Dutch-patrician major general, commander of the Northern Department — the man whose axemen made Burgoyne's road south a nightmare, and the man Congress sacked anyway (replaced by Horatio Gates, August 1777). Court-martialed at his own demand; acquitted 1778; resigned 1779. Later US senator; Alexander Hamilton's father-in-law.
  - Portrait: **File:Philip Schuyler by John Trumbull.jpg** (Trumbull, 1792, PD) — VERIFIED.
### British / German
- **John Burgoyne (1722–1792)** [HIGH]: "Gentleman Johnny" — cavalryman, playwright, gambler, MP, celebrated for dashing raids in Portugal (1762). He sold London the Canada invasion plan and got command of it over Carleton. Ticonderoga was his career's high-water mark: parliament cheered, the King was (reportedly) ecstatic. Three months later he surrendered the same army at Saratoga. He spent his last years writing successful comedies for the London stage.
  - Portrait: **File:General John Burgoyne - Reynolds c. 1766.jpg** (Joshua Reynolds, c. 1766, Frick Collection, PD) — VERIFIED.
- **Simon Fraser (1729–1777)** [HIGH]: a Scottish professional from Balnain who had been INSIDE this story before — he served at Louisbourg and Quebec in the French and Indian War. As brigadier he led Burgoyne's Advanced Corps, the army's cutting edge: first onto Mount Hope, the driving force (with Phillips) behind the Mount Defiance road, and commander of the dawn attack at Hubbardton. Mortally wounded at Bemis Heights that October (the sharpshooter-Timothy-Murphy attribution is legend, not record [DISPUTED]), buried at dusk in the Great Redoubt.
  - Portrait: **NO certain life portrait.** The Government Art Collection canvas is catalogued only as "possibly" Fraser — **File:British School - General Simon (possibly General Simon Fraser of Balnain, 1729–1777) - 17967 - Government Art Collection.jpg** (anon., 1790, PD) — VERIFIED but flag "possibly" in any credit. Scene alt: **File:John Graham (1755-1817) (after) - The Burial of General Simon Fraser (1729–1777), after the Battle of Saratoga, 1777 - NAM. 1960-12-200 - National Army Museum.jpg** (c. 1800, PD) — VERIFIED (belongs to the Saratoga beat; OK as a forward-pointer image).
- **Friedrich Adolph, Baron Riedesel (1738–1800)** [HIGH]: the Brunswick general commanding the ~3,000 German troops the Duke of Brunswick rented to George III. A Seven Years' War veteran, he led the eastern pincer against Mount Independence and force-marched to save Fraser at Hubbardton — sending his grenadiers in singing, with the band playing, to sound like an army. His wife Friederike followed the campaign with their three small daughters; her journal is one of its best sources. Captured with Burgoyne at Saratoga.

## 7b. BRUNSWICKERS & NATIVE ALLIES — EVIDENCE LEVEL
- **The Germans** [HIGH]: ~3,116 of Burgoyne's regulars were German auxiliaries — mostly from Brunswick (plus a Hesse-Hanau contingent), under a subsidy treaty with the Duke of Brunswick; NOT "Hessians" strictly, though colonists used the word for all of them (author may note this). Riedesel commanded; units included the Prinz Friedrich regiment (later left as part of the Ticonderoga garrison) and Breymann's grenadiers. Their hymn-singing advance at Hubbardton is in the German accounts [HIGH].
- **Native allies** [MED on numbers]: roughly 400–500 warriors marched with Burgoyne from Canada at the outset (figures to ~800 appear; use "several hundred"), scouting, screening, and terrifying the countryside — exactly as Burgoyne's bombastic June proclamation intended. Their campfires on Sugar Loaf the night of July 4 helped reveal the British battery [MED]. **BOUNDARY: the Jane McCrea killing (July 27, near Fort Edward) and the recruitment backlash it fueled belong to the SARATOGA page** — one forward-pointing clause at most here.

## 8. VERIFIED QUOTES (byte-exact where marked; chains given)
1. **John Adams to Abigail Adams, Aug 19, 1777** [HIGH — byte-verified against the Adams Papers / Mass. Historical Society digital edition]: "I think We shall never defend a Post, untill We shoot a General." — note the period spelling "untill," and note the common misquote "never be able to defend a post." Quote exactly or paraphrase; don't quote the misquote.
2. **Washington to Schuyler, July 15, 1777** [HIGH — standard text per Washington Papers Rev. War Series vol. 10; secondary confirmations JAR + Mount Vernon]: the evacuation was "an event of chagrin and surprise not apprehended, nor within the compass of my reasoning." Punctuation varies slightly between printings; safest as a partial quote.
3. **Council of war, July 5, 1777** [HIGH — quoted in St. Clair court-martial record via JAR]: "it is impossible with our force to defend Ticonderoga and Mount Independence" and "a retreat ought to be undertaken as soon as possible."
4. **St. Clair to Congress (report), July 14, 1777** [MED — as quoted in secondary literature]: "I have made good a retreat from under the nose of an army at least four times their numbers."
5. **St. Clair's character-vs-army reflection** [MED — HANDLE WITH CARE]: the famous antithesis — that holding the post would "save his character and lose the army," retreating would save the army and lose his character — comes from his own later defense and is paraphrased variously. Use as reported speech ("he said afterward that..."), not as a verbatim line spoken on July 5.
6. **William Phillips on Mount Defiance** [DISPUTED — attribution traditional, no contemporary document]: "Where a goat can go, a man can go; and where a man can go, he can drag a gun." Even Fort Ticonderoga's own historians present it as "attributed." Usable ONLY with "is supposed to have said" framing — never as documented speech.
7. **George III** [DISPUTED — anecdote of uncertain origin, no contemporary source]: the story that the King burst into the Queen's chamber crying "I have beat them! beat all the Americans!" Variants abound ("I have beat them! I have beat all the Americans!"). Tell it as the legend it is ("the story London told afterward...") or skip; NEVER as fact.
8. **Hessian/Brunswick soldier Johannes Schwalm** [MED — via Revolutionary War Journal]: "If the enemy had made a truly determined effort to defend the post, we could not have taken it..." Good German-side counterweight if kept as a clipped partial quote.
9. **Burgoyne's proclamation (late June 1777)** [MED on byte-exactness; the proclamation itself HIGH]: his threat that he had "but to give stretch to the Indian forces under my direction, and they amount to thousands" — widely reprinted and mocked in 1777 (the "thousands" were a few hundred). Verify exact text against a printed facsimile before quoting more than this clause.
10. **Anthony Wayne on Ticonderoga (1776–77 winter)** [UNVERIFIED — DO NOT USE AS QUOTE]: the famous "the last part of the world that God made &... finished in the dark" line is everywhere in popular histories but could not be confirmed against Wayne's papers (his verified Ticonderoga letters — e.g., to Franklin, July 31, 1776; "For God's sake, rouse your field and other officers from their lethargy... There is not one moment to spare," March 1777 [MED]) — if the darkness line is wanted, it must be "Wayne is supposed to have grumbled..." or cut.
11. **Seth Warner at Hubbardton** [DISPUTED]: the scatter order "meet me at Manchester" is traditional wording from later Vermont accounts, not a contemporary record. Reported-speech framing only.

## 11. DOSSIER STATS (ranges for the stat block)
- **Date/duration**: July 2–8, 1777 (evacuation night July 5–6; Hubbardton July 7; Fort Anne July 8) — a one-week episode.
- **Forces**: British/German ~7,800–8,000 (plus several hundred Native allies and auxiliaries) vs. American ~2,500 effectives (~3,000–3,500 total with militia/artificers).
- **Casualties**: at the fort, a handful; the bill came at Hubbardton — American ~320–370 (≈40 killed / ≈100 wounded / ≈230–300 captured) and Fort Anne (uncertain, tens); British/German ~200–230 at Hubbardton (~50–60 killed) + ~35–40 at Fort Anne. Materiel: dozens of cannon (period claims 70+), the flotilla, and the depot.
- **Winner line**: British victory — Burgoyne took the Gibraltar of the North in four days without storming it; but the army he failed to trap re-formed down the road, and the fort's price was a garrison detached and a supply line stretched toward Saratoga.

## 12. KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES (top HANDLE-WITH-CARE list)
1. **The Howe/Germain cooperation question** — present as assumed-but-never-ordered cooperation; do NOT tell the "pigeonholed order" / lost-dispatch myth as fact [DISPUTED].
2. **George III "I have beat all the Americans!"** — legend framing only [DISPUTED].
3. **Phillips's goat line** — "attributed" framing only [DISPUTED].
4. **Wayne's "last place God made... finished in the dark"** — UNVERIFIED; reported-speech or cut.
5. **Fermoy's fire betraying the retreat** — he burned his quarters against orders [HIGH], but British accounts credit deserters, and dawn was an hour away; don't make the fire the single cause of the pursuit [MED/DISPUTED].
6. **Hubbardton numbers** — American strength (1,000–1,200 organized + stragglers, counts to ~2,000) and losses (41–150 killed) spread widely; use ranges, lean on Ketchum's low-end killed figure.
7. **Cannon left behind** — period claims run 70–128; "dozens of guns" is the safe floor [DISPUTED].
8. **The drunk (or asleep) bridge rearguard** — British-side anecdote; color, not load-bearing fact [MED].
9. **Native ally numbers** (~400–800) and the "fires on Sugar Loaf tipped the Americans" beat [MED].
10. **Warner's "meet me at Manchester"** and the exact moment Francis fell — traditional details from later accounts [MED/DISPUTED].
11. **"Two 12-pounders" on Defiance** — solid for the first guns up; popular accounts inflate the battery; the guns NEVER FIRED before the evacuation (the threat alone won) [HIGH on never-fired].
12. **Adams quote** — byte-exact text is "I think We shall never defend a Post, untill We shoot a General." (Aug 19, 1777); the "never be able to defend" version is a misquote.
13. **The 1758 echo** — genuine and verified (Clerk urged Rattlesnake Hill in 1758; Trumbull/Wayne/Arnold proved the climb in 1776–77) — but keep the two neglects distinct: Abercromby's was an attacker declining a shortcut; the Americans' was a defender leaving its own key undefended.
