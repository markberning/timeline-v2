# Comprehensiveness Audit — "Years of Disaster" (Phase 2, French and Indian War)
> Critic: COMPREHENSIVENESS gate. Storytelling-first: selective is correct; flag only genuinely essential gaps as MUST-ADD; enriching-but-optional as SHOULD-CONSIDER.

---

## Must-Cover Checklist

### 1. The 1755 Four-Pronged Plan
- [x] COVERED — All four prongs named (Braddock / Shirley-Niagara / Johnson-Crown Point / Monckton-Acadia), the Congress of Alexandria framing, and the outcome of each. Complete.

### 2. Braddock's Expedition and the Monongahela
- [x] COVERED — March, road-building, Shingas exchange, the battle mechanics (column jam / flanking fire / officer casualties), Braddock's wound and death, burial in the road, Washington's quote and near-miraculous survival, Dunbar's burning of stores and abandonment of the frontier.
- [x] COVERED — Beaujeu killed early, Dumas takes command. The tactical inversion (French doctrine fit the terrain; British doctrine did not) clearly explained.
- [x] COVERED — Native political consequence: Ohio nations tip to France; Shingas becomes "Shingas the Terrible."

### 3. Shirley's Niagara Prong
- [THIN] — Draft says it "bogged down at Oswego in supply failures and a personal rivalry with Johnson and never reached its target." True, but this is two clauses. The fact pack has richer material (French reinforcement of Fort Frontenac made advance too risky; the Shirley-Johnson feud is substantive). Given that this prong links directly to the later loss of Oswego (same fort, same pattern of British inability to act coherently), a sentence more on *why* it collapsed would sharpen the structural argument.

### 4. Battle of Lake George
- [x] COVERED — Johnson's background and Mohawk bond, Molly Brant named, Theyanoguin introduced and his London visit as context for why the alliance ran through him. Dieskau's force composition (including Caughnawaga as mirror-image Mohawk). The three phases (Bloody Morning Scout / camp assault / Bloody Pond). Johnson and Dieskau both wounded.
- [x] COVERED — The Caughnawaga detail ("Mohawk of the valley against Mohawk of the missions, the same people on opposite sides") is present and well-placed.
- [x] COVERED — Theyanoguin's death weighted appropriately; "the man who had held the Mohawk alliance together was not replaceable" lands correctly.
- [THIN] — The draft doesn't name Phineas Lyman or clarify who effectively commanded the camp while Johnson was wounded. Minor — not blocking.
- [THIN] — The draft says Johnson "never marched on Crown Point, his actual objective, which stayed French" but doesn't explain *why* (no boats, extended supply lines, provincial troops' enlistments ending, etc.). The fact pack notes this gap. It reads a little flat as-is — the strategic incompleteness is the point of this section and could land harder.

### 5. The Acadian Expulsion
- [x] COVERED — Long-standing neutral status, the 1730 promise, the oath crisis and coherent reasons for refusal, the deportation order, scale of displacement, death toll (including the 53% post-Louisbourg wave), Cajun diaspora chain.
- [x] COVERED — Moral/legal framing is handled honestly: "ethnic cleansing" vs. "genocide" debate present; 2003 Royal Proclamation noted; the author's own judgment ("The bare facts need no inflation") is earned.
- [x] COVERED — Mi'kmaq named as neighbors and longtime Acadian allies — gives the Mi'kmaq brief presence without over-promising.
- [THIN] — The Mi'kmaq's own active resistance to British expansion (they were fighting alongside Acadian militia and were separately targeted by British scalp proclamations) is absent. Not the chapter's focus, but their erasure from what happened to Acadia is a real omission given the Native-agency mandate.
- [THIN] — The draft gives individual colony deportation numbers (Massachusetts 2,000, Virginia 1,100, etc.) — good — but doesn't say anything about what *happened* to the Acadians in those colonies. Many were treated essentially as prisoners, refused entry, put on ships that sat in harbors. One or two lines would round out the human picture.

### 6. The War Goes Global (1756)
- [x] COVERED — Diplomatic Revolution named and explained (Convention of Westminster → Treaty of Versailles → France-Austria flip). Formal British declaration of war on France (May 17, 1756). Global scope named (Europe, Caribbean, West Africa, India).
- [THIN] — Minorca: the draft mentions Minorca as the formal trigger for the declaration of war but does NOT cover the Minorca loss itself — the naval failure, Byng's court-martial, and shooting — until the final "Britain at the Bottom" section, where it appears as a brief clause. The fact pack treats Minorca as a significant public humiliation event. In the narrative it currently reads as an afterthought. The Byng story (shot "pour encourager les autres," as Voltaire quipped) is a vivid moment of British crisis that belongs somewhere visible, not buried in a summary paragraph.
- [MISSING] — The Voltaire line on Byng ("In this country it is thought wise to kill an admiral from time to time, to encourage the others") is one of the most famous reactions to the whole period. Not required — but flagged as a top-tier SHOULD-CONSIDER.

### 7. Fort Oswego
- [x] COVERED — Strategic location, Montcalm's force, Mercer killed by cannonball, Littlehales surrenders, captured materiel (121 cannon), French destroy everything and march prisoners to Quebec.
- [x] COVERED — Iroquois ripple: Oneida and Seneca shift toward France after watching Oswego fall. The conditional nature of the Iroquois alliance (earned and lost on military performance) stated explicitly.
- [x] COVERED — Montcalm refusing full honors of war; some British soldiers killed in the disorder.
- [THIN] — The 1,700 prisoners figure is mentioned. What happened to them (marched to Quebec, held there) is in the fact pack but absent from the draft. One clause would close the loop.

### 8. Native Agency — Great Lakes Nations Chose France
- [x] COVERED — The Shingas exchange is the clearest statement of why Ohio nations chose France (British victory = land loss). The structural reasons (fur trade, French integration vs. British settler expansion, land security) are in the fact pack but not as prose in the draft.
- [THIN] — The draft's treatment of *why* the Great Lakes nations (Odawa, Potawatomi, Ojibwa, etc.) were at Fort William Henry is present as "a coalition reaching from the Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence... some of whom had traveled hundreds of miles" but doesn't say what they came *for* — until the massacre section, which explains their expectations (captives, scalps as marks of warrior standing). That explanation of Native war aims is good when it appears, but the reader has to wait for the "massacre" to learn what the warriors wanted. A single sentence in the Oswego or FWH setup would front-load their agency more usefully.
- [THIN] — Charles Michel de Langlade (French-Ojibwa), who organized the Native coalition at Monongahela and appears again at Fort William Henry, is in the fact pack but absent from the draft. His mixed heritage and organizing role is one of the clearest illustrations of the French-Native integration that made the difference. SHOULD-CONSIDER.

### 9. Fort William Henry and the "Massacre"
- [x] COVERED — Siege mechanics, Monro's garrison (many sick with smallpox), Webb's refusal (deceived into believing 11,000–12,000 French), the intercepted letter, surrender terms, Montcalm's warning to Monro.
- [x] COVERED — Native warriors' motivations and the logic of unmet expectations. Montcalm's personal intervention. The casualty debate (Steele's 69–184 vs. the 1,500 myth). Cooper and the novel-to-myth chain. The ~200–300 captives taken and at least 40 who stayed.
- [x] COVERED — Smallpox aftermath: warriors carry disease home, Potawatomi nearly wiped out. This is excellent — it closes the Native cost loop in a way the draft handles better than most treatments.
- [x] COVERED — Monro's fate (died Albany, November 3, 1757 — stroke, possibly precipitated by rage and trauma).
- [THIN] — The draft correctly distinguishes the 1,500 myth from Steele's range, but doesn't name Ian Steele or his book title in the prose (only "the historian Ian Steele, whose 1990 study *Betrayals* is the standard academic account"). That attribution IS present — good. The draft is solid here.
- [THIN] — The warriors who came from most distant nations (Menominee, Winnebago, Potawatomi from the western Great Lakes) are specifically noted in the fact pack as the primary actors in the violence, distinct from the Abenaki and Caughnawaga who had longer relationships with France and were more constrained. The draft lumps the coalition without this distinction. This matters for Native agency accuracy.

### 10. Britain Reeling / Pitt
- [x] COVERED — The structural catalogue: no theater commander after Braddock, Shirley's failures, Montcalm's initiative, Ohio nations raiding the frontier, Iroquois shift, Minorca, Newcastle ministry collapse.
- [x] COVERED — Pitt's emergence, the Newcastle-Pitt partnership, Pitt's strategy sketched. The "meanwhile" card to Chapter 3 is clean.
- [THIN] — Byng's execution (not just court-martial) is present in the fact pack as a named event but appears as "shot" in the draft's summary paragraph only. The emotional weight of the British public's reaction to Byng — shooting their own admiral — deserves slightly more than a dependent clause.

## Blind Spots in the Fact Pack

1. **Mi'kmaq active resistance** — The fact pack covers the Acadians' Mi'kmaq relationship as social context but doesn't flag the Mi'kmaq as a fighting force with their own British bounty proclamations. The expulsion section of the draft consequently treats them as neighbors rather than co-belligerents.

2. **Langlade's role at Monongahela** — The fact pack lists him in the cast (Section 9) and notes him in Section 6 (FWH commanders), but doesn't give him a narrative moment despite being one of the most important French-Native officers in the theater. The Monongahela section of the fact pack attributes Native organizing credit to Dumas, passing over Langlade almost entirely.

3. **What happened to Acadian deportees in the receiving colonies** — The fact pack has the destination distribution table but nothing on the conditions of reception (refusals of entry, imprisonments, dispersed families). The human texture of the diaspora beyond the numbers is absent.

4. **Why Johnson didn't march on Crown Point** — The fact pack notes the objective wasn't taken (Section 3) but doesn't list the specific reasons (no lake transport, provincial enlistments, supply exhaustion). The draft repeats the lacuna.

5. **Shirley's Niagara failure — depth** — The fact pack says "logistical failure, supply shortages, interprovincial rivalry with Johnson; French reinforcement of Fort Frontenac made advance too risky." This is richer than what got into the draft and should travel.

6. **Vaudreuil-Montcalm tension at Fort William Henry** — The fact pack mentions Vaudreuil argued Montcalm should have pressed on to Fort Edward after taking Fort William Henry. This civil-military tension (governor vs. general) is a structural feature of French command problems that eventually contributes to France's own unraveling. Brief but useful.

7. **The Voltaire / Byng quip** — Not in the fact pack at all. A live blind spot.

## MUST-ADD (Blocking)

**MUST-ADD 1 — Native war aims front-loaded before the FWH massacre section**
The draft explains what the warriors came for (captives, scalps, warrior standing) only *inside* the massacre explanation. That frame needs one sentence earlier, at or before the FWH siege setup, so the reader understands the coalition's logic before the violence, not only in retrospect. As written, the Native actors spend most of the chapter as an undifferentiated mass until the "massacre" section. This is the single biggest Native-agency gap.

**MUST-ADD 2 — Byng's court-martial and shooting**
The draft reduces the Byng story to "shot" in a summary clause. Byng losing Minorca and being executed for it is a defining moment of British public crisis — "pour encourager les autres" — and the connection between Minorca's fall and the Newcastle ministry's collapse is a causal link in the "Britain reeling" argument. One short beat is needed.

**MUST-ADD 3 — Why Johnson didn't march on Crown Point**
The draft says he didn't take Crown Point but not why. The incomplete victory is the point of the section; the reader is left puzzling at what stopped him. Even a clause ("no boats on the lake, provincial enlistments running out, his men exhausted") closes the structural argument.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER (Enriching, Not Blocking)

- **Langlade at Monongahela** — one sentence on his mixed French-Ojibwa identity and organizing role would make the French-Native integration argument concrete rather than asserted.
- **Acadian deportees' reception in the colonies** — one or two lines on what "sent to Massachusetts" actually meant (refused disembarkation in some ports, families split, quasi-imprisoned) would weight the human cost correctly.
- **Mi'kmaq as co-belligerents** — brief acknowledgment that the Mi'kmaq were fighting Britain alongside the Acadians and were separately targeted; ties the expulsion to a wider pattern.
- **Western vs. eastern Native distinction at FWH** — the fact pack correctly notes that Potawatomi/Odawa/Menominee were the primary actors in the violence while Abenaki and Caughnawaga were more restrained. Adding this distinction (one sentence) serves both accuracy and Native agency without slowing the narrative.
- **Vaudreuil-Montcalm tension** — brief mention that the governor wanted Montcalm to press to Fort Edward; Montcalm declined. Seeds the French command fracture that matters later.
- **Shirley's Niagara failure — one more sentence** to note the Fort Frontenac reinforcement dynamic that made the advance genuinely impossible, not merely incompetent.
- **Voltaire on Byng** — if Byng gets any expansion, the Voltaire quip is the punctuation. Treat as optional color.

---

## Summary Verdict

The draft is strong. Braddock's Monongahela is full, vivid, and correctly centers the Shingas exchange as the political pivot. Lake George lands Theyanoguin's death with appropriate weight. The Acadian Expulsion is handled with moral seriousness and correct numbers. Fort William Henry correctly debunks the massacre myth while holding the atrocity in full. The smallpox consequence for the Native nations is the draft's best original beat.

The gaps are real but narrow: **3 MUST-ADDs**, all correctable in the revision pass with one sentence to a short paragraph each. No section needs a structural rewrite.

**MUST-ADD count: 3**
**Single most important omission: Native war aims not established before the Fort William Henry massacre — warriors are background until the violence explains them, which is the wrong order for a storytelling-first chapter that claims Native agency was equal to European strategy.**
