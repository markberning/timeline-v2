## MUST-FIX (ship-blocking)

### 1. Fort Sumter surrender date — **§ raising-armies, parenthetical**

**Claim in draft:** "the federal fort in Charleston harbor whose surrender on **April 14, 1861**, opened the war"

**Status:** Contested / ambiguous — and the draft has it wrong relative to the most widely cited reference for this application.

**What the sources say:**
- History.com and several primary-source timelines record the **formal surrender agreement as April 13**; Anderson agreed to terms on April 13, 1861.
- Wikipedia ("Battle of Fort Sumter"), American Battlefield Trust, and NPS give **April 14** as the date of the formal evacuation/march-out.
- The standard shorthand used in most Civil War histories and the fact pack is April 14 — the day Anderson and his garrison physically left.

**Assessment:** April 14 is defensible and matches the fact pack's own scope note ("Fort Sumter surrendered April 14, 1861"). However, because sources split on this (April 13 = capitulation agreement; April 14 = formal evacuation), the draft should add a brief qualifier — "whose garrison evacuated on April 14" rather than "surrender on April 14" — to be precise and avoid the reader finding conflicting dates if they click the Fort Sumter dossier.

**Recommended fix:** Change "whose surrender on April 14, 1861" to "whose garrison evacuated on April 14, 1861" (or "whose fall on April 13–14, 1861"). The date itself (April 14) is consistent with the fact pack; the word "surrender" on that date is what creates the discrepancy with sources that record the capitulation agreement as April 13.

**Source:** [Battle of Fort Sumter — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Fort_Sumter); [Fort Sumter — American Battlefield Trust](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/civil-war/battles/fort-sumter); [History.com — Fort Sumter surrenders (April 13)](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/april-13/fort-sumter-surrenders)

> **Note:** If the Fort Sumter dossier itself uses "April 14" as the surrender date, this MUST-FIX degrades to a SHOULD-FIX (consistency-within-app). Either way the word "surrender" on April 14 should be tightened.

## SHOULD-FIX (precision / nuance)

### A. Lincoln's Black Hawk War rank omitted — **§ two-commanders**

**Claim in draft:** "His entire military résumé was a brief, combat-free stint in the Illinois militia during the Black Hawk War of 1832"

**What's missing:** Lincoln was elected **captain** of his first company — a detail the fact pack includes ("a brief, combat-free stint as a captain/private") and which Lincoln himself said gave him "more pleasure than any I have had since." He later re-enlisted as a private. The draft skips both ranks.

**Why it matters:** Lincoln's self-deprecating "mosquitoes" joke lands harder when the reader knows he actually held a rank, because the gap between "captain" and "commander-in-chief of the Union army" is part of the irony. The omission is not false, but it slightly weakens the passage.

**Recommended fix:** Add "(he was elected captain of his company — a rank he joked later gave him more pleasure than almost any since)" or simply note he served "as an elected captain, then private, in the Illinois militia."

**Source:** [Lincoln in the Black Hawk War — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln_in_the_Black_Hawk_War)

### B. Winfield Scott's age — **§ the-anaconda**

**Claim in draft:** "by 1861, in his mid-seventies"

**What's precise:** Scott was born November 13, 1786. In early/mid 1861 (when he wrote the Anaconda letters in May) he was **74**. He turned 75 in November 1861. "Mid-seventies" overstates his age by about a year at the time the plan was conceived; he was at the *start* of his mid-seventies at best.

**Recommended fix:** Change to "in his mid-to-late seventies" — or more precisely, "74 years old" or "nearing his mid-seventies." "Mid-seventies" is a borderline claim, not a fabrication.

**Source:** [Anaconda Plan — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaconda_Plan); Scott DOB confirmed multiple sources.

---

### C. Davis wound: "heel" vs. "foot/right ankle" — **§ two-commanders**

**Claim in draft:** "wounded in the heel at the Battle of Buena Vista in 1847"

**What sources say:** Multiple sources (including Wikipedia's Jefferson Davis article and Emerging Civil War) say Davis was wounded "in the foot" or "in the right ankle/foot." Some sources do use "heel." The variation appears to reflect the same wound described differently. "Heel" is used in several secondary sources and is not invented.

**Assessment:** Not clearly wrong, but imprecise. "Right foot" or "right ankle" is the most common formulation in scholarly sources; "heel" is used but less consistently.

**Recommended fix:** Consider changing to "wounded in the right foot" (safest cross-source formulation). "Heel" is defensible but will generate reader challenges.

**Source:** [Jefferson Davis — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefferson_Davis); [Battle of Buena Vista — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Buena_Vista)
