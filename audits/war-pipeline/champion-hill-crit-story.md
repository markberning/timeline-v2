# Champion Hill — STORYTELLING critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

Judged AS A STORY first (#1 goal). Voice baselined against the shipped Shiloh
sections (`src/app/war-civil-war/western/shiloh/s/[section]/section-narrative.tsx`).
Fact correctness is NOT my lane — that's the parallel fact-check gate. I flag a
fact only where it damages the *story* (a hedge that deflates a moment, an
unsupported beat the reader will feel as thin).

**This is a NOTES-ONLY pass. No draft/app edits, no commit.**

---

## OVERALL: GOOD (ship-able after the MUST-IMPROVE list)

This is a genuinely strong selective battle narrative. It does the single hardest
thing right: it makes the reader *care about a hill* by making the hill mean the
road home, the road home mean the creek, the creek mean the army, and the army mean
the campaign — a clean chain of consequence stated outright ("Lose the hill, lose
the crossroads; lose the crossroads, lose the way over the creek..."). The voice is
on-model with Shiloh: confident, plain, "forget the picture of...", "that was the
whole tactical problem of the day, stated in one sentence." Selective is exactly
right and the draft is selective — it never tries to be a campaign page.

Two things keep it from STRONG overall, and both are fixable:

1. **The Vicksburg cross-refs pile up and DO threaten the stand-alone feeling** —
   not because any single one is wrong, but because the **final section** leans on
   `(see Vicksburg)` for almost every payoff beat (Big Black, the siege, the July 4
   surrender, the river opening, emancipation). The climax of *this* battle gets
   narrated as a relay of pointers to *another* page. See MUST-IMPROVE #1.

2. **The two human gut-punches the brief names — the hill changing hands, and
   Loring's lost division — are present but UNDER-FELT.** They're explained more
   than they're dramatized. The Shiloh reference voice would put a *body* in them
   (Sherman riding the line under fire, three horses shot from under him; Johnston's
   boot quietly filling with blood). Champion Hill gestures at this (Tilghman's death
   is the one place it lands) but mostly tells me the stakes instead of staging them.
   See MUST-IMPROVE #2 and #3.

The slavery-as-cause throughline is woven in well in §1 and §2 and never feels
bolted on. By §3/§4 it's getting repetitive as a *phrase* ("slaveholders' republic
cut in half at the river" appears in near-identical form three times) — that's a
voice note, not a framing failure (SHOULD-IMPROVE #4).

## §1 — "The Hill That Held the Road Home" (the-crossroads-hill)
### Grade: STRONG

The best section. This is how you make a reader care about terrain.

- **Hook & stakes — STRONG.** "Major General Ulysses S. Grant (North) was loose in
  the interior of Mississippi, and there was no good way for the Confederacy to put
  him back." That's a real opening line — it has a posture and a problem, Shiloh-grade.
  The "loose," "cut himself free of his own supply line," "wrecked and left smoking
  behind him" verbs carry momentum.
- **The terrain-as-stakes chain — STRONG, the standout move.** "Lose the hill, lose
  the crossroads; lose the crossroads, lose the way over the creek; lose the way over
  the creek, and you lose the army." This single sentence is the whole section's job
  done. Keep it exactly.
- **Pemberton's impossible orders — GOOD.** "the order chased a thing that no longer
  existed" is a sharp, voicey way to render the contradiction. Good human dimension
  on the command level — Pemberton is set up as a man trapped, not just a name.
- **The "forget the numbers" beat — STRONG.** "So forget the picture of a much larger
  army steamrolling a smaller one. Champion Hill was a near-even, savage fight, and
  Grant won it not by weight of numbers but by where he landed the blow." This is the
  exact Shiloh register ("He was about to be very nearly proven wrong.") and it
  correctly pre-loads the thesis the whole battle then proves.
- **Voice/pacing — STRONG.** Reads aloud cleanly. The Grant *Memoirs* quote is placed
  well and earns its block.
- **One SHOULD note:** the *Meanwhile* (Johnston gathering at Jackson) is good but its
  last sentence re-explains the wedge a third time ("the whole campaign, in a sense,
  came down to keeping those two Southern forces apart") — the body already said this
  twice. Tighten so the sidebar adds the *Johnston* fact, not a third restatement of
  the wedge.

## §2 — "Up the Jackson Road, Onto the Crest" (the-jackson-road)
### Grade: GOOD

- **Hook — GOOD.** Opening on Stephen D. Lee seeing it first ("The first man to grasp
  what was happening was on the Confederate side") is a smart POV inversion — it gives
  the *defender* the foresight and the dread. Good dual-POV instinct.
- **Play-by-play tedium — FLAG, mild.** "By about half past eleven they were against
  the Confederate main line... around one o'clock he broke it." This is the one section
  where the clock-stamping starts to read like a timetable rather than a story. Shiloh
  uses times too, but anchors each to a *thing happening to a person* ("Around 2:30
  p.m., Johnston rode forward..."). Here the times attach to abstractions ("the fight
  became a climb"). Not bad, but watch it — see MUST-IMPROVE #2.
- **"The hill of death" — GOOD beat, slightly muffled by its own caveat.** The line
  "They called it the hill of death" is strong and earned. But the bracketed
  fact-pack caveat is sitting *in the prose stream* ("[The fact pack flags this as a
  paraphrased attribution...]"). That's a drafting artifact, not reader copy — it must
  be lifted out to the ledger / a footnote so the moment lands clean. As written it
  detonates the emotional beat by immediately lawyering it. (Shiloh handles the same
  problem gracefully: it states the legend, then an *italic* aside hedges it — never a
  bracketed editor's note mid-sentence.) MUST-IMPROVE #2.
- **The slavery paragraph ("the ground itself was the argument") — STRONG.** "The hill
  of death was a hill on a plantation, and that was not incidental to the war; it was
  the war." This is the framing gate done as *storytelling*, not as a compliance line.
  Best integration of the throughline in the draft.
- **OUTCOME block — GOOD**, does its job. "the right blow in the right place" echoes
  the thesis well.
- **The "Meanwhile — McClernand idle" — GOOD and important.** "a whole corps standing
  idle while a near-even fight was decided a mile to the north" is a real
  what-if and well told.

## §3 — "Bowen's Charge and the Crest Retaken" (the-hill-changes-hands)
### Grade: GOOD (the hand-changing is the brief's #1 human beat and it's under-felt)

- **Hook — GOOD.** "Pemberton's army had its own best weapon, and now he used it." Clean
  turn into the counterattack. Bowen's division characterized fast and well ("the finest
  division in the Confederate force, hard-fighting Missourians and Arkansans").
- **The hill changing hands — GOOD, but TELLS the drama it should STAGE. (brief calls
  this out specifically — MUST-IMPROVE #3.)** The sequence is *narrated* — "drove the
  Federals back down the slope... pushed them nearly to the Champion house" — but the
  reader is kept at map distance. "For a savage half hour or so this was the most
  furious fighting of the whole day" *asserts* the fury rather than letting us feel it.
  The Shiloh comparison is instructive: the Hornet's Nest doesn't say "this was furious
  fighting," it gives us "the men gave it a name out of the sound the bullets made
  tearing through the thickets." Champion Hill needs one or two concrete, ground-level
  details (the fact pack permitting) so the third-time-up-the-same-slope exhaustion is a
  thing the reader's body registers, not a count.
- **"a counterattack is only as good as the reserve that follows it, and Bowen had
  outrun his" — STRONG line.** This is the analytical voice at its best and it makes
  Bowen's failure *legible* and tragic in one stroke. Keep.
- **Play-by-play — minor flag.** "up and over the same slope a third time" — the
  draft itself worries (correctly) in the ledger about "several times" vs "twice." As
  *story*, the three-times-up framing is the right call (it's vivid and it's the whole
  point of the section title) — but lean into it as drama, not as a tally. Right now
  "changed hands several times in a few hours — Union, then Confederate under Bowen,
  then Union again" reads like a scorecard.
- **Loring set-up in the Meanwhile — STRONG placement.** Ending §3 on "The same
  division that would not march to save the hill was about to march out of the campaign
  entirely" is an excellent hand-off hook into §4. This is the kind of cross-section
  thread Shiloh does well (Bowen carried to the surrender). Keep exactly.

## §4 — "Across Baker's Creek, and the Division Lost Forever" (the-lost-division)
### Grade: GOOD (strongest human moment in the draft lives here; weakest cross-ref
discipline also lives here)

- **Tilghman's death — STRONG, the single most *felt* moment in the whole draft.**
  "Tilghman bought it and paid for it: a Union artillery shell struck and killed him at
  the crossing. He died holding the way open for the men streaming past him." This is
  exactly the register the rest of the draft needs more of — a named man, a concrete
  death, a concrete place, a human cost you feel. This is the proof the author *can*
  do it; the note for §2/§3 is "do this more."
- **Loring's lost division — GOOD, but the brief says it must be FELT and it's
  explained.** The facts are all here and the irony is set up well ("a loss with no
  body count to put on a casualty return" is a great line). But the *emotional* core —
  thousands of men who survived the battle and simply vanished from the campaign that
  needed them — is delivered as exposition. There's no moment, no image of the column
  peeling away "south and east" into the dark, no Pemberton-walking-into-Vicksburg-
  a-division-short scene rendered as a scene. "Pemberton would walk back into the
  fortress city having lost an entire division he would need for the siege" is the
  sentence to *expand into a beat*, not summarize past. MUST-IMPROVE #3.
- **Cross-ref overload — MUST-IMPROVE #1, the draft's biggest story risk.** The
  back third of this section is: "ran Pemberton's rearguard off the field at Big Black
  River Bridge (see Vicksburg)... shut up inside Vicksburg's works. The siege that
  followed... (see Vicksburg)... When Vicksburg fell... (see Vicksburg)." Five `(see
  Vicksburg)` pointers cluster in the closing beats. Individually each is correct
  pipeline discipline. Collectively they make the *payoff* of this battle feel like a
  table of contents for another page. The reader who came for Champion Hill should get
  Champion Hill's *own* ending landed before being waved toward the campaign. Right now
  the climax outsources itself.
- **The closing paragraph — GOOD when it stops pointing.** "All of that was decided,
  in the open, on the sixteenth of May — on the crest of a slaveholders' plantation,
  over a crossroads, in a near-even fight that Grant won because of where he put the
  blow." That's a real closing line — it gathers the whole section's threads. The
  problem is everything *before* it is `(see Vicksburg)` relay; the landing is good but
  it's landing on a runway of pointers.
- **The flagged Grant quote block ("Grant's own verdict... [VERIFY exact words]")** —
  as *story*, a block-quote frame wrapped around an admission that the words might not
  be the words is self-defeating. Either the substance carries it as prose (no quote
  frame) or it's cut. Don't set a `>` quotation block and then tell the reader inside
  it that the quotation is unreliable. SHOULD-IMPROVE.
- **The Bowen "Meanwhile" thread — STRONG.** "The man who almost saved the hill lived
  just long enough to give the city away." Excellent — this is the Shiloh "Ben-Hur" /
  "Few telegrams aged worse" move, a thread that carries a character past the battle's
  edge. Keep.

# MUST-IMPROVE (story-blocking — fix before ship)

1. **De-clutter the §4 climax's `(see Vicksburg)` pile-up so this battle lands its
   OWN ending.** Keep ONE forward pointer to the campaign at the very end; convert the
   Big-Black / siege / surrender / river beats from a relay of pointers into one or two
   sentences of *Champion Hill's* consequence, stated in this page's own voice, with the
   single `(see Vicksburg)` doing the hand-off. The reader must not feel the climax was
   a footnote to another page. (Brief's central risk, confirmed.)

2. **Lift the bracketed fact-pack editor's notes OUT of the prose stream** — at
   minimum "The fact pack flags this as a paraphrased attribution..." after "the hill of
   death" (§2), and the `[VERIFY exact words]` inside the §4 Grant block. These are
   drafting scaffolding, not reader copy; mid-sentence they defuse the exact emotional
   beats they sit on. Route the caveat to an italic aside or the ledger, the way Shiloh
   does (state the line, then hedge in a separate italic sentence — never `[brackets]`
   inside the moment).

3. **STAGE the two human gut-punches the brief names, don't just narrate them:**
   - **The hill changing hands (§3):** add one or two ground-level, concrete details
     (fact-pack permitting) so the third-time-up-the-same-slope exhaustion and the
     furious half hour are *felt*, not asserted as "the most furious fighting of the
     whole day." Right now it's a map-distance account.
   - **Loring's lost division (§4):** turn "Pemberton would walk back into the fortress
     city having lost an entire division" into an actual beat — the column peeling away
     into the dark / the army that needed it walking into the siege without it. The
     irony is set up perfectly; let the reader *feel* the hole, the way Tilghman's death
     (the draft's own proof-of-skill) is felt.

---

# SHOULD-IMPROVE (polish — strengthens but not blocking)

4. **Vary the slavery-throughline PHRASING.** "slaveholders' republic [cut/sawed] in
   half at the river" appears in near-identical form in §1, §3, and §4. The *framing*
   is right and required; the *repetition of the exact image* dulls it. Recast two of
   the three instances so the cause is named freshly each time (Shiloh varies it:
   "strangle a slaveholding republic," "saw that republic in two," "slavery destroyed
   with it"). This is a voice note, not a gate note.

5. **Trim the §1 *Meanwhile* so it adds the Johnston fact, not a third restatement of
   the wedge** (the body already states the keep-them-apart point twice).

6. **§2 clock-stamps:** anchor at least one of the morning time markers to a person or
   image rather than to an abstraction ("the fight became a climb"), per the Shiloh
   pattern. Light touch.

7. **§4 flagged Grant quote:** drop the `>` quotation frame; render the substance as
   prose or cut. Don't quote-frame an admittedly-unreliable quote.

# What NOT to touch (working — protect in revision)

- The §1 "Lose the hill, lose the crossroads..." consequence chain.
- The §1 "forget the picture of a much larger army steamrolling a smaller one" thesis set-up.
- §2 "it was not incidental to the war; it was the war."
- §3 "a counterattack is only as good as the reserve that follows it, and Bowen had outrun his."
- §3 closing hook into §4 ("about to march out of the campaign entirely").
- §4 Tilghman's death (the draft's best-felt human beat).
- §4 Bowen *Meanwhile* thread ("lived just long enough to give the city away").
- The selective scope itself — this correctly is NOT a campaign page. Don't let the
  cross-ref fix tempt anyone into *adding* campaign material; the fix is to point LESS,
  not to re-tell more.
