# STORYTELLING CRITIC · Battle of Trenton draft (rev-trenton)

Draft reviewed: `audits/war-pipeline/rev-trenton-draft.md`
Bar: `audits/war-pipeline/fi-carillon-draft.md` (shipped, gated)
Lane: storytelling only. Accuracy is the fact-checker's lane.

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is a strong draft. Section 1's arc is the best thing in it: the calendar-as-enemy
paragraph ("This was not a risk of mass desertion; it was a scheduled, legal dissolution")
makes the gamble read genuinely forced, not bold, which is exactly what the brief demands.
The myth retirements are real pleasure beats, the Hessians get their dignity inside the
battle, and the closing line of Section 3 ("Ten dollars and six weeks: that is the margin
by which the United States kept an army") lands the title perfectly. What keeps it from a
clean PASS: two meta-narration intrusions (a locked ship-blocker class), and the night
march/crossing — the emotional core of the whole story — is summarized rather than
inhabited. Carillon goes inside the abatis with "Imagine being one of them"; Trenton never
goes inside the boats or the march, and the night march is the natural home for that move.

---

## BLOCKER

### B1. Meta-narration (two instances) — voice ship-blocker class

**(a) Section 3:** "Honesty requires the next sentence."
The narrator steps out of the story to narrate his own prose. It is charming, but it is
exactly the move the voice rules retire. The paragraph it introduces is strong enough to
carry itself.
- **Rewrite:** cut the sentence entirely and let the turn do the work: "Almost no one fell
  to Hessian fire, but the weather was another army, and it collected its own casualties."
  Or, if a hinge is wanted, keep it in-story: "The famous number hides the real bill."

**(b) Section 1:** "(German soldiers in British service; who they were, and how they came
to be in New Jersey, is a story of its own)"
"Is a story of its own" narrates the content architecture — and the tease points nowhere
(there is no Hessians PILL; the story is actually told in Section 3 of this same battle
page). Link-down narration with a dead link is the worst version of the move.
- **Rewrite:** "(German soldiers rented to Britain by their princes — Section 3 tells how
  they got here)" is still meta; better is to just gloss and move on: "(German soldiers in
  British service)" — the full who-they-were story already pays off in Section 3 without
  being promised. If an off-the-battlefield Hessians theme page exists, the reviser can
  PILL it instead; do not keep the unanchored tease.

### B2. The night is told about, not inhabited — the carillon bar is not met

The crossing and march are vivid in summary but the camera never gets in a boat or on the
road. Compare the bar (carillon, the abatis): "Imagine being one of them. You hit a belt
of felled trees too thick to run through... you can hear the order to fire before each one
lands." Trenton's equivalent material is all there — and compressed into single clauses:

> "Nine miles of road in driving sleet, the storm at the army's back, men with broken
> shoes leaving what soldiers described as bloody footprints in the snow."

That is one sentence doing the work of the story's emotional center. The wet-powder beat
("use the bayonet, for the town must be taken") is likewise reported at arm's length.
- **Rewrite (insert after "Washington judged retreat now more dangerous than attack, and
  went on," before the split paragraph), shape not script:** "Imagine being one of the
  2,400. You have been awake since before the crossing, your shoes — if you still have
  shoes — have been wet since the riverbank, and the sleet is coming over your shoulder
  and down your collar because the storm is at the column's back. The order is silence.
  Nine miles in the dark, the password from the order of march running in your head:
  victory or death. Somewhere on that road you check your musket and find the priming
  soaked, which means that whatever happens at the far end will happen with the bayonet."
  This also gives the wet-powder/bayonet exchange a body to land in. Every fact in that
  shape is already in the draft/pack; the fix is camera position, not new material.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. Section 2 ends on bookkeeping, not on a story beat
The section's last prose sentence is the duration accounting: "First shots to last
surrender had taken roughly an hour and a half to two hours; the decisive fight in the
streets, the part tradition compresses to forty-five minutes, was over in less than an
hour." Carillon's fight section ends "...and when that did not work it ran." The clock
sentence is required content (the pack mandates the rendering), but it should not be the
landing.
- **Rewrite:** reorder the final paragraph: duration sentence first (or mid-paragraph
  after the Knyphausen surrender), then end on the escape over the open bridge —
  "...several hundred Hessians to get away south toward Bordentown before Sullivan sealed
  it." That ending also hands off perfectly into the Bordentown MEANWHILE that follows.

### S2. Section 2 pull-quote sits at a sub-peak, not the peak
The QUOTE is Knox's "The night was cold and stormy..." — placed at the crossing. The
section's climax is the street fight, where the draft's best battle line, "These, in the
twinkling of an eye, cleared the streets," is buried inline. Carillon's fight section
pulls its quote at the assault's height (the Charles Lee line), not at the approach.
- **Rewrite:** promote "These, in the twinkling of an eye, cleared the streets" to the
  QUOTE (placed after the King/Queen Street paragraph) and keep the Lucy-letter line
  inline at the crossing — it reads beautifully in running prose. If the author wants the
  storm quote pulled instead, that is defensible, but the current draft pulls the lesser
  of its two Knox lines.

### S3. The MEANWHILEs restate their own sections (S1 worst, S3 close behind)
S1's MEANWHILE ("The rising") repeats the in-section paragraph nearly beat for beat:
plunder, the proclamation, "ambushing patrols," "grinding down the garrison" → "worn down
for weeks by neighbors with muskets." Carillon's MEANWHILEs reframe ("One man's worth");
they do not re-list facts the reader finished thirty seconds ago. S3's MEANWHILE repeats
blood money + stayed-in-America from the Hessian-system paragraph.
- **Rewrite (S1):** trim the in-prose paragraph to its function (the alarms are grinding
  the garrison down — the setup Section 2 cashes) and let the MEANWHILE own the
  occupation-defeating-itself angle; or keep the prose and re-aim the MEANWHILE at what
  the alarms felt like from inside the garrison (Rall's men sleeping in uniform — it
  pre-seeds Section 2's payoff instead of echoing Section 1).
- **Rewrite (S3):** cut "with premium blood-money rates for the killed and wounded" and
  "never went home" from the MEANWHILE or from the body paragraph — each fact should read
  once; the MEANWHILE's own angle (soldiers as the export economy, "entries in that
  ledger") is strong and survives the trim.

### S4. Section 2's opening sentence is an overloaded single breath
"On Christmas night 1776, George Washington (American), commander in chief of a rebel
army that would mostly dissolve when its enlistments expired on December 31, put about
2,400 men on the bank of the Delaware River at McConkey's Ferry, nine miles upstream of
Trenton, New Jersey, and its garrison of about 1,500 Hessians (German troops in British
service) under Colonel Johann Rall." — the reader has to re-parse "nine miles upstream of
Trenton... and its garrison" (the bank is not upstream of "Trenton and its garrison" on
first read).
- **Rewrite:** split: "...put about 2,400 men on the bank of the Delaware at McConkey's
  Ferry. Nine miles downstream sat Trenton, New Jersey, and its garrison of about 1,500
  Hessians (German troops in British service) under Colonel Johann Rall."

### S5. Split the giant garrison paragraph in Section 2
The "Ahead of them, the garrison was not what the legend says" paragraph carries the
drunk-myth retirement, the exhaustion explainer, the Anderson raid, the three warnings,
the bayonet scoff, AND the pocket-note story in one block. Each beat is good; stacked,
the later ones (the note especially) lose their air.
- **Rewrite:** paragraph break before "And he had been warned" — myth/exhaustion/raid in
  one paragraph, warnings/scoff/note in the next. No prose changes needed.

## TRIM

### T1. S1 MEANWHILE / body duplication (see S3 above — the trim lives in one of the two).

### T2. Section 3, the Wilkinson aside
"Major James Wilkinson's memoirs, written forty years later by a man with a famously high
opinion of himself, have Washington saying that morning, 'This is a glorious day for our
country'; take it as Wilkinson remembered it." — delightful, keep it; but it interrupts
the Rall deathbed sequence (deathbed → Wilkinson → burial → court-martial). Consider
moving the Wilkinson sentence to the end of the paragraph or cutting it to keep the
Rall thread unbroken from deathbed to unmarked grave to posthumous blame. Cut only if the
paragraph still feels long after the move; the line itself earns its place.

## NIT

### N1. "and then the sentence that explains everything" (S1, Reed-letter setup)
Mild quote-setup meta. Tolerable in house voice; if the reviser is already in the
paragraph, "and then the sentence the whole plan rests on" keeps the build without the
narrator grading his own quote.

### N2. "Knox wrote to his wife Lucy about that night." (S2)
Flat lead-in sentence for the QUOTE. If S2 of this critique is adopted (Lucy line goes
inline), this disappears naturally; otherwise: "Knox, writing to his wife Lucy, set the
scene in one line."

### N3. "struck their colors (lowered their flags in surrender)" (S2)
The required gloss deflates the surrender beat — the emotional peak of the Hessian side
gets a dictionary note inside it. Keep the gloss but let the moment close: "...surrounded,
they struck their colors — lowered their flags — and it was over." (NB em-dash forbidden:
render with commas or parens: "they struck their colors, lowered their flags, and it was
over.")

### N4. The pocket-note beat could click into Section 3's court-martial
"It is the story the Hessians told afterward" is correctly framed and already a pleasure
beat. One clause would make it sing: the Hessians had a reason to tell it — it put the
whole disaster on a dead man. Section 3 has the matching half ("fixed the blame on Rall,
who was dead and could not answer"); a light touch in either place ("a story that put the
disaster in a dead man's pocket") welds the two and rewards the reader who reads both
sections. Optional; do not force it if it reads as a wink.

## SECTION-BY-SECTION ARC NOTES (for the reviser)

**Section 1 — PASS as drafted (modulo B1b, S3).** December 1776 reads like the war
actually dying: the defeat litany is fast, the boats detail is concrete, and the
enlistment-cliff paragraph is the draft's best writing — the Dec 31 clock is rendered as
a legal mechanism, not a mood, so "a gambler's plan, because only a gambler's plan fit
the situation" arrives earned. "Three columns, one night, a dawn attack, and an army with
eight days to live if it did nothing" is a fine section-ender. The Paine pull-quote is at
the right peak, and the read-aloud-scene honesty ("Maybe it happened. What is certain is
the text itself") is house voice at its best.

**Section 2 — PASS once B2/S1/S2 land.** Escalation is continuous: storm → two failed
crossings (the stakes ratchet: "Washington's column was now the whole attack") → daylight
forced → march → split → wet powder → the cut to the garrison at maximum dread → first
shots in German → the guns → the orchard. The cut-away to the Hessians ("Ahead of them,
the garrison was not what the legend says") is placed exactly right, at the top of the
tension, and the drunk-myth retirement IS a pleasure beat — "an accidental gift" (the
Anderson raid making Rall stand down on the worst possible morning) is a better story
than the myth, and the draft knows it. What's missing is only interiority (B2).

**Section 3 — PASS (modulo B1a, S3, T2).** No sag: the trifling-quote irony into the
exposure bill is the strongest aftermath turn; Rall's deathbed request keeps the German
dead human; the who-were-the-Hessians paragraph delivers the brief's humanity requirement
(conscripted subjects, a prince balancing his books, many chose to stay); and the title
beat is paid off twice, mid-section and in the final sentence, without feeling repeated.
The Princeton paragraph stays one beat, as it should.

**Both-sides check — PASS.** The Hessians fight inside the battle, not just the
aftermath: Wiederholdt's picket "fired and fell back fighting," the alarm is shouted in
German in his own words, the three regiments are named trying to form under fire, Rall
counterattacks twice and retakes guns, the colors are struck, the Knyphausen regiment
bogs in the marsh. Rall is brave and wrong, not a buffoon. This meets the bar.

**Voice sweep — clean except B1.** No drama-bold anywhere. No em-dashes in our prose
(the one "will—nay" is inside the verbatim Reed quote, correctly preserved). PILL
placements sensible: S1's 1776-context pill early, S3's enlistment-theme pill exactly at
the re-enlistment beat. S2 carries no pill, which is right — nothing should interrupt
the fight.
