# FACT PACK — "A Civil War Among Themselves" (1775–1783)

*American Revolution vertical, CAUSES arc, chapter 6 (route slug `civil-war-among-themselves`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against the cited sources; confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges and "flag" guidance for contested specifics. **This is the FLAGSHIP chapter of the causes arc** — it carries the vertical's locked framing thesis: the Revolution was the first civil war.*

**Orientation.** Ch.5 ended with the Declaration — thirteen colonies formally out of the empire. This chapter steps OUT of the political timeline and RANGES across the whole war (1775–1783) to tell the story the political narrative hides: the Revolution was a civil war, three ways at once. (1) **Britons against Britons** — in 1775 nearly everyone on both sides was a British subject arguing about the rights of Englishmen; Edmund Burke never called it a revolution at all, only a civil war within the empire. (2) **Americans against Americans** — roughly a fifth of the white population stayed loyal to the king; some 19,000 Americans put on the king's uniform in organized Loyalist regiments; at Kings Mountain (October 1780) the only non-American on the field was the British major commanding the Loyalists. (3) **Families split down the middle** — Benjamin Franklin's only son was the last royal governor of New Jersey and ran a Loyalist raiding board out of occupied New York; the break never healed. The chapter's machinery: who the Loyalists actually were (and the famous "one-third" misquote); the coercion both sides built (committees, test oaths, confiscation, tar — and Loyalist counter-coercion where the king's men held ground); the dirty war in Westchester, New Jersey, and the southern backcountry; the exodus of some 60,000 people who chose the empire; and the strange quiet with which most who stayed were folded back in. Battles appear here ONLY as evidence of American-on-American violence — Kings Mountain gets its own battle page later; do not narrate it tactically. Enslaved people's war (Dunmore's offer and what followed) is its own chapter (`rev-slavery`) — note Dunmore only as a driver of choosing sides. Native nations likewise have their own chapter.

## 1. THE THESIS — A CIVIL WAR THREE WAYS

- **Leg 1 — Britons vs Britons.** In 1775 the colonists *were* Britons; the argument was conducted in the language of English rights, and contemporaries on both shores experienced the war's opening as a fratricidal conflict inside one people. (HIGH — framing well supported by scholarship.)
  - **Edmund Burke never called the conflict "the American Revolution" — he called it "the American war," a civil war within the British Empire**, and from the opening of hostilities in spring 1775 he thought of it as a civil war with little hope of restoring a peaceful empire. (HIGH — Burke scholarship, OUPblog/Burke literature.)
  - British officers and politicians of the era routinely spoke of it as an "unnatural" war among fellow subjects. (HIGH — pervasive period language; use the adjective "unnatural," which both sides used.)
- **Leg 2 — Americans vs Americans.** Modern scholarship puts Loyalists at roughly a fifth of the white population (§2); ~19,000 Americans enlisted in regular-status Loyalist provincial regiments (§2); whole campaigns in the South in 1780–82 were fought militia-on-militia between neighbors (§5). Kings Mountain is the emblem: of ~2,000 men on the field, the ONLY Briton was Major Patrick Ferguson. (HIGH)
- **Leg 3 — families split.** Franklin/Franklin is the spine (§3); also the Morrises of New York and the Quincys of Massachusetts (§3) — verified, named, documented splits, not anecdote.
- **Historian's framing to lean on:** Alan Taylor (*American Revolutions*, 2016) argues the Revolution was truly America's first civil war; his rough proportions: **~20% Loyalist, ~40% Patriot, ~40% a wavering middle** that chose sides by local pressure, kin, and which army was nearest — not principle. (HIGH that Taylor argues this; the percentages are his estimate — present as one scholar's split, not census fact.)
- Thomas B. Allen's popular history is literally titled *Tories: Fighting for the King in America's First Civil War* (2010) — the chapter's thesis is mainstream scholarship, not a hot take. (HIGH)

## 2. WHO THE LOYALISTS WERE

### The "one-third" myth — MYTH-CHECK (load-bearing)
- The endlessly repeated claim — "John Adams said a third of Americans were for the Revolution, a third against, a third neutral" — is a **misreading**. The source is **Adams's letter to James Lloyd, 28 January 1815** (Founders Online, Adams/99-02-02-6401), and in it Adams is describing American opinion about **the French Revolution** in the 1790s, not the American one. (HIGH — verified against the Founders Online text and the Journal of the American Revolution's dedicated debunk, "John Adams's Rule of Thirds.")
- Adams's actual words in that letter: the divisions concerned "that third part of the People, who had been averse to the Revolution" — but read in context the thirds he sketches (one third averse, a lukewarm middle "principally the yeomanry," one third enthusiastic) describe attitudes toward **France vs Britain in the 1790s**. (HIGH)
- **Ship-safe phrasing:** "the famous 'one-third' line attributed to John Adams is about the French Revolution, not this one." Do NOT say Adams never estimated Loyalist numbers anywhere (he made varying guesses in other late letters); just kill the one-third-of-three-ways legend cleanly. (HIGH on the narrow claim; keep it narrow.)

### Modern numbers
- **Paul H. Smith, "The American Loyalists: Notes on Their Organization and Numerical Strength," *William and Mary Quarterly* 25 (1968)** — the standard quantitative study, built from Loyalist militia/provincial enlistments, claims records, and exile counts: Loyalists were about **16% of the total population**, or about **19.8% of white inhabitants** (his much-quoted "roughly 20% of white Americans"). (HIGH — verified figure and citation.)
- Broader scholarly range: **15–20% of white adults**; Patriots a plurality but not an overwhelming one; a large middle tried to sit it out. (HIGH as a range; never assert a single point figure.)
- **DENOMINATOR CARE:** the ~20% figure is **of the WHITE population**. Smith's 16% is of total population including the enslaved. Always say which denominator you're using; "a fifth of white Americans" is the precise, safe formulation. (HIGH — this is a locked HANDLE-WITH-CARE.)
- **Who fought:** the British "provincial line" (Americans enlisted on regular army status) enrolled about **19,000 Loyalists over the course of the war, in ~50 units and 312 companies**; peak strength at any one time was **~9,700 (December 1780)**. (HIGH — Smith's figures, reproduced in standard references. Phrase as "over the whole war… never more than about 10,000 at once.") Beyond the provincials, thousands more served in Loyalist militia, irregular bands, and as guides/spies — uncounted precisely. (HIGH that they're uncounted; don't invent a total.)

### Geography
- Loyalism was strongest in the **middle colonies and the Deep South**: New York (especially New York City, Long Island, the lower Hudson), New Jersey, Georgia, and the South Carolina backcountry; weakest in New England and Virginia. (HIGH — standard.)
- Within the South the split often ran **backcountry vs tidewater**: many backcountry settlers (recent arrivals, small farmers, people with grudges against the lowcountry Patriot gentry) took the king's side against the coastal elite that ran the Revolution. (HIGH as a tendency — phrase as tendency, not law; backcountry NC/SC also produced ferocious Whig militia.)
- New York supplied more soldiers to the king than to the Continental Army by some counts — use the softer verified form: **New York was the single largest source of Loyalist troops**. (MED — the "more than to Washington" comparison floats around; the superlative-among-colonies form is safe.)

### Motivations (the honest taxonomy — Loyalists were not one thing)
All verified as recognized categories in the scholarship (HIGH as categories; individual proportions unknowable):
- **Royal officeholders** — governors, judges, customs men; their livelihoods and oaths were the Crown.
- **Anglican clergy and many parishioners** (especially in New England and New York, where Anglicans were a self-conscious minority) — clergy had sworn allegiance to the king as head of their church.
- **Recent immigrants** — above all **Scottish Highlanders** (Carolinas, Mohawk Valley), many of whom had taken oaths to the Crown after the '45 and/or held land on Crown terms; also many recent English arrivals.
- **Merchants tied to London credit and trade.**
- **Tenant farmers** — e.g., in the Hudson Valley, where great Patriot landlords (Livingstons) had crushed rent rioters in the 1760s, some tenants chose the king to spite the landlord. (HIGH as documented pattern in NY/NJ.)
- **Enslaved people who answered British offers of freedom** — Dunmore's November 1775 proclamation (freedom to the enslaved of rebels who would serve the king) made Loyalism the emancipation side for tens of thousands of Black Americans. **Note it here in ONE beat as a motivation; the story itself belongs to `rev-slavery` — do not tell it in this chapter.** (HIGH; cross-chapter boundary locked.)
- **Principled constitutionalists** — people who agreed taxes were unjust but held that armed rebellion against the lawful sovereign was worse, that the empire protected the colonies, and that mob rule was the greater tyranny. (HIGH — e.g., Peter Van Schaack of NY, William Smith Jr.; this category is essential to the both-sides-fairness gate.)
- **Conservative temperament** — order, fear of the mob, sentimental attachment to king and mother country. (HIGH)
- **"Tory" vs "Loyalist" NAMING:** "Loyalist" is what they called themselves; "Tory" is what Patriots called them — borrowed from British party politics and used as a slur (as "Whig" = Patriot). Use both, but flag the valence on first use. (HIGH)

## 3. FAMILIES SPLIT — THE FRANKLIN BREAK AS SPINE

### Benjamin Franklin vs William Franklin (the chapter's human spine)
- **William Franklin** (c. 1730–1813), Benjamin's only surviving son (born out of wedlock, raised by Benjamin and Deborah), was royal **governor of New Jersey from 1763** — a post obtained while father and son were in London, at the height of their partnership (William assisted the kite experiment era work and was with his father for years in England). (HIGH)
- As the crisis came, William stayed the king's man. In **January 1776** the New Jersey militia put him under watch; after he tried to convene the royal legislature, he was **arrested in June 1776**, declared "an enemy to the liberties of this country" by the New Jersey Provincial Congress, and shipped to **Connecticut**. (HIGH — note: some timelines emphasize the January house arrest, the formal arrest is June 1776.)
- He spent **two years a prisoner in Connecticut** (Wallingford, Middletown); after he was caught issuing pardons/communicating for the Crown, he got **~8 months of solitary confinement in Litchfield jail** — filthy, no writing materials; his wife **Elizabeth died in British-held New York in 1777** and Washington/Congress refused him parole to see her. (HIGH on imprisonment, solitary, and that Elizabeth died while he was held; the refused-deathbed-visit detail is well attested — MED, verify phrasing at authoring.)
- **Exchanged in late 1778** (for John McKinly, the captured Patriot president of Delaware), he went to occupied **New York City**. (HIGH)
- There he became president of the **Board of Associated Loyalists** (chartered ~1780–81) — organizing Loyalist irregular raids out of New York (§4). Its most infamous act: the **hanging of Patriot captain Joshua Huddy, 12 April 1782** (§4), approved under William's board. (HIGH)
- **August 1782**: William sailed into exile in Britain. He never lived in America again. (HIGH)
- **The attempted reconciliation, 1784:** from London, **William wrote his father (22 July 1784)** seeking to revive "that affectionate Intercourse and Connexion" — and defended himself: he had "uniformly acted from a strong Sense of what I conceived my Duty to my King, and Regard to my Country, required." (HIGH — verbatim verified, Founders Online, Franklin/01-42-02-0275.)
- **Benjamin's reply, 16 August 1784** (from Passy) — the wound, verbatim (Founders Online, Franklin/01-43-02-0004, HIGH):
  > "Indeed nothing has ever hurt me so much and affected me with such keen Sensations, as to find my self deserted in my old Age by my only Son; and not only deserted, but to find him taking up Arms against me, in a Cause wherein my good Fame, Fortune and Life were all at Stake."
  - In the same letter Franklin concedes "We are Men, all subject to Errors" — and then takes it back: "there are Natural Duties which precede political Ones." (HIGH — both fragments verified in the same letter.)
- **One cold meeting:** father and son met once more, at **Southampton in July 1785** (as Benjamin passed through England going home) — mostly to settle property/business; no real reconciliation. (MED — well attested in Franklin biographies; verify framing at authoring, keep brief.)
- **The will (1788/probated 1790)** — Benjamin left William almost nothing, with the cut explicit (verbatim verified, HIGH):
  > "The part he acted against me in the late war, which is of public notoriety, will account for my leaving him no more of an estate he endeavoured to deprive me of."
  (He left William some Nova Scotia land claims and forgave debts — i.e., pointedly token.) (HIGH)
- William died in London in **1813**, still an exile. (HIGH)

### The Morrises of New York
- **Gouverneur Morris** (Patriot; later penman of the Constitution): his **mother, Sarah Gouverneur Morris, was a Loyalist** who turned the family estate, Morrisania (across the Harlem River from Manhattan), over to British military use after the 1776 occupation. (HIGH)
- His **half-brother Staats Long Morris was a major-general in the British Army**; the War Office posted him to the Minorca garrison "so that he would not be forced to fight on his native soil." (HIGH)
- Meanwhile another half-brother, **Lewis Morris, signed the Declaration of Independence**. One household: a Signer, a British general, a Loyalist mother, a Patriot framer. (HIGH — gorgeous, verified, use it.)

### The Quincys of Massachusetts
- **Samuel Quincy** — Massachusetts solicitor general, the man who **prosecuted** the Boston Massacre soldiers in 1770 — became a **Loyalist** and sailed for England in **May 1775**; his wife disagreed and stayed behind with the children. (HIGH)
- His brother **Josiah Quincy Jr.** — who had **defended** those same soldiers alongside John Adams — was the **Patriot**, and died at sea in **April 1775** returning from a political mission to England, just before Samuel sailed the other way. (HIGH — the brothers' role-reversal at the Massacre trial, then opposite ships in 1775, is verified and almost too good; it also back-links neatly to ch.3.)
- Their father Colonel Josiah Quincy watched the British evacuation fleet leave Boston in March 1776 carrying his loyalist son away — Samuel never returned (d. 1789 at sea). (MED on the father-watching framing — sourced to family histories; the never-returned fact is HIGH.)

## 4. THE MACHINERY OF COERCION — BOTH DIRECTIONS

### Patriot coercion (where the Revolution held ground)
- **Committees of safety / inspection / observation.** Born to enforce the Continental Association boycott (1774), they evolved into local loyalty police: summoning suspects, extracting recantations published in the newspapers, licensing travel, disarming the "disaffected," recommending arrests. By 1775–76 they were the de facto government nearly everywhere. (HIGH — frame precisely: elected/quasi-elected local bodies, not lynch mobs, but with real coercive teeth.)
- **Test oaths / test acts.** Every one of the thirteen states required some loyalty oath; **eleven required explicitly renouncing allegiance to the king** (oaths of "abjuration"; New Jersey's as early as September 1776). (HIGH)
  - Penalties for refusing (varied by state, all verified categories — HIGH): **disarmament** (militia officers empowered to take guns from non-jurors); **double or treble taxation** (Maryland used treble assessments); loss of the vote, office, jury service, ability to sue in court, buy/sell land, practice professions; in Massachusetts, refusers of the oath of allegiance-and-abjuration could be **jailed and deported within 40 days at their own expense**. (HIGH)
- **Confiscation acts.** Every state passed laws to seize Loyalist property. (HIGH)
  - **New York's Forfeiture Act (October 1779)** was the most aggressive: it attainted **59 named Loyalists** (starting with the colonial elite), declared their lives forfeit if they returned, **banished them, and seized their estates for sale** by Commissioners of Forfeiture; sequestration committees had been seizing abandoned Loyalist property since March 1777. (HIGH — verify the "59 named" count at authoring if used; the act, date, mechanism, and commissioners are HIGH.)
  - **Massachusetts Banishment Act (September 1778)**: banned **~300 named Loyalists** (most sources: **308**, headed by ex-governor Thomas Hutchinson and Francis Bernard) from returning; a banished person who returned after transportation faced **death "without benefit of clergy."** (HIGH)
  - **Pennsylvania** attainted Joseph Galloway and confiscated his family's estates (§6, Grace Galloway). Pennsylvania also actually executed two Quakers for treason — **John Roberts and Abraham Carlisle, hanged in Philadelphia, November 1778** — the rare death-penalty edge of the machinery. (MED — well documented but not web-verified this pass; verify before shipping, or cut.)
  - **Scale in £:** no reliable single total exists for property confiscated. The best verified proxy is what Loyalists later claimed in London: **3,225 claims seeking £8,216,126** (§7). Use the claims figures for scale, never an invented confiscation total. (HIGH on the proxy framing.)
- **Tarring and feathering — what it actually did.** Pine tar heated to pour; victim stripped, tar applied, feathers; usually paraded by cart. It was **torture and theater, but rarely fatal** — a survey of 70+ recorded incidents (1766–1776, Benjamin Irvin's study) records **no deaths from the tarring itself**. State that honestly: agonizing, humiliating, scarring — not generally lethal. (HIGH)
  - **The documented worst case: John Malcolm, Boston, 25 January 1774.** Customs officer (already tarred once before in Maine), caned the shoemaker George Robert Twelves Hewes in a street dispute; that night a crowd dragged him out, **stripped him, poured scalding tar on bare skin, feathered him, carted him for hours in winter, whipped him, threatened him with hanging and with losing his ears at the Liberty Tree until he cursed the king**. Thawing afterward, his **tarred flesh peeled off in "steaks"**; he was bedridden roughly **eight weeks**. Hewes himself — the man Malcolm had caned — was so appalled he tried to cover Malcolm with his own jacket. (HIGH — Massachusetts Historical Society, contemporary accounts. The Hewes-jacket beat is verified and is the both-sides-humanity beat; use it.)
  - (Malcolm later went to England and petitioned for redress — reportedly carrying a piece of his own detached, tarred flesh as evidence. MED — colorful, attested in secondary accounts; verify or soften to "reportedly.")
  - NOTE: Malcolm is January 1774, technically pre-1775 — use him as the established *practice* the war then inherited and escalated. The wartime southern equivalent is Thomas Brown (§5/§6).
- **Banishment + internal exile:** beyond formal acts, committees exiled suspects from coastal/strategic zones inland, required bonds for good behavior, and broke up Loyalist gatherings. (HIGH — general practice.)

### British/Loyalist counter-coercion (where the king held ground)
- **Occupied New York City (1776–83)** was the Loyalist capital — tens of thousands of Loyalist refugees; rebel-sympathizers' property requisitioned; the city under military rule (no civil courts for most of the war), with Patriot-leaning residents subject to suspicion, seizure, and the infamous prison ships in Wallabout Bay (the prison-ship dead belong to a different story — touch only if needed for balance). (HIGH on occupation/refuge framing.)
- **The Board of Associated Loyalists** (chartered ~1780–81, president **William Franklin**) — organized Loyalist irregulars to raid the New Jersey and Connecticut shores from New York: plunder, prisoner-snatching, retaliation killings. (HIGH)
  - **The Huddy affair:** **12 April 1782** — Associated Loyalists under Captain Richard Lippincott took the captured Monmouth County Patriot captain **Joshua Huddy** from custody, rowed him to the Jersey shore, and **hanged him on the beach** as revenge for the death of the Loyalist Philip White, pinning a placard to his chest (commonly rendered **"Up goes Huddy for Philip White"**). Washington threatened retaliatory execution of a British captain chosen by lot (the Asgill affair); the scandal helped finish the Board and sped William Franklin's departure for England. (HIGH on the hanging, date, Lippincott, retaliation context; MED on the exact placard wording — verify or paraphrase.)
- **Clinton's proclamation, 3 June 1780** (after Charleston fell): voided the paroles of most South Carolinians and **required a full oath of allegiance — including willingness to bear arms for the king — on pain of being treated as rebels. Neutrality was abolished.** Men who had been sitting out the war now had to choose, and thousands chose rebellion; the proclamation is widely judged one of Britain's worst blunders, and it reignited the backcountry civil war (§5). (HIGH — verified; this is the mirror-image of the Patriot test acts and the chapter's symmetry beat.)
- In the occupied South the British also sequestered rebel estates (a commissioner of sequestered estates operated out of Charleston). (MED — real but lightly verified this pass; keep to a clause or cut.)

## 5. THE DIRTY WAR

### The Neutral Ground (Westchester County, NY, 1776–83)
- Between the British lines (Manhattan/lower Westchester) and the American lines (the Hudson Highlands) lay ~30 miles of **no-man's-land covering most of Westchester County — "the Neutral Ground"** — raided continuously for seven years. (HIGH)
- **"Cowboys"** = the Loyalist-aligned cattle raiders (the term's first American use!) who stole livestock and drove it to the British market in New York; **"Skinners"** = the label that stuck to Patriot-aligned plunderers. (HIGH on cowboys; **CARE on "skinners":** the term was popularized — arguably coined as a fixed label — by James Fenimore Cooper's novel *The Spy* (1821); period usage was loose, and bands on BOTH sides plundered indiscriminately. Use the pair as the famous shorthand, then immediately complicate it.) (HIGH on the Cooper point.)
- Result: farms stripped, fences burned, fields abandoned; travelers robbed regardless of allegiance; a depopulated wasteland by 1783. (HIGH — period descriptions are uniform.)

### New Jersey — the war of neighbors
- New Jersey changed hands and allegiance repeatedly ("the cockpit of the Revolution"); the **winter 1777 "Forage War"** of constant small-unit raiding shaded into years of neighbor-on-neighbor violence, worst in **Monmouth County**: a failed Loyalist insurrection (early 1777) left armed Loyalists hiding in the pine woods — the **"Pine Robbers"** (Jacob Fagan, Lewis Fenton, John Bacon) — part partisans, part bandits, economically tied to British New York, which paid hard coin for Jersey lumber and food. (HIGH)
- Patriot vigilantes answered: Monmouth's **Association for Retaliation** (1780) was formed to protect its members — and itself plundered and abused peaceable Crown sympathizers and settled private grudges. (HIGH — the symmetry is verified; keep it.)
- The Huddy hanging (§4) was the climax of exactly this Monmouth cycle. (HIGH)

### The southern backcountry, 1780–82 (the war's ugliest theater)
- After Charleston fell (May 1780) and Clinton's no-neutrality proclamation (§4), the Carolina–Georgia backcountry collapsed into Whig-vs-Tory partisan war: militia on militia, neighbors on neighbors, reprisal on reprisal. (HIGH)
- **Nathanael Greene, arriving to command in December 1780, verbatim (to Alexander Hamilton, 10 January 1781; Founders Online, Hamilton/01-02-02-1014 — HIGH):**
  > "The division among the people is much greater than I imagined and the Whigs and Tories persecute eachother, with little less than savage fury. There is nothing but murders and devastations in every quarter."
  - The version in the brief — "The whole Country is in Danger of being laid Waste by the Whigs and Tories who pursue each other with as much relentless Fury as Beasts of Prey" — is also Greene, December 1780, and is widely quoted in the scholarship, **but I could not pin the recipient/letter this pass**. (MED — if you want "beasts of prey," verify the citation first; otherwise use the Hamilton letter, which is byte-verified.)
- **The Waxhaws, 29 May 1780** ("Buford's Massacre"): Banastre Tarleton's British Legion (a largely LOYALIST provincial corps — note that; the "British" cavalry hacking Virginians were mostly Americans) caught Abraham Buford's ~420 Virginia Continentals. Of them roughly **113 killed, ~150 wounded, ~53 captured**. Whether it was a deliberate massacre is DISPUTED: accounts agree Tarleton's horse went down (possibly shot during a surrender flag), his men believed their commander killed, and they kept killing men trying to surrender; equally sourced accounts say Tarleton never ordered it and arranged care for wounded prisoners after. What is NOT disputed: Patriots got a war cry — **"Tarleton's quarter!"** (= no mercy) and **"Remember Buford!"** — that licensed their own atrocities. (HIGH on numbers/cry; DISPUTED on intent — present both halves.)
- **Kings Mountain, 7 October 1780** — the thesis exhibit. ~900 Patriot backcountry militia (including the **"Overmountain Men"** from the far side of the Appalachians, mustered at Sycamore Shoals 25 September) stormed Major **Patrick Ferguson's** force of ~1,100: roughly **100–120 Loyalist "Provincial" regulars from New York/New Jersey/Connecticut plus ~800 Carolina Loyalist militia**. **Ferguson was the only Briton on the field** — every other man, both sides, was American. Ferguson died in the battle (shot from his horse; he had killed a man demanding his surrender). (HIGH — the "only Briton present" claim is verified across NPS/American Battlefield Trust/standard accounts. PRECISE WORDING for the gate: "the only British soldier on the field"; the ~100 Provincials were American colonists in red coats, which is what makes the line true and devastating.) **Do NOT narrate the battle further — battle page later.**
- **The hangings after Kings Mountain — Bickerstaff's Old Fields (near Gilbert Town, NC), 13–15 October 1780:** a week after the battle, the victorious Patriot militia convened a drumhead court; **~30 Loyalist prisoners were tried and convicted, 12 condemned, and NINE actually hanged** (among them Col. Ambrose Mills) — justified by the victors as retaliation for British/Loyalist hangings of captured Patriots at Camden, Ninety Six, and Augusta. (HIGH — Journal of the American Revolution, "The Fruits of Victory"; NC markers. The prompt's "nine hanged at Gilbert Town" is confirmed in substance — site = Bickerstaff's Old Fields near Gilbert Town.)
  - Color, verified: one condemned man named **Baldwin escaped when his younger brother, feigning a farewell embrace, cut his bindings with a hidden knife** and both fled into the dark. (MED — single-thread sourcing via JAR/local accounts; charming enough to use with "the story goes.") A Loyalist officer-prisoner, Lt. **Anthony Allaire**, recorded bitterly that the hanged died defiant. (MED — Allaire's diary via JAR.)
- **Pyle's Massacre (Haw River, NC), 24 February 1781:** ~300–400 Loyalist militia under Dr. John Pyle, riding to join the British, mistook **"Light-Horse Harry" Lee's Legion** (green jackets like Tarleton's) for the British Legion; Lee rode the length of their line trading compliments before the killing began. **At least ~90 Loyalists were sabered to death**; Patriot militiaman **Moses Hall's pension account** records six prisoners afterward **"hewed to pieces with broadswords"** to cries of **"Remember Buford."** (HIGH on event/mistake/scale; HIGH that Hall's account says this — it's among the most-cited atrocity testimony of the war.)
- **Cloud's Creek, SC, November 1781** (sources split on the 7th vs 17th — give "November 1781"): Loyalist Major **William "Bloody Bill" Cunningham** — a former Continental soldier turned Loyalist — caught ~30 Patriot militia under Captains Sterling Turner and James Butler Sr. in a cabin with rain-soaked guns; after they surrendered, **all but two were killed**. It opened his **"Bloody Scout,"** a weeks-long revenge expedition (~300 riders) of house-burnings and executions across the SC backcountry (Hayes Station, etc.). (HIGH on Cloud's Creek/no-quarter/Bloody Scout; the folkloric explanations of Cunningham's personal grievances — abuse of his epileptic brother and aged father by Whig raiders — are DISPUTED/legend-tinged: attribute as "the story his side told.")
- **Thomas "Burnfoot" Brown** — the man the mob made (full story §6): tortured by Patriots in 1775, raised the **King's (Carolina) Rangers**, and prosecuted the Loyalist war in Georgia with notorious harshness. Stories that he hanged 13 wounded Patriot prisoners at Augusta (1780) while watching from his sickbed are **DISPUTED** — repeated in Patriot memory, doubted by his biographer; if used, frame as the reputation, not established fact. (HIGH on his torture + command; DISPUTED on the Augusta hangings story.)
- **The cycle, stated plainly:** Camden/Ninety Six/Augusta hangings → Kings Mountain → nine hanged at Bickerstaff's → Pyle's "Remember Buford" → Bloody Scout. Each side's atrocity was the other side's justification. That cycle — not any battle — is the chapter's spine in the South. (HIGH as framing.)

## 6. HUMAN-SCALE STORIES (verified, named)

1. **The Franklins** (§3) — the spine. Father and son, the empire's most famous American family, broken for good. All quotes verified.
2. **Grace Growden Galloway** (1727–1782), Philadelphia. Wife of Joseph Galloway (Pennsylvania's leading Loyalist politician, attainted of treason); when Joseph fled to the British and then to England in 1778 **taking their daughter Elizabeth**, Grace stayed to try to save the estates — much of the property was HER inheritance (Growden lands), legally swallowed by her husband's ownership under coverture. Her diary (first entry 18 June 1778: parted "with my dear Husband & child") tracks the squeeze day by day: warned by **Charles Willson Peale** — the painter, acting as confiscation commissioner — "that he must take possession of my house for the state"; on **20 August 1778** Peale's party forced her door and **put her out of her own house** at Market and Sixth, a block from the State House. She lived on dependent on friends, never saw her husband or daughter again, and died in 1782 still dispossessed. (HIGH — diary survives at HSP; details verified via Historic New England/JAR/HistoryNet. Her diary's verbatim lines beyond those quoted above MUST be re-verified if used.)
   - Why she's the chapter's second pillar: she personifies (a) confiscation as it landed on a household, (b) the gendered trap — a woman punished for her husband's politics, with her OWN inherited property, (c) a "Loyalist" who never chose a side so much as had one assigned by marriage. (HIGH as framing.)
3. **Thomas Brown** (1750–1825), Augusta, Georgia — villain AND victim, the both-sides parable. **2 August 1775**: a Liberty crowd (~130 men) demanded the young English-born planter swear to the Patriot cause; he refused and resisted. They fractured his skull with a musket butt, **tied him to a tree, burned his feet (he lost two toes), scalped him in places, and tarred and feathered him**. "Burnfoot" Brown suffered headaches (and reportedly a laudanum dependence) the rest of his life — and became one of the king's most relentless partisan colonels (King's Rangers), feared across Georgia. After the war: exile. (HIGH on the torture and career; see §5 for the DISPUTED Augusta-hangings story.)
   - Use him to make the locked point: **the mob made its own enemies.** A man tortured into the king's camp then gave the backcountry war its cruelty back, with interest.

## 7. THE LOYALIST EXODUS

- **Scale (Maya Jasanoff, *Liberty's Exiles*, 2011 — the standard modern count):** about **60,000 Loyalists left** the thirteen colonies/states during and after the war — **including some 8,000 free Black Loyalists** — and they took with them about **15,000 enslaved people**: roughly **75,000 people in all**, about **1 in 40 of the American population**. (HIGH on 60,000 + 15,000 enslaved, verified across reviews/LOC; MED on the "1 in 40" ratio — Jasanoff's own framing, verify phrasing at authoring. Older scholarship ran as high as 80,000–100,000 émigrés; give Jasanoff as the careful modern floor and note earlier estimates ran higher — DISPUTED range, never one number.)
- **Destinations (Jasanoff's breakdown — HIGH, present as approximate):**
  - **~38,000 to remaining British North America** — ~30,000 to the Maritimes (Nova Scotia, and the new colony it spawned), ~5,500–6,000 to Quebec.
  - **~13,000 to Britain** (~8,000 white + ~5,000 free Black Loyalists).
  - **~6,000 (mostly southerners) to Jamaica and the Bahamas**, carrying the great majority of the 15,000 enslaved with them — i.e., the "loyalist" migration was also a forced migration of the enslaved. (HIGH — say this plainly.)
  - Smaller streams: Britain's East Florida (temporarily), the Mosquito Coast, even India. (MED — Jasanoff's global canvas; one clause max.)
- **New Brunswick was carved out of Nova Scotia in 1784** specifically because of the Loyalist influx up the Bay of Fundy. (HIGH)
- **Shelburne, Nova Scotia — the boomtown parable.** Loyalist associates from New York chose the harbor of Port Roseway; first fleet landed **4 May 1783**; Governor John Parr named it **Shelburne** (22 July 1783) after the PM. Within about a year it held **~10,000 people — for a moment the fourth-largest town in North America, behind only New York, Philadelphia, and Boston**. Then: government rations stopped (1787) — that same year ~360 houses stood deserted; by 1789 two-thirds of the town was empty; by 1816 the population was **374**. (HIGH on dates/arc; the "fourth largest" superlative is repeated by Parks Canada-adjacent sources — MED, fine with "for a fleeting moment.")
  - Adjacent **Birchtown** became the largest community of free Black people in North America (~1,500 in Shelburne County). In **1792, 1,196 Black Loyalists sailed from Halifax in 15 ships to found Freetown, Sierra Leone** (organized by Thomas Peters with abolitionist John Clarkson; ~65 died en route), driven out by denied land grants, denied votes, and Nova Scotian racism. (HIGH — **BUT this is the Black Loyalist story and belongs at full length to `rev-slavery`; here it gets ONE tight beat as the exodus's bitterest epilogue, cross-linked.** Boundary locked.)
- **The claims commission (London).** Parliament established a commission (1783) to compensate Loyalist losses. Verified totals: **3,225 claims filed seeking £8,216,126; 2,291 claimants awarded a total of £3,033,091** — roughly **37 pence on the pound claimed**, after years of humiliating documentary inquisition (witnesses, deeds, proof of loyalty). Hearings ran in London and Halifax into the late 1780s; records fill 146 volumes (AO 12). (HIGH on the 3,225/£8.2M/£3.03M set — UK National Archives-derived; **NOTE:** other tallies in the literature count ~5,000+ total memorials by counting differently — if a different total surfaces in critique, both are defensible; cite the claims-filed basis.)
  - The commission's existence is itself a thesis point: **the British state formally recognized and (partly) paid its American losers** — these were Britain's war refugees.

## 8. AFTER THE WAR — THE QUIET REABSORPTION

- The **Treaty of Paris (1783), Articles 5–6**: Congress would "earnestly recommend" that states restore confiscated Loyalist property and stop further persecution. The states mostly ignored the recommendation at first — confiscations were not undone — but the treaty marked the official end of open season. (HIGH)
- **What did NOT happen is the headline:** no mass executions, no permanent proscribed class, no generation of guerrilla revanche. For an 18th-century civil war, the aftermath was startlingly mild. (HIGH as scholarly consensus framing — Jasanoff and the reintegration literature both stress it.)
- **The mechanics of forgiveness were cheap and procedural:** there were far too many Loyalists to try for treason, so states used **oaths of allegiance** — quick, uniform, face-saving — plus vouching by Patriot neighbors. Most Loyalists who stayed **kept (or recovered) property and became citizens**. (HIGH — reintegration scholarship, e.g., the South Carolina and Pennsylvania studies.)
- Discriminatory laws were dismantled across the 1780s: **Pennsylvania repealed its Test Act in 1789**; New York's anti-Loyalist statutes were rolled back (with **Alexander Hamilton defending Loyalist clients** under the Trespass Act — *Rutgers v. Waddington*, 1784 — arguing the treaty overrode state revenge laws). (HIGH on the PA repeal and Hamilton's Loyalist defense work.)
- Many exiles drifted back in the late 1780s–90s once tempers cooled; **the permanent exiles were disproportionately the named, the prominent, and the banished** — the Hutchinsons, Galloways, and William Franklin, for whom there was no road home. (HIGH as pattern.)
- **Closing symmetry for the chapter:** the war that began among Britons ended by sorting one people into two countries — the United States, and English Canada, which Loyalist refugees substantially founded. The "first civil war" didn't just divide America; it populated the empire's second try. (HIGH — Jasanoff's core argument; safe as framing.)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **The one-third myth is the chapter's mandatory myth-kill.** Adams's 1815 "thirds" letter is about the **French** Revolution. Replace with Smith's ~16% of total / ~19.8% of white population, presented as "roughly a fifth of white Americans" with the 15–20% scholarly range. Never print thirds as fact.
2. **Denominators.** "A fifth" = of the WHITE population (Smith's 19.8%); of the total population including the enslaved it's ~16%. Say which, every time. (And enslaved people who fled to the British complicate "Loyalist" as a category — they chose freedom, not George III.)
3. **Loyalists are neither villains nor martyrs.** The framing gate will check both-sides fairness. The pack supplies both directions on purpose: Patriot tar, confiscation, test acts, Bickerstaff's hangings, Pyle's ↔ Loyalist/British counter-coercion, the Huddy lynching, Clinton's no-neutrality oath, Cloud's Creek, the Bloody Scout. Hold the symmetry; resist underdog romance AND traitor talk.
4. **"Tory" is the Patriots' slur; "Loyalist" the self-name.** Use both with the valence flagged once.
5. **Kings Mountain wording.** Safe sentence: "every man on that field was American — except one, the British major commanding the Loyalists." The ~100 red-coated Provincials were American colonists; that's what makes the line true. Don't say "no British units" sloppily (the Provincials were on the British establishment); say "the only British *soldier*/Briton." NO tactical narration — battle page later.
6. **Waxhaws intent is DISPUTED.** Killing of surrendering men: yes. Tarleton ordering it: not established (horse shot, men enraged, control lost; he later arranged care for wounded prisoners). What matters for THIS chapter is the war cry it spawned. Also: Tarleton's British Legion was largely American Loyalists — even "Tarleton's quarter" was substantially American-on-American.
7. **Tarring and feathering: torture, rarely fatal.** ~70+ recorded incidents 1766–76, no documented deaths from the tarring itself. Tell Malcolm honestly (scalded flesh peeling "in steaks," eight weeks bedridden) without inflating to lethal, and keep the Hewes-covers-him-with-his-jacket grace note.
8. **Bloody Bill Cunningham's motive-legends** (abused brother/father) and **Thomas Brown's Augusta staircase hangings** are partisan memory — attribute, don't assert. Brown's own torture IS established.
9. **The "beasts of prey" Greene quote** is everywhere in the literature but I could not pin its letter; the Hamilton letter (Jan 10, 1781) is byte-verified. Use the verified one.
10. **Exodus numbers are ranges.** Jasanoff: ~60,000 + ~15,000 enslaved (≈75,000); older scholarship up to 80,000–100,000. Claims commission: 3,225 claims/£8.2M sought/£3.03M paid (other counting methods give ~5,000+ memorials). Ranges, attributed.
11. **Boundary discipline:** Dunmore's offer = one beat (motivation), full story in `rev-slavery`; Sierra Leone/Birchtown = one beat (exodus epilogue), full story in `rev-slavery`; Native nations = `rev-native`; Kings Mountain tactics = future battle page; the political road to July 1776 = ch.5. The Book of Negroes (the 1783 evacuation ledger) belongs to `rev-slavery` — don't use it or its imagery here.
12. **Don't flatten the middle.** Taylor's wavering ~40% is the biggest "side." Many people's "allegiance" was the nearest army, the local committee, or a husband's politics (Grace Galloway). Choosing-not-to-choose was its own, often impossible, position — Clinton's June 1780 proclamation and the Patriot test acts BOTH abolished neutrality; that pincer is the chapter's sharpest structural irony.
13. **Reintegration ≠ reconciliation for everyone.** The quiet reabsorption is real and surprising — but the banished-by-name, the prominent, and the Black Loyalists in Nova Scotia got no such quiet. End balanced.
