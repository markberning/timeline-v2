# STORYTELLING CRITIC — The Battle of Quebec (1775)

Draft: `audits/war-pipeline/rev-quebec-draft.md`
Bar: `audits/war-pipeline/fi-carillon-draft.md` (shipped, gated)
Lane: storytelling only; accuracy is the fact-checker's lane. Where a fix needs a new
detail, it is conditional on the fact pack supporting it.

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is a strong draft. The arc the brief demands is fully present: section 1 builds the
enlistment cliff honestly ("Attack before New Year's, or watch the army dissolve") and
ends on the held breath of waiting for weather; section 2 runs from signal rockets to
Morgan's sword-to-a-priest with real shape; section 3 lands the bitter meaning in one of
the best sentences in the war vertical so far ("The city was substantially defended by
Canadians, and that fact is itself the answer to the question the invasion had been sent
to ask"). The 1818 ending aches. Carleton reads smart and human, not as a foil. The voice
scans are clean: zero em-dashes in prose, zero drama-bold, no chapter-about-itself
meta-narration.

What keeps it from a clean PASS is one gap against the Carillon bar (we are never put IN
the snow the way Carillon puts us in the abatis) and a handful of placement/density
problems at the story's single biggest moment.

---

## BLOCKER

### B1. No "imagine being one of them" passage — we watch the columns, we never join one
Carillon's bar passage stops the clock and puts the reader inside the obstacle: "Imagine
being one of them. You hit a belt of felled trees too thick to run through... The volleys
come from a wall of logs you cannot see over, close enough that you can hear the order to
fire before each one lands." The Quebec draft's nearest equivalents are good but stay in
third-person summary:

> "The shore path was narrow, drifted deep, squeezed between the cliff face and the ice
> of the frozen river."

> "The route led under the walls past the Palace Gate, single file through the drifts,
> under musket fire and grenades from above."

Each is one camera pan, not an immersion. For a battle whose whole texture is a blizzard
night assault, this is the draft's missing signature scene. Add ONE sustained
second-person beat (do not add two; one is the Carillon dose). The best home is
Montgomery's approach, between "squeezed between the cliff face and the ice of the frozen
river" and the carpenters sawing the palisades. Sketch (every concrete detail must be
checked against the fact pack before use; cut any the pack does not carry):

> "Imagine being one of the three hundred. The blizzard is in your face the whole way,
> the river ice groaning on your right, the black cliff going up on your left into snow
> you cannot see through. You walk single file because the path allows nothing else,
> one hand keeping the snow out of your musket's lock, knowing the powder is probably
> soaked anyway and the night's work may come down to the bayonet. Somewhere ahead of
> you, men are carrying ladders. Somewhere above you, on the walls you cannot see, the
> city is awake."

The brief also expects lamps in the storm among the night's sensory furniture; the draft
has signal rockets but no lamps. If the fact pack carries the defenders' lamps (or the
attackers'), hang them in this passage or in the rockets sentence. If the pack is silent,
skip it; do not invent.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. Montgomery's death paragraph is overloaded — the blast never gets its beat
The single most important moment of the battle (the grapeshot blast, the general dead in
the snow) shares one paragraph with: Macpherson and Cheesman, BOTH tradition badges
("Push on, brave boys" AND McQuarters), Campbell's withdrawal order, and the
historiographical defense of Campbell. Each piece is individually well written; stacked,
they smother the death. Specifically, "Tradition likewise names the man at the gun"
arriving immediately after "Tradition fills in the seconds before the blast" turns two
beautifully handled tradition beats into a back-to-back fact-check digression at the
story's peak.

Fix by splitting, not cutting: paragraph 1 ends on the blast and the dead ("Montgomery
was killed instantly, struck through the head. His aide Captain John Macpherson and
Captain Jacob Cheesman died with him, and most of the men beside them went down.").
Paragraph 2 carries the two traditions (the "Push on, brave boys" handling is superb as
written — "nearly everyone who could have heard them died in the same moment" is the
rare badge that ADDS dread — keep it verbatim; vary the McQuarters opener so it is not a
second "Tradition likewise...", e.g. "The other name tradition supplies is the man at the
gun: Hugh McQuarters... the record only says the defenders fired."). Paragraph 3 is
Campbell and the withdrawal.

### S2. Section 1 MEANWHILE restates the paragraph directly above it
"The arithmetic of December 31" lists smallpox / no reinforcement / expiring enlistments /
walls, food, relief fleet — the exact inventory the prose just gave in "That left the
Americans with arithmetic instead of options..." Carillon's MEANWHILEs earn their box by
changing the angle ("Armies are not only numbers"). Re-angle this one rather than recap.
Two candidate angles, both already paid for by the draft: (a) the wager framing — "Every
day of waiting made the defenders relatively stronger" is the one genuinely new sentence
in the box; build the whole box on that single asymmetry (time fights for Carleton, so
the weakest hour of the American army is also its only hour). Or (b) the men's-eye view
of the calendar: what it means to ask soldiers to volunteer for a blizzard assault
seventy-two hours before they could lawfully walk home.

### S3. The 1759 PILL interrupts the assault at maximum tension
The pill to the F&I Quebec page sits between Montgomery's column filing out of Wolfe's
Cove and the Près-de-Ville blast — a nav invitation to leave for a different war in the
last seconds of the general's life. The Wolfe's Cove echo motivates it, but the S2
MEANWHILE ("The general who stayed inside") makes the SAME 1759 argument at a natural
rest point. Move the pill to directly after that MEANWHILE (or to end-of-section). The
assault should run unbroken from the signal rockets to the surrenders. (Carillon's
mid-section pills all sit in setup or aftermath prose, never inside the assault clock.)

### S4. Mid-action dictionary gloss stalls Arnold's wounding
> "At the first barricade in the Sault-au-Matelot (a barricade is a makeshift wall
> blocking a street), Arnold took a musket ball..."

The "a barricade is..." formula is the one gloss in the draft that reads like a footnote
breaking into a firefight (compare the grapeshot gloss, which earns its place by being
vivid: "turns the gun into a giant shotgun"). Either compress to "(a makeshift street
wall)" or, better, seed it back in the plan paragraph where the Sault-au-Matelot is first
named ("through the barricaded streets called the Sault-au-Matelot" with the gloss
there), so the wounding sentence runs clean.

## TRIM

### T1. "...and it deserves to be remembered as one."
> "It was a genuinely respectful gesture toward a fallen enemy, and it deserves to be
> remembered as one."

The second clause is the narrator instructing the reader how to feel — the closest thing
to meta-narration in the draft. The gesture, plainly told (Cramahé paying for the coffin
himself), already does the work. End the sentence at "fallen enemy."

### T2. "What followed says something about the man and about his enemy."
A signpost telling us the next paragraph will be meaningful. Acceptable in the
popular-history register, but the section is stronger opening cold on the body found in
the snow. Optional cut; if cut, open: "On New Year's Day the defenders found Montgomery
in the snow at Près-de-Ville..." (which is already the sentence).

### T3. Section 2's opening paragraph runs ~230 words before its (excellent) button
Geography + plan + both feints + both columns + the betrayal + the storm + the rockets in
one block. The button ("Brown's men fired the signal rockets") is worth protecting; on a
phone the paragraph is a wall. Split after "...and it could be entered from either end
along the shore." (city paragraph / plan paragraph). No prose changes needed.

---

## NIT

### N1. Morgan's lashes beat — passes the brief, one word of tightening available
The legend is correctly told as the man's own performance ("who told a story on himself
ever after"; "Morgan showed the scars") — texture, not aside, and "At the barricade he
was exactly the man the moment required" snaps us back to the fight. It does land
mid-firefight only because Morgan gets no section-1 intro, but the cost is two sentences
and the payoff (who this man is, six years before Cowpens) is worth it. Leave as is, or
trim "ever after."

### N2. The "You shall never blush" beat — works, with an optional deferred echo
As placed (parting promise in section 1), the quote informs more than aches, but the
draft's structure is a planted gun: "Janet remembered, years afterward" quietly sets up
the 1818 veranda, the dead march, and "wife for two years; widow for fifty-three." That
payoff aches on its own and the brief's demand is met by the PAIR, not the first
placement. Optional strengthening: nothing. Resist the temptation to add "he kept his
promise" or any echo at 1818 — the restraint is what makes the veranda paragraph hurt.
Flagging only so the reviser does not "fix" this by moving the quote to section 3, which
would orphan the parting scene.

### N3. Both-sides check — PASSES, worth saying so
Carleton's defense reads as intelligence, not luck or villain-foil: "his plan was simple
and correct. Never come out"; the Montcalm-inversion MEANWHILE ("Patience was the
generalship"); the honorable burial; "treated his prisoners decently." The defenders also
get faces below command level (Chabot and Picard, the mixed Canadian-British picket),
which quietly pre-loads section 3's thesis that Canadians defended the city. No change.

### N4. Voice scans — clean
Grep-verified: zero em-dashes in the prose sections (the ledger's are structural, not
reader-facing), zero bold. The one historiographic aside in prose ("The assault on Quebec
sometimes gets told as a doomed romantic gesture, and it was not") is doing the fact
pack's no-doom-music guard-rail work and earns its keep. "Patience was the generalship"
and "which is to say it ate the campaigning season" are exactly the house voice.
