### THIN (present but underweight)

**T1 — Confederate substitute costs in the South**
The draft covers the Confederate substitution loophole and its abolition. But it does not mention that Confederate substitute prices rose to $3,000 in specie (coin) — far above the Union's $300 commutation. This is a meaningful comparative data point that sharpens the "rich man's war" argument on the Confederate side and distinguishes the two systems. The web search confirms "prices ranged as high as $3,000 in specie and even higher in Confederate currency" (NCpedia). The fact pack's Beat 1 only says substitution was "heavily criticized as a loophole for the well-off" without quantifying it. THIN because the class-inequity argument on the Confederate side is made structurally (Twenty-Negro Law) but not quantitatively for substitution. Adding one sentence with the $3,000 figure (flagged as a range, since sources vary) would strengthen the parallel with the $300 commutation story.

**T2 — Southern Ladies Aid Societies / Confederate civilian relief**
The draft covers the U.S. Sanitary Commission and its women's volunteer network comprehensively for the North. For the South, it mentions women working, including munitions and government clerkships, but does not acknowledge the parallel Confederate relief infrastructure — the Ladies' Aid Societies and soldiers' sewing circles that were the South's civilian-side equivalent. The web search confirms these existed across the Confederacy (7,000–20,000 societies total North and South), operating at local level, sewing uniforms and collecting supplies. The fact pack (Beat 7) acknowledges cross-ref sources but does not include Confederate relief organizations as a distinct beat. This is THIN rather than MISSING because the women's-labor beat is otherwise strong — but the North/South asymmetry in relief coverage is noticeable given the draft's explicit contrast thesis.

**T3 — Morrill Tariff 1861 absent from the Northern boom section**
The draft covers the income tax, greenbacks, war bonds, National Banking Acts, Homestead Act, and Pacific Railway Act. The Morrill Tariff of 1861 (signed March 2, 1861 — protectionist tariff raising rates, enabled by Southern departure from Congress) is the missing piece of the "South-gone Congress" economic picture. The web search confirms it raised average duties significantly and inaugurated 52 years of protectionist policy. The fact pack mentions it only as a parenthetical possibility in the Morrill Land-Grant College Act note. It is not essential in the way the financial instruments are — customs revenue was significant ($345M, 43% of federal tax revenue per one estimate) but it belongs more to the economic-policy story than the civilian home-front story. THIN, not MISSING.

### FACT PACK BLIND SPOTS

**BP1 — Confederate substitute price range ($3,000 in specie)**
The fact pack mentions substitution was expensive and a rich-man's loophole but does not give the Confederate price range. The Union $300 commutation is precisely quantified; the Confederate substitute cost is not. A verifiable range ($300–$3,000+ in specie; higher in Confederate paper) is in NCpedia and other sources and should be in the pack for the author's use.

**BP2 — Southern Ladies Aid Societies as a parallel to the Sanitary Commission**
The fact pack's Beat 7 is North-dominant. It covers the Sanitary Commission fully but has no equivalent Confederate civilian relief beat. The parallel existed and is well documented. Not adding it creates a structural imbalance in the North/South contrast the section is built around.

**BP3 — Morrill Tariff 1861**
Mentioned as an aside in Beat 8's parenthetical list (Morrill Land-Grant College Act note). Given that it passed because Southern members had left Congress, it belongs in the "South-gone Congress passes nationalist agenda" beat — at minimum as a one-line entry with the rate and the revenue figure.

**BP4 — Revenue Act of 1862 (graduated income tax / IRS precursor)**
The draft correctly notes the Revenue Act of 1861 created the first income tax. The fact pack mentions in passing that it "was reworked by the Revenue Act of 1862 into a graduated tax and created the office that became the IRS." This 1862 rework (introducing progressive rates and the Commissioner of Internal Revenue) is historically significant and the fact pack soft-pedals it. Not an essential gap in the draft — the section thesis doesn't hinge on it — but worth a fact-pack note.
