## FIRST-CHECK (ship-blocking)

### (a) Are Native nations first-class actors with their own aims, not props? — PASS (FAIR)

The draft makes this its spine, not an add-on:

- **Opens on Native agency, not empire.** The lede reframes the war's own name: the "Indians... fought on every side of the war, and most of all they fought for themselves, for nations that had held the interior of the continent for far longer than either European empire had existed." This is the strongest possible first-class framing and it is the first thing the reader meets.
- **Their diplomacy is rendered as *their* statecraft and *their* leverage**, not as being used: "they played two European empires against each other... That need was leverage." The collapse is told as the loss of *their* strategic position ("this was a catastrophe before a single shot of the new war was fired").
- **Coalition over king.** The "A Coalition, Not a King" section explicitly dismantles the great-man reading: "there was no supreme commander... a broad, decentralized coalition of many nations, each rising for its own reasons." It names many nations correctly (Odawa/Ottawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Wyandot/Huron, Lenape/Delaware, Shawnee, Seneca/Mingo, plus Miami, Wea, Kickapoo, Mascouten) and their own leaders (Pontiac/Obwandiyag, Guyasuta, Charlot Kaské, the prophet Neolin). This matches modern scholarship (verified: the coalition idea is credited to figures like Guyasuta/Tahaiadoris with Pontiac catalyzing it, not commanding top-down).
- **Their cosmology and motive on their own terms.** Neolin's vision and the Master of Life are presented as a real spiritual cause "an empire could not match," with proper care that the surviving text is a colonial recording ("as remembered by Neolin, as recited by Pontiac, as written down and translated by a colonial hand. It is not a transcript").
- **The closing locks it.** "What has to be held onto is whose war this was and what it was about... not as rebels against a rightful master but as nations defending their own land against an empire that had simply decided it owned them."

At no point are the nations reduced to a backdrop or a tool of either empire. PASS.

### (b) Is the human cost / dispossession named plainly in the section's own prose? — PASS (FAIR)

- **Dispossession is named as the structural heart of the aftermath**, not euphemized: "the dispossession the rising had been fought to stop simply went on, slower than before but unbroken," and "they could not win the longer war over the land itself." The "slow catastrophe" eyebrow and "underneath the concessions lay a catastrophe that no treaty could touch, because it was structural and it was permanent" name the loss directly.
- **The land is named as what the contest was ultimately over** — the lede, the leverage section, and the close all center land and sovereignty.
- **Human cost is itemized for all sides** (the "What the Rising Won" section): ~400 British soldiers, Croghan's estimate of ~2,000 settlers killed/captured and ~4,000 displaced, 200+ Native warriors, plus the smallpox toll. Critically the draft refuses abstraction: "These were not abstractions. They were burned cabins and missing children on one side and bodies left on the portages and around the forts on the other."
- **The smallpox-as-weapon episode is named plainly** as "one of the documented attempts by a European power to use disease deliberately as a weapon of war," with the intent/act/effectiveness distinction handled honestly (verified against Ranlet, HISTORY, ASM: intent and the Trent act are not contested; only the medical result is — the draft says exactly this).

PASS.

### Naming-of-the-war point — HANDLED WELL

The brief asks that, where natural, the section acknowledge that "French and Indian War" centers the British-colonial viewpoint. The draft does this in its first sentence ("When the British colonists named their war, they named it for their enemies") and immediately corrects the erasure ("The 'Indians' were not one enemy. They fought on every side... and most of all they fought for themselves"). Fully satisfied.

### Acadian expulsion — correctly out of scope (NOT a finding)

The first-check lists Acadian expulsion as a "where relevant" item. It is not relevant to *this* section: this theme covers the post-1763 interior (Great Lakes/Ohio) aftermath and Pontiac's War; the Acadian deportations (1755–1764, Atlantic Canada) belong to a different section of the war and the fact pack does not raise them here. Omission here is appropriate, not a failure.

## OTHER FINDINGS (false balance, romanticization, presentism, sanitized/gratuitous violence)

### 1. Empire is not romanticized — FAIR
Amherst is shown as contemptuous and as the author of the smallpox plan; the British "decided it owned them"; the Proclamation Line is named as unenforced and ignored "from the day it was signed." No glamorizing of conquest. Bouquet's tactical win at Bushy Run is reported without valorization and paired with its cost (a quarter of his force). FAIR.

### 2. No false balance on the smallpox episode — FAIR
The draft does not "both-sides" the intent. It states the British tried to use disease as a weapon and the commanding general endorsed it in writing, and confines the debate strictly to medical effect ("The intent is not in question. Only the result is."). This is the correct, evidence-based framing (verified against Ranlet and ASM). The `[VERIFY]` bracket on the "vilest race / meritorious act" wording is appropriately deferred to the fact-checker — note for integration: that bracket and the in-prose "[VERIFY: ...]" at draft line 34 must be resolved/removed before ship, but that is a fact-check/clean-up item, not a framing defect.

### 3. Violence is neither sanitized nor gratuitous — FAIR
Fort captures, Bloody Run ("left the water running red"), Devil's Hole, and the smallpox blankets are reported with enough specificity to convey what happened without dwelling for shock. The Michilimackinac lacrosse ruse is told as clever stratagem and ends on the analytical point ("it worked because the British could not imagine the people they had insulted were already at war with them") rather than spectacle. FAIR.

### 4. Presentism — none material — FAIR
Modern scholarly framing (multi-nation coalition; first major resistance not ending in defeat; precedent for Brant and Tecumseh) is used as historical interpretation, attributed in spirit to "modern scholarship," not as anachronistic moralizing. Verified as a standard, well-supported reading. FAIR.
