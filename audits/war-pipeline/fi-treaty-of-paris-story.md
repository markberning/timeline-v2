# STORYTELLING CRITIC REPORT — "The Treaty of Paris & the Proclamation Line"

Section: French & Indian War, Off-the-Battlefield theme (kind=theme, POLITICS).
Judged as a STORY: hook & stakes, human dimension, pacing, voice, and whether it
avoids dry play-by-play / list-of-facts tedium. House-voice rules enforced
separately below.

**VERDICT: PASS**

This is a strong, well-shaped piece of narrative history. It opens on a real hook,
carries a clear spine (two documents, one promise kept and one broken), gives the
abstraction of treaty-making a human face (Washington scheming under "the pretence
of hunting other Game"), and lands its framing argument without preaching. No
heading falls to NEEDS WORK or REWRITE, and no locked house-voice rule is violated.

---

## HEADING-BY-HEADING

### Lead-in (untitled opening, "In 1763 two pieces of paper...") — STRONG
The single best move in the draft. "Two pieces of paper rearranged the map" is a
concrete, sticky frame that the whole section pays off. The closing beat of the
paragraph — "The nations whose homelands were being signed back and forth were not
in the room for any of it" — plants the framing spine on the first screen and earns
the tragedy at the end. Stakes and human absence established in five sentences.
Nothing to fix.

### A World War That Outlived Its North American Front — STRONG
"North America was just the corner of it that happened to be on fire in the
colonists' own backyard" is exactly the house voice: vivid, informal, true. The
Spain beat is told as a *bet that went bad* ("Spain had entered the war to help
France and instead lost two of its richest colonial cities"), which is story, not
chronology. "Britain held nearly every good card... The only question left was how
much of that haul Britain would keep" sets up the next section with real momentum.
This is how you make a diplomatic-pressure section move.

### The Treaty of Paris, 10 February 1763 — GOOD
The substance is well-handled and the New Orleans irony is genuinely satisfying:
"France was not keeping a foothold on the mainland. It was leaving entirely, just
through two different doors." "France had lost a continent and held on to its sugar
and its cod" is a great close.

One pacing caution (not blocking): the signatory roster in the second paragraph
("grandees with long names: for Britain, John Russell... for France, César
Gabriel de Choiseul... for Spain, Jerónimo Grimaldi... The man steering the French
side overall was the foreign minister, Étienne-François, Duc de Choiseul") is the
one stretch where the piece reads like a list. It is saved by the self-aware
"grandees with long names" wink and by the fact that Choiseul reappears with a real
function in the sugar section, so the names are not inert. Consider trimming Praslin
and Grimaldi to a half-sentence and keeping the spotlight on Choiseul, who actually
does narrative work later. Optional polish, not required.

### A Few Acres of Snow — STRONG
The strongest interior section. "Here is the part that sounds invented and is not:
in London, serious people argued that Britain should give Canada back and keep a
sugar island instead" is a perfect hook — it tells the reader why this dull-sounding
debate is actually wild. Voltaire's sneer is deployed for color, the contested
verdict is handled honestly ("argued then and is argued now"), and the section
respects the fact pack's caution to attribute rather than assert. Human, surprising,
fast.

### Master of the East — GOOD
Does its job as the hinge: triumph that is also a problem ("That was a triumph, and
it was also a problem"). The forward handoff to revolution is correctly brief and
explicitly bounded ("But that is a story for later, on the road to revolution"),
honoring the dedup boundary. The State Department quote is used as evidence, not
filler. Slightly the most expository section, but it is short and earns its place as
connective tissue. No fix needed.

### A Line Down the Mountains — GOOD
Clean reveal of "the second piece of paper," which pays off the opening frame. The
mechanism is explained, not just stated: the no-private-purchase clause is brought
to life with "A frontier speculator could no longer ride west, find a chief, and
trade rum for a valley." That single concrete image does more than the verbatim
clause it follows. Good.

### Why the Line Was Drawn — GOOD
"The Crown did not draw the line out of tenderness" is the right tone, and "A
promise of protection was cheaper than another war" is a sharp, cynical-true line.
The Calloway "scholars disagree" beat handles the contested meaning honestly without
breaking stride. The Pontiac's War reference is named-as-cause and pill-linked, not
re-narrated, exactly per dedup. Solid.

### The Line Nobody Obeyed — STRONG
This is the human-dimension payoff and it lands. Washington as "the clearest window
into how the colonial gentry actually treated the line" turns an abstract policy
failure into a person doing something specific and a little shabby. The "under the
pretence of hunting other Game" detail is the kind of concrete, slightly damning
fact that makes a reader remember the whole section. "If a man as prominent as
Washington was already scheming to grab western land while pretending to hunt, the
line stood no chance" is a clean, earned thesis sentence. Pacing accelerates
correctly toward the consequence.

### A Promise Drawn on a Map — STRONG
The framing spine pays off without sermonizing. "They were not absent from the land,
only from the table" is the line the whole piece was built to deliver. The nations
are named (a representative set, per the fact pack's "don't over-stuff" note),
credited with their own agency ("playing France and Britain against each other...
fighting for themselves rather than for either crown"), and Pontiac is named-and-
linked rather than re-narrated. The closing two sentences — "One of those would
last. The other was already being broken as the ink dried" — are a genuinely good
ending that closes the two-documents frame from the lead. Strong.

## HOUSE-VOICE COMPLIANCE (all locked rules)

- **NO em-dashes in prose: PASS.** The only two "—" characters in the file are in
  the `> quote:` attribution lines (Voltaire and Washington), which is the standard
  quote-attribution separator, not prose. Body prose uses commas, periods, and
  parentheses throughout — including correctly using parentheses for inline
  definitions ("land speculators (men who bought up large tracts...)", "the
  Bourbons", "Obwandiyag"), per the writing rules.
- **No meta-narrator: PASS.** The narrator addresses the reader's curiosity ("Here
  is the part that sounds invented") and stages a deliberate "Step back from the
  diplomats" pivot, but never narrates the act of writing, the section's own
  structure, or "as we'll see / in this section" scaffolding. The "but that is a
  story for later" handoff is content sequencing, not meta-narration.
- **Little bold: PASS.** Zero bold in body prose (the eyebrows and FIGURE/quote
  markers are structural metadata, not styled emphasis).
- **Full-rank/full-name-first: PASS.** People are introduced in full on first use:
  "John Russell, 4th Duke of Bedford," "César Gabriel de Choiseul, Duke of
  Praslin," "Étienne-François, Duc de Choiseul," "King George III," "George
  Washington," "William Crawford," "the writer Voltaire," "the scholar Colin
  Calloway," "the Odawa war leader Pontiac." Side/affiliation tags are applied
  ("(British)", "(French)", "(Spanish)") on first mention of the negotiators and on
  Crawford, consistent with the war person-side-tag rule.
- **Cross-refs as standalone pill lines: PASS.** Both cross-references are on their
  own lines as bare `[Title](/path)` pills — "[The Ohio Country & the Ohio
  Company](/war-french-indian/off-the-battlefield/ohio-company)" and "[Pontiac's War
  & the Aftermath for Native Nations](/war-french-indian/off-the-battlefield/
  pontiacs-war)" — not woven inline into prose.
