# NEWCOMER-CLARITY CRITIC · Bonhomme Richard vs. Serapis draft

*Critic pass on `audits/war-pipeline/rev-bonhomme-richard-draft.md`, 2026-06-12. Reader
model: zero prior knowledge — has never heard "frigate," "broadside," "rake," "strike the
colors," "quarter," or "East Indiaman"; cannot place Flamborough Head, Yorkshire, or the
Texel on a mental map; does not know who John Paul Jones was or what the Continental Navy
was; cannot picture the size difference between a 44-gun ship and a 40-gun ship; does not
know why an American squadron would be off the English coast in 1779.*

---

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

The draft is unusually strong for a first naval page — the author honored the mandate to
define every nautical term on first use, and the grappling-fight sequence is completely
followable even for a reader who has never seen a ship. The strategic logic (why Jones is
here, what the convoy means, what winning actually looks like on both sides) is present and
plain from the first paragraph. The famous-line section handles the evidence chain
precisely. The main clarity problems are: one BLOCKER (the Texel geography, left undefined
until the last section where it matters most), one near-BLOCKER (the 44 vs 40-gun scale is
stated but never made FELT), and a small cluster of lesser items — the "commodore" gloss
arrives late relative to first use, "double-headed shot" goes undefined, the word "consort"
is used before its gloss, "tonnage" is never addressed, and the MEANWHILE geographies
(Leith, Firth of Forth) land cold in Section 1. No structural BLOCKERS on battle sequence,
cast clarity, or naval vocabulary. The cast bios are excellent: Jones, Dale, Landais,
Pearson, and Pearson's mission logic are all first-class.

## BLOCKER

**B1. The Texel: a key geography term used throughout Section 3 but never defined until the
MEANWHILE block, after it has already been load-bearing.**

"Ran east across the North Sea for neutral Holland" appears at the end of Section 2. Then
Section 3 opens: "The squadron limped into the Dutch anchorage of the Texel on October 3..."
— but the word "Texel" first appears in the dossier stats ("Winner" note) and in the
Strategic Locator specs, both before the prose, and the Section 3 MEANWHILE is titled "The
Texel, Holland | The pirate who would not be handed over." The outcome block also uses
"Texel." A newcomer has encountered this name four or five times before reaching the single
sentence that explains it ("the neutral Dutch roadstead of the Texel"). That sentence is in
the MEANWHILE, which many phone readers will never tap.

The first load-bearing prose use is in the Section 3 coda: "The squadron limped into the
Dutch anchorage of the Texel on October 3." The word has already appeared in the dossier
stats, outcome block, and locator captions without a gloss.

- FIX (Section 3, at the first prose use): change "the Dutch anchorage of the Texel" to
  "the Texel, a sheltered anchorage on the Dutch coast where neutral Dutch territory gave
  him cover from the British fleet waiting outside." Then the MEANWHILE title can remain as
  is, since the reader now has the frame.

- SECONDARY FIX (dossier stats/outcome block): the "Texel" in the Winner note and the
  outcome text also land cold. A one-word addition — "the Texel (a Dutch anchorage)" —
  is enough in the dossier. The locator caption already explains it fully, but locator
  captions are not the primary prose read.

## SHOULD-FIX

**S1. The 44 vs 40-gun gap is stated but never made meaningful.**

The dossier sides note gives the Richard as "~40 guns" and the Serapis as "rated 44 guns,
brand new." The fact ledger explains the real disparity: the Serapis's 44 included twenty
heavy eighteen-pounders on her lower deck, while the Richard's "about forty" included only
six old eighteen-pounders, two of which burst at the first shot. The NUMBER gap (44 vs 40)
is almost nothing; the POWER gap is enormous. But the prose never consolidates this into
a single plain sentence that a newcomer can carry into the battle.

Current draft (Section 2): "his lighter guns against her twenty heavy eighteen-pounders on
the lower deck" — this is the right fact, but it comes AFTER the explanation of the burst
guns, in the context of "why Jones had no choice but to grapple." A newcomer who only reads
the dossier stats sees near-parity (44 vs ~40) and may not feel the asymmetry.

- FIX (Section 2, in the burst-guns paragraph, after the burst-guns fact): add one sentence
  consolidating the power gap before the "fight a gunnery duel now" logic. For example:
  "Even before those guns burst, the match had never been fair: the Serapis carried twenty
  heavy eighteen-pounders on her lower deck alone, while Jones's 'about forty guns' included
  only six eighteen-pounders — and two of those had just blown themselves apart." That
  sentence makes the grappling logic feel inevitable rather than dramatic.

**S2. "Consort" used before its gloss.**

Section 2, the Alliance paragraph: "...the Alliance did it to her own flagship. Jones's
report is anguished and precise..." and then the report quote. But the word "consort" first
appears in the dossier note ("The American side note: his own consort raked him from behind")
and in the battle note at the top. The note text uses "consort" cold; the first gloss is
in Section 2 where the Alliance is described as "the ship that was supposed to be helping
her." That gloss is clear but comes by implication, not definition.

The dossier note reads: "Jones lost by every measure on the way to it: two of his big guns
burst at the first fire, his own consort raked him from behind, his ship was shot through
and through..." — this is the very first text most readers see, and "consort" is undefined.

- FIX (dossier note): change "his own consort raked him from behind" to "his own consort
  (the Alliance, the other American warship in his squadron) raked him from behind." The
  rest of the draft can use "consort" freely.

**S3. "Commodore" glossed in Section 1 but used cold in the dossier sides note first.**

Section 1 correctly defines: "Jones, who was commodore (the squadron's commander) in little
more than name." But the dossier sides note for the American side says "cmd: Capt. John Paul
Jones" — this is fine — and the British sides note says "guarding a 40-plus-ship convoy."
The structural problem is that the dossier sides note, which many readers see first, does
not carry the "commodore" term. This is minor because the Section 1 gloss is placed well
and the dossier isn't heavy on the term.

However, the CONCORDAT explanation — "the captains had signed an agreement that left each
of them nearly independent of Jones, who was commodore in little more than name. He could
give orders. Whether his captains obeyed them was, by the paperwork, up to the captains" —
is the essential setup for the Alliance storyline, and it lands perfectly. No structural
problem here, gloss timing is acceptable.

- FIX: NONE REQUIRED. The gloss timing in Section 1 is fine. Mark as PASS.

**S4. "Double-headed shot" never defined.**

Section 2 (Jones concentrating his quarterdeck guns on the Serapis's mainmast): "He had
concentrated those guns on the Serapis's mainmast, firing double-headed shot to bring it
down." The draft's voice compliance note at the end of the fact ledger lists double-headed
shot in the glossed-terms list, but it does not appear in the prose with a definition. The
reader is told the shot-type and the goal but not why that specific shot type was chosen to
bring down a mast.

- FIX (Section 2, at the double-headed shot sentence): "...firing double-headed shot
  (two iron balls linked by a short bar, which spun through the air and cut through mast
  and rigging) to bring it down."

**S5. "Firth of Forth" and "Leith" in the Section 1 cruise paragraph land cold.**

Section 1: "In mid-September he stood into the Firth of Forth meaning to descend on Leith,
the port of Edinburgh, and got within cannon shot before a gale blew the squadron back out
to sea." "Leith" is glossed as "the port of Edinburgh" — that lands fine. But "Firth of
Forth" is used as if the newcomer knows this is a long sea inlet cutting into Scotland's
east coast. It is the CONTEXT that makes the Leith descent audacious (Jones was sailing
into the heart of the Scottish capital's harbor), and the term "Firth" (an arm of the sea,
an inlet) is not obvious.

The Strategic Locator caption handles this with "stood within sight of Edinburgh until a
gale blew it back out," which is cleaner than the prose. But the prose sentence is the
one most readers will get.

- FIX (Section 1): "he stood into the Firth of Forth" → "he sailed up the Firth of Forth
  (the long sea inlet that runs into the heart of Scotland's east coast as far as Edinburgh)
  meaning to descend on Leith, the port of Edinburgh."

**S6. The "gun-rating" scale for a newcomer: what does "rated 44 guns" mean as a SIZE
signal?**

The British sides note defines "frigate" correctly: "a fast, single-gun-deck warship rated
by her guns, here a powerful two-decker carrying 44." But the phrase "a powerful two-decker
carrying 44" may confuse: a newcomer reading "single-gun-deck" then "two-decker" hears
a contradiction (it is two-decks of ship with one gun deck, but the newcomer does not know
that). More importantly, there is no size-anchoring — a reader needs a rough sense of how
BIG a 44-gun frigate was vs. what Jones sailed in.

The dossier notes already handles the Richard's size well: "an old, slow, converted East
Indiaman... her crew swept up from half the ports of Europe." The Serapis description is
"brand-new," "powerful," and "launched only that March." But no sentence ever says, in
plain terms, that the Serapis was approximately the same SIZE as the Richard — the fight
felt even in scale even though it was badly uneven in firepower.

- FIX (British sides note, frigate definition): simplify to "(a fast warship of the line
  carrying 44 guns — roughly the same size as the Bonhomme Richard but far more heavily
  armed, with twenty heavy cannon on her lower deck alone)." This removes the
  confusing "single-gun-deck / two-decker" language and directly sets up the asymmetry.

## POLISH

**P1. "Privateer vs. naval officer" distinction is implicit in Section 1 but the legal
difference never surfaces.**

Section 1 defines "privateering (private armed ships licensed to take enemy vessels for
profit)" — good. But the caricature caption and the Jones bio both lean on Britain's view
of Jones as "not a naval officer at all but a brigand sailing under no recognized flag."
The newcomer never gets a plain sentence on WHY Britain contested Jones's status: he sailed
under Continental Navy commission, which Britain did not recognize as lawful (the United
States was not yet a recognized nation to Britain). This is the legal foundation for the
"pirate" charge and explains why the Texel crisis had real legal teeth.

- FIX (Section 1, at the "Paul Jones the Pirate" paragraph): after "The reputation was not
  entirely unearned," add: "The legal charge was simpler than the moral one: Britain had
  not recognized the United States as a sovereign nation and therefore did not recognize a
  Continental Navy commission as a lawful naval flag. To British admiralty law, Jones was
  sailing under no recognized authority, which made him a pirate by their definition,
  whatever his own understood himself to be."

**P2. The Alliance's ownership is briefly confusing in the dossier sides note.**

"the Continental frigate Alliance (a fast new warship of 36 guns) under the French captain
Pierre Landais" — "Continental frigate" means it was an American ship; "French captain"
means its commander was French. This combination is exactly what produced the crisis
(French captain in an American ship with French money, nearly independent). But a newcomer
reading the dossier sides note does not yet have the frame to distinguish "a French-financed
squadron under American colors" from "American ships with French crews." The sides note
could add a clause: "under the French captain Pierre Landais, who had been given the ship
by France as a gift to the American cause."

- FIX (dossier sides note, Alliance entry): "the Continental frigate Alliance (an American
  warship of 36 guns, given to the cause by France) under the French captain Pierre
  Landais." This primes Landais's peculiar status before the reader hits his full bio.

**P3. The grenade-sailor's yard is not quite physically clear.**

Section 2: "A man worked his way out along a yard, one of the long spars crossing a mast,
until he hung over the Serapis's deck, and dropped grenades down at her open hatches." The
reader is told what a yard is. But "hung over the Serapis's deck" is slightly ambiguous:
why could the man hang over the ENEMY's deck? The ships were lashed together, so the yards
of the Richard (high on her masts) crossed over the Serapis's deck at the grapple. A one-
clause addition would make the geometry snap: "A man worked his way out along a yard, one
of the long spars crossing a mast — the ships were lashed so close that the Richard's
upper yards swung directly over the Serapis's open deck — until he hung over the enemy and
dropped grenades down at her open hatches."

- FIX: add the geometry clause as above, or a simpler version: "the two ships' yards
  overlapping across the locked hulls."

**P4. "Transoms" in the sinking passage is undefined.**

Section 2 (the sinking): "her old timbers, in Jones's words, 'mangled beyond my power of
description.'" — the Jones quote does the heavy lifting here and is fine. But two sentences
earlier: "The Richard's rudder was shot away, her transoms torn out..." — "transoms" is
unexplained nautical vocabulary. Most newcomers will simply skip the word, but a gloss is
easy.

- FIX: "her transoms (the structural timbers across the stern) torn out."

**P5. "Warrant officers" has a brief gloss gap.**

Section 2: "Three of her warrant officers, the gunner among them, decided she was sinking
and that the only sane thing left was to surrender." "Warrant officers" is unexplained. In
18th-century naval usage these were specialist officers (gunner, boatswain, carpenter, etc.)
ranking below commissioned lieutenants. The newcomer may assume "warrant officer" means
something generic, or mistake them for senior officers.

- FIX: "Three of her warrant officers (the specialist petty officers who ran the guns,
  rigging, and hull), the gunner among them..."

**P6. "Squadron vs. fleet" distinction is never explicitly made.**

The Section 1 setup correctly uses "squadron" throughout and the dossier notes say "a
small squadron." But the Section 1 framing of the American naval war mentions "the handful
of ships the Continental Congress could float were never going to fight that fleet for
command of the water." "Fleet" here = the Royal Navy. A newcomer may wonder why Jones
commands a "squadron" rather than a "fleet." One parenthetical would close this:

- FIX (at first use of "squadron" in Section 1): "Jones's squadron (a small task group of
  ships — a 'fleet' in name only, nothing like the Royal Navy's hundreds of warships)." Or
  more simply, a single line in the cruise setup: "A naval squadron was a handful of ships;
  Britain's fleet was the sea itself."

**P7. The miles-first rule: the distances given are all contextual ("a few miles off the
Head") rather than stated numerically.**

The draft uses "a few miles off" for the battle site and "a few hundred yards" for the
distance between the two actions. These informal distances are appropriate and readable. The
only numerical distances are in the locator captions (not prose surfaces). This is fine as
long as no km-first phrasings appear — none do. PASS.

**P8. The "East Indiaman" gloss is excellent.**

The dossier sides note: "The flagship was an old, slow, converted East Indiaman (a merchant
ship built for the long voyage to India)." Section 1 uses it again: "France had bought him
an old East Indiaman, a merchant ship built in 1765 for the long trade run to India, worn
out from two voyages to China." This is correct and well-timed. PASS.
