**Orientation.** This is a SPINE chapter — the McPherson braid for 1777: the year of the great disconnect. Two British armies fight two different wars on the same continent, hundreds of miles apart, each commander sure his own campaign is the main event — Burgoyne grinding south from Canada toward Albany while Howe sails AWAY from him, south to the Chesapeake, to take Philadelphia. ## 1. THE PLAN THAT WASN'T ONE

- **Burgoyne's scheme.** **Lt. Gen. John Burgoyne** ("Gentleman Johnny" — playwright, MP, cavalry showman; in London over the winter of 1776–77 he lobbied past his superior Carleton) submitted his "Thoughts for Conducting the War from the Side of Canada" (February 1777): a main army from Canada up Lake Champlain to **Albany**, a smaller diversionary force down the **Mohawk Valley**, the whole point being to **seize the Hudson corridor and split New England from the rest** — the granary-and-manpower head cut from the body. (HIGH on the plan and its logic; the "strangle New England" framing is Burgoyne's own.)
- **Howe's obsession.** **Gen. Sir William Howe** in New York had his own plan — and changed it repeatedly. His November 1776 plan included a 10,000-man Hudson prong to junction with the northern army; by his letters of **2 and 5 April 1777** he had dropped it: he would take **Philadelphia** — the rebel capital, the largest city in America — **by sea**, and explicitly told London he could no longer support a junction with the northern army. He expected taking the capital to draw Washington into the decisive battle the war kept refusing to produce. (HIGH — the sequence of Howe's plan-revisions is documented in his correspondence with Germain; JAR, "William Howe: Taking the Slow Boat to Philadelphia.")
- **Germain approves BOTH.** **Lord George Germain** (Secretary of State for America, running the war from London at a 6–10-week one-way letter lag) approved Burgoyne's plan in **March 1777** (Burgoyne had it in hand in Quebec by **6 May**) and approved Howe's Philadelphia plan too (Howe received approval **5 June**, while already preparing to embark). On **18 May 1777** Germain wrote Howe approving the Philadelphia expedition while *trusting* it would be executed in time for Howe to cooperate with the army from Canada — a hope, not an order, and it reached Howe only in mid-August, **at sea, past the point of return**. Nobody in London ever issued a positive order making one plan serve the other. (HIGH on dates and the trust-not-order character; phrase the 18 May letter's content as approval-plus-expressed-expectation.)
- **THE "PIGEONHOLE" STORY IS MOSTLY MYTH — handle precisely.** The famous anecdote: a dispatch positively ordering Howe up the Hudson was drafted, but Germain — impatient to leave for his Kent estate — wouldn't wait for a fair copy, and the order was pigeonholed, never sent, dooming Burgoyne. The story traces to a much-later secondhand reminiscence (the Shelburne circle), and 20th-century scholarship working from the full published correspondence rejects it as the explanation: **Howe knew Burgoyne's plan** (Germain sent him a copy of Burgoyne's instructions), Howe had **told London in April he would not support a junction**, and Germain **approved the Philadelphia move anyway**. The failure was a system that let two field commanders file separate war plans and a minister who ratified both without forcing them to mesh — bureaucratic and strategic, not clerical. A missing piece of paper is a better story; it isn't the true one. (HIGH that the disconnect was structural and documented; the pigeonhole anecdote itself DISPUTED — usable only AS a myth, named as one.)
- **The standing frame.** Spell the geometry once: in summer 1777 Britain's two field armies in the Thirteen Colonies moved in **opposite directions** — Burgoyne south toward Albany expecting cooperation, Howe south by SEA toward the Chesapeake, away from him — with the Atlantic too slow for anyone to fix it. **Sir Henry Clinton**, left holding New York with a reduced garrison (~7,000, mostly loyalist and Hessian units), warned Howe to his face that the move was wrong; he was overruled. (HIGH on the geometry and Clinton's objection; Clinton's garrison figure MED — use "about 7,000.")
- **The alliance stake.** France (Vergennes) had been funneling covert aid through Beaumarchais's front company since 1776 but would not move openly without proof the rebellion could win battles and survive. Both 1777 campaigns were therefore being watched from Versailles. **One clause only — `french-alliance` owns the arc.** (HIGH)

## 2. BURGOYNE COMES SOUTH (June–August)

### Ticonderoga abandoned — the fortress that fell to a hill
- Burgoyne left Canada in June with roughly **7,800–8,000 regulars** (about 3,700–4,000 British, ~3,000–3,900 Brunswick and Hanau Germans under **Maj. Gen. Friedrich Riedesel**), plus artillery (~130 guns at the start, most later left behind), several hundred Canadians and loyalists, and ultimately ~**500 Native allies** (numbers fluctuated; ranges only). By 1 July he was before **Fort Ticonderoga** — the "Gibraltar of the North," held by Maj. Gen. **Arthur St. Clair** with an understrength garrison of ~**2,500–3,000**. (HIGH on the shape; all strengths as ranges.)
- **Mount Defiance decides it without a battle.** Southwest of the fort rose **Sugar Loaf hill (Mount Defiance)**, ~750–850 feet, dominating both Ticonderoga and its sister works on Mount Independence. The Americans had left it unfortified — too steep for guns, the assumption ran. Burgoyne's engineers cut a road and dragged cannon up it (the line attributed to his artillery chief **Maj. Gen. William Phillips** — "where a goat can go, a man can go; and where a man can go, he can drag a gun" — is traditional, not documented contemporaneously; attribute as "as the army's story had it"). When the garrison saw the battery on the summit, the position was untenable. (HIGH on the event; the Phillips quote MED/traditional — badge it.)
- **Night of 5–6 July 1777:** St. Clair evacuated — garrison across to Mount Independence and away southeast, supplies by boat to Skenesborough. The British occupied the works on **6 July without a fight**. The rearguard was caught and mauled at **Hubbardton (7 July)** — the only real battle of the episode, a sharp defeat in which Seth Warner's covering force nonetheless got the army away. The American public, fed on "Gibraltar," was stunned; St. Clair was court-martialed (and acquitted, 1778) for saving his army by giving up the fort. George III is said to have burst in on the Queen crying "I have beat them! beat all the Americans!" — a story from Horace Walpole's gossipy memoirs; badge it as Walpole's. (HIGH on dates/events; the George III scene MED — Walpole, secondhand, hostile witness.)
- **The echo:** this is the same ground as the F&I story — in 1758 a British army bled to death in front of these walls at **Carillon** (pill to `/war-french-indian/battles/carillon`); in 1777 the fort changed hands without a battle, twice renamed and twice abandoned. One-beat echo, no more. (HIGH)

### The wilderness slog — Schuyler trades space for time
- From Skenesborough, Burgoyne chose the **overland route** to the Hudson through the drowned lands of Wood Creek rather than backtracking to the Lake George portage. Maj. Gen. **Philip Schuyler** (commanding the Northern Department) made the woods a weapon: ~**1,000 axmen** felled great trees across the track, destroyed upwards of **40 bridges** (one causeway ran two miles), rolled boulders into Wood Creek, burned crops, and drove off cattle. Burgoyne's army — hauling artillery and an enormous baggage train (Burgoyne's personal kit famously filled carts; the champagne-and-mistress flourish is period gossip, badge or omit) — covered roughly **20–23 miles in about three weeks**, closer to **a mile a day**, reaching **Fort Edward** on the Hudson **29–30 July**. (HIGH on the obstruction program and the ~1 mile/day pace as a characterization; exact axmen/bridge counts MED — use "about a thousand" / "some forty bridges"; the dates HIGH.)
- Schuyler got no credit: Congress, with New England delegates who detested him (and with Ticonderoga's fall wanting a scapegoat), replaced him with **Maj. Gen. Horatio Gates** (4 August; Gates took command 19 August). The fairness point — Schuyler's delay bought the time that made September possible — is the modern consensus; say it plainly. (HIGH)

### Jane McCrea — a killing becomes a weapon (27 July)
- **What is solid:** **Jane McCrea**, a young woman from a patriot family but **engaged to a loyalist officer in Burgoyne's army** (David Jones), was taken near **Fort Edward on 27 July 1777** by Native warriors attached to Burgoyne's advance and was killed and scalped; her scalp was brought into the British camp, where Jones recognized her hair. (HIGH)
- **What is NOT solid:** *how* she died. The man blamed (known as Le Loup or Wyandot Panther) was said by one account to have killed her in a quarrel over escort rights/reward; he himself claimed she was killed by a pursuing American party's musket fire. A 19th-century exhumation was reported to show bullet wounds and no tomahawk wound — itself contested. The honest line: killed in the custody of Britain's Native allies; the details beyond that were contested from the first week and still are. (DISPUTED on mechanism — never narrate the tomahawk scene as fact.)
- **What mattered was the story.** Burgoyne demanded the killer's execution, then pardoned him — fearing the loss of his entire Native contingent — which read as proof he would not police his own allies. The killing of a *loyalist's fiancée* became the patriot recruiting poster: a British army that could not protect even its friends' women. The story ran through the New England press and recruiting speeches all August; militia turnout in the following weeks — the flood that swelled Gates's army — was fed by it (effect documented in the recruiting literature; avoid single-cause claims: Bennington and Ticonderoga's shock fed the same surge). **Horatio Gates**, in a public letter to Burgoyne (early September), made her a set piece: a young woman "dressed to receive her promised husband" murdered and scalped by "the famous Lieutenant General Burgoyne['s]" hired killers — propaganda built to travel, and it traveled to London and into the parliamentary opposition. (HIGH on the propaganda career and Burgoyne's pardon; the Gates-letter phrases MED until re-verified verbatim — paraphrase or check before quoting.)

### Bennington — the detachment that never came back (16 August)
- Short of horses, draft animals, and food, Burgoyne sent **Lt. Col. Friedrich Baum** with ~**700–800 men** (dismounted Brunswick dragoons, loyalists, Canadians, Natives, a pair of guns) toward **Bennington** (in the Hampshire Grants — present Vermont) to seize rebel stores and mounts, on intelligence the depot was lightly held. It was not: Brig. Gen. **John Stark** (a Bunker Hill and Trenton veteran who had quit the Continental Army over a passed-over promotion, then raised ~1,500 New Hampshire militia in under a week on the condition he answer only to New Hampshire) was there. (HIGH; Baum's force as the range.)
- **16 August:** Stark, with ~**1,500–2,000** militia plus **Seth Warner's** Green Mountain men arriving late in the day, enveloped Baum's hilltop position (many of Baum's loyalist-recognition assumptions failed him — accounts that armed men slipping around his flanks were taken for friendly loyalists are period and plausible but tellable only loosely). Baum's force was destroyed and Baum mortally wounded; **Lt. Col. Heinrich von Breymann's** ~550–650-man relief column arrived hours too late, was wrecked in a second fight at dusk, and barely got away. (HIGH; strengths as ranges.)
- **Cost:** German/British losses about **900 men Burgoyne could not replace** — commonly given as ~**200 killed and ~700 captured** — plus four guns. American losses ~**30 killed, ~40 wounded**. Stark's famous battlefield line — "There are your enemies, the redcoats and the Tories... or this night Molly Stark sleeps a widow" — is **19th-century tradition**, recorded in variant forms decades later; usable only as "tradition has it," or omit. (HIGH on the loss scale with ranges; the Molly Stark line DISPUTED-as-verbatim.)
- **The hinge it turned:** Burgoyne lost a tenth of his army, got no supplies, and his Native allies — already alienated by his McCrea scolding — began drifting away in numbers. His own assessment to Germain (20 August letter) is the campaign's epitaph in advance: the Hampshire Grants, "a country unpeopled and almost unknown in the last war, now abounds in the most active and most rebellious race of the continent, and hangs like a gathering storm upon my left." In the same letter he told Germain he had had no word from Howe confirming cooperation. (HIGH on the letter's existence/import; the quoted phrase is widely reproduced from the 20 Aug 1777 dispatch — re-verify verbatim at authoring if quoted in full.)

## 3. ORISKANY + FORT STANWIX — THE WESTERN PRONG (August, ONE braided beat)

*(Terse — `native-nations` owns the Iroquois-civil-war story (Oriskany as the confederacy's fratricide). The spine carries only: the third prong existed, it failed, and how.)*

- **The prong:** Brig. Gen. (local rank) **Barry St. Leger** with ~**1,700–2,000** (a few hundred regulars and Hessian jäger, loyalist regiments including John Johnson's Royal Greens and Butler's rangers, and ~800–1,000 Haudenosaunee — largely Mohawk and Seneca — with **Joseph Brant/Thayendanegea**) came east via Lake Ontario and Oswego to take the Mohawk Valley and meet Burgoyne at Albany. In his way: **Fort Stanwix** (rebuilt and garrisoned by ~750 Continentals under Col. **Peter Gansevoort**; the Americans called it Fort Schuyler). Siege opened **2–3 August 1777**. (HIGH; strengths as ranges.)
- **Oriskany, 6 August:** a relief column of ~**800 Tryon County militia** (heavily German-Palatine settlers) under Brig. Gen. **Nicholas Herkimer**, with Oneida allies, was ambushed in a ravine ~6 miles short of the fort by Brant's warriors and loyalist troops. Point-blank, hand-to-hand, neighbor against neighbor and **Oneida against Mohawk/Seneca — the Haudenosaunee confederacy's own civil war beginning inside the American one** (one clause; the theme owns it). Herkimer, shin shattered, propped against a tree directing the fight (the pipe-smoking tableau is tradition — badge or omit), died of the amputation ten days later. American casualties were appalling — commonly given as ~**380–450 of ~800**, among the war's bloodiest small actions; the column never reached the fort. But while the ambushers were away, the garrison sortied and **sacked the nearly empty Native and loyalist camps** — a material and symbolic blow that soured St. Leger's allies on the whole siege. (HIGH on event/shape; casualty figure MED — contemporary counts vary widely, use the range with "commonly given.")
- **Arnold's ruse — tell at its evidence level.** Schuyler (before his removal) sent **Maj. Gen. Benedict Arnold** west with ~900–950 Continentals. Short of men to fight through, Arnold weaponized a prisoner: **Hon Yost Schuyler**, a loyalist-leaning Tryon County man under sentence from a court-martial, respected among the Mohawks, was released (his brother held as surety, his coat shot through for stage dressing) to run ahead and spread word that Arnold was coming in overwhelming force — by the storybook version, answering "how many?" by pointing at the leaves. **The core is documented:** St. Leger's own 21 August report records word of Arnold advancing "by rapid and forced marches, with 3,000 men" (roughly triple the truth) — and an Oneida messenger working the same rumor among the warriors mattered at least as much as Hon Yost. The garnish (the leaves line, the hanging sentence — the court record shows a flogging sentence, apparently embellished later) is 19th-century storytelling; badge it. **22 August:** St. Leger's Native allies quit, and the siege collapsed in a near-rout back to Oswego — tents standing, guns abandoned. Burgoyne's western prong was gone without Arnold firing a shot. (HIGH on the siege's collapse and St. Leger's exaggerated-numbers report; the ruse's theatrical details MED-to-legend — tell with the wink.)

## 4. HOWE SAILS AWAY — THE PHILADELPHIA CAMPAIGN (July–October)

### The armada heading the wrong way (the chapter's signature simultaneity)
- **5–23 July 1777:** Howe embarked ~**15,000–17,000 troops** on a fleet commonly given as ~**225–270 ships** (his brother Adm. Richard Howe commanding); they **sailed from Sandy Hook on 23 July** — the same month Ticonderoga fell and Burgoyne hacked toward the Hudson. Their destination was a secret even Washington couldn't crack for weeks; he marched the army back and forth across New Jersey and Pennsylvania chasing guesses. (HIGH on dates; fleet/troop figures as ranges.)
- Off the **Delaware Capes (29–31 July)** Howe took naval advice that the Delaware River approach was too defended, and stood back out to sea for the **Chesapeake** — adding three sweltering weeks. The fleet beat up the bay and finally landed at **Head of Elk, Maryland (25 August)** — ~55 miles from Philadelphia and, the spine's point, some **400 miles by sea AWAY from Burgoyne** — after roughly a month at sea that wrecked the cavalry and draft horses and cost the whole campaigning season's first half. The braid beat: **Howe's troops were wading ashore in Maryland in the same ten days that Bennington wrecked Burgoyne's flank detachment and St. Leger's siege collapsed.** (HIGH; "400 miles" phrase as "hundreds of miles.")
- When Germain's 18 May letter (§1) caught up with him, hoping Philadelphia would still leave time to help Burgoyne, Howe was at sea and the season was spent. He had also written Burgoyne (April, via Carleton) that he should expect "little assistance from hence." (HIGH on the sequence; the quoted clause MED — widely cited from Howe's correspondence; re-verify verbatim or paraphrase.)

### Brandywine — Long Island again (11 September)
- Washington (~**11,000–14,000**, commonly "about 11,000" effectives) made his stand behind **Brandywine Creek** at **Chadds Ford** on the Philadelphia road. Howe (~**15,500**) replayed his Long Island hit: **Knyphausen's** division demonstrated noisily at the ford while **Cornwallis**, with Howe and ~**8,000–9,000 men**, swung 17 miles north through fords Washington's intelligence said didn't matter (**Trimble's and Jefferis fords**, above the forks), and came down on the American right rear in the afternoon. (HIGH; strengths as ranges. The echo-of-Long-Island framing is standard and fair.)
- The American right (Sullivan, Stirling, Stephen) wheeled and fought hard around **Birmingham Meetinghouse** — this was not the Long Island panic; **Greene's** division and Washington's late-day stands covered a real retreat in order. The 19-year-old **Marquis de Lafayette**, two months into his American service and a volunteer without command, was **shot in the leg** rallying troops — worth one clause for the alliance braid. By dark the army was beaten but intact, falling back toward Chester. (HIGH)
- **Casualties:** British ~**580–600** killed and wounded; American commonly given as ~**1,000–1,300** total (about 200 killed, 500–600 wounded, ~400 captured). (HIGH with ranges — American returns are incomplete; never a hard number.)
- One-beat aftermath: a planned rematch on 16 September dissolved when a downpour soaked tens of thousands of cartridges (the "Battle of the Clouds" — name optional); Congress had already slipped out of Philadelphia in the small hours of **19 September**, warned that the river fords were open. (HIGH)

### Paoli — the bayonets in the dark (night of 20–21 September; ONE beat)
- Brig. Gen. **Anthony Wayne**, detached with ~1,500 men to harry Howe's rear near his own boyhood home, believed his camp near **Paoli Tavern** was secret. It wasn't (local loyalist intelligence). **Maj. Gen. Charles Grey** attacked at midnight with ~1,200 men, **flints removed from the muskets** — bayonets only, no shot to give the camp warning ("No Flint" Grey afterward). The camp was overrun in minutes by firelight. (HIGH)
- **The "massacre" question — per evidence:** American dead commonly given as **~53 killed** (with ~100–200 more wounded/captured; British losses trivial), many by multiple bayonet wounds, which fed sworn atrocity depositions and the name **"Paoli Massacre."** Modern assessments (American Battlefield Trust et al.) read it as a **legal, brutally efficient night attack** — quarter was given, prisoners were taken (~70); the no-quarter charge was propaganda, though the bayonet-by-firelight reality needed little embellishment. Use the name with its quotation marks ON; "Remember Paoli!" became an American revenge cry (and a reason bayonet-trapped British units at Germantown expected none). (HIGH on the event and ~53 dead; the naming-as-propaganda point HIGH per ABT; the revenge-cry detail MED.)

### Philadelphia falls — and decides nothing (26 September)
- **26 September 1777:** Cornwallis marched ~3,000 men into **Philadelphia** unopposed, cheered by the loyalist remnant of a half-emptied city. The largest city in America — the seat of Congress, the symbol Howe had spent the year and the strategy on — was British. (HIGH)
- **Congress did not capitulate; it commuted.** Having left on the 19th, it sat **one day in Lancaster (27 September)**, crossed the Susquehanna, and reconvened in **York, Pennsylvania (30 September)**, where it would sit through the winter — running the war from a courthouse town of ~2,000. In European terms, taking the enemy's capital ended wars. This capital was a meeting; the meeting moved. (HIGH — the chapter's cleanest irony; the York population figure MED, use "a small courthouse town.")
- The river was not even open: the Delaware forts (Mifflin and Mercer) and chevaux-de-frise kept Howe's supply fleet out until late November — months of fighting after the city "fell." One clause, no more (stay in the spine). (HIGH)

### Germantown — the attack that impressed by failing (4 October)
- Howe posted ~**9,000** men at **Germantown**, five miles above Philadelphia. Washington — reinforced and stung — attacked at dawn with ~**11,000** in **four converging columns** on a complicated night-march plan. In **dense fog** thickened by powder smoke, the plan came apart: columns lost the schedule and each other; Stephen's and Wayne's divisions apparently fired into each other in the murk. (HIGH; the friendly-fire incident MED on details — keep it brief and hedged as "in the fog, American units fired on each other.")
- **The Chew House (Cliveden):** ~**120** British under Lt. Col. **Musgrave** barricaded the stone mansion in the American line of advance; instead of masking it, the command (Knox's by-the-book artillery argument prevailing in a roadside council) spent men and the morning's momentum battering it. Roughly a third of American losses fell around the house. (HIGH on the event; the "third of losses" figure MED — "a large share.")
- **Casualties:** American **~152 killed, ~521 wounded, ~400 captured/missing** (per the October return); British **~70–71 killed, ~450 wounded**. A defeat — but the army marched off intact and *proud of itself*, and Washington reported its "spirits" unbroken. (HIGH with the figures so sourced.)
- **The "French impression" claim — handle at evidence level.** It is widely asserted (down to park signage) that Germantown impressed Versailles as much as Saratoga — an army beaten three weeks earlier attacking a victorious enemy's camp. The kernel is real: French observers and ministers tracked American fighting capacity closely, and the autumn's news package that reached Paris carried Philadelphia's loss WITH Germantown's audacity WITH Saratoga's proof. But the strong single-quote version ("nothing struck Vergennes so much as Germantown") is a 19th-century-flavored claim that is **hard to pin to a contemporary document**. State it softly: the French read even this defeat as evidence the American army would not die. (MED at best — never quote Vergennes on it; DISPUTED as a specific attributed remark.)

## 5. SARATOGA (September–October)

### The trap assembling
- Burgoyne **crossed to the west bank of the Hudson on 13–15 September** on a bridge of boats — cutting his own line back to Canada to gamble everything on reaching Albany. Gates's army — now ~**7,000–9,000 and swelling weekly** with militia (the McCrea/Bennington surge) — had dug in on **Bemis Heights**, bluffs commanding the river road, with works laid out by the Polish engineer **Thaddeus Kościuszko**. Burgoyne had ~**6,000–7,000** left and about a month of rations. (HIGH on the shape; all strengths as ranges.)

### Freeman's Farm (19 September) — a British "victory" that bled them
- Burgoyne probed for the American left with three columns. **Daniel Morgan's** riflemen (the corps Washington had sent north — one clause for the braid) opened the fight at a clearing called **Freeman's Farm**; through the afternoon **Arnold's wing** (Morgan, Dearborn, Poor's and Learned's regiments) fought the British center to a standstill in volley-and-countercharge over the farmyard. **Riedesel's** Germans, marching from the river road toward the guns, struck the American flank at dusk and saved the field. (HIGH on the action's shape. **Arnold's personal whereabouts that day are genuinely debated** — on the field rallying units, per participant accounts; held at headquarters, per others. His WING fought the battle either way; don't stage him on a horse at a specific fence.)
- **The arithmetic:** the British held the ground — and lost ~**600** killed, wounded, or captured (heaviest in the center brigade and its officers) against ~**300** Americans. Burgoyne could not replace one man; Gates's camp grew by more than the day's losses within the week. Burgoyne dug in (the **Balcarres and Breymann redoubts**) to wait for help from New York. (HIGH; figures as "nearly 600"/"more than 300.")
- **Clinton's gesture up the Hudson (one beat):** Sir Henry Clinton sortied from New York in early October, **took Forts Montgomery and Clinton in the Highlands (6 October)** and burned **Kingston (16 October)** — too small, too late, and 130+ miles short; his couriers to Burgoyne were caught (one famously swallowing a silver bullet that was retrieved by emetic and hanged him — period-documented, usable as "one was caught with the message in a silver bullet"). The relief that the whole northern plan assumed never existed. (HIGH on the raid and dates; the silver-bullet courier (Daniel Taylor) HIGH as an episode, MED on retelling details — keep to one clause.)

### The Gates–Arnold feud — told straight, no traitor-foreshadowing
- After the 19th, **Gates** reported the victory to Congress **without naming Arnold**, stripped Morgan's corps from his command, and in the ensuing shouting match **relieved Arnold of command**. Arnold — vain, brave, already nursing a year of real grievances over rank — refused a pass to leave and hung around camp without a job. Tell it as a command-politics story between two difficult men, **one of whom was the best combat officer in the army that fall** — and write Arnold as 1777 saw him. NO references forward to 1780. (HIGH on the feud's documented sequence; its tone per standard accounts.)

### Bemis Heights (7 October) — the army comes apart
- Rations failing and the season closing, Burgoyne pushed a **reconnaissance-in-force of ~1,500–1,700** toward the American left. Gates: by his aide's later telling, "order on Morgan to begin the game" (badge as the aide Wilkinson's memoir). Morgan, Poor, and Learned broke the probe in under an hour; Brig. Gen. **Simon Fraser**, rallying the line, was mortally shot by one of Morgan's riflemen — **the named-sniper tradition (Timothy Murphy) is a much-later attribution; say "one of Morgan's riflemen."** (HIGH on the action; Murphy DISPUTED.)
- **Arnold, without orders or a command, rode into the fight** — accounts have Gates sending an aide to recall him, who never caught him — helped sweep the field, led the failed rushes on the **Balcarres redoubt**, then swung to the **Breymann redoubt** on the British extreme right and was in it when it fell; in the last volleys his horse was shot down on him and his **left leg was shattered — the same leg wounded at Quebec**. The Breymann redoubt's fall left Burgoyne's whole camp turnable; he pulled back that night. (HIGH on the wound, the redoubt, and Arnold's unauthorized role; the runaway-aide anecdote MED — Wilkinson again. The "wish it had been my heart"-type deathbed lines and the Boot Monument's pathos belong to later memory, not this chapter.)
- **The day's cost:** British ~**600–900** (including ~240 captured with the redoubt and ten guns) against American ~**130–150**. (MED-HIGH; sources vary — use ranges.)

### Surrender (17 October) — and the Convention Army's weird afterlife
- Burgoyne fell back to **Saratoga** (now Schuylerville) in rain, was enveloped by an American force grown to ~**17,000–20,000** with militia still arriving, and opened talks. He extracted paper terms striking for a beaten army: not a "surrender" but a **"Convention"** — his troops to march to Boston, sail home, and not serve in North America again (a fig leaf with a hole in it: they would free others to serve, or could themselves be rotated). **17 October 1777:** roughly **5,800–6,200** British and German troops grounded arms at the meadow by the Hudson; American bands by tradition did NOT jeer; Gates received Burgoyne with courtesy ("the fortune of war, General Burgoyne" / "I shall always be ready to bear testimony that it has not been through any fault of your Excellency" — the exchange is reported in early accounts; badge as "as the story was told"). (HIGH on date and the number range; the courtesy-exchange wording MED/traditional.)
- **The afterlife (one beat):** Congress, smelling the fig leaf (and after Burgoyne refused a troop list and grumbled about breach), **repudiated the Convention** — the army never sailed. The **"Convention Army"** sat a year around Boston, then in November 1778 was **marched ~700 miles to Charlottesville, Virginia**, leaking deserters and escapees the whole way (hundreds slipped off, many melting into the American population); by war's end only a fraction remained in custody. A treaty-shaped object that became a long captivity — tell it dry. (HIGH on repudiation/march; attrition described loosely, not with fake precision.)
- **Scale check for the close:** with Ticonderoga's garrison, Bennington, the campaign's battles, and the Convention surrender, Britain's northern army of ~10,000 (regulars plus auxiliaries) had simply ceased to exist. (HIGH as characterization; phrase the aggregate loosely.)

## 6. THE BRAID — YORK, PARIS, VALLEY FORGE (October–December)

- **Congress in exile keeps legislating.** At **York** the displaced Congress did the year's most underrated work: on **15 November 1777** it adopted the **Articles of Confederation** (sent to the states for ratification) — the rebel government wrote its constitution while homeless. One beat; `idea-of-independence` owns the arc. (HIGH)
- **The news races to Paris.** Massachusetts put the Saratoga dispatches on the brigantine *Perch* within hours (sailed ~31 October); the courier, **Jonathan Loring Austin**, landed at Nantes ~30 November and reached **Franklin at Passy on 4 December 1777**. The scene every account keeps because the sources do: Franklin, braced for confirmation that Philadelphia had fallen, calling out before Austin dismounted — *is Philadelphia taken?* — "Yes sir," and then Austin's: **"But sir, I have greater news than that. GENERAL BURGOYNE AND HIS WHOLE ARMY ARE PRISONERS OF WAR!"** (the exchange comes from Austin's own later account via early biographers — badge as "as Austin told it"). **Within 48 hours Vergennes's office asked the American commissioners to renew the alliance conversation; France committed to recognition in mid-December; the treaties were signed 6 February 1778.** One beat and OUT — `french-alliance` owns the treaty arc; the spine carries only the hinge: the army lost in the Adirondack woods purchased a navy. (HIGH on the courier chronology and the December–February sequence; the dialogue MED/as-told.)
- **Franklin's quip for the other capital:** told that Howe had taken Philadelphia, Franklin: **"I beg your pardon, sir — Philadelphia has taken Howe."** Reported early and often; still an anecdote — badge it. (MED — "the story went.")
- **Valley Forge (19 December, one beat).** Washington's army — ~**11,000–12,000** men, perhaps a third without serviceable shoes — marched into winter quarters at **Valley Forge**, 18 miles (29 km) from a British army wintering warm in the capital. The institution story (the hunger, Steuben, the drill, the army that marched out) belongs to `army-from-nothing`; the spine carries only the year-end freeze-frame: the losing army intact and encamped, the winning army holding a city that had stopped mattering. (HIGH; strength as range. Steuben arrives Feb 1778 — do NOT narrate him into December.)
- **The Conway Cabal — at its documented level, no more.** The fall's whispering campaign: Brig. Gen. **Thomas Conway** (Irish-born French-service officer, vocal Washington critic) wrote Gates praising him at Washington's expense; Gates's aide **James Wilkinson**, drunk, leaked a line; it reached Washington, who on **9 November** fired a one-sentence note at Conway quoting it back: in Conway's reported words, "Heaven has been determined to save your Country; or a weak General and bad Councellors would have ruined it." Congress then made Gates **president of the new Board of War (27 November)** and Conway **inspector general (December)** — Washington's critics riding Saratoga's prestige into supervisory posts over him. **Modern consensus: a loose network of grumblers (Mifflin, Rush, some delegates), not an organized plot to depose Washington — no motion to remove him was ever made**; the affair collapsed in early 1778 (one clause, don't narrate ahead). The word "Cabal" is the survivors' label, not a description; use it with tongs. (HIGH on the documented letters/appointments; the quoted Conway line is as-quoted-by-Washington — Conway denied the wording; DISPUTED as Conway's verbatim sentence. "Loose network, not conspiracy" per Mount Vernon/ABT.)

## 7. CLOSE — A CAPITAL FOR AN ARMY

- The 1777 ledger, told flat: **Britain took Philadelphia — and got nothing for it.** Congress governed from York; the Continental Army stood intact at Valley Forge; the city would be evacuated within a year (do NOT say so here — stay in 1777; the close can say "a city that decided nothing"). **Britain lost an entire field army** — surrendered with its general, its guns, and its band playing, the first time in the war and a thing European opinion could measure. (HIGH)
- The strategic close: the disconnect of §1 ran to its conclusion — **two campaigns, two prizes, and only one of them was real.** Howe got the capital; Burgoyne needed the army Howe took to get it. And the proof France required — that this rebellion could destroy a British army in the field — sailed for Nantes on a fast brigantine while the Convention Army marched the other way, into a captivity that outlasted the war. (HIGH as framing.)
- End on the simultaneity freeze-frame (the chapter's method, one last time): York's borrowed courthouse; the snow starting at Valley Forge; Burgoyne's veterans tramping toward Boston under guard; and at Passy a printer from Philadelphia reading that his city was lost and the war, quite possibly, won. (Directive)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **The Germain "pigeonhole" story is mostly MYTH — the chapter must get this right.** The tale of the positively-worded order to Howe left unsigned and filed because Germain wouldn't miss his trip to Kent is a much-later, secondhand anecdote. The documented record: Howe told London in April 1777 he would NOT support a junction; Germain knew both plans and approved both (March–May); his 18 May letter to Howe *hoped* for cooperation but ordered nothing and arrived too late to matter. The true failure is duller and worse — a war run by post across an ocean, with no one empowered (or willing) to make two field marshals serve one plan. Usable AS a myth, explicitly named as one; never as the explanation.
2. **Jane McCrea — separate the documented from the contested.** Documented: taken near Fort Edward 27 July 1777 by Native warriors attached to Burgoyne's army; killed; scalped; scalp recognized in the British camp; Burgoyne demanded the killer's death and then pardoned him to keep his Native allies; the patriot press and Gates made her the campaign's recruiting story. Contested: HOW she died (quarrel-and-tomahawk vs American pursuing-party musket fire; the 19th-century exhumation reports are themselves disputed). Never narrate the tomahawk scene as fact; the propaganda career — a loyalist's fiancée, killed by the king's allies — is the verified story. Vanderlyn's 1804 painting is propaganda's afterlife, not evidence (see images).
3. **"Paoli Massacre" — keep the quotation marks.** ~53 Americans killed in a flints-out midnight bayonet attack, many with multiple wounds — but quarter was given and ~70 prisoners taken; the no-quarter atrocity charge was propaganda (per American Battlefield Trust's assessment and the sworn-deposition record read critically). Use "the Americans called it a massacre, and the name stuck"; tell the brutality straight without endorsing the legal charge.
4. **Hon Yost Schuyler — documented core, legendary garnish.** Core (solid): Arnold released a court-martialed loyalist sympathizer to spread exaggerated numbers ahead of his relief column; an Oneida messenger seconded the rumor; St. Leger's own 21 Aug report shows the inflated "3,000 men"; the Native contingents quit and the siege collapsed 22 August. Garnish (badge or drop): the death sentence (record shows flogging), the coat shot through for effect, the pointing-at-the-leaves answer — 19th-century storytelling.
5. **Arnold told straight — ZERO traitor-foreshadowing.** In 1777 Arnold is the best combat general on the continent and everyone north of Albany knows it: Stanwix relieved by bluff, the wing at Freeman's Farm, the unauthorized charge at Bemis Heights, the shattered leg. The Gates feud is real and documented (the unmentioning report, the stripped command, the shouting). No winks at 1780, no "would later," no Boot Monument pathos. Also: his exact whereabouts during Freeman's Farm are genuinely debated — don't stage him.
6. **No Saratoga-as-destiny.** Saratoga did not "win the war" and France did not ally because of one battle alone (the decision rode a year of covert aid, naval rearmament, and ministerial argument; Germantown's showing and Philadelphia's loss were in the same dispatch pouch). State the hinge precisely: Saratoga gave Vergennes the proof he needed to do what he already wanted. Equally: don't let the chapter read 1777 backward — for most of the year the rebellion was LOSING (Ticonderoga, Brandywine, Paoli, the capital).
7. **The Convention Army — a fig leaf, then a captivity; tell it dry.** Burgoyne negotiated "convention" terms (march to Boston, sail home, never serve in North America again) that would have freed equivalent troops for America; Congress found reasons to repudiate (Burgoyne's refusal of a muster list among them) — legally shabby on the American side and strategically obvious; say both. The army never sailed: a year near Boston, a ~700-mile winter march to Charlottesville in Nov 1778–Jan 1779, and years of attrition by escape and desertion. Keep to one beat; loose numbers only.
8. **Quote hygiene:** Stark's "Molly Stark sleeps a widow" — 19th-century tradition in variant forms, never contemporary; Phillips's "where a goat can go…" — traditional; Gates's "order on Morgan to begin the game" and the recalled-aide story — Wilkinson's self-serving memoir decades later; the Gates–Burgoyne courtesy exchange at the surrender — early but secondhand; George III's "I have beat all the Americans!" — Walpole gossip. All usable ONLY with attribution-as-story. Timothy Murphy as Fraser's killer — much-later attribution; say "one of Morgan's riflemen."
9. **Numbers discipline.** Use ranges everywhere: Burgoyne's start ~7,800–8,000 regulars (+auxiliaries); Ticonderoga garrison ~2,500–3,000; Bennington German losses ~200 killed/~700 captured, Stark ~1,500–2,000; Oriskany American casualties ~380–450 of ~800 (MED — contemporary counts vary badly); Howe's fleet ~225–270 ships / ~15,000–17,000 troops; Brandywine American losses ~1,000–1,300, British ~580–600; Germantown per the verified return (152/521/~400) vs British ~70/~450; Freeman's Farm ~600 vs ~300+; Bemis Heights ~600–900 vs ~130–150; surrendered at Saratoga ~5,800–6,200; Valley Forge ~11,000–12,000. Where sources differ, the pack's range governs.
10. **Both sides as reasoning actors.** Howe's Philadelphia logic (take the capital, force the decisive battle, rally the loyalists Galloway promised) was a real theory wrongly weighted, not idiocy; Burgoyne's plan made sense ON PAPER given 1776's lessons; Germain was running a war at a six-week letter lag. The McCrea pardon was a real dilemma (execute an ally's warrior and lose the contingent — he chose wrong, but choose is the verb). No buffoon-Burgoyne caricature — his men kept fighting for him to the end; the London-society "Gentleman Johnny" mockery gets one wink at most.
11. **Native allies are actors, not props.** On both fronts Native nations made their own strategic choices (joining, quitting after Bennington and the Stanwix camp-sacking, the Haudenosaunee fracture at Oriskany). One-clause discipline here; `native-nations` owns the full story — but never write "Burgoyne's Indians melted away" as if weather; they withdrew, for reasons.
12. **Stay in 1777.** No Monmouth, no Philadelphia evacuation (June 1778), no Steuben drill-mastery (he reaches Valley Forge Feb 1778), no Arnold-at-Philadelphia, no treaty celebration (signed 6 Feb 1778 — the chapter may land on the news reaching Paris and the door opening, nothing further). The Conway affair's 1778 fizzle gets one clause.
