# Storytelling Critic — "Years of Disaster" (French & Indian War, Chapter 2)

**Target voice reference:** `improvising-a-war/page.tsx`

---

## Overall Grade: GOOD

Strong factual spine, clean section-to-section flow, and a few passages that genuinely sing. The draft earns its structure. What holds it below STRONG: the opening self-narrates its own chapter ("this is the second chapter of the military story… and the shape is this"), the Acadian section's numbers pile up before the human story lands, and the Fort William Henry section slogs through procedural logistics before it earns its emotional payoff. No house-voice violations (no em-dashes in prose, no meta-narrator asides, no over-bolding) — clean on that front.

---

## Section Grades

### Hook / Intro paragraph
**GOOD**

Opens on action and consequence. "It sent the regular army" is a nice dry beat. The inventory of disasters — army destroyed, victory that didn't take its objective, a people removed, two forts lost — works as a shape-of-the-chapter tease.

**Flag:** The second paragraph is a meta-narrator statement that nearly crosses the line:
> "This is the second chapter of the military story. It carries the shape of the disaster years and links down to the battles rather than re-fighting them. And the shape is this."

Compare the target voice, which does exactly this — *"This is the first chapter of the military story… What it carries is the shape of the whole thing"* — but the target buries it mid-paragraph after a substantive opening beat about slavery. Here the structural disclaimer is its own standalone paragraph, which makes the reader feel like they're reading a table of contents instead of falling into a story. Merge it with the preceding paragraph or cut the framing and let the sections speak for themselves.

---

### Braddock Marches West
**STRONG**

The best section in the draft. The Shingas exchange is the draft's single best human moment — a question, an answer, a consequence — all in three sentences. "He did not need their Help" and then Shingas spending three years gutting the frontier: cause and effect told through people rather than through abstractions. The road-that-outlasted-the-campaign is a good detail. Washington sick in a wagon is excellent texture.

**One note:** The Congress of Alexandria parenthetical — "(April 14, 1755)" — and the four-pronged plan unfold in a list-y way. The target voice integrates dates inline without stopping to inventory. Consider whether all four prongs need naming here vs. in the battle links below; the Beauséjour prong could just appear when the Acadian section opens.

---

### Catastrophe at the Monongahela
**GOOD**

The 70 percent casualty figure is devastating and lands correctly. Washington's four bullets through his coat and the letter quote are strong. The wagon-over-the-grave image is the draft's best single sentence.

**Needs work — two passages:**

1. The French force enumeration — 891 total, 637 Native, 108 regulars, 146 militia, listing six nations — is the kind of arithmetic the reader can't hold. The target voice uses round numbers and focuses on the human fact: "the great majority of them Native warriors" is enough; the six-nation roll call is reference material, not story. Cut or compress to one sentence.

2. "What happened next was less a battle than a slaughter, and it happened because British doctrine had no answer for the ground." This is telling-not-showing. The next two sentences DO show it (dense mass, fire from cover, officers on horseback). Flip the order: open with the image, then name the lesson.

**Beaujeu's death:** "Beaujeu was shot dead in the opening minutes" — fine, but the draft has already named him as the commander; the reader needs one more beat here. The target voice uses a single detail that does double work. "Beaujeu charged out in full dress uniform to inspire his men and was shot dead in the first volley" would earn his death rather than just report it.

---

### The Hudson Held at Lake George
**GOOD**

Hendrick / Theyanoguin is the strongest character in this section, and the draft treats him well. The Mohawk-on-both-sides beat — "the same people on opposite sides of the same field" — is exactly the kind of line the target voice reaches for. Good.

**Pacing sag:** The section front-loads Dieskau's numbers (200 grenadiers, 600 militia, 700 Native allies) before the reader is emotionally invested in the outcome. The Braddock section earned its numbers by first earning Braddock. Dieskau just appears with an inventory. A single human detail — his European general's certainty that frontier militia would break under formed troops — would open the section with stakes rather than a ledger.

**Flag — thin stakes on the objective:** The Monongahela section ends with consequence cascading through Native diplomacy. Lake George ends with "Theyanoguin was dead, and the man who had held the Mohawk alliance together was not replaceable." That's the right note, but it arrives too fast. One sentence on what losing Hendrick *would mean going forward* — that the Iroquois Confederacy's lean toward Britain now rested on thinner credit — would land the consequence as the Braddock section does with Shingas.

---

### Le Grand Dérangement
**NEEDS WORK**

The Acadian section has two problems working against each other: it delays the human story behind the procedural cause (oath refusal, Lawrence's order, the roundup) and then floods the reader with displacement arithmetic before the dying lands.

**The hook is passive:** "While armies maneuvered, Britain made war on a population of farmers." Strong sentence, but the next four paragraphs explain why the Acadians refused the oath rather than opening with the uprooting itself. The target voice drops the reader into the scene first, explains the mechanism second.

**Suggested structural reorder:** Open with the ships — a concrete image of families loaded at gunpoint onto transports — one sentence. Then the backstory of forty years of accepted neutrality. Then the oath demand and refusal. The reader understands *why* the refusal was coherent and *how* Britain responded, in that order, but the human fact of the deportation has already been established.

**Number fatigue:** The paragraph listing 2,000 to Massachusetts, 1,100 to Virginia, 1,000 to Maryland, 700 to Connecticut reads like a census table. The point is dispersal across an ocean — one or two destinations is enough. The Cajun origin paragraph earns its place and should stay; it is the draft's best long-range consequence beat.

**The "what to call this" paragraph** is good — the draft doesn't force a verdict and lets the facts carry weight. "The bare facts need no inflation" is the right ending note. Keep it.

---

### The War Goes Global, and Oswego Falls
**GOOD**

The Diplomatic Revolution set-up is efficient. "France's answer in North America arrived the same year, and his name was Montcalm" is the draft's best chapter-opening line.

**Flat passage:** The Fort Oswego section itself is the draft's most procedural — garrison strength, casualty counts, cannon captured — without a human beat to anchor it. Mercer killed by a cannonball while inspecting the walls is in there, but it passes in half a sentence. Compared with Braddock dying in the road and Washington's letter, Mercer's death is a logistics footnote. The section ends on a strong strategic note (Iroquois wavering after a French win) but the middle sags.

**One line to add:** After Montcalm takes Oswego in two days with 30 killed against the British garrison's 80–150, the reader should feel the efficiency of it — how effortless it looked to every watching nation. "Two days, thirty men, and a lake" is the kind of compressed beat the target voice would use here.

---

### Fort William Henry
**STRONG** (with one fix owed)

The best-structured section after Braddock. The setup — Montcalm's 33-nation coalition, the sick garrison, Webb never coming — builds properly. The warning letter Montcalm handed to Monro is an extraordinary detail and the draft uses it correctly. The Ian Steele historiography paragraph is needed given how loaded the Cooper myth is, and the draft handles it without becoming academic. The smallpox closing — the side that won paid for it in a currency no European army could have inflicted — is the draft's best closing line.

**One fix:** The first sentence of the massacre paragraph is analytical before it is human:
> "The Native nations in Montcalm's army had not crossed half a continent for a European general's idea of an honorable surrender."

This is exactly right as a statement — but open with the scene first. One concrete image of what actually happened before the explanation of why. The "how many died" paragraph is already the corrective to legend; the paragraph before it should earn that correction with physicality.

**Flag — Johnson's quote on Webb:** "the only Englishman he ever knew who was a coward" is a great line and the attribution is appropriately hedged ("a remark passed down through later accounts"). Keep it.

---

### Britain at the Bottom
**GOOD**

Good summary beat. The Pitt arrival is efficiently handled. "A line passed down by his admirers" is the right hedge on the famous quote.

**Weak ending:** "What came next would look nothing like what had come before" is a tease that tells the reader they're about to get a better story without giving them any of it. The target voice ends sections on a human or concrete note — "They had improvised their way into it. Now they would have to learn how to fight it." Consider: what was Pitt actually doing at the end of 1757 that would "look nothing like what came before"? Name one thing. End on that, not on the promise of a turn.

---

### Meanwhile card
**GOOD** — efficient and accurate. "Pitt's Turn" correctly frames the bridge to the next chapter.

## House-Voice Compliance

- **Em-dashes in prose:** None found. Clean.
- **Meta-narrator asides:** The second-paragraph structural disclaimer ("This is the second chapter") is borderline — compare with how the target voice handles the same move. Flag for softening, not a hard violation.
- **Over-bolding:** None.
- **Cross-refs:** Pill links appear as standalone lines, not buried inline. Correct.

---

## MUST-IMPROVE

1. **Acadian section structure** — open with the ships, not the oath rationale. Compress the displacement statistics. The human story is buried under the procedural explanation.

2. **Monongahela enumeration** — the six-nation roll call and the 891/637/108/146 arithmetic read as footnotes dropped into a story paragraph. Round numbers and human facts only; reference-level detail belongs in the battle link.

3. **"Britain at the Bottom" closing** — "would look nothing like what came before" is a promise, not an ending. Name one concrete thing Pitt actually changed and end on that.

4. **Intro structural disclaimer paragraph** — merge with the preceding paragraph or cut. The self-narration stalls the opening before the story starts.

---

## NICE-TO-HAVE

- **Dieskau** needs one human beat before his army inventory — his European general's certainty about frontier militia, or something similar.
- **Fort Oswego** middle sags; the two-days-and-thirty-men compression would fix it without adding length.
- **Theyanoguin's death** could do one more sentence on what his absence means going forward — the Iroquois lean now resting on thinner credit — rather than ending on the death itself.
- **Beaujeu** could earn his death rather than just report it (one detail about his charge).
