## 1. Dates / Location / Theatre

- **Dates:** April 30 – May 6, 1863 (main fighting May 1–6; turning march April 27–30). The campaign as a whole is often dated April 27 – May 6. [S1][S2]
- **Location:** Spotsylvania County, Virginia. The crossroads of **Chancellorsville** (not a town — a single large brick house, the **Chancellor house**, at a road junction). [S1][S2]
- **Theatre:** Eastern Theater. Army of the Potomac (North) vs. Army of Northern Virginia (South), roughly 10–12 miles west of Fredericksburg. [S1]
- **Result:** Confederate (South) victory. [S1][S2]

## 2. Forces & Commanders (each tagged SIDE)

### North — Army of the Potomac
- **Maj. Gen. Joseph Hooker (North)** — overall commander, Army of the Potomac. [S1][S2]
- **Maj. Gen. Oliver O. Howard (North)** — XI Corps (the corps crushed by Jackson's flank attack). [S1][S3]
- **Maj. Gen. John Sedgwick (North)** — VI Corps (the Fredericksburg / Salem Church front). [S1][S4]
- **Maj. Gen. Daniel Sickles (North)** — III Corps (held Hazel Grove, withdrawn May 3). [S1]
- **Maj. Gen. Darius Couch (North)** — II Corps; senior subordinate Hooker refused to hand command to after being concussed. [S1]
- **Maj. Gen. Carl Schurz (North)** — division, XI Corps (one of the more disciplined stands during the rout). [S1]

### South — Army of Northern Virginia
- **Gen. Robert E. Lee (South)** — overall commander. This is widely called **"Lee's perfect battle" / his masterpiece**. [S1][S2]
- **Lt. Gen. Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson (South)** — Second Corps; led the flank march; mortally wounded May 2. [S1][S3][S5]
- **Maj. Gen. J.E.B. "Jeb" Stuart (South)** — cavalry commander who took over Jackson's Second Corps after the wounding (his first infantry command). [S1]
- **Maj. Gen. Jubal Early (South)** — left at Fredericksburg with the small holding force (~9,000–11,000). [S1][S6]
- **Maj. Gen. Lafayette McLaws (South)** — division detached to Salem Church to stop Sedgwick. [S1][S4]
- **Brig. Gen. Robert Rodes (South)** — division that led the dusk assault and "rolled completely over" the XI Corps. [S1]
- **Col. E. Porter Alexander (South)** — artillery; massed ~30+ guns at Hazel Grove on May 3. [S1]

### Strengths (Hooker's ~2:1 edge — figures vary by source; give the RANGE)
- **North:** **~97,400 (ABT) to ~133,868 (Sears/Wikipedia infobox)** men. The ~134k counts the full force brought to the field at the start; ~106,000 at Chancellorsville proper plus ~28,000 on the Fredericksburg/Salem Church front. 413 guns. [S1][S2]
- **South:** **~57,400 (ABT) to ~60,298 / ~60,892 (Wikipedia)** men. 220 guns. [S1][S2]
- **Bottom line for prose:** Hooker held roughly a **2:1 numerical advantage** (≈130,000 vs ≈60,000). Lee won anyway. Use the ~130k vs ~60k framing; flag that exact "engaged" counts differ. [S1][S2]
- **Local imbalance reversed by maneuver:** in the May 1 fighting near Chancellorsville the Federals were actually outnumbered in the immediate sector, roughly **48,000 to 30,000** in the Confederates' favor — Sears's point that Hooker's caution wasn't pure timidity. [S1][S9]

## 4. Geography (relative positions for map accuracy)

- **Rappahannock River** — the main east–west river line; Fredericksburg sits on its south bank. Hooker's army started on the **north** bank. [S1]
- **Rapidan River** — tributary joining the Rappahannock from the west; Hooker's turning column crossed it after the Rappahannock to come in behind Lee. [S1]
- **The fords (west → east):**
  - **Kelly's Ford** — on the Rappahannock, far upstream (west); where the V, XI, XII Corps first crossed. [S1]
  - **Germanna Ford** and **Ely's Ford** — on the Rapidan; the same column crossed here next, then marched down to Chancellorsville. [S1]
  - **U.S. Ford** — on the Rappahannock, closer in; two II Corps divisions crossed here; later the army's lifeline back across. [S1]
  - **Banks' Ford / Scott's Ford** — downstream near Salem Church; Sedgwick's VI Corps retreated north across pontoon bridges here May 4–5. [S4]
- **The Wilderness (of Spotsylvania)** — dense second-growth scrub forest, grown up over abandoned charcoal/iron furnaces. Choked artillery and large-unit maneuver; favored the defender and concealed Jackson's march. Chancellorsville crossroads sits in it. [S1][S3]
- **The two roads (east–west axis through the Wilderness):**
  - **Orange Turnpike (Orange Plank Road runs roughly parallel just south)** — the corridors connecting Fredericksburg ↔ Chancellorsville ↔ Orange. Jackson's flank attack came down/along the **Orange Turnpike** into the Union right; Jackson was shot on the **Orange Plank Road**. [S1][S3]
- **Catharine Furnace** — an iron furnace south of Chancellorsville; landmark on Jackson's flank route. Furnace proprietor **Charles C. Wellford** showed Jackson's mapmaker **Jedediah Hotchkiss** a concealed road. The **23rd Georgia (South)** fought a rear-guard delay here against Sickles's probe. [S1][S3]
- **Hazel Grove** — commanding high open ground SW of Chancellorsville; held by Sickles (North), **abandoned on Hooker's order May 3** — a gift to Lee. Alexander (South) massed guns there. [S1]
- **Fairview** — Union artillery height just NE of Hazel Grove; the Confederate guns at Hazel Grove dueled and overpowered the Federal guns at Fairview, which fell ~9:30 a.m. May 3, collapsing the Union line. [S1]
- **Wilderness Church** — landmark on the XI Corps front, where Jackson's assault struck. [S3]
- **Salem Church** — ~4 miles west of Fredericksburg on the Orange Plank Road; where McLaws/Early (South) stopped Sedgwick's VI Corps May 3–4. [S4]
- **Fredericksburg / Marye's Heights** — Sedgwick's front; **Second Battle of Fredericksburg**, May 3, Sedgwick stormed Marye's Heights (the same wall that slaughtered the Federals in Dec 1862) and drove Early off, then advanced west toward Salem Church. [S1][S4]

**Relative layout (for the mapmaker):** Fredericksburg on the right (east) on the Rappahannock; Chancellorsville crossroads ~10–12 mi west in the Wilderness; fords strung along the rivers to the north and west; Hazel Grove/Fairview just southwest/south of the crossroads; Catharine Furnace further south on Jackson's loop; Salem Church between Fredericksburg and Chancellorsville on the Plank Road.

## 5. Sequence (turning points)

1. **Hooker's bold turning march (Apr 27–30, North).** Rather than re-attack Fredericksburg head-on, Hooker sent three corps (V, XI, XII) on a wide swing — cross the Rappahannock at Kelly's Ford, then the Rapidan at Germanna and Ely's Fords — to come in **behind** Lee's left, concentrating at Chancellorsville by Apr 30. Two II Corps divisions crossed at U.S. Ford. Sedgwick's VI Corps (with I Corps) demonstrated below Fredericksburg to pin Lee. A genuinely excellent plan; Hooker reportedly boasted Lee "must ingloriously fly" or be destroyed **[VERIFY exact wording]**. [S1][S2]
2. **Hooker loses the initiative (May 1, North).** Union columns advanced east out of the Wilderness toward open ground. Jackson's lead elements hit them. Despite holding the advantage, **Hooker ordered a pullback into the Wilderness** — surrendering the open ground where his numbers and artillery would have told. A subordinate said he came back convinced "my commanding general was a whipped man" **[VERIFY exact wording/speaker]**. First turning point: the battle's psychology flips. [S1]
3. **Lee's first audacious division (May 1, South).** Outnumbered ~2:1, Lee split his army — left ~9,000–11,000 under Early at Fredericksburg, took the rest west to face Hooker. [S1][S6]
4. **Lee's SECOND division — Jackson's flank march (May 2, South).** Lee divided the already-divided army again. **Jackson took ~28,000–30,000 men (his whole Second Corps) on a ~12-mile march** beginning ~7–8 a.m., looping south past Catharine Furnace on concealed roads (found via Wellford/Hotchkiss) to reach the **exposed Union right**. Sickles spotted the column and probed at Catharine Furnace; the 23rd Georgia bought time; Hooker misread the move as a retreat. [S1][S3]
5. **The XI Corps disaster (May 2, ~5:30 p.m.).** Jackson's corps burst out of the woods on the **Orange Turnpike** into Howard's XI Corps (North) — facing the wrong way (south, not west), unentrenched, cooking supper. Rodes's division "rolled completely over them." The corps disintegrated; Confederates drove ~1.25 miles toward Chancellorsville before dark and disorganization stopped them. Second turning point. [S1][S3]
6. **Jackson mortally wounded (night of May 2, South).** Scouting forward on the **Orange Plank Road** for a night attack, Jackson and staff were fired on in the dark by their **own men — the 18th North Carolina (South)**. Three balls struck him; his **left arm was shattered and amputated** the next day. [S1][S5]
7. **Stuart takes command (South).** Cavalryman **J.E.B. Stuart** assumed command of the Second Corps — his first infantry command — and renewed the assault May 3. [S1]
8. **Hazel Grove / Fairview artillery (May 3).** Hooker **ordered Sickles off Hazel Grove**, the key high ground. Alexander (South) immediately massed ~30+ guns there and overpowered the Union guns at Fairview (fell ~9:30 a.m.) — one of the rare times Confederate artillery dominated in Virginia. The Union line around Chancellorsville collapsed inward. [S1]
9. **Hooker concussed (May 3, ~9:15 a.m., North).** A Confederate cannonball struck a **pillar of the Chancellor house** Hooker was leaning against, knocking him senseless (likely concussion) for over an hour. He refused to relinquish command to Couch. Union decision-making stayed paralyzed. [S1]
10. **Sedgwick: Second Fredericksburg & Salem Church (May 3–4, North/South).** Sedgwick's VI Corps stormed Marye's Heights May 3, drove Early off, and pushed west — only to be stopped at **Salem Church** by McLaws (and then Early returning), May 3–4. Lee, with Hooker passive, **turned part of his army around** to deal with Sedgwick. Sedgwick withdrew north across Banks'/Scott's Ford May 4–5. [S1][S4]
11. **Hooker quits (night of May 5–6, North).** With Sedgwick repulsed and his own nerve gone, Hooker recrossed the Rappahannock to the north bank. Campaign over; Lee had won despite the odds. [S1][S4]

## 6. The Cause — Slavery (SHIP-BLOCKING throughline)

Per `feedback_name_the_cause`: the section's own prose must name slavery as the cause. Documented hooks:

- **The war's stakes were now explicitly emancipation.** The **Emancipation Proclamation took effect January 1, 1863** — four months before Chancellorsville — declaring enslaved people in the rebelling states free and committing the Union war effort to ending slavery. Chancellorsville is fought *after* the war became, openly, a war over slavery's survival. [S7]
- **Lee's victory was the springboard to invade the North — into the teeth of that new policy.** Buoyed by Chancellorsville, Lee moved north in June 1863 (the Gettysburg campaign). His army was shadowed by **6,000–10,000 enslaved people** serving as cooks, teamsters, blacksmiths, hospital hands, and officers' "body servants" — and on free soil those men were at risk of liberation; some Confederate units also seized free Black Pennsylvanians and sent them south into slavery. The Confederate war machine ran on enslaved labor, at the front and behind it. [S8]
- **Why the South fought at Chancellorsville at all** traces directly to defending the slaveholding social order the Confederacy was founded to preserve — the same throughline every Civil War section must name in its own voice (Lost-Cause omission is an auto-fail). [S7][S8]

## 7. Human Cost (named people, documented detail)

- **Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson (South)** — shot by the 18th North Carolina the night of May 2; **left arm amputated May 3**; moved ~27–30 miles to the Chandler plantation outbuilding near **Guinea (Guiney's) Station**; developed **pneumonia** and **died May 10, 1863, age 39.** Lee on hearing of the amputation reportedly said the army had lost its right arm (see quotes). [S1][S5]
- **The XI Corps (North)** — largely German-American ("Dutch") regiments, scapegoated afterward as the "Flying Dutchmen" though poorly posted by their own command; ~2,500 casualties in minutes. Human texture: men were cooking supper, deer and rabbits reportedly bolted out of the woods ahead of Jackson's line as the first warning **[VERIFY the wildlife detail — common in accounts, confirm before printing]**. [S1][S3]
- **The Wilderness fires** — wounded of both sides burned to death in brush fires set by shellfire and musketry in the dry scrub; a recurring, documented horror of fighting in this terrain. (Vivid but **[VERIFY exact source/extent before quoting figures]**.) [S1]
- **Scale:** ~30,000 casualties in roughly six days, in tangled woods that hid the dead and trapped the wounded. The South's ~13,000 came out of an army half the size of Hooker's — and one of them was Jackson.

## 9. Significance / What It Changed

- **Lee's tactical masterpiece — "Lee's perfect battle."** Outnumbered ~2:1, he won by dividing his army twice and out-maneuvering a larger, well-positioned foe. The high-water mark of Lee's generalship. [S1][S2]
- **But a Pyrrhic one.** ~13,000 irreplaceable casualties (a fifth of his army) and, above all, **Stonewall Jackson** — Lee's most aggressive corps commander, the engine of the flank attack. Lee never found an equal replacement; the Second Corps was reorganized before Gettysburg. [S1][S2][S5]
- **It set up Gettysburg.** Confidence from Chancellorsville convinced Lee to invade the North again in June 1863 — the campaign that ended at **Gettysburg (July 1–3, 1863)**, the Confederacy's decisive defeat in the East. Many historians draw a straight line from Jackson's death to the failures of Lee's reorganized command at Gettysburg (e.g., Ewell on the first evening). [S1]
- **For the North:** another humiliating Eastern defeat with superior numbers; cost Hooker his command (replaced by Meade just before Gettysburg). Confirmed the Army of the Potomac's command crisis. [S1]
