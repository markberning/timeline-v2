**✅ CONFIRMED** — "perhaps 80,000 men" phrase.
*Source: Wikipedia "Anaconda Plan"*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — ~3,000 miles (Scott's framing) vs ~3,500 miles (5,600 km) as implemented, 12 major ports.

**⚠️ UNSUPPORTED — "Anaconda Plan" attribution:** The draft says "A loud faction of Union generals wanted blood now… They mocked it. They likened it to the coils of an anaconda." This is technically accurate — the plan was widely derided and the coils-of-an-anaconda image did come from critics — but one notable and well-sourced detail is omitted that affects framing: it was specifically **McClellan himself** who originated the snake comparison (not anonymous "generals"), and **Northern newspaper editors then seized on it** to popularize "Anaconda Plan" as the mocking label. This is a framing weakness, not a factual error — flagged for the SHOULD-FIX list. The draft's caution against inventing "a single quoted speaker" was appropriate for a direct quote, but McClellan's authorship of the image is actually *confirmed by multiple sources* and should be credited.
*Source: Wikipedia "Anaconda Plan"; americanhistorycentral.com "Anaconda Plan"; abc27.com "May 3, 1861: Winfield Scott and the Anaconda Plan"*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Farragut captured New Orleans April 1862, Grant took Vicksburg July 1863; historians debate decisiveness of seaward blockade alone.
*Source: Britannica "Anaconda plan"*

### Mallory's gamble

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Stephen Mallory as CS Secretary of the Navy.
*Source: USNI Naval History Magazine "Proving the Power of Iron Over Wood" (Apr 2012)*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Mallory quote: "I regard the possession of an iron-armored ship as a matter of the first necessity" + second quote about traversing the coast and preventing all blockades.
The draft dates this as "in the spring of 1861, weeks into the war" — this is correct per CONTESTED #3 guidance (most sources give May 10, 1861; some give May 8 or an earlier April 26 letter).
*Source: USNI Naval History Magazine "Proving the Power of Iron Over Wood" (Apr 2012)*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Gosport Navy Yard abandoned April 20, 1861; *Merrimack* burned to waterline and scuttled; Confederates secured yard the next day (April 21).
*Source: Wikipedia "USS Merrimack (1855)"; ABT "The Gosport Navy Yard in the Civil War"*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Hull raised by May 30, 1861.
*Source: Wikipedia "CSS Virginia"; Wikipedia "USS Merrimack (1855)"*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Rebuilt as CSS *Virginia*; casemate ironclad defined; iron ram; commissioned February 17, 1862; length ~275 ft; draft ~21–22 ft (Wikipedia gives 21 ft exactly, but the 21–22 range is established in the fact pack and used by multiple sources).
*Source: Wikipedia "CSS Virginia"; Wikipedia "Battle of Hampton Roads"*

**⚠️ UNSUPPORTED — Virginia armor description:** The draft states "Her armor was about four inches of iron (two two-inch plates) backed by roughly two feet of iron and pine." The four-inch total and two-inch-plate construction are correct for the casemate *sides*. However, the backing is described in the Wikipedia article as "24 inches (61 cm) of oak and pine in several layers, topped with two 2-inch layers of iron plating" — so the "two feet" is 24 inches of *wood* (oak and pine), not "iron and pine." The draft's phrasing "two feet of iron and pine" is ambiguous and could be misread as a mix of iron and pine, when it is specifically 24 inches of *wood* with *iron* on top, not interspersed. This is a minor but real imprecision; the numbers are right but the material composition could mislead.
*Source: Wikipedia "CSS Virginia"*

### Both ships gone by Christmas

**❌ WRONG — Tattnall's rank and title:** The draft calls him "Captain Josiah Tattnall III (Confederate)." His actual rank when he commanded and destroyed CSS Virginia was **Flag Officer**, not Captain. Multiple authoritative sources (NHHC, The Mariners' Museum, Wikipedia "Josiah Tattnall III," Wikipedia "CSS Virginia") consistently call him "Flag Officer Tattnall" or "Flag Officer Josiah Tattnall III." He was appointed Flag Officer and Commander of Confederate Naval Forces on March 25, 1862 — weeks before he ordered the Virginia's destruction on May 11, 1862. Calling him "Captain" is a rank error.
*Source: Wikipedia "Josiah Tattnall III"; The Mariners' Museum "Flag Officer Josiah Tattnall: Last Commander of CSS Virginia"; Wikipedia "CSS Virginia"*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Virginia scuttled/blown up May 11, 1862, near Craney Island; couldn't escape up James River due to deep draft; destroyed rather than surrendered.
*Source: Wikipedia "CSS Virginia"; Wikipedia "Battle of Hampton Roads"*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Monitor: near-flush deck unseaworthy in open water; foundered in storm under tow off Cape Hatteras NC; sank December 31, 1862; 16 crew lost.
*Source: Wikipedia "USS Monitor"; NOAA "Loss of the USS Monitor"; USNI "The Lost Men of the Monitor"*

**❌ WRONG — Monitor wreck discovery date:** The draft states "Her wreck was finally located in 1973." This is **partially wrong**: the wreck was *initially discovered* in August 1973 by Duke University researchers (correct), but it was not confirmed/positively identified until **April 1974** — five months later, after intensive study of the visual evidence. The discovery expedition was 1973; formal identification was 1974. More importantly, the draft's claim is an acceptable approximation (the 1973 expedition is the discovery event), but the wreck was also designated as a national marine sanctuary on **January 30, 1975** — and the turret was not raised until **August 5, 2002**, not in 1973. The draft correctly separates "wreck found 1973" from "turret at The Mariners' Museum" without implying the turret was at the museum since 1973, so this is borderline. The date "1973" for the find is correct as far as the discovery expedition goes; confirmation was April 1974. Flag for SHOULD-FIX as potentially imprecise: "located in 1973 and confirmed in 1974" is more accurate.
*Source: NOAA "Discovery and Designation" (Monitor National Marine Sanctuary); Duke University "Duke Researchers Locate USS Monitor"*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Monitor turret at The Mariners' Museum, Newport News (raised 2002, conserved there).
*Source: The Mariners' Museum and Park "USS Monitor Conservation"; NOAA Monitor National Marine Sanctuary*

**✅ CONFIRMED** — Global ironclad arms race; HMS *Warrior*, French *Gloire*; "monitor" became a ship-type.
*Source: Wikipedia "Battle of Hampton Roads"; NHHC "The Battle of Hampton Roads"*

## MUST-FIX (every ❌ and material ⚠️)

### ❌ ERROR 1 — Tattnall's rank (WRONG title, MUST correct)
**Draft phrase:** "Captain Josiah Tattnall III (Confederate) ordered her destroyed"
**Correct:** Flag Officer Josiah Tattnall III — he held the rank of Flag Officer (appointed March 25, 1862) when he ordered CSS Virginia's destruction on May 11, 1862. "Captain" is his earlier Confederate Navy commission; he had been promoted to Flag Officer before taking command of Virginia.
**Correction:** Change "Captain Josiah Tattnall III (Confederate)" to "Flag Officer Josiah Tattnall III (Confederate)"
**Source:** Wikipedia "Josiah Tattnall III"; Wikipedia "CSS Virginia"; The Mariners' Museum "Flag Officer Josiah Tattnall: Last Commander of CSS Virginia"; NHHC biography

### ❌ ERROR 2 — Lincoln quote status (UNVERIFIED, treat as apocryphal)
**Draft phrase:** "Lincoln himself, shown Ericsson's bizarre turret concept, is said to have remarked, 'It strikes me there's something in it.'" [with inline [VERIFY] flag]
**Problem:** Independent web research found this quote surfacing primarily in a 1991 TV movie (*Ironclads*, IMDB) and popular retellings. It cannot be verified against Lincoln's papers or any primary document. The ABT page that the fact pack cites as the source does not itself provide a primary citation. This appears to be an apocryphal quote that has circulated in popular history.
**Correction:** Cut the Lincoln quote entirely, OR recast without quotation marks: "Lincoln, shown Ericsson's radical design, was reportedly intrigued." Do not present in quotation marks as a real Lincoln statement.
**Source:** IMDB "Ironclads" (1991); absence of primary Lincoln papers citation across all sources checked

### ⚠️ IMPRECISION 1 — Virginia armor backing described as "iron and pine"
**Draft phrase:** "Her armor was about four inches of iron (two two-inch plates) backed by roughly two feet of iron and pine."
**Problem:** The backing is 24 inches of oak and pine (wood), with two 2-inch iron plates on top. The phrase "two feet of iron and pine" implies a mixture that could mislead — the backing is *wood* (oak and pine), not a mix of iron and wood.
**Correction:** "Her armor was about four inches of iron (two 2-inch plates) backed by roughly two feet of oak and pine."
**Source:** Wikipedia "CSS Virginia"

### ⚠️ IMPRECISION 2 — Monitor wreck "located in 1973"
**Draft phrase:** "Her wreck was finally located in 1973"
**Problem:** The Duke University expedition discovered the wreck in August 1973, but *positive identification* wasn't confirmed until April 1974. "Located in 1973" is a defensible shorthand but imprecise.
**Correction (optional):** "Her wreck was discovered in 1973 and confirmed in 1974" — or simply leave as-is and accept as the standard shorthand; this is a judgment call.
**Source:** NOAA "Discovery and Designation" (Monitor National Marine Sanctuary); Duke "Duke Researchers Locate USS Monitor"
