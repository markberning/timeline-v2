## spring-hill · November 29 · The night the army walked past

### How Hood lost his prey before he ever fought for it

To understand why roughly twenty thousand men charged two miles of open field into a wall of dug-in guns at dusk, you have to start the night before, on a road in the dark.

Back up first to why any of this was happening in Tennessee at all. In September 1864, **Atlanta fell** to the Union army of Maj. Gen. William T. Sherman (North). The Confederate Army of Tennessee — one of the South’s two great field armies — had failed to hold it, and its commander, Lt. Gen. **John Bell Hood (South)**, was left with a hard choice: chase Sherman, or do something else. He did something else. Rather than follow Sherman (who was, in fact, about to turn the other way and march for the sea), Hood drove his army *north*, gambling that an invasion of Tennessee and a threat toward the Ohio River might pull Sherman back, rally a sagging cause, and win back ground the South had lost. It was a long shot dressed up as a strategy.

The shot only worked if Hood could catch and destroy the Union force in his path before it reached safety. That force belonged to Maj. Gen. **John M. Schofield (North)**, commanding a combined column — his own Army of the Ohio plus an attached corps, something on the order of twenty-seven thousand men. Schofield was falling back north toward Nashville, where Maj. Gen. **George H. Thomas (North)** was concentrating roughly twenty-five thousand more Federals behind heavy fortifications. The arithmetic was simple and brutal for Hood: if Schofield and Thomas joined up inside the Nashville works, Hood’s already-outnumbered army had no realistic road to victory. He had to smash Schofield *in the open*, alone, before the two halves of the Union force became one.

On November 29 he very nearly did. Hood out-marched Schofield — he peeled most of his army off on a flanking march, crossed the Duck River east of Columbia, and by nightfall **Confederate troops had reached the Columbia Pike at Spring Hill: the exact road Schofield’s army had to use to retreat north.** A flanking march, here, just means swinging wide around an enemy’s side instead of hitting him head-on; Hood had swung wide and landed *behind* his quarry, astride its only escape route. On paper, Schofield was trapped.

And then he wasn’t. Through a cascade of command failures that night — disputed orders, confusion in the dark, and Hood’s own premature confidence that the Union army was already bottled up — the Confederates never closed the road and never attacked. All through the night of November 29–30, **Schofield’s entire army and its wagon train marched up the Columbia Pike and slipped past**, in some accounts close enough to the sleeping Confederate camps to see their fires. Nobody stopped them. By dawn, Schofield had reached the town of **Franklin**, about twelve miles north, and put his men to digging in.

Hood woke up to find his prey gone. The American Battlefield Trust describes him that morning as “wrathy as a rattlesnake,” accusing his own army of cowardice. What he did next — and *why* — is one of the war’s genuine historical arguments, and it’s worth being honest about where fact ends and interpretation begins. Many historians read the assault Hood was about to order as an act of fury and humiliation, a punitive “prove yourselves” charge aimed at the army he blamed for Spring Hill. Hood himself gave a colder, strategic reason: with Schofield this close to the safety of Nashville and no distance left to flank him again, Hood decided to make one more attempt to overtake and destroy the Union army before it got behind its walls. Both readings survive in the sources. What is *not* in dispute is what the decision produced.

> **MEANWHILE — Sherman, marching the other way.** It’s easy to picture Hood and Sherman locked in a chase, but they weren’t. While Hood drove north into Tennessee, Sherman (North) had turned his back on him entirely and started his March to the Sea across Georgia. Hood’s whole campaign was, in part, a bid to make Sherman *care* — to pull him back north by threatening his rear. Sherman declined to care. He left Thomas (North) to deal with Hood and kept marching. Franklin is what that gamble cost the men who had to execute it.

## the-open-ground · November 30 · Twenty thousand men, two miles, no cover

### Why this charge was suicidal before the first shot

To see why Franklin became a slaughter, you have to stand for a moment where Hood’s soldiers stood and look at the ground.

The town of **Franklin** sits on the south bank of the **Harpeth River**, which loops around its north and east. That detail matters more than it sounds, because it means the river was *behind* the Union line — Schofield’s (North) army had its back to the water, with only the bridges and fords as a way out. That’s exactly why Schofield fortified instead of just running: he needed to hold a perimeter long enough to get his wagon trains across the Harpeth before he could safely withdraw the rest of the way to Nashville. His men dug a strong arc of **works** — the soldiers’ word for field fortifications, meaning a ditch fronting an earthen wall thrown up high enough that an attacker has to climb it under fire — curving south and southwest of the town, from the river on one side around to the river on the other. It was a bridgehead: a defended semicircle guarding the southern approaches and the crossings to its rear. At the center, behind the main wall, the Federals had even added a second “retrenchment” line about two hundred feet back — a fallback wall behind the first one.

The **Columbia Pike** ran straight north into the town and punched through the Union line at its center. The defenders had deliberately left a **gap in the works where the Pike passed**, so wagons and troops could move through — and that gap, with the retrenchment line waiting behind it, would become the place everything broke open. Just inside the line, on the west side of the Pike, stood the **Carter House**, the home that sat at the literal center of the Union position; it was the headquarters of Brig. Gen. **Jacob D. Cox (North)**. A little to the east of the Pike, right at the line, stood the **Carter cotton gin** (a barn-like building for cleaning cotton), forming a small salient — a bulge in the line that juts toward the enemy and catches fire from three sides.

Now the Confederate problem. Hood (South) formed his army on the open plain about two miles *south* of those works, and to reach them his men had to advance north across roughly two miles of open, gently rolling farmland — no woods, no walls, no cover, in full daylight view of the dug-in Federals the entire way. **A frontal assault** — marching infantry straight at a fortified position head-on, rather than slipping around its flank — is the costliest attack in the tactical book even in good conditions. These were not good conditions. And Hood ordered it anyway, over objections from his own subordinates. His cavalry commander, Maj. Gen. **Nathan Bedford Forrest (South)**, had found a crossing of the Harpeth and argued for a flanking move to turn Schofield out of his position instead of charging it. Maj. Gen. **Benjamin F. Cheatham (South)**, whose corps would lead the main assault, is reported to have warned that he did not like the look of the fight — the enemy held an excellent, well-fortified position. Hood overruled them both.

Then came the detail that turned a long-shot attack into a near-certain massacre: **there was essentially no artillery preparation.** When the Confederates made their famous charge at Gettysburg the year before, roughly a hundred and fifty guns hammered the Union line for about two hours first, trying to soften it up before the infantry stepped off. At Franklin, Hood sent his men forward behind only a battery or two. That’s why the battle earned its nickname — **“the Pickett’s Charge of the West”** — but the honest comparison is that Franklin was *worse*: a larger assault, across roughly twice the open ground, into stronger works, with almost no guns to clear the way, timed to hit right at dusk.

Around four in the afternoon, with the sun setting about half an hour later, some twenty thousand Confederates stepped off and started walking north into the open.

## the-cotton-gin · The five hours · The breach at the Carter House

### Where the Army of Tennessee bled to death

For a few minutes, it looked like it might work — and the reason why is one of the cruelest accidents of the day.

Out in front of the main Union line, south of the works, sat two brigades belonging to Brig. Gen. **George D. Wagner (North)**, posted in an exposed advanced position. When the Confederate wave reached them, they were overwhelmed and fled back north toward the safety of the works — running, in effect, straight up the same ground the attackers needed to cross. The defenders on the main line **held their fire to avoid shooting their own retreating men**, so the last half-mile of the Confederate advance went largely uncontested. Wagner’s broken brigades partly masked the Union guns at the worst possible moment, and the attackers rode that lull right up to the wall. At the center, troops under Maj. Gen. **Patrick R. Cleburne (South)** and Maj. Gen. **John C. Brown (South)** drove through the deliberate gap at the **Columbia Pike** and into the Carter House yard. For one moment the Confederates were *inside* the Union line.

It didn’t hold, and the reason it didn’t is a single colonel’s stubbornness. Col. **Emerson Opdycke (North)**, a brigade commander, had **flatly refused** to put his men out on the exposed forward line earlier in the day; he posted his brigade instead about two hundred yards behind the Carter House, in reserve. When the Confederates burst through the gap, Opdycke’s reserve was exactly where it needed to be. He counterattacked straight into the breach, gathered up rallied troops from Wagner’s broken brigades and the Carter House defenders, and **drove the Confederates back out of the works**. The one Confederate penetration of the line was sealed shut almost as fast as it had opened. Maj. Gen. **David S. Stanley (North)** was wounded helping rally the broken center near the Carter House; Col. **Arthur MacArthur Jr. (North)** — the “Boy Colonel,” and the father of a future WWII general — fought there too and was shot but survived.

And then, with the breach closed, the rest of the battle became the thing the open field had promised: not a breakthrough but a grinding, point-blank slaughter against the parapet. Along the line — and worst of all at the **cotton gin** and the Columbia Pike — the fighting went hand-to-hand, muzzle to muzzle, in the dark, for something like **five hours**. Confederate brigades came up against the ditch and the earthen wall and were simply shot to pieces, rank after rank, with nowhere to go but forward into the guns or back across the open ground they’d just crossed. This stretch of dirt around the cotton gin is where the Army of Tennessee lost its officer corps. It is where **Cleburne (South)** fell, on foot after his horse was shot, somewhere in front of the cotton gin he’d been driving toward.

When it was over, Schofield (North) did the thing that makes Franklin such a strange “victory.” Holding the town had never been his goal — getting his army and his wagons safely across the Harpeth and on to Nashville was. Once the trains were over the river, he carried out the plan he’d had all along: **after dark, his army quietly withdrew across the Harpeth and marched north**, leaving the battlefield to Hood. Hood (South) held the field at dawn, which let him call it a win. It was a hollow one. His army was wrecked, and it limped north in Schofield’s wake toward Nashville — where, two weeks later, it would be destroyed outright.

> **MEANWHILE — the killing field for generals.** Franklin did something no other day of the war managed: it killed Confederate *generals* faster than Gettysburg, Antietam, Chickamauga, or Spotsylvania. Five Confederate generals were killed outright in the assault — Cleburne (South), John Adams (South), States Rights Gist (South), Hiram Granbury (South), and Otho Strahl (South) — and a sixth, John C. Carter (South), was mortally wounded and died ten days later. Six general officers killed or mortally wounded in a single afternoon, plus roughly a half-dozen more wounded and one captured — somewhere around fourteen Confederate generals went down as casualties, along with some fifty-five regimental commanders. An army can replace privates. It cannot quickly replace the men who lead it, and Franklin gutted that leadership top to bottom in five hours.

## what-it-cost · The reckoning · The dead on the porch, and what they died for

### Six generals, a son a few hundred yards from home, and the thing the South would not give up

The numbers tell one story; the names tell the real one.

In roughly five hours, the Confederates lost on the order of **six to seven thousand men** killed, wounded, captured, and missing — the most-cited total is about 6,250, though Hood (South) himself reported a much lower figure and the count is genuinely contested, so a range is the honest way to give it. Around **1,750** of those men were killed outright. Against that, Schofield’s (North) Union loss came to about **2,300**. Hood, in other words, lost roughly three times what his enemy did, while *failing* to take the position — and the men he lost were exactly the ones his army could least afford. The Army of Tennessee, the sources say, had shattered itself beyond any possibility of doing so again.

The human core of it sat on a back porch the next morning. On December 1, 1864, the bodies of **four Confederate generals — Cleburne (South), Granbury (South), Adams (South), and Strahl (South)** — were laid out together on the rear porch of **Carnton**, the McGavock plantation that had become the largest Confederate field hospital in the area. Hundreds of wounded men were carried into that house during the battle and all the night after; when the rooms filled, the yard was used until it too filled with the wounded and the dead. Carnton’s wooden floors are still stained from it. Cleburne — Irish-born, the army’s most respected combat general, the man they called “the Stonewall of the West” — had reportedly told one of his brigadiers before the charge that if they were to die, they should die like men. He went forward on foot after his horse was killed and never came back.

A few hundred yards away, the Carter House gave the battle its most unbearable image. Capt. **Theodrick “Tod” Carter (South)** was a Confederate officer and the son of the very family whose house anchored the Union center. Captured the year before, he had escaped Federal captivity and made his way back; Franklin was his first time home in about three years. His duties didn’t require him to charge, but he rode forward with the attack — by tradition, calling out that he was almost home — and was shot down a few hundred yards from his own front yard. His family, who had spent the night of the battle sheltering in the cellar while the bloodiest fighting of the war raged through their yard and around their outbuildings, found him on the field afterward, carried him into the house, and there he died two days later, inside the home he had been trying to reach. The Carter outbuildings still carry hundreds of bullet holes, among the most battle-scarred structures of the entire war.

And now the thing that has to be said plainly, because the porch full of dead generals can make Franklin feel like nothing but a brave, doomed waste — and it was a great deal more specific than that. **These men died to preserve slavery.** Hood’s army had marched into Tennessee on behalf of a Confederacy whose reason for existing was the ownership of human beings, and the proof of that is standing right here on this battlefield, in the person of its most famous dead man.

Ten months before he was killed at the cotton gin, in January 1864, **Patrick Cleburne (South)** circulated a written proposal to the Army of Tennessee’s commanders arguing that the Confederacy should **arm enslaved men as soldiers and promise freedom** to those who fought for the South. He named slavery, bluntly, as a military liability — calling it, in a military point of view, one of the South’s chief sources of weakness. The reaction is the entire argument of the war in miniature. The proposal was met with fury; one of the generals who would die beside him at Franklin, **States Rights Gist (South)**, reportedly called it monstrous; it was protested up the chain of command; and **President Jefferson Davis (South) ordered the whole matter suppressed and the copies of the document destroyed.** The Confederate leadership would sooner bury the idea — and bury the man who raised it, professionally — than win the war by freeing the enslaved. That is the cleanest possible proof of what the war was about: a Confederate general suggested trading slavery for survival, and his own government chose slavery. He was sent to die at the cotton gin without ever being promoted past division command.

The cause was not abstract on this ground, either. **Forty-four enslaved people were held at Carnton** — the very plantation that became the death-house field hospital where Cleburne’s body was laid out. The men who bled in the ditch charged for a government built on that property line.

And the war’s cause had the last word. Two weeks after Franklin, Hood’s wrecked army limped on to **Nashville**, where it was destroyed in battle on December 15–16, 1864. Fighting in the Union army that finished it were roughly **thirteen thousand U.S. Colored Troops** — Black soldiers, the largest such force yet assembled on any Civil War battlefield, many of them formerly enslaved men from Middle Tennessee itself. People who had been held as property put on Union blue and helped annihilate the army that had marched north to keep them enslaved. The same Army of Tennessee that bled to death at the cotton gin was put out of its misery, in part, by the very people its existence depended on owning.

In early 1866, John and Carrie McGavock set aside about two acres beside their family plot at Carnton and reburied nearly **1,500 Confederate soldiers** killed at Franklin — the largest privately owned military cemetery in the United States. The strategic verdict on Hood’s whole Tennessee gamble — Spring Hill, Franklin, Nashville — is that it failed catastrophically, costing the Confederacy one of its two great field armies for nothing. But the deepest verdict on Franklin is the simplest one, and it’s the one Cleburne tried to make his own side hear before they sent him to die: the South had bet its army, and these men’s lives, on keeping human beings in chains, and it lost both.
