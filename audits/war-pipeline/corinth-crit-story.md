# Corinth draft — STORYTELLING critic gate (war pipeline, step 4)

> Judged AS A STORY first (the #1 goal), accuracy as a hard floor. Voice baseline =
> the shipped Shiloh battle sections (`src/app/war-civil-war/western/shiloh/s/[section]/
> section-narrative.tsx`). Grades: STRONG / GOOD / NEEDS WORK / REWRITE.
> Findings split MUST-IMPROVE (ship-blocking for the story gate) vs SHOULD-IMPROVE
> (polish). I did NOT edit the draft or app; this is a critique only.

## Overall: **GOOD** (ship-able after the MUST-IMPROVE list)

This is a real story, not a play-by-play with a moral stapled on. The spine — "the
railroad is the *object*, slavery is the *reason*, and the proof is standing on the
edge of town" — is set up in §1, paid off in §4, and it lands. That through-line is
the draft's biggest storytelling asset and it is genuinely Shiloh-caliber. The voice
matches the reference well: confident, plain, willing to say "we won't pretend to it"
about disputed facts (the Shiloh sections do exactly this with the Sunken Road, the
friendly-fire bullet, the "I can't spare this man" quote). Dual POV is honored — Van
Dorn's gamble and Rosecrans's stand both get the camera. "Selective is GOOD" is
respected: §2 and §3 don't drown in regiment-by-regiment detail.

**The two things that keep it from STRONG are emotional, not factual:** the two
moments the brief flags as the heart — the slaughter in the Robinett ditch and the
Contraband Camp payoff — are *told* more than *felt*. They're well-written summary.
The reference file makes you feel Johnston's boot filling with blood and Sherman
under the tree in the rain. Corinth's two biggest moments are currently a notch
cooler than that. Fix those two and this is STRONG.

## §1 — the-crossroads ("Why two armies wanted one small Mississippi town") — **GOOD**

### Hook & stakes
Strong, economical open: *"There was nothing much to Corinth, Mississippi ... except
the one thing that made it priceless."* That's a real house-voice hook — the
Shiloh-grade move of stating the smallness, then the leverage. The "object vs reason"
framing is the best structural decision in the draft.

### What works
- The promissory note is excellent and rare: *"Hold the thought; by the end of this
  story it will be standing on the edge of the battlefield, in human form, and
  impossible to miss."* That earns the §4 payoff and gives the reader a reason to
  stay. Keep it verbatim.
- The Iuka "MEANWHILE" is a clean windup: *"Iuka was the windup; Corinth was the
  punch."* Good rhythm, good orientation.

### MUST-IMPROVE
- **The dispersal-figures paragraph stalls the open.** *"roughly 12,000 at Bolivar,
  about 7,000 under Sherman at Memphis, some 6,000 in reserve at Jackson ... around
  23,000 ... roughly 22,000 men"* — five numbers in two sentences, this early, reads
  like an order-of-battle table, not a story. Compare Shiloh, which states "roughly
  45,000 men" and "roughly 44,000" and otherwise keeps numbers out of the hook. Keep
  the *gambler's logic* (Van Dorn must hit before Grant concentrates) but cut to one
  or two numbers — "Grant's forces were scattered across the region; Rosecrans held
  Corinth with about 23,000." The reader needs the *idea* of dispersal, not the
  ledger.

### SHOULD-IMPROVE
- The Siege-of-Corinth disambiguation (*"not to be confused with the two-day battle
  this story is about"*) is necessary but currently reads as a footnote inside the
  paragraph. Fine to keep; tighten if it can be one clause.
- "a cavalier of a general with a reputation for boldness ... His logic was a
  gambler's" — good characterization, but Van Dorn never becomes a *person* the way
  Grant/Sherman/Johnston do in the reference. He's the draft's protagonist-antagonist
  and stays a label. See the §2/§3 note — one concrete human beat for Van Dorn would
  pay off across all three battle sections.

## §2 — october-third ("Van Dorn's good day") — **GOOD**

### Hook & stakes
*"Van Dorn's good day"* as the title + *"The first day went, plainly, to Van Dorn"*
is a clean, honest framing that sets up the reversal. Good.

### What works
- **"a gap in a battle line is a doorway"** — this is the house voice exactly. Plain
  mechanism, vivid image. Keep.
- The sunset turn is the section's best storytelling: *"And then the sun did the Union
  a favor the Union could not have done for itself."* That's a Shiloh-grade sentence.
- The garbled-order beat (*"so much time was lost that it was sunset"*) lands the irony
  well, and the Van Dorn lament quote — *"One hour more of daylight and victory would
  have soothed our grief"* — is a strong button IF the fact-checker confirms it. The
  indirect fallback is correctly pre-written.

### MUST-IMPROVE
- **Play-by-play tedium in the middle.** The paragraph *"Rosecrans had his garrison
  sorted into the standard ladder ... McKean held the left, Davies the center,
  Hamilton the right, Stanley in reserve ... Lovell came in against the left, Maury
  pressed Davies's flank in the center"* is the one stretch that reads like troop
  bookkeeping. Six generals and four map-positions in two sentences. The reference
  handles this exact problem by introducing the army-ladder *once* as an aside and
  then naming only the commanders who matter to the *story* (Sherman holds the right,
  Prentiss/Wallace hold the center). Recommend: keep the double-envelopment *idea*
  (Van Dorn tried to fold both ends inward), keep Davies (center, where the gap tears)
  and the reserve, and demote McKean/Hamilton/Lovell/Stanley to the minimum the
  next beats actually require. Right now the reader is asked to hold a roster they
  don't yet need.

### SHOULD-IMPROVE
- The two-Union-generals-down beat (*"It cost the Union army two of its generals in a
  single afternoon"*) is good and human — but the Oglesby-future-governor parenthetical
  is a [VERIFY] aside that, even if confirmed, drains tension at the worst moment (mid
  Confederate-momentum). Cut or move; the reference saves "Lew Wallace wrote Ben-Hur"
  for a *lull*, never mid-climax.
- The closing "MEANWHILE — inside the inner ring" does real work (it makes the reader
  understand Van Dorn won the wrong ground) and ends well: *"The ground that mattered
  most was still in front of him."* Keep. Minor: it re-explains the three-tier defense
  the reader half-learned in §1; trim the overlap.

## §3 — battery-robinett ("Where Van Dorn's gamble bled out") — **NEEDS WORK**

This is the section the brief says must be *felt*, and right now it's the section
with the most going on and the least emotional temperature relative to its material.
Graded NEEDS WORK specifically on the human dimension — the prose is competent
throughout.

### Hook & stakes
The cold open (*"The second day began in the dark with noise"*) is good. But the
section immediately detours into Hébert's illness and the command reshuffle before
the reader has any stakes — see below.

### What works
- **Battery Powell as the false summit** is structurally smart: the Confederates
  *take* a fort (*"the high-water mark of the whole Confederate effort"*) and lose it
  to a counterattack within the same breath. Good rise-and-collapse.
- Rogers's burial coda is the emotional high point of the whole draft and it works:
  *"the victorious Rosecrans ordered Rogers — an enemy colonel — buried where he fell,
  with full military honors."* That's the Shiloh move (find the human grace note).
  Keep it, and the obelisk detail.
- The honesty about the disputed cause of death (*"the popular tale of a specific
  number of bullets is not something the records support, so we won't pretend to it"*)
  is pitch-perfect house voice. Keep verbatim.

### MUST-IMPROVE
- **The Robinett ditch — the slaughter the brief says must be FELT — is told at
  arm's length.** Right now it's: *"The attackers ran into 'a terrific musket and
  artillery fire,' crossed the killing ground, dropped into the five-foot ditch, and
  clawed up the parapet."* That's an accurate *summary of an event*. It is not the
  reference standard. Compare Johnston in the Shiloh file: *"His boot quietly filled
  with blood."* — one concrete physical image does the emotional work of a paragraph.
  Robinett needs that. The material is right there in the draft's own [VERIFY] note:
  *the LOC photograph of the Confederate dead heaped in the Robinett ditch* — one of
  the most famous death-photos of the war. The story should END the assault on that
  ditch as a *place* — the killing ground left full of the men who'd just been alive
  and charging — not summarize past it to the casualty count. **This is the single
  biggest storytelling miss in the draft.** Make the ditch a thing the reader sees,
  pending the fact-checker clearing the photo's caption/date.
- **Rogers himself is under-built for the weight he's asked to carry.** He's
  introduced (*"a 42-year-old Texas lawyer ... brave to the edge of recklessness"*)
  and killed in the space of a few sentences, then honored. The burial coda only
  lands as hard as the reader's investment in the man. The reference always *plants*
  before it *pays* (Sherman's "lost his nerve" backstory before his stand; Johnston's
  numbed-leg dueling-injury detail before he bleeds out). Give Rogers one more concrete
  human beat — colors in hand, on the parapet, the moment before the volley — so his
  honored burial closes a person, not a name.

### SHOULD-IMPROVE
- **The Hébert-illness / Green-takes-over opening saps momentum before it's earned.**
  *"Brig. Gen. Louis Hébert reported himself too ill to command, and Brig. Gen. Martin
  Green had to take over ... The reshuffle cost the morning's momentum, the second day
  in a row that Van Dorn's timing slipped."* The "timing slipped two days running"
  *theme* is good and worth keeping — but leading day two with a sick general and a
  personnel swap is an anticlimactic curtain-raiser. Consider opening on the 4:30 a.m.
  bombardment and the looming inner line, then fold the Hébert delay in as the reason
  the hammer fell late.
- Phifer's brigade breaking *into the streets of Corinth* (*"for a moment the
  Confederates were in the town"*) is a great almost-moment and is currently a half-
  sentence buried after Robinett. It's arguably a bigger stakes-beat than Powell —
  the enemy is literally in the objective — and deserves a touch more room.
- The casualty paragraph is handled well (the honest range, no false precision, *"the
  signature price of throwing infantry against fortified earthworks"*). No change.
- The Hatchie's Bridge / Van Dorn-court-of-inquiry / Grant-grumble "MEANWHILE" is
  doing three jobs (the pursuit, Van Dorn's fall, Rosecrans's rise) and gets crowded.
  Selective is good — consider whether the Grant pursuit-quote needs to be here or
  belongs in §4's strategic close.

## §4 — what-it-protected ("What the victory protected") — **STRONG**

### Hook & stakes
This is the payoff of the §1 promissory note and it delivers. *"But the deepest
meaning of Corinth was not on the railroad. It was on the edge of town, and it is the
answer to the question this story opened with."* The pivot from strategy to the camp
is exactly the structure the draft promised.

### What works
- **The Contraband Camp payoff mostly lands** — and the *closing the circle* logic is
  the best thing in the draft: *"People who had been somebody's legal property a year
  earlier put on Union blue and took up arms, in sight of the battlefield where Van
  Dorn's army had just tried, and failed, to win the war for the other side."* That
  sentence is the whole argument of the war in one image. Keep verbatim.
- *"the railroad was the object, slavery was the reason, and the proof of the reason
  was a camp full of free people on the town's edge"* — clean restatement of the
  spine, earned by now. Strong.
- Slavery is named as the cause in the section's own prose, repeatedly — satisfies the
  framing floor (the Lost-Cause-omission gate). Good.
- The strategic close (*"The little town with the railroad crossing was a hinge, and
  it turned the right way"*) is a good house-voice button.

### MUST-IMPROVE
- **The camp is described as an institution, not yet *felt* as people** — and the
  brief explicitly wants this payoff FELT. The current beats are stats and facilities:
  *"numerous homes, a church, school and hospital ... a clear profit of four to five
  thousand dollars ... over a thousand Black children and adults ... had learned to
  read ... roughly 6,000 ... passed through."* It's a strong *institutional* portrait,
  but the emotional charge is in the human act underneath it: people walking
  *themselves* out of slavery and into a town they then made profitable, then sending
  their own men back armed. The draft has the right closing image (the bronze statues,
  *"the freedom-seekers"*) — but the body should give the reader at least one *person-
  scaled* beat (the walk in; learning to read; the choice to enlist) before the
  numbers, the way §1 promised a thing "in human form, impossible to miss." Right now
  the humans arrive mostly as aggregate figures. Lead with the act, support with the
  stats — not the reverse.

### SHOULD-IMPROVE
- The two consecutive [VERIFY]-heavy interpretive sentences (the 300-acres / $5,000-a-
  month figures; the "benevolent organizations" vs AMA caution) read slightly like
  the fact ledger leaked into the prose. The caution is *correct* and must survive the
  fact-checker, but in the final prose the hedging should be invisible — pick the
  cleared figures and state them plainly. (This is a note for the integrate step, not
  a rewrite of the argument.)
- The closing "MEANWHILE — what the railroad really carried" partly *re-argues* §1's
  object/reason frame rather than advancing it. It's well-written (*"The contraband
  camp is not a footnote to the battle of Corinth. It is the point of it."* is a great
  last line) — but the section already made this point in its body. Consider whether
  the sidebar should add a *new* angle (e.g., the four million in bondage the rails
  served, the specific irony of supply-vs-self-liberation at the same depot) rather
  than restate. The final sentence is keeper-grade either way.

## Cross-cutting notes

- **Voice match: GOOD.** Inline definitions (*redan*, *double envelopment*, *contrabands*,
  the army-ladder), the willingness to flag disputed facts honestly, the terse muscular
  buttons — all match the Shiloh reference. No academic drift, no sanded-down voice.
- **Dual POV: present and balanced.** §2/§3 carry both armies. Good.
- **Pacing: §1 front-loads numbers, §3 front-loads a personnel reshuffle.** Both delay
  the reader's emotional entry into the section. The reference consistently leads with
  a *hook or a human*, then back-fills mechanics. Apply that ordering to §1 and §3.
- **The recurring miss is the same one in both flagged moments (§3 ditch, §4 camp):
  the draft summarizes human horror/triumph in aggregate instead of grounding it in
  one concrete, person-scaled, *seen* image.** That is the exact thing the reference
  file does best (the boot of blood; Sherman in the rain). Fixing those two moments is
  what moves the draft from GOOD to STRONG.
