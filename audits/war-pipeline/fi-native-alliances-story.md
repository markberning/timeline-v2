# Storytelling Critic Report — "The Contest for Native Alliances"

**Section:** French & Indian War off-the-battlefield theme (kind=theme, SOCIETY)
**Draft:** `audits/war-pipeline/fi-native-alliances-draft.md`
**Fact pack:** `audits/war-pipeline/fi-native-alliances-factpack.md`
**Verdict: PASS**

This is a strong piece of narrative history. It has a real thesis ("the war turned
on Native nations, and they were players, not pawns"), it carries that thesis as a
through-line from the title gloss to Pontiac's War, and it consistently keeps Native
nations in the subject position the way the fact pack's framing directive demands.
The voice is the house voice: confident, plain, occasionally wry ("a paper claim on
their homeland"), never academic and never showy. No heading falls below GOOD, and
no house-voice rule is broken. Notes below are polish, not blocks.

## Per-heading grades

### Opening (untitled lede) — STRONG
The "they named the war after the people they were most afraid of" hook is excellent:
it earns the thesis instead of asserting it, and "The label is a point of view... a
British porch looking west" is exactly the house voice. The map paragraph (line 6)
does real work setting stakes ("whether a column of redcoats walked out of the forest
or didn't") and ends on a tricolon that previews the whole arc. Keep as is.

### What France had built — STRONG
The mechanism is explained, not just asserted: gift-giving "wasn't" bribery, and the
draft says *why* in Native diplomatic terms. The Kahnawake "Mohawk would fight Mohawk"
beat (line 19) is a genuine human hook and it pays off later at Lake George. "Onontio,
a father figure, but a particular kind of father" lands the middle-ground idea in one
clause. Tiny polish: "Its collapse, years later, is the end of this story" (line 23) is
a faint forward-promise that flirts with meta-narration; it stays on the right side of
the line, but if you want it airtight, "Its collapse is where this story ends" reads
less like authorial stage-direction. Optional.

### What Britain had, and what it didn't — STRONG
The contrast engine of the piece. "a silver chain that had to be polished now and then
to keep it bright" is vivid and sourced. The pivot "By the 1750s the chain was tarnishing
fast, and the reason was land" is a clean, propulsive turn. The Johnson paragraph handles
the Molly Brant caution from the fact pack correctly ("household partner," "ran his
household," no asserted marriage) while still giving her agency ("a power broker in her
own right"). Joseph Brant is correctly kept to a teenager ("his fame would come later").
Well done.

### The Iroquois play their own game — GOOD
Does its job — it refuses the "Britain's allies" filing and states the balance-of-power
strategy cleanly ("A nation everyone needs and no one owns can play the empires against
each other"). It is the most expository, least scenic heading in the piece, which is
fine as connective tissue. One soft note: it is short and abstract sitting between two
vivid headings; it could carry one concrete image (the Seneca/western Iroquois leaning
the other way, or a council detail) to keep the texture up. Not required — it functions.

### Hendrick at Albany — STRONG
The strongest character beat. The "do not conflate the two Hendricks" trap from the fact
pack is handled gracefully inside the prose (line 52) instead of as a footnote. The
"contradiction in Hendrick is the contradiction in the whole British alliance" (line 60)
is the single best analytical sentence in the piece — it turns one man into the thesis.
The "too few / too many" quote is correctly flagged as remembered ("passed down rather
than written at the time"). The Mohawk-killed-Mohawk close (line 66) is devastating and
earned. Keep verbatim.

### How Native allies won France its early war — STRONG
The numbers do the arguing, exactly as the fact pack's takeaway wanted: "France did not
beat Braddock at the Monongahela. France's Native allies did." The figures match the
fact pack's preferred breakdown (~250 French/Canadian, ~640 Native). Washington appears
in a single deft clause without hijacking the Native-agency frame. The closing line ties
back to the thesis without restating it flatly. Strong.

### The victory that cost France its allies — STRONG
"Two years later France won again, and the winning is where it began to lose" is a superb
hinge line. The draft nails the contested death-toll handling — it names the legend (1,500,
Cooper), gives the modern range (69–184), and includes the forensic disease finding — and
it correctly presents Montcalm's culpability as a live debate rather than picking a side.
The "they had been denied their due, then sickened for their trouble" beat fuses the human
cost to the strategic cost. This is the emotional and structural center of the piece and
it holds.

### Easton: the Ohio nations change the war — STRONG
"The decisive blow to France in the interior was not struck by a British army. It was
negotiated at a council." is a clean, surprising thesis-restatement that keeps Native
agency central. The payoff ("the French garrison... lost the Native shield that had made
it dangerous") makes the diplomacy *military*, which is the hard thing to pull off, and it
does it. The Royal Proclamation forward-link ("like most such promises to Native nations,
it would not hold") is mordant and correct. Strong.

### Allies, not pawns — GOOD
The explicit thesis statement. It risks being a recap, and in a couple of spots it is
("Step back from the battles and a pattern is plain" then re-lists the four episodes). But
it earns its place because it converts the episodes into the framing claim the fact pack
mandates, and the land paragraph (line 122) lands the cumulative human cost as a single
gut-punch: "a contest over Native land, and the people whose land it was would pay for the
outcome no matter who won." Soft note: the four-clause recap (lines 118) is the one place
the piece tells what it already showed; you could trim it to two clauses and lose nothing.
Optional, not a block.

### Winning the war, losing the peace — STRONG
The right ending. It reframes the whole theme ("Britain won the war and then lost the
peace, because it forgot why the alliances had ever mattered") and makes Amherst the
anti-Onontio, closing the loop the France section opened. The smallpox material is handled
with exactly the fact pack's discipline: documented intent (Amherst's own hand) is asserted,
causation is flagged as debated ("Whether the blankets caused an outbreak is debated; the
intent in Amherst's own hand is not"). The Amherst quote is verbatim from the ledger. Ends
the piece on its strongest structural idea.

## Pacing & shape (whole-piece)

Excellent macro-rhythm: thesis → France's model → Britain's broken model → the Iroquois
hinge → a character (Hendrick) → a battle (Monongahela) → a betrayal (William Henry) → a
treaty (Easton) → the framing → the collapse (Amherst/Pontiac). Scene and exposition
alternate so the reader is never in a long dry stretch. The only two slightly flat patches
are the two most expository headings ("The Iroquois play their own game" and "Allies, not
pawns"), both GOOD not below, and both structurally necessary. No play-by-play tedium; even
the battle heading is argued through numbers and meaning, not blow-by-blow.

## Human dimension

Strong throughout: Hendrick (the aggrieved orator who dies for the people who wronged him),
Molly Brant, the Great Lakes warriors "denied their due, then sickened for their trouble,"
the Kahnawake-vs-valley-Mohawk tragedy. The piece never lets the nations dissolve into
abstractions, which is the exact failure mode the framing directive guards against.

None of these change the verdict.
