## 1. Dates / Location / Theatre

- **Date:** **November 24, 1863** (a single day; fighting roughly 8:00–8:30 a.m. into
  the late afternoon, with the Confederate withdrawal overnight Nov 24→25). [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- **Location:** **Lookout Mountain, overlooking Chattanooga, Tennessee** — the fight
  was on the mountain's **northern face / slope**, between the cliff-line (palisade)
  near the summit and the Tennessee River at its base. Coordinates ~35°01′N 85°20′W. [WP]
- **Theatre:** **Western Theater**, part of the **Chattanooga Campaign**. (Map lane
  colour for the reader = **Western = blue**.) [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- **Popular name:** **"The Battle Above the Clouds."** Real, contemporary, and a load-
  bearing part of the story — but treat the name itself as **romantic framing**, not a
  military assessment (see §8 — Grant himself deflated it). [WP]
- **Result:** **Union victory.** [WP] (Tactically modest; strategically part of lifting
  the siege — see §9.)

## 2. Forces & Commanders (each tagged SIDE)

> **Side convention (locked):** Civil War **North = Union**, **South =
> Confederate**. Tag EVERY named person on a side on first mention per section —
> "Hooker (North)", "Stevenson (South)" — once per person per section. [`feedback_war_person_side_tags`]

### Union (North) — the attacking force
- **Maj. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant (North)** — **overall Union commander** at Chattanooga;
  designed the three-day breakout. He did **not** direct the Lookout Mountain assault in
  person and was famously unimpressed by it (§8). [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- **Maj. Gen. Joseph "Fighting Joe" Hooker (North)** — **led the assault on Lookout
  Mountain**; commanded the ad-hoc force (~three divisions) that climbed the mountain.
  *(Same Hooker who lost at Chancellorsville — see Chancellorsville — here partly
  redeeming himself; the author may note the arc lightly, not re-tell it.)* [WP, HoW]
- **Brig. Gen. John W. Geary (North)** — **XII Corps** (2nd Division); led the main
  ascent / flanking column that crossed Lookout Creek and rolled up the slope. The man
  who literally "stormed the inaccessible heights" (§8 quote). [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- **Brig. Gen. Peter J. Osterhaus (North)** — **XV Corps** division; part of Hooker's
  attacking force (his division had been cut off on the wrong side of the river and was
  attached to Hooker for the assault). German-born "Forty-Eighter" émigré. [WP, HoW]
- **Brig. Gen. Charles Cruft (North)** — **IV Corps** division/brigades; the third
  element of Hooker's force. [WP, HoW]
- Brigade leaders worth a mention (Union): **Col. George A. Cobham Jr.** (Pennsylvanians)
  and **Col. David Ireland** (New Yorkers) — Cobham's flanking fire from the upper slope +
  Ireland's frontal press is what broke the Cravens-House line. [WP, ABT-Chatt]

### Confederate (South) — the thin, outnumbered defenders
- **Gen. Braxton Bragg (South)** — **overall Confederate commander**, Army of Tennessee;
  held the heights ringing Chattanooga after Chickamauga. He had **thinned the Lookout
  Mountain garrison** (pulling Walker's division off the mountain) shortly before the
  attack — a key reason the defenders were so badly outnumbered. [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- **Maj. Gen. Carter L. Stevenson (South)** — **field commander on Lookout Mountain**
  (the infobox's Confederate commander of record); responsible for the whole mountain,
  with too few men to hold its long northern face. [WP]
- **Brig. Gen. Edward C. Walthall (South)** — Mississippi brigade; the **forward
  defenders** who took the first blow and fought the delaying action, then the stand at
  the Cravens House. The "thin defenders" of the brief. [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- **Brig. Gen. John C. Moore (South)** — Alabama/Mississippi brigade; mounted the ~1 p.m.
  **counterattack** near the Cravens House that the Union crossfire stopped cold. [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- (Also present / reinforcing late: brigades under **Brown** and **Pettus**, brought down
  from the summit; coordination wrecked by fog.) [WP]

### Sizes (a lopsided fight — the story's spine)
- **Union: ~10,000–12,000 engaged.** ABT/WP infobox = **~12,000**; ABT's campaign article
  and HoW say Hooker attacked with **~10,000**. **Use "~10,000" in prose** (the most-cited
  attacking figure) and the **~12,000** only if matching the WP infobox stat card; note the
  range. [WP, ABT-Chatt, HoW]
- **Confederate: ~8,700 total on the mountain** (**8,726** is the WP infobox figure) — **but
  far fewer actually engaged on the northern slope at the point of contact.** HoW puts the
  **western-slope defenders at only ~3,000 (three brigades)**; the rest of the ~8,700 were
  spread along the mountain and the summit and could not be brought to bear (the palisade and
  fog split the position). **The honest framing: ~8,700 on paper, a few thousand in the actual
  fight, badly outnumbered at the decisive point.** [WP, HoW]
- **Net:** the romance of "above the clouds" rides on a real military fact — **a steep,
  supposedly impregnable height held by too few men, taken by a larger force that simply
  climbed it.** [WP, HoW]

## 3. Casualties (relatively LIGHT — the story is not a bloodbath)

The most-cited (WP/ABT infobox) totals:

| Side | Killed | Wounded | Captured/Missing | **Total** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Union (North)** | 89 | 471 | 111 | **~671** |
| **Confederate (South)** | ~187 (killed+wounded) | | **1,064** captured/missing | **~1,251** |

[WP, ABT-Chatt]

- **Both sides' losses were light by Civil War standards** — fewer than ~2,000 total, vs.
  ~34,000 at Chickamauga two months earlier (see Chickamauga). This is part of why Grant
  belittled it as "no battle." Make the contrast for the reader. [WP, GRANT]
- **The Confederate loss was overwhelmingly PRISONERS, not dead** — **~1,064 of ~1,251
  Confederate casualties were captured/missing**, men cut off and rounded up as the line
  was flanked in the fog, not killed. A height "stormed" with very little blood. [WP]
- **Scholarly range / caution (`[VERIFY]`):** Union killed is given as **81** by some
  sources (ABT campaign article: "81 killed out of 629 total"; HoW: "81 killed, 629 total")
  and **89** by the WP infobox; Union total ranges **~629–671**. **Pin one set in the stat
  card** — recommend the WP infobox set (**89 / 471 / 111 / 671**) for consistency with the
  other dossiers, and **note the ~629/81 variant** rather than asserting a single hard
  number. Confederate killed-in-action is not cleanly broken out across sources; **lead with
  the captured figure (~1,064), which all sources agree on**, and give total as ~1,251. [WP,

## 4. Geography (relative positions for map accuracy)

**North is up.** Chattanooga sits in the river valley to the **north-northeast**; Lookout
Mountain looms over it from the **southwest**. The fight is on the mountain's **north face**.

- **Lookout Mountain** — a long, steep ridge **~1,200 ft above the river** at its north
  end, capped by a **vertical rock cliff-line, the "palisade"** (or "palisades"), near the
  summit. The palisade is **un-assaultable** — so the fight was NOT for the summit; it was
  for the **slope and the bench BELOW the cliffs.** The summit itself was never stormed; the
  Confederates simply abandoned it overnight. (Critical for the map and the prose: the famous
  flag-raising the next morning was on a height the attackers had *walked up to*, not fought
  for.) [WP, HoW, NPS]
- **The Tennessee River** — sweeps around the **northern base** of the mountain in a great
  loop. **Moccasin Bend** is the dramatic hairpin bend of the river directly **north** of
  Lookout Mountain (named for its moccasin-footprint shape); the mountain looks straight down
  onto it. A signature map feature — label it. [WP, NPS]
- **Chattanooga** — in the valley to the **north-northeast** of the mountain, across the
  river bend. The whole point of holding Lookout Mountain was that its guns looked down on
  the city and the river/rail supply lines. [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- **Lookout Creek** — runs along the **west / southwest base** of the mountain (between
  Lookout Mountain and Raccoon Mountain); **swollen by recent rain**, it had to be bridged.
  **Hooker's men crossed Lookout Creek to begin the climb** — the jump-off line. The ford /
  crossing area near **Wauhatchie** (to the southwest) is where Geary got across and onto the
  slope. [WP, HoW]
- **The Cravens House** (the Cravens farm, built ~1855) — a farmhouse on a **small flat
  shelf / plateau bench about two-thirds of the way up the northern slope**, **below the
  palisade**. This is **the key terrain and where the battle centered**: the strongest
  Confederate works were here, and it's where Walthall's stand, Moore's counterattack, and
  the decisive crossfire all happened. **Pin "Cravens House" on the tactical map as the
  focal point.** [WP, ABT-Chatt, HoW]
- **The fog / mist** — a real, recurring weather feature: **a band of cloud/fog settles
  partway down the mountain**, leaving the summit clear above and the valley clear below.
  On Nov 24 it **clung to the slope and thickened ~2 p.m.**, hiding the fighting from
  observers in the valley (who could see the mountain-top above the cloud but not the
  combat) — hence "the Battle Above the Clouds." It also **wrecked Confederate coordination**
  (Pettus/Brown couldn't see to maneuver). The fog is BOTH the romantic image AND a real
  tactical factor — use it for both. [WP, HoW, NPS]

**Relative-position summary for the cartographer:** Tennessee River + **Moccasin Bend**
along the **north** base; **Chattanooga NE** across the bend; **Lookout Creek** along the
**west/SW** base (Wauhatchie crossing to the SW); the **palisade** as the cliff-line near the
summit (top); the **Cravens House** on the bench two-thirds up the **north face** as the
focal point; blue Union arrows crossing Lookout Creek (SW) and climbing **up and across the
north slope toward the Cravens House**, rust Confederate blocks thin along the slope and at
Cravens, withdrawing **east/over the mountain toward Missionary Ridge** overnight.

## 5. Sequence (turning points)

1. **Setup (cross-reference, don't re-tell).** After **Chickamauga (see Chickamauga)**, the
   Union army was besieged in Chattanooga with Bragg's army on the heights — **Lookout
   Mountain (SW) and Missionary Ridge (E)**. **Grant arrived (Oct 23), opened the "Cracker
   Line"** supply route via Brown's Ferry (Oct 27), fed and revived the army, and planned a
   breakout. **Lookout Mountain is Day 2** of that breakout (after Orchard Knob, Nov 23).
   [WP-Chatt, ABT-Chatt; see chickamauga-factpack §9]
2. **Hooker crosses Lookout Creek (early morning, ~8 a.m.).** Hooker's force (~10,000)
   crossed the rain-swollen **Lookout Creek** at the mountain's western base — Geary's
   column flanking via the **Wauhatchie** crossing to the southwest, others bridging the
   creek under fire. The low cloud helped screen the move. [WP, HoW]
3. **The climb and the sweep across the slope.** Geary's men climbed the steep western
   slope to the base of the **palisade**, then **wheeled and swept eastward / northward
   ACROSS the north face**, rolling up the thin Confederate picket line from the flank — the
   defenders (**Walthall, South**) badly outnumbered, falling back from one position to the
   next. [WP, ABT-Chatt, HoW]
4. **The fight at the Cravens House (late morning–afternoon).** The Confederates made their
   real **stand at the Cravens House** on the plateau bench, behind their strongest works.
   **Ireland's New Yorkers pressed the front while Cobham's Pennsylvanians poured flanking
   fire from the higher ground** — a crossfire the defenders couldn't answer. **Moore's
   (South) counterattack (~1 p.m.) was stopped by that same crossfire.** [WP, ABT-Chatt]
5. **THE TURNING POINT — flanked off the bench in the fog.** Outnumbered, out-positioned,
   and unable to coordinate reinforcements (Pettus/Brown couldn't see through the thickening
   ~2 p.m. mist), the Confederates were **driven off the Cravens plateau** and back/eastward
   around the mountain. The fight sputtered out in fog and near-darkness; ammunition ran low
   on both sides. [WP, HoW]
6. **The mist that named it.** Through the afternoon the cloud-band hid the combat from the
   tens of thousands of Union soldiers and officers watching from the valley below — they
   could see the mountain-top above the cloud and hear the guns inside it. **Quartermaster
   Gen. Montgomery C. Meigs (North), watching from Orchard Knob, coined "the Battle Above
   the Clouds."** [WP]
7. **Confederate withdrawal overnight (Nov 24→25).** **Bragg decided he needed every man on
   Missionary Ridge** and **ordered the Lookout Mountain force withdrawn during the night**,
   pulling them east across the valley to the ridge (the Summertown road off the mountain
   stayed open). They **abandoned the summit without a fight.** [WP, HoW, ABT-Chatt]
8. **The flag at dawn (Nov 25).** The next morning a patrol of the **8th Kentucky Infantry
   (North)** climbed to the deserted summit and **raised the U.S. flag on the point of
   Lookout Mountain** — visible to the whole army in the valley. A famous image; note it was a
   flag raised on an *abandoned* height, not one stormed. [WP] `[VERIFY]` 8th Kentucky as the
   unit (well-attested) and that the summit was already abandoned.
9. **Hand-off to Missionary Ridge (don't tell it).** With Lookout Mountain cleared, **Hooker
   was to push across Lookout Valley to strike Bragg's left at Missionary Ridge on Nov 25** —
   the climactic day of the breakout (he was delayed crossing the valley; the ridge was
   carried by Thomas's men in the famous spontaneous charge). **Flag the hand-off; Missionary
   Ridge is its own section.** [WP-Chatt, ABT-Chatt]

## 6. The Cause — Slavery (SHIP-BLOCKING — must appear in the section's own prose)

> **NAME THE CAUSE (locked, standing rule).** This section MUST state, in its OWN
> words, that slavery was what the war (and each side) was ultimately fought over.
> Omission — not euphemism — is the default Lost Cause failure mode the framing gate
> fails sections for. A reader landing on Lookout Mountain alone must be told what the
> killing was about. The light casualty count makes this EASIER to forget, not harder —
> the prettiness of "above the clouds" is exactly the warmth that fills the vacuum if
> the cause is left out. [`memory/feedback_name_the_cause`]

Hooks for the author (use at least one, in the section's own prose):
- **Chattanooga was the gateway to the Deep South — the heart of the slave economy.** It
  was the **rail hub** through which an army could drive into Georgia, toward **Atlanta** and
  the plantation belt. Lifting the siege and clearing these heights **opened the road to
  Atlanta and Sherman's 1864 campaign / March to the Sea** — the campaign that broke the
  South's slaveholding heartland. So this small, pretty fight is one hinge of the door into
  the slave South. [ABT-Chatt: "opened up the Deep South for a Union invasion and set the
  stage for ... Sherman's Atlanta Campaign"]
- **The war was a war over slavery.** The Confederacy existed to protect and extend slavery;
  by November 1863 (post–Emancipation Proclamation, Jan 1, 1863 — see Emancipation) the
  Union's war aim explicitly included its destruction. Bragg's army held these mountains to
  keep that door shut; Grant's army climbed them to kick it open. [standing rule; WP-Chatt]
- One clear sentence at the section's stakes-setting moment is enough — **but it is
  mandatory.** The prettiness is the trap; name the cause anyway.

## 7. Human cost (named people, documented detail, the toll)

- **The defining human fact here is how FEW died** — fewer than ~2,000 total casualties,
  and most of the Confederate loss was **men captured (~1,064), not killed** (§3). This is a
  battle whose human story is **near-misses, exhaustion, fog-blind confusion, and surrender**,
  not slaughter. Don't manufacture carnage the sources don't support. [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- **The terrain was the real enemy.** Soldiers described it as brutal ground — a sergeant
  major of the **96th Illinois (North)** reportedly called it "undoubtedly the roughest
  battle field of the war" — men climbing a near-cliff, over boulders, through fog so thick
  they could see only a few dozen yards. The cost was measured more in exhaustion and falls
  than in the casualty list. [WP] `[VERIFY]` exact wording + attribution of the "roughest
  battle field" quote.
- **The captured Confederates** are the human weight of the day: hundreds of men cut off and
  rounded up in the mist as their thin line was flanked — a defense that dissolved into
  surrender rather than a fight to the death. [WP]
- **The watching army.** A real human dimension worth using: **tens of thousands of Union
  soldiers in the valley stood watching the mountain all day**, seeing the cloud and hearing
  the unseen battle inside it — and erupting when the flag appeared on the summit at dawn.
  The "audience" is part of why the fight became legend. [WP, HoW]

## 8. Key quotes (exact attribution + source; shaky ones flagged)

  battle and no action even worthy to be called the battle on Lookout Mountain. It is all
  poetry."** — **Ulysses S. Grant (North)**, *Personal Memoirs* — Grant himself **deflating
  the "Battle Above the Clouds" legend** as more romance than decisive fight. **This is the
  single most important quote for the section's framing** — it lets the author celebrate the
  romance AND puncture it honestly. [GRANT, via WP] `[VERIFY]` exact wording against the
  memoir text (well-attested; confirm punctuation).
- **"Battle Above the Clouds"** — coined by **Brig. Gen. Montgomery C. Meigs (North)**, the
  Union quartermaster general, **watching from Orchard Knob**; described as "the first writer
  to name it." The romantic name is itself a documented act of myth-making in real time. [WP]
  `[VERIFY]` Meigs as originator + the Orchard Knob vantage.
- **"I stormed what was considered the inaccessible heights"** — **Brig. Gen. John W. Geary
  (North)**, in a letter to his wife, on taking the mountain. Good for the attacker's POV /
  pride. [WP] `[VERIFY]` exact wording + that it's the wife letter.
- **"undoubtedly the roughest battle field of the war"** — a **sergeant major of the 96th
  Illinois (North)** on the terrain. [WP] `[VERIFY]` exact wording + attribution.
- **Framing note (NOT a quote to assert):** the "Battle Above the Clouds" name is **romantic

## 9. Significance

- **Tactically a clear, low-cost Union win** — Hooker's force cleared Lookout Mountain, drove
  an outnumbered Confederate force off it, and took ~1,000+ prisoners for under 700 of their
  own losses. [WP, ABT-Chatt]
- **Strategically it was one piece of LIFTING THE SIEGE, not the decisive blow.** It cleared
  the western height and the river/rail approaches, and it **set up the climactic day** —
  Hooker was meant to swing across the valley and hit Bragg's left at **Missionary Ridge the
  next day (Nov 25)**, the day the siege was truly broken. **Some historians call Lookout
  Mountain "effectively a drawn battle"** in that Hooker's men were **delayed crossing the
  valley and arrived at Missionary Ridge too late to play a big part** — so its real payoff
  was clearing the board and the morale jolt, not winning the campaign by itself. [HoW, WP]
- **It is the romance of the campaign** — the image that survives ("above the clouds," the
  flag on the summit at dawn) — even though Grant rated it the least of the three days. Holding
  that tension IS the section. [WP, GRANT]
- **The campaign it belongs to opened the gateway to the Deep South.** Chattanooga lifted →
  **road to Atlanta open → Sherman's 1864 Atlanta Campaign and March to the Sea.** The small
  pretty fight on the mountain is one hinge of the door into the slave South (§6). [ABT-Chatt]
- **Net verdict for the Outcome card:** *Union victory — small fight, large legend.* Hooker
  cleared Lookout Mountain at light cost, capturing over a thousand outnumbered Confederates
  who slipped away to Missionary Ridge overnight. It lifted the western height, set up the
  decisive charge up Missionary Ridge the next day, and helped break the siege of Chattanooga
  — opening the road to Atlanta and the Deep South. Grant called it "all poetry"; the romance
  ("the Battle Above the Clouds," the flag at dawn) outran the modest fighting.
