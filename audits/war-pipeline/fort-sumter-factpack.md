## 2. Casualties

### The key fact: NO ONE died in the bombardment itself.
Through ~34 hours of Confederate shelling and Union counter-fire, there were **zero deaths on either side** from enemy action. (Wikipedia; Britannica; NPS; ABT)

### The surrender salute accident (April 14, 1861)
Anderson negotiated, as his single condition of surrender, the right to fire a 100-gun salute to the U.S. flag before evacuating. The salute began at approximately 2:00 p.m. on April 14. (NPS; Wikipedia)

**What happened at gun #47:**
A spark in the gun barrel prematurely ignited a cartridge. The explosion blew off Private Daniel Hough's right arm, killing him almost instantly. The detonation also set off ammunition stored beside the gun, wounding five other soldiers. (Wikipedia — Daniel Hough article; NPS)

**The dead:**
- **Private Daniel Hough**, Battery E, 1st United States Artillery — born c. 1825 in Borrisokane, County Tipperary, Ireland. Killed instantly (or within moments) when the accidental explosion blew off his right arm. He is considered the first Union soldier — and the first man — to die in the American Civil War, though his death was accidental. He was buried in the Fort Sumter parade ground within approximately two hours of his death. The location of his remains today is unknown; possibilities include the Fort Moultrie burial ground, St. Lawrence Cemetery in Charleston, or destruction during the later Siege of Charleston (1863–65). (Wikipedia — Daniel Hough; Irish Central; NY DMNA)

**The mortally wounded who died later:**
- **Private Edward Galloway** (also spelled "Gallway" in some sources): mortally wounded in the same explosion; died days later (one source says five days) at Chisholm's Hospital in Charleston. (Wikipedia; NPS)

**The other wounded:**
- **Private George Fielding**: severely wounded; lost an eye; eventually evacuated after six weeks. (Wikipedia; NPS)
- Three additional members of Company E wounded in the same explosion. (NPS; Wikipedia)

**Salute cut short:** After the explosion, the 100-gun salute was stopped at 50 rounds. (Wikipedia; NPS; ABT)

## 3. What Came Before

### Secession winter
- **November 6, 1860**: Abraham Lincoln elected president. (general historical record)
- **December 20, 1860**: South Carolina secedes from the Union — the first state to do so. (Wikipedia; ABT; NPS)

### Anderson moves to Fort Sumter
- **December 26, 1860**: Major Robert Anderson surreptitiously moves his 85-man garrison from the indefensible Fort Moultrie (on Sullivan's Island, easily assailable from the rear on land) to the stronger, harbor-island Fort Sumter. The move was made at night without notifying Washington or the South Carolina authorities. South Carolina's Governor Francis Pickens considered the move a breach of faith with the Buchanan administration. (Wikipedia; ABT; NPS)

### The Star of the West incident
- **January 9, 1861**: The unarmed U.S. merchant steamer *Star of the West* attempts to resupply and reinforce Fort Sumter. As it enters the harbor channel, a battery on Morris Island — manned by cadets from the South Carolina Military Academy (now The Citadel), including cadet gunner George E. Haynsworth — opens fire. The ship is struck three times. Captain John McGowan turns back; the resupply mission fails. Anderson, unaware the expedition had been dispatched, chose not to engage the shore batteries in response. (Wikipedia; History.com; Imaginative Conservative; ABT)
- Some historians argue this is the actual first hostile military action of the Civil War. (Wikipedia; Genealogy Bank)

### The Confederacy forms
- **February 1861**: Seven seceded states form the Confederate States of America, adopting a constitution and electing Jefferson Davis as provisional president. Beauregard is appointed the first general officer commissioned by the CSA in March 1861. (Wikipedia)

### The dwindling supplies
By the time Lincoln was inaugurated, the garrison at Fort Sumter had only approximately six weeks of rations remaining. The fort's condition steadily deteriorated: 40+ guns were technically available but the garrison could only man a fraction simultaneously; they had only ~700 powder cartridges at the outset (additional cartridges were improvised from soldiers' uniforms and blankets). (Wikipedia; NPS; ABT)

### Lincoln inaugurated
- **March 4, 1861**: Lincoln inaugurated. Upon taking office he was confronted immediately with the Fort Sumter supply crisis. (general record)
- **April 4, 1861**: Lincoln orders a relief expedition under Gustavus V. Fox. (Wikipedia)
- **April 6, 1861**: Lincoln notifies South Carolina Governor Francis Pickens that a supply-only expedition (no troops, no weapons — provisions only) is en route. This notification was deliberate: Lincoln calculated that if the Confederates fired on a peaceful supply ship, the moral responsibility for starting the war would rest on them. (Wikipedia; Britannica)

## 4. The Decision to Fire

### Lincoln's notification and the Confederate dilemma
Lincoln's April 6 notification to Governor Pickens put the Confederate government in a bind: allow the resupply (tacitly acknowledging Union sovereignty over Fort Sumter) or fire (taking the responsibility for opening the war). (Britannica; Wikipedia)

### Confederate cabinet debate in Montgomery
- **April 9, 1861**: Jefferson Davis and the Confederate cabinet decide to act. The cabinet in Montgomery endorsed the decision to demand evacuation and fire if refused. (Wikipedia; American History Central)
- **The lone dissenter**: Confederate Secretary of State Robert Toombs opposed the attack, reportedly telling Davis: "It is unnecessary. It puts us in the wrong. It is fatal." He warned it would "strike a hornet's nest" and lose the Confederacy every friend in the North. (Wikipedia; American History Central — note: exact quote wording varies slightly across sources; the substance is well-attested)
- **April 10, 1861**: Confederate Secretary of War Leroy P. Walker telegraphs Beauregard with instructions to demand Fort Sumter's evacuation, and if refused, to "reduce" the fort. (Wikipedia; NPS; American History Central)

### Beauregard's ultimatum — April 11
- Beauregard dispatches three aides — Col. James Chesnut Jr., Col. A.R. Chisholm, and Capt. Stephen D. Lee — to deliver a formal written evacuation demand to Anderson. (Wikipedia; NPS)
- **Beauregard's demand (abridged)**: "I am ordered by the Government of the Confederate States to demand the evacuation of Fort Sumter... The flag which you have upheld for so long and with so much fortitude, under the most trying circumstances, impresses me with a just appreciation of your situation." He offered generous terms: Anderson and his garrison could march out with their arms and salute their flag. (ABT primary sources; Wikipedia)
- **Anderson's formal reply (April 11)**: Anderson declined: "it is a demand with which I regret that my sense of honor, and of my obligations to my Government, prevent my compliance." (ABT primary sources)
- But in conversation, Anderson reportedly told the aides: "I shall await the first shot, and if you do not batter us to pieces, we shall be starved out in a few days." (Wikipedia; multiple secondary sources) — this remark was relayed to Beauregard.

### The final ultimatum exchange — April 11–12
- Anderson's informal remark about starvation prompted Beauregard (with Davis's authorization) to send a second message offering to hold fire if Anderson would name a specific evacuation date and agree not to fire unless fired upon first. (Wikipedia; ABT)
- **1:00 a.m., April 12**: Beauregard's aides deliver the second message.
- **Anderson's conditional reply**: He would evacuate by noon on April 15, 1865 [sic — April 15, 1861], unless he received "controlling instructions from my Government or additional supplies." (Wikipedia; ABT primary sources)
- The Confederates judged this insufficient — the condition allowing resupply or new orders to override the evacuation date was unacceptable. (Wikipedia)
- **3:20 a.m., April 12**: Chesnut and Lee formally notify Anderson: "Sir: by authority of Brigadier General Beauregard, commanding the Provisional Forces of the Confederate States, we have the honor to notify you that he will open fire of his batteries on Fort Sumter in one hour from this time." (Wikipedia; ABT primary sources — exact quote)
- Anderson escorted the aides to their boat. His reported parting words: "If we never meet in this world again, God grant that we may meet in the next." (Wikipedia)

### Roger Pryor's refusal
Roger Pryor, a former Virginia congressman who had been urging South Carolinians to "strike a blow," was offered the honor of firing the first shot by Capt. George S. James. He declined, reportedly saying: "I could not fire the first gun of the war." He then got in a boat and rowed away before the bombardment began at ~4:15 a.m. (Wikipedia; Military.com; Civil War Talk)

## 5. The Bombardment

### Who fired the first shot — the central dispute
The "first shot" question has two distinct answers depending on what is being asked:

**The actual first round fired:**
At **4:30 a.m., April 12, 1861**, Lieutenant Henry S. Farley, on the order of Captain George S. James, pulled the lanyard on a 10-inch mortar at **Fort Johnson** on James Island. This was a signal shell — it arced over the harbor and burst above Fort Sumter, signaling all Confederate batteries to open fire. (Wikipedia; NPS; Military.com; Now We Know Em)

**Edmund Ruffin's shot:**
Edmund Ruffin, a 67-year-old Virginia secessionist and fire-eater, had traveled to Charleston specifically to be present at the opening of hostilities. He was stationed at the Iron Battery at Cummings Point on Morris Island. After Farley's signal round, Ruffin fired a 64-pound shell from the Iron Battery — one of the first shots in the general bombardment, but NOT the first shot overall. He claimed and was credited with firing the first shot in many contemporary Southern accounts, a legend that persisted. Most modern historians agree the signal mortar fired by Farley at James's order was the true first round. (Wikipedia; Encyclopedia Virginia; NPS — Edmund Ruffin page; Warfare History Network)

> **Contested:** The "first shot" attribution between Farley/James vs. Ruffin is explicitly disputed in the historical record. See Section 10.

### The ring of batteries opens fire
After Farley's signal shell, all 43 Confederate guns and mortars opened in sequence, firing at approximately 2-minute intervals in a counterclockwise pattern around the harbor. (Wikipedia)

### Union response — delayed and limited
Anderson held his fire until daylight. At approximately **7:00 a.m.**, Captain Abner Doubleday commanded and aimed the first Union shot — a 32-pound cannonball at the Iron Battery at Cummings Point. It bounced off the slanted iron roof and splashed into a swamp nearby, causing no damage. Doubleday chose a 32-pounder in the right gorge angle. After ~4 hours at the guns, he was relieved by Captain Truman Seymour. (NPS — Doubleday page; ABT; Military.com)

**Why Union fire was so limited:**
- The fort's upper-tier (barbette) guns were the heaviest and most effective, but using them would expose gunners above the parapet to direct fire from all sides. Anderson ordered fire only from the lower-tier (casemate) guns. (Wikipedia)
- Anderson could safely man only 21 working guns on the lowest level; gun design limitations prevented effective counter-fire trajectories against the surrounding land-based Confederate artillery. (Wikipedia)
- By the second day, Anderson reduced the active firing to only **6 guns**, due to ammunition and cartridge shortages. The garrison had begun with only ~700 cartridges; additional cartridges were improvised from soldiers' blankets and uniforms. (Wikipedia; NPS)

### The fires
The Confederates used **heated shot** (cannonballs heated red-hot in furnaces) aimed at the fort's wooden interior structures. By afternoon of April 12, fires were breaking out inside the fort. A rain shower at approximately 7:00 p.m. on April 12 temporarily extinguished the flames. The full bombardment resumed April 13. By noon on April 13, the main wooden barracks, main gate, and surrounding wooden structures were burning. The fires produced choking smoke that made the lower casemates nearly uninhabitable. (Wikipedia)

### The flag comes down
At approximately **1:00 p.m., April 13**, the fort's central flagpole was shot away, bringing down the U.S. flag. This was the visible signal that prompted Wigfall's approach. (Wikipedia; NPS)

### Wigfall's unauthorized truce
Louis T. Wigfall — a former U.S. senator from Texas, now a volunteer aide to Beauregard — observed the flag coming down from Morris Island. Without authorization from Beauregard or anyone else, he commandeered a small boat, had an enslaved man row him to the fort under a white handkerchief, and clambered through an embrasure. He told Anderson: "You have defended your flag nobly, Sir... General Beauregard wants to stop this fight." Anderson agreed to cease fire at approximately 2:00 p.m. (Wikipedia; History.com; NPS — Wigfall page)

**The confusion that followed:**
Shortly after, an official Beauregard delegation arrived (including Stephen D. Lee, Porcher Miles, and Roger Pryor) — unaware of Wigfall's mission. They told Anderson that Wigfall had not been near Beauregard for some time and had no authority. Anderson was furious and threatened to resume firing. Beauregard then formally offered the identical surrender terms Wigfall had proposed, and Anderson accepted. (Wikipedia; History.com)

### Surrender terms
Anderson's main negotiated condition: the garrison could fire a **100-gun salute** to the U.S. flag before evacuating. Beauregard agreed. (Wikipedia; NPS)

### The relief fleet's failure
Gustavus V. Fox's relief fleet had begun arriving off the harbor bar on April 12, but the flagship USS *Powhatan* had been diverted to Fort Pickens, Florida (by a separate order from Secretary of State Seward, unknown to Fox), depriving the fleet of its most powerful vessel. Heavy seas and Confederate artillery fire prevented supply boats from entering the harbor. Fox waited for darkness on April 13 but by then the fort had surrendered. (Wikipedia)

### Evacuation
- **April 14, 1861, ~2:00 p.m.**: The 100-gun salute begins. The accident at gun #47 kills Pvt. Hough and wounds five others; the salute is cut at 50 rounds.
- Anderson and his garrison evacuate the fort with "colors flying and drums beating," marching out with their arms. They board a Confederate steamer (*Isabel*) and are transferred overnight to Fox's ship, the USS *Baltic*.
- **April 15, 1861**: Garrison departs Charleston Harbor for New York. Anderson carries the Fort Sumter flag north as a rallying symbol. (Wikipedia; Anderson's official report)

**Beauregard's final ammunition estimate:** Beauregard had calculated his ammunition would last only 48 hours; the bombardment ended just within that window. (Wikipedia)

**Fort's final state:** Anderson reported to the Secretary of War on April 18: "Having defended Fort Sumter for thirty-four hours, until the quarters were entirely burned, the main gates destroyed by fire... with four barrels of powder and three cartridges remaining, and no provisions but pork..." (Anderson's official report, cited by Wikipedia)

## 7. What It Meant

### Lincoln's call for volunteers
- **April 15, 1861**: Lincoln issues a proclamation calling for **75,000 volunteer troops** (90-day enlistments) to suppress the insurrection. (Wikipedia; West Virginia Public Broadcasting; Proclamation 80)
- The response was overwhelming: Ohio alone could have supplied all 75,000 within 16 days. (Wikipedia)

### The Upper South secedes
Lincoln's call for troops — asking states to supply militias to suppress fellow Southern states — triggered the secession of four more states:
- **Virginia**: secedes April 17, 1861 (2 days after Lincoln's proclamation) (Wikipedia)
- **Arkansas**: secedes May 6, 1861 (had rejected secession in a March convention; reversed course after Lincoln's call) (Wikipedia; WV Public Broadcasting)
- **Tennessee**: secession vote May 7, 1861; popular referendum June 8, 1861 (Wikipedia; WV Public Broadcasting)
- **North Carolina**: secedes May 20, 1861 (Wikipedia; WV Public Broadcasting)

This expanded the Confederacy from 7 to 11 states by June 1861. (Wikipedia)

### Border state defusals
Governors of the remaining slave states that did not secede were nonetheless hostile to Lincoln's call:
- **Missouri Governor Claiborne Jackson**: "Not one man will the state of Missouri furnish to carry on any such unholy crusade." (Wikipedia)
- **Kentucky Governor Beriah Magoffin**: "Kentucky will furnish no troops for the wicked purpose of subduing her sister Southern states." (Wikipedia)

### Both sides rally
The fall of Fort Sumter galvanized Northern public opinion in support of the Union as powerfully as it galvanized Southern opinion for independence. Anderson returned to the North carrying the Fort Sumter garrison flag, which became a Union rallying symbol. The war that followed lasted four years, cost an estimated 620,000–850,000 American lives (scholars dispute the high end), and ended with the abolition of slavery. (Wikipedia; ABT; Britannica)

### Strategic symbolism over military value
Britannica notes that Fort Sumter held "no strategic value to the North" militarily at that moment, but possessed "enormous value as a symbol of the Union." Lincoln understood this; his deliberate choice to frame the supply mission as purely humanitarian (food for soldiers, not arms) was calculated to make the moral case internationally and domestically. (Britannica)

### Charleston Harbor remained Confederate throughout the war
Fort Sumter and Charleston Harbor remained in Confederate hands until February 1865, when Sherman's Carolinas campaign forced evacuation. The fort changed hands again on April 14, 1865 — exactly four years after the evacuation — when Anderson raised the same flag. Lincoln was assassinated that night. (Wikipedia)

## 9. Geography for a Map

### Charleston Harbor layout
Charleston Harbor opens to the **Atlantic Ocean to the southeast**. The harbor is shaped roughly like a funnel, with the city of Charleston at the northwest head and the harbor mouth at the southeast.

**Fort Sumter** sits on an artificial island (built on a sandbar using ~70,000 tons of New England granite, construction begun 1829) in the **main ship channel** near the harbor mouth. It commands the entrance to the harbor. (Wikipedia — Fort Sumter; ABT)

**Compass summary for map:**
- Harbor opens SE to the Atlantic
- Fort Sumter = center of harbor mouth, on artificial island
- Fort Moultrie = NE (Sullivan's Island, north shore)
- Fort Johnson = W/SW (James Island, south/west shore)
- Cummings Point = SSE/S (northern tip of Morris Island, south of channel)
- Charleston city = NW (up the harbor)
- The Confederate batteries formed a near-complete ring around Fort Sumter

> **Source caveat**: Precise compass bearings and mile-distances between each battery and Fort Sumter are not stated in precise numeric form in the sources consulted. The above orientation is consistent with all sources and with the known coordinates (32°45′N 79°52′W) but should be confirmed against a period map (e.g., the 1861 Perry map at the Library of Congress: LOC item 99448818) before committing specific mileage figures to prose.

## 10. Contested / Uncertain Facts

### A. Who fired the first shot?
**The dispute**: Edmund Ruffin is celebrated in Southern tradition as having fired the first shot of the Civil War at Fort Sumter. Modern historians generally credit the actual first round to Lt. Henry S. Farley, on Capt. George S. James's order, from the Fort Johnson mortar battery at 4:30 a.m.

**The resolution most historians accept**: Farley's signal mortar was the first projectile fired. Ruffin fired one of the first shots in the subsequent general bombardment, from the Iron Battery at Cummings Point, but this was after the signal round. Encyclopedia Virginia says Ruffin "is erroneously credited with firing the first shot" and that "most scholars agree that the person who fired the first shot is unknown" — meaning even Farley's precise primacy is not universally settled, though he is the most commonly identified candidate. (Encyclopedia Virginia; Wikipedia; NPS — Ruffin)

**For the author**: The safest framing is that Farley fired the signal round that opened the general bombardment; Ruffin was one of the first in the general barrage; the legend of Ruffin as "first shot" is a Confederate myth that was cultivated at the time.

### B. Exact garrison strength: 80 or 85?
Wikipedia and most academic sources cite 85 men. The American Battlefield Trust summary gives 80. The discrepancy likely reflects whether certain categories of personnel (officers, non-combatants, workmen) are included. Use 85 with a note. (Wikipedia; ABT)

### C. Salute gun number
Wikipedia and the Daniel Hough article consistently say the accident occurred at gun **#47** of the 100-gun salute. Some sources simply describe "a gun" without specifying the number. The #47 figure is well-attested and specific enough to use; it likely derives from Anderson's official report or eyewitness accounts. [Flag for author: cite as "the 47th gun" with a sourcing note]

### D. Edward Galloway's death timeline
Wikipedia says Galloway "died days later"; the more specific "five days later" appears in the Daniel Hough Wikipedia article. Some sources say he died "in a Charleston hospital" without specifying days. Use "days later" with "about five days" as a more precise but slightly less certain figure. (Wikipedia — Daniel Hough; NPS)

### E. Duration: "34 hours" vs. "nearly 36 hours"
Anderson's own official report says "thirty-four hours." The ABT site says "nearly 36 hours." The 34-hour figure is the primary-source-grounded one (Anderson's report). The discrepancy may arise from how one counts: 4:30 a.m. Apr 12 to ~2:00 p.m. Apr 13 is ~33.5 hours of bombardment; if you count through the formal signing of surrender terms, it approaches 36. Use **~34 hours** (Anderson's own number) and note the variation. (Wikipedia; Anderson official report; ABT)

### F. Toombs quote exact wording
The Robert Toombs "hornet's nest" speech to Jefferson Davis is well-attested in substance — multiple sources report it, and it became famous. However, the exact wording varies across sources. It was apparently spoken in a cabinet meeting, not written, and was recorded in diaries and memoirs after the fact. Treat as a reliable paraphrase of his position; do not present as a verbatim transcript. (Wikipedia; American History Central)

### G. Anderson's West Point role
Multiple sources confirm Anderson was Beauregard's artillery instructor at West Point, and that Beauregard was his favorite student and stayed on as his assistant after graduation (1838). This is consistent across sources. (Patriots Point; Walking Charleston; Tulane Beauregard biography; NPS)

### H. Was the Star of the West firing "the first shot of the Civil War"?
Some historians argue the Jan 9, 1861 firing on the *Star of the West* was the true first hostile act of the war. The conventional historical consensus treats Fort Sumter (April 12) as the opening battle. This is worth one sentence of acknowledgment in the narrative. (Imaginative Conservative; Genealogy Bank)
