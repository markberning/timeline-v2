### The mass surrender — the headline number
- **~29,495 Confederates surrendered and were PAROLED** at Vicksburg on July 4, 1863 (the figure most sources cite; round it as **"about 29,000"** or **"nearly 30,000"** in prose). Source: WP-Camp, WP-Siege, ABT.
- Grant **paroled** the garrison rather than ship ~30,000 prisoners north — they signed not to fight until formally exchanged. (Some later returned to the ranks, a point of postwar criticism.) Source: ABT; Grant's memoirs via ABT primary source.
- Also captured: **~172 cannon** and tens of thousands of small arms. Source: ABT ("10 Facts").

> **[contested]** Civil War casualty/strength numbers are genuinely disputed; let the fact-checker note the scholarly range. The ~29,495 surrender figure is stable across sources; the killed/wounded totals vary by ±hundreds depending on whether the inland battles are folded in.

## 4. Geography (for map accuracy — relative positions)

- **The Mississippi River** runs roughly north–south past Vicksburg; the city sits on **HIGH BLUFFS on the EAST bank**, above a **hairpin bend** in the river. Its guns commanded the channel — riverboats had to pass directly under them. This is why Vicksburg was "the key": it (with Port Hudson, LA, downstream) held the **last Confederate-controlled stretch of the river**. Source: WP-Camp; ABT.
- **De Soto Point** — the low, swampy peninsula on the **WEST (Louisiana) bank** opposite the city, inside the river bend. Site of "Grant's Canal." Source: WP-Camp; WP "Grant's Canal."
- **The bypass "experiments" (winter 1862–63), all FAILED** — Grant tried to get below/around the batteries through water:
  1. **Grant's Canal** across De Soto Point — to let boats skip the bend; abandoned ~March 1863 (river wouldn't cooperate). Source: WP "Grant's Canal."
  2. **Lake Providence** (LA, ~75 mi above Vicksburg) — a long bayou route to reach the river far below; Ed Bearss called it the "Lake Providence Boondoggle." Abandoned late March. Source: WP-Camp.
  3. **Yazoo Pass Expedition** — breach the levee near Moon Lake, descend the Coldwater→Tallahatchie→Yazoo to land above the city; halted by the hastily built **Fort Pemberton** (cotton-bale fort) at Greenwood. Source: WP-Camp.
  4. **Steele's Bayou Expedition** — Porter's gunboats up the Yazoo delta via Deer Creek to outflank Fort Pemberton (begun Mar 14); Confederates felled trees front and rear, the boats got stuck, nearly captured; Sherman's infantry rescued them. Source: WP-Camp.
- **Running the batteries (night of April 16, 1863)** — Porter ran **a fleet of gunboats + transports** SOUTH past the Vicksburg guns under a 2½-hour bombardment; most got through (one transport lost). A second run followed Apr 22. This put the fleet BELOW the city to ferry the army across. Source: WP-Camp; ABT.
- **Bruinsburg crossing (Apr 30, 1863)** — Grant marched the army DOWN the Louisiana (west) bank, then Porter's fleet ferried them ACROSS to **Bruinsburg, MS**, south of Vicksburg — the largest amphibious operation in U.S. history before D-Day (1944). Army stepped onto Mississippi soil Apr 30. Source: WP-Camp; ABT.
- **The inland march (the five battles), west→east then back:**
  - **Port Gibson** (May 1) — secures the beachhead, ~28 mi S of Vicksburg.
  - **Raymond** (May 12) — ~15 mi SW of Jackson.
  - **Jackson** (May 14) — the **state capital**, ~40 mi E of Vicksburg; Grant took it, wrecked its rail/industry, scattered Johnston, then turned WEST.
  - **Champion Hill** (May 16) — ~20 mi E of Vicksburg, **the decisive battle**, on the road back toward the city.
  - **Big Black River Bridge** (May 17) — the river crossing just E of Vicksburg; Pemberton's rearguard routed, fell back into the fortifications.
- **The Vicksburg fortifications** — a ~9-mile arc of earthworks, redans, and lunettes ringing the city's landward (east) side, anchored on the river bluffs at both ends. Key strongpoints in the assaults: the **Stockade Redan**, the **Great Redoubt**, the **Railroad Redoubt**, the **Third Louisiana Redan** (later mined/exploded June 25). Grant's **siege lines** dug ~15 miles of approach trenches facing them. Source: WP-Siege; ABT; NPS.
- **The caves** — civilians dug **~500 "bombproof" caves** into the soft yellow loess clay of the hillsides to shelter from bombardment. Source: WP-Siege; ABT; NPS.
- **Milliken's Bend, LA** — Union supply depot on the WEST bank ~15 mi above Vicksburg; site of the June 7 battle (see §6). Source: NPS.

## 5. Sequence & Turning Points

1. **The long winter of failed approaches (Dec 1862 – Mar 1863).** Sherman repulsed at **Chickasaw Bayou** (Dec 26–29, 1862) trying the bluffs north of the city; then the four water "experiments" (canals + bayou expeditions, §4) all fail. The Northern press calls Grant stuck, drunk, finished. Source: WP-Camp.
2. **The bold gamble (April 1863).** Grant's plan: **run the batteries** (Apr 16) to get the fleet below; **march the army down the west bank**; **cross at Bruinsburg** (Apr 30). To distract Pemberton he sends **Grierson's cavalry raid** (Apr 17–May 2) slashing 600 miles through Mississippi, and feints with Sherman at Haynes' Bluff. Source: WP-Camp; ABT.
3. **Cut loose from his base.** Once across, Grant **abandoned his supply line** and lived off the country — a daring move — driving inland fast. Source: ABT; WP-Camp.
4. **Drive a wedge between Johnston and Pemberton — win five battles in 18 days:** Port Gibson (May 1) → Raymond (May 12) → **take Jackson** (May 14, scatter Johnston, wreck the capital) → wheel west → **Champion Hill (May 16, the decisive battle)** → Big Black River Bridge (May 17). Pemberton, beaten, retreats inside the Vicksburg works. Source: WP-Camp; battle articles.
   - **Turning point:** Champion Hill **sealed Vicksburg's fate** — it ensured Grant stayed *between* the two Confederate armies so they could never unite. Loring's whole division was cut off and never rejoined Pemberton. Source: WP "Champion Hill"; ABT.
5. **The two failed assaults.** Grant tried to storm the works before settling in:
   - **May 19** — assault on the Stockade Redan repulsed. Union ~1,000 casualties (157 killed, 777 wounded, 8 missing) to Confederate ~70. Source: WP-Siege.
   - **May 22** — a broader assault across a 3-mile front at 10 a.m. also failed bloodily: Union ~3,200 (502 killed, 2,550 wounded, 147 missing) to Confederate under 500. (McClernand's overstated claim of a breakthrough drew reinforcements into a slaughter and helped get him relieved.) Source: WP-Siege.
6. **The 47-day siege (May 18 – July 4).** Grant: "I now determined upon a regular siege — to 'out-camp the enemy,' as it were, and to incur no more losses." (Grant, *Memoirs*, via WP-Siege.) Union dug approach trenches, brought up ~220 guns; the navy shelled from the river. Confederates and **~3,000+ civilians** trapped inside. Source: WP-Siege.
7. **Starvation.** Food ran out: beef gone, then **mule and horse meat**; **rats** sold in the meat market (reportedly ~$3.50 each, two weeks of a private's pay); pea/corn "bread"; "shoe leather became a last resort of sustenance for many adults." By late June **half the garrison was sick or hospitalized** (scurvy, malaria, dysentery, diarrhea). Source: WP-Siege; NPS; ABT.
8. **The mine.** June 25 — Union engineers tunneled under and **exploded a mine beneath the Third Louisiana Redan**, blowing a crater; the storming party couldn't hold it. A second mine July 1. Source: WP-Siege.
9. **Surrender, July 4, 1863.** Pemberton, out of food and hope and getting nothing from Johnston, opened negotiations July 3 (Bowen carried the flag). Grant first demanded **unconditional surrender**, then **offered parole** (he didn't want to ship 30,000 prisoners north). Pemberton surrendered the city and ~29,495 men on **July 4**. Source: WP-Siege; ABT; NPS.
   - **Pemberton's July-4 reasoning [VERIFY wording]:** Grant's *Memoirs* record that Pemberton (Northern-born, from Pennsylvania) believed he'd get **better terms on Independence Day**, banking on Union pride in the date — "I know my people; I know their dispositions... I shall surrender on the Fourth of July." Treat the exact quote as **[VERIFY]**; the gist (he chose/expected July 4 for terms) is well attested. Source: Grant's *Memoirs* via ABT/Cleveland CWRT.
   - Vicksburg reputedly **did not officially celebrate the Fourth of July for ~81 years** (until WWII / 1945). Treat the exact span as **[VERIFY]** (commonly cited as "81 years," sometimes other figures). Source: ABT; History.com.

## 6. THE CAUSE — SLAVERY (SHIP-BLOCKING; the documented throughline)

**The section MUST name slavery as the cause in its own prose.** The war was fought over slavery; at Vicksburg the abstraction became concrete — **emancipation happened on the ground as Grant's army moved.** Strong, sourced hooks:

- **Slavery freed by the army's advance.** As the Army of the Tennessee pushed through the Mississippi Valley (1862–63), **thousands of enslaved people self-emancipated** — walking off plantations to Union lines for freedom. **"July 4, 1863: Slavery Ends in Vicksburg"** is an actual NPS article title — the surrender ended slavery in the city itself. Source: NPS.
- **Self-emancipation, not just liberation.** NPS stresses that enslaved men and women **"more often already had taken steps to secure their own emancipation"** — young men slipping away in small groups to Union lines — rather than passively waiting to be freed. Source: NPS "African Americans and the campaign for Vicksburg."
- **Contraband camps.** Grant established **refugee/"contraband" camps** across the Mississippi Valley housing thousands of freedpeople; conditions were harsh (disease, prejudice, violence), some were put to leased-plantation wage labor. Adjutant Gen. **Lorenzo Thomas** recruited freedmen for the army — **~70,000 joined from the Mississippi Valley alone.** Source: NPS articles; Mississippi Encyclopedia.
- **Black soldiers in the fight — Milliken's Bend (June 7, 1863).** A Union supply depot on the Louisiana bank, garrisoned largely by the **African Brigade** — **newly recruited, formerly enslaved men** (9th, 11th, 13th Louisiana Infantry (African Descent), 1st Mississippi Infantry (African Descent)), poorly trained and poorly armed, plus the 23rd Iowa. **McCulloch's Texas Brigade (~1,500)** attacked at dawn; some of the **fiercest hand-to-hand combat of the war** (bayonets across the levee, cotton-bale barricades). Union gunboats **USS *Choctaw*** and **USS *Lexington*** broke the attack.
  - **Casualties:** Union ~**652** (≈101 killed, 285 wounded, 266 captured/missing); Confederate ~**185** (44 killed, 131 wounded, 10 missing). *(NPS figures; ABT/American History Central cite ~492 Union — **[contested]**, use a range.)* The **9th Louisiana lost ~68% of its strength** — the **greatest percentage loss of any Black regiment in the war**; its 66 dead were the most killed in any single Union regiment on any one day of the Vicksburg campaign. Source: NPS; ABT; American History Central.
  - **Why it matters:** Milliken's Bend (with Port Hudson and Fort Wagner) **proved Black men would and could fight**, shattering the claim they'd lack courage. **Sec. of War Edwin Stanton:** the formerly enslaved man **"proved his manhood"** in these fights. Source: NPS.
- **Lincoln tied the river victory and Black soldiers together explicitly** in his Aug 26, 1863 public letter (see §7) — the "unvexed" river *and* the "black men... they have helped mankind on to this great consummation." Use that to bind the cause to the victory. Source: Conkling.

> **Author hook:** the campaign that "split the Confederacy" also **split open slavery** along the river — every mile Grant advanced, the institution the South seceded to protect dissolved behind him. Name it plainly.

## 7. Key Quotes (exact attribution; shaky ones flagged [VERIFY])

- **Lincoln on Vicksburg's importance (1862):** *"Vicksburg is the key. The war can never be brought to a close until that key is in our pocket."* — Attributed to Lincoln (to his Cabinet/Porter). Widely cited; treat exact wording as **[VERIFY]** but the substance is standard. Source: ABT; multiple.
- **Lincoln on hearing of the fall (July 1863):** on Grant's news he reportedly sighed *"Thank God,"* then — and famously, in his **Aug 26, 1863 letter to James C. Conkling** — wrote:
  - *"The Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea. Thanks to the great Northwest for it. Nor yet wholly to them. Three hundred miles up, they met New-England, Empire, Key-stone, and Jersey, hewing their way right and left."* — **VERIFIED exact**, Conkling letter, Aug 26, 1863. Source: Conkling (abrahamlincolnonline.org).
  - On Black soldiers, same letter: *"...there will be some black men who can remember that, with silent tongue, and clenched teeth, and steady eye, and well-poised bayonet, they have helped mankind on to this great consummation."* — **VERIFIED exact.** Also: *"If they stake their lives for us, they must be prompted by the strongest motive—even the promise of freedom. And the promise being made, must be kept."* — **VERIFIED exact.** Source: Conkling.

- **Grant on Champion Hill's result [VERIFY exact]:** *"We were now assured of our position between Johnston and Pemberton, without a possibility of a junction of their forces."* — attributed to Grant. **[VERIFY]** exact wording. Source: secondary (American History Central).
- **Pemberton on choosing July 4 [VERIFY]:** the claim that he expected better terms on Independence Day ("I know my people... I shall surrender on the Fourth of July") — **[VERIFY]** exact words; gist attested via Grant's *Memoirs*. Source: Cleveland CWRT; Grant *Memoirs*.

## 8. Human Cost (named people + documented vivid detail)

- **The civilians in the caves.** ~3,000+ residents endured 47 days under bombardment; **~500 caves** ("bombproofs") dug into the loess hills, some single-room, some large enough for several families; residents learned the **shelling rhythm** and timed movement to it. Despite **~22,000 shells** fired into the town, **fewer than a dozen civilians were killed.** Source: WP-Siege; ABT; NPS.
  - **Mary Webster Loughborough**, 26 — trapped while visiting, wrote *My Cave Life in Vicksburg* (1864), a primary cave-diary. (See §7.) Source: ABT; women's history sources.
- **The starving garrison.** Soldiers and civilians ate **mule, horse, dog, rat, frog**; **rats sold in the public meat market** (~$3.50 each); **"shoe leather became a last resort of sustenance."** By late June **half the garrison was sick/hospitalized** — scurvy, malaria, dysentery, diarrhea. Source: WP-Siege; NPS; ABT.
- **Maj. Gen. John S. Bowen (South)** — Pemberton's best division commander, carried the surrender flag; **died of dysentery on July 13, 1863**, days after the surrender — a human marker of the disease toll. Source: WP-Camp; Bowen biographies.
- **The Black soldiers of Milliken's Bend** — formerly enslaved men, days into uniform, fighting hand-to-hand on the levee; the **9th Louisiana's ~68% loss** is the human core of the emancipation-on-the-ground story. Source: NPS; ABT. (§6)
- **The paroled army** — ~29,495 men marched out, stacked arms, signed paroles, and walked home gaunt; many had eaten mule and rat for weeks. Source: ABT; WP-Siege.

## 9. Significance (for the "what it meant" close)

- **Split the Confederacy in two.** With Vicksburg's fall (and **Port Hudson, LA, surrendering July 9, 1863**, five days later), the Union controlled the **entire Mississippi River** end to end; Texas, Arkansas, and most of Louisiana (the Trans-Mississippi) were **cut off** from the rest of the Confederacy. Source: WP-Camp; Britannica; History.com.
- **"The Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea"** — Lincoln's line for exactly this: the river was open and Union traffic could flow unmolested. **VERIFIED** (Conkling, Aug 26, 1863). Source: Conkling. §7.
- **Twin turning point with Gettysburg.** Vicksburg surrendered **July 4, 1863**, the day after the Confederate repulse at Gettysburg (Jul 1–3). The two together are conventionally the **military turning point of the war** — the Confederacy never recovered the initiative. Source: ABT; Britannica; History.com.
- **Made Grant.** The campaign cemented Grant's reputation (boldness, river-crossing, living off the land, the wedge between two armies) and put him on the path to general-in-chief in March 1864. Source: ABT; standard.
- **Emancipation made real.** It ended slavery in Vicksburg ("July 4, 1863: Slavery Ends in Vicksburg," NPS) and the river valley behind the army, and Milliken's Bend helped win the argument for arming Black men. Source: NPS. §6.
