## A. STAKES AND STRATEGIC CONTEXT

A1. Quebec City was the capital and heart of New France (France's North American colony of Canada). Its fall effectively decided the war and the fate of North America. [WP, ABT, CE]

A2. 1759 was Britain's "Annus Mirabilis" (year of wonders / year of victories) in the wider Seven Years' War, with British triumphs in several theatres that year. Quebec was the great North American prize among them. [WP, general SYW context]

A3. The British siege of Quebec was a combined army–navy operation. The fleet under Vice Admiral Charles Saunders (British) carried Maj. Gen. James Wolfe's (British) army up the St. Lawrence River, a notoriously difficult navigation. [USNI, WP]

A4. The 1758 British capture of Louisbourg (the great French fortress guarding the mouth of the Gulf of St. Lawrence) opened the river road to Quebec and made the 1759 expedition possible. [general SYW context; PILL target]

## B. THE SUMMER SIEGE AND BOMBARDMENT

B1. British forces began landing on Île d'Orléans (an island in the St. Lawrence just below Quebec) on June 26, 1759. [ABT]

B2. By about July 12, 1759, Wolfe had positioned mortars and cannon at Point Lévis (Pointe-Lévy), on the south shore directly across the river from Quebec, and began bombarding the city. The bombardment continued for weeks and was aimed in part at civilian morale and the city's buildings. [ABT, DCB-W]

B3. The siege was long, frustrating, and largely deadlocked through the summer. Montcalm's main French army was dug into the Beauport lines (entrenchments along the north shore east of the city) and would not be drawn into open battle. [WP, ABT, DCB-M]

B4. Wolfe was a major-general and commander-in-chief of the British land forces; he was 32 years old in 1759. [DCB-W]

B5. Wolfe fell ill during the campaign. In mid-August 1759 he was so sick he was confined to bed; sources note "gravel & Rheumatism" and an attack of dysentery. [DCB-W]

## C. THE SCORCHED-EARTH RAIDS (tell honestly)

C1. Wolfe turned to a scorched-earth policy, ordering the systematic burning of farms, houses, and outbuildings across the Canadian countryside around Quebec, to make the militia desert their posts and to exhaust the colony. [NBC, multiple secondary accounts]

C2. Major George Scott reported burning "998 good buildings" on the south shore alone. [DCB-W]

C3. DCB-W notes that communities on both shores of the river below Quebec "had been largely destroyed, only churches being spared." [DCB-W]

C4. DISPUTED TOTAL: Estimates of houses/farms destroyed across the whole campaign reach "nearly 1,400" by one fairly conservative estimate; some French-side accounts say no village was spared and many would have to be rebuilt entirely. Parishes named as ravaged include L'Ange-Gardien, Château-Richer, and Saint-Joachim (late August), with destruction reported as far downriver as Kamouraska. RANGE: ~1,000 buildings (south shore, Scott's count) to ~1,400+ (campaign total). Use the conservative framing; do not assert a single false-precise number. [NBC, DCB-W, secondary]

C5. CAUTION / not fully verified for our use: a "Saint-Joachim massacre," in which prisoners or surrendering Canadians were killed, is reported in some accounts of the late-August raids. Treat as a contested atrocity claim; the verified core is that the parishes were burned. Do not assert specific killings as fact in the draft beyond the documented burning. [flagged]

## D. THE MONTMORENCY ASSAULT (the failed attack, July 31, 1759)

D1. On July 31, 1759, Wolfe attempted a landing and frontal assault near the Montmorency Falls, downriver (east) of Quebec, to break the French Beauport position. [ABT, BB, DCB-W]

D2. The assault failed. Tidal conditions and disorganization disrupted the landing, and the grenadiers' frontal attack on the French entrenchments was repulsed with heavy loss. [ABT, BB, DCB-W]

D3. DISPUTED CASUALTIES (British): figures given as ~443 killed and wounded (ABT), "around 500" (BB, grenadiers + 60th), and "over 200 killed" (DCB-W). French losses ~60 (ABT). RANGE for British: roughly 440–500 total killed and wounded; use "more than four hundred" or "roughly four hundred and fifty." [ABT, BB, DCB-W]

D4. Wolfe exposed himself recklessly to French fire at Montmorency. [DCB-W]

## E. THE PLAN AND THE NIGHT DESCENT TO THE ANSE AU FOULON

E1. Wolfe chose to land upstream (west) of Quebec at the Anse au Foulon, a small cove below steep cliffs, from which a narrow path climbed to the heights and the plateau outside the city walls. DCB-W: "For reasons that are still obscure" Wolfe chose this risky site; the final successful plan was basically the brigadiers'. [DCB-W, WP]

E2. The cliff/path at the Foulon was steep; the climb was made by pulling up using roots and rocks along the narrow trail. [WP search snippet, ABT]

E3. On the night of September 12–13, 1759, Admiral Saunders staged a diversionary feint and bombardment opposite the French Beauport lines (between Beauport and the St. Charles River), drawing Montcalm's attention downriver while the real landing went in upstream. [ABT, USNI, DCB-W]

E4. The landing convoy: around 30 boats/landing craft. Around 2:00 a.m. on the 13th, HMS Sutherland hoisted two lanterns as the signal to start; the boats set out with the ebb tide on a roughly 10-mile trip downriver to the cove. [USNI]

E5. The advance force landed at the Anse au Foulon at about 4:00 a.m. on September 13, 1759. By sunrise Wolfe and the first division were on the plateau; by about 8:00 a.m. the whole force of roughly 4,400–4,500 men had assembled on the Plains of Abraham. [USNI, BB]

E6. PASSING THE SENTRIES: As the boats drifted down, a French sentry challenged them; a Highland officer who spoke French answered in French and the boats were allowed to pass. (One account names Captain Donald MacDonald of the 78th Fraser Highlanders, formerly in French service, telling the sentries in French that he was relieving the post; the French were partly expecting a provisions convoy that night.) Treat the exact name/wording as a well-attested tradition with some variation. [BB, WP search snippet]

E7. Light infantry led the climb. Lieutenant Colonel William Howe (British) led companies up the cliff and overwhelmed the small French outpost guarding the path; the ascent "seems to have been improvised." Captain Delaune and the light infantry are named in some accounts. [ABT, DCB-W, search snippet]

E8. THE GUARD AT THE TOP: The post above the cove was held by a small detachment of Canadian militia (about 100) under militia captain Louis Du Pont Duchambon de Vergor (French/Canadian), who was caught off guard and quickly overrun. [search snippet/kronoskaf, BB]

E9. Captain James Cook (the later Pacific explorer, then a naval master) had earlier helped chart the treacherous St. Lawrence for the fleet; the navigation up the river was regarded as a remarkable feat of seamanship. [USNI, general]

## F. THE BATTLE ON THE PLAINS OF ABRAHAM (September 13, 1759)

F1. The battle was fought on the Plains of Abraham, a plateau just outside the walls of Quebec. It involved fewer than 10,000 troops total and the fighting itself was very short, lasting on the order of fifteen minutes to about an hour depending on what is counted. [WP, ABT]

F2. BRITISH FORCE: 4,426 men (Government of Canada figure), all British Army regulars, with two bronze 6-pounder cannon hauled up. [WP] (ABT: ~4,400.)

F3. FRENCH FORCE ENGAGED: Montcalm brought up roughly 4,500 men to fight, of an army around Quebec that totaled far more (figures of ~13,000 to ~16,000 for the whole defense). The battle force is variously given as ~1,900 French regulars + ~600 Canadian regulars + ~1,500–1,800 militia and Native allies; ABT gives 2,000 regulars, 600 colonial regulars, 1,800 militia + Native allies. RANGE: French regulars ~1,900–2,600 (incl. colonial), militia + allies ~1,500–1,800; total engaged ~3,500–4,500. [WP, ABT, CE, DCB-M]

F4. NATIVE ALLIES: roughly 400 warriors, including Mi'kmaq, Maliseet, Abenaki, Potawatomi, Odawa (Ottawa), and Wendat (Huron); Odawa under Charles-Michel Mouet de Langlade. They and the Canadian militia fought hard on the flanks and from the woods, and their fire prevented the British from destroying the retreating French. [ABT, DCB-M]

F5. CONTESTED DECISION TO FIGHT IN THE OPEN: Montcalm chose to attack immediately at mid-morning with the troops at hand rather than wait for the ~3,000-man force under Col. Louis-Antoine de Bougainville (French), which was upriver to the west, or for other reinforcements. DCB-M is sharply critical: "With the enemy virtually at his mercy, Montcalm chose the one course of action that ensured his defeat." DCB-M also notes Montcalm failed even to notify Bougainville that the British had landed; it was Governor Vaudreuil who sent word. Montcalm's stated reasoning was that the British were entrenching and could not be allowed to dig in. There was long-running tension between Montcalm and Governor Vaudreuil over how to defend the colony. [DCB-M, WP, ABT]

F6. THE FRENCH ADVANCE: The French regulars advanced toward the British line; their formation included militia mixed into the battalions. The advance was ragged. Many militiamen, untrained in European line tactics and lacking bayonets, fired and then dropped to the ground to reload, breaking up the line. [ABT, DCB-M]

F7. THE BRITISH VOLLEY: The British infantry held their fire until the French were very close (accounts give roughly 30–40 yards; BB: "the range is 35 yards, it is said"), then delivered crashing, disciplined volleys by battalion. Two volleys were enough to shatter the French line. [BB, ABT, DCB-M]

F8. THE ROUT: The French line broke and fled back toward the city and gates. The British swept forward, taking the field and capturing hundreds of prisoners (ABT: ~350 captured). [WP, ABT]

## G. THE TWO GENERALS FALL

G1. WOLFE: hit first in the wrist/hand (which he disregarded), then struck in the body (many accounts say two bullets / chest and stomach); the body wound was fatal. He died on the field at the moment of victory, as the French broke. [DCB-W, ABT]

G2. WOLFE'S BODY was brought aboard ship (HMS Lowestoft per DCB-W) about 11 a.m. [DCB-W]

G3. MONTCALM: mortally wounded during/after the rout (hit in the lower body / below the ribs; one account says grapeshot), and carried into the city through the Saint-Louis gate. He received the last sacraments that night and died at dawn the next day, September 14, 1759. He was buried in a shell crater under the floor of the Ursuline convent chapel. [DCB-M, WP, ABT]

G4. DISPUTED detail: the exact anatomical wound location for both men varies by source; do not state a single precise wound site as certain. [DCB-W, DCB-M]

## H. CASUALTIES (battle of Sept 13)

H1. Both sides each lost on the order of 600+ killed and wounded. [WP, ABT]

H2. BRITISH: ~58 killed, ~600 wounded (ABT); ~650 total killed/wounded (WP). [ABT, WP]

H3. FRENCH: ~640 total (WP); ABT gives 116–350 killed (sources vary), ~600 wounded, ~350 captured. RANGE; use "about six hundred" or "roughly six hundred and forty." [WP, ABT]

H4. Both commanders among the dead. [WP, ABT, CE]

## I. SURRENDER AND AFTERMATH

I1. After the battle the French field army withdrew; Quebec City capitulated. The capitulation was signed September 18, 1759 (five days after the battle). [ABT, WP, BB]

I2. THE WAR WAS NOT QUITE OVER. In the spring, the French under the Chevalier de Lévis marched on the British-held city and won the Battle of Sainte-Foy on April 28, 1760, defeating the British garrison under Brig. Gen. James Murray outside the walls. It was bloodier than the Plains of Abraham: ~833 French casualties to ~1,124 British. [WP-Sainte-Foy, ABT]

I3. Despite the victory at Sainte-Foy, Lévis could not retake the city. In mid-May 1760, British (not French) ships arrived in the St. Lawrence; with no French naval relief, Lévis raised the siege and fell back to Montreal. [WP-Sainte-Foy]

I4. Montreal, the last stronghold of New France, surrendered in September 1760 (Sept 8, 1760) to British forces converging from three directions, ending French rule in Canada. [WP, ABT; PILL target]

I5. The Treaty of Paris (1763) formally ceded New France to Britain. [WP, ABT]

## J. BOUGAINVILLE

J1. Col. Louis-Antoine de Bougainville (French) commanded a mobile force (~3,000) upriver west of Quebec, watching for a British landing above the city. He arrived too late to affect the main battle; after Montcalm's defeat the British rear (under Brig. Gen. George Townshend) had to fend off / face Bougainville's force, which then withdrew. [BB, DCB-M] Bougainville later became famous as a Pacific explorer.

## K. KEY PEOPLE (full names, sides)
- Maj. Gen. James Wolfe (British) — land commander, killed Sept 13.
- Lt. Gen. Louis-Joseph de Montcalm, Marquis de Saint-Véran (French) — French army commander, mortally wounded Sept 13, died Sept 14.
- Vice Adm. Charles Saunders (British) — naval commander; the fleet that brought the army up the river and ran the feint.
- Brig. Gen. George Townshend (British) — took British command after Wolfe fell; faced Bougainville and oversaw the surrender.
- Brig. Gen. James Murray (British) — later defeated at Sainte-Foy 1760.
- Lt. Col. William Howe (British) — led light infantry up the cliff (later commander in the American Revolution).
- Col. Louis-Antoine de Bougainville (French) — mobile force upriver, arrived too late.
- Capt. Louis Du Pont Duchambon de Vergor (French/Canadian militia) — commanded the small guard at the top of the Foulon path, overrun.
- Charles-Michel Mouet de Langlade (Canadian) — led Odawa allies.
- Pierre de Rigaud, Marquis de Vaudreuil (French) — Governor of New France; at odds with Montcalm; sent word to Bougainville.
- Chevalier de Lévis (French) — Montcalm's successor; won Sainte-Foy 1760.
- (Capt. James Cook, British — helped chart the river; not at the battle.)

## L. LEGENDS — FLAGGED, DO NOT ASSERT AS FACT

L1. **Wolfe reciting Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard"** the night before, supposedly dwelling on the line "the paths of glory lead but to the grave." This comes from historian Francis Parkman and is UNVERIFIED literary tradition, not documented fact. May be mentioned in the draft ONLY as a famous legend, explicitly labeled as such. [ABT]

L2. **Wolfe singing "How Stands the Glass Around"** — explicitly labeled a myth. Do not use. [ABT]

L3. **A French traitor pointing out the Foulon path to Wolfe** — DCB-W: "no evidence...ever been adduced." Do not assert. [DCB-W]

## M. UNVERIFIED / TRADITION-ONLY QUOTES — DO NOT USE AS VERBATIM

These dying-words quotations are famous but rest on secondhand tradition with variant wording. DO NOT present any as a verbatim QUOTE in the draft. They may be referenced ONLY as "by tradition" / "as the story is told," clearly hedged, and NOT in a QUOTE: line.

M1. Wolfe, on being told the French run: reportedly "Who? ... Now, God be praised, I will die in peace." [ABT, BB — tradition, variant wording]

M2. Montcalm, told he had only hours to live: reportedly "All the better...I will not see the English in Quebec" (or "So much the better, I shall not live to see the surrender of Quebec"). [ABT — tradition, variant wording]

Neither DCB-W nor DCB-M records last words; their absence in the scholarly biographies is itself a caution. The draft contains NO QUOTE: lines for either general's dying words.
