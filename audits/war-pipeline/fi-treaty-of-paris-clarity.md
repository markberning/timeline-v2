# NEWCOMER / CLARITY Critic Report
## Section: "The Treaty of Paris & the Proclamation Line" (French & Indian War — theme)
## Read cold by a sharp 15-year-old with zero prior knowledge
## Date: 2026-06-02

**VERDICT: PASS** — no genuine comprehension-breaker (LOST) remains.

The draft is unusually reader-friendly for a dense diplomatic topic. Nearly every
specialized term is glossed inline on first use, named diplomats carry a
nationality tag in parentheses, and the two documents are introduced concretely
("two pieces of paper") before any abstraction. A cold reader can follow the whole
arc. The findings below are NEEDS-GLOSS (clarity polish, not blocking) and one
note on a borderline wall-of-text. No MUST-FIX items.

## What works (CLEAR)

- **The two-documents framing** (¶1) is the best possible on-ramp: it tells the
  reader exactly what the section is about before any jargon.
- **Glossed-on-first-use, done right:**
  - "French and Indian War, named for the enemies they faced, the French and their
    Native allies" — CLEAR.
  - "Seven Years' War, a global conflict that raged in the Caribbean, in India..." — CLEAR.
  - "New France, France's whole mainland empire" — CLEAR.
  - "Third Family Compact... an alliance between the two Bourbon monarchies (Spain
    and France were ruled by branches of the same royal family, the Bourbons)" —
    CLEAR, and it pre-empts the exact question a newcomer would ask.
  - "Louisiana, meaning everything west of the Mississippi, plus New Orleans
    itself" — CLEAR.
  - "Land speculators (men who bought up large tracts hoping to resell them at a
    profit)" — CLEAR, glossed exactly where needed.
  - "the crest of the Appalachian Mountains, the long ridge that separated the
    settled colonies on the Atlantic coast from the interior" — CLEAR.
- **Diplomat side-tags:** Bedford (British), Praslin (French), Grimaldi (Spanish),
  Choiseul (French), Crawford (British) all carry a tag — newcomer never has to
  guess whose side a long foreign name is on. Good.
- **Voltaire / Candide** is introduced as "The writer Voltaire, in his novel
  *Candide*" — enough scene-set for a cold reader; the French phrase is translated.
- **George Washington** needs no scene-set (marquee name); the draft still adds why
  he matters here ("promised land bounties for his service"). CLEAR.
- **Pontiac** is glossed ("the Odawa war leader Pontiac, whose name in his own
  language was Obwandiyag"). CLEAR.
- **The Native-nations list** (¶117) gives the common English name in parens for the
  unfamiliar ones (Odawa (Ottawa), Lenape (also called the Delaware)) — exactly
  right for a newcomer.

## NEEDS-GLOSS (clarity polish — not blocking)

1. **"with Portugal in agreement" (¶26).** A cold reader has heard nothing about
   Portugal anywhere else in the section and will briefly stall on why Portugal is
   suddenly a party to a Britain/France/Spain peace. One half-clause ("Portugal,
   Britain's ally, also signed") would remove the snag. Minor; the sentence still
   parses without it.

2. **"the river Iberville" inside the verbatim border quote (¶29).** The treaty
   quote names "the river Iberville," a river no newcomer has heard of and which is
   never explained. It is inside a verbatim quotation so it cannot be reworded, but
   a reader briefly wonders what/where it is. The surrounding prose already says the
   border is "the middle of the River Mississippi," so comprehension of the main
   point is not lost — this is a flicker, not a break. Optional: a trailing
   half-sentence locating it ("...the Iberville, a channel near the river's mouth").

3. **"Pondicherry in India, Senegal in West Africa" (¶19) vs. later "Chandernagore
   and Pondicherry... trading posts in India" (¶35).** First mention (¶19) lists
   Pondicherry in a haul of place-names without saying what it is; the gloss
   ("trading posts in India") only arrives 16 paragraphs later in ¶35. A newcomer
   meets the word cold the first time. Not a break (the later gloss lands), but the
   gloss is in the wrong order. Optional: tag it as a trading post at first mention.

4. **"grandees with long names" (¶27).** "Grandee" is mildly above the common-English
   line for a 15-year-old, though context ("with long names," followed by the names)
   makes the meaning recoverable. Borderline; flagging for awareness only, not a fix
   I'd insist on — over-glossing common-ish words is itself a defect.

5. **"theatres goes quiet" / "one front" (¶13, ¶15).** "Front" is glossed-by-context
   in ¶13 ("just the corner of it that happened to be on fire"); "theatre" appears
   once in ¶15 meaning the same thing. A newcomer who maps "front" = "theatre" is
   fine, but the two words are used interchangeably without a bridge. Trivial;
   context carries it. Awareness only.

6. **"Gorée in West Africa" (¶35).** Listed among what France kept without a word on
   what it is (an island/trading post). Same class as Pondicherry but lower stakes
   because it's in a "France kept its sugar and cod" summary the reader can skim. The
   reader does not need to know what Gorée is to get the paragraph's point. Awareness
   only.

## Wall-of-text / pacing check

- **¶31 (the New Orleans / Fontainebleau exception)** is the densest paragraph in
  the piece: a verbatim quote, a secret prior treaty, a "two different doors"
  metaphor, and a parenthetical about 1769. It is long, but it is *sequenced* well
  (treaty says X → in reality Y → therefore Z) and the "two different doors" line
  lands the point cleanly. A cold reader can follow it on one read. **NOT a
  wall-of-text fail** — flagging only that it is the single heaviest lift in the
  section and sits right at the comprehension ceiling. No change required.

- No other passage overwhelms. The eyebrows and short sections keep the load light,
  and the recurring "two pieces of paper" spine gives the reader a handrail.

## Unexplained-leap check

- **The "back door of the Thirteen Colonies" (¶53)** assumes the reader pictures
  Canada sitting behind/above the colonies. A 15-year-old with a rough map sense
  gets it; the metaphor is self-explaining enough. CLEAR.
- **"the road to revolution" / "war against Britain itself" (¶63, ¶107)** are
  forward handoffs, explicitly flagged as "a story for later" — the leap is
  *labeled* as out of scope, so it does not read as a hole. CLEAR.
- **The Washington letter (¶101)** could have been opaque (1767 letter to a land
  agent) but the draft sets up who Crawford is, why Washington is writing, and what
  "hunting other Game" really means. CLEAR.
