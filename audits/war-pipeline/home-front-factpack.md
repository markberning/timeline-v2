## BEAT 1: The Confederate Draft — First Conscription in American History (April 1862)

- **The South drafted BEFORE the North.** The First Conscription Act was passed by the Confederate Congress and signed **April 16, 1862** — the first national military draft in American history, predating the Union's by nearly a year. This is a frequently-missed irony: the states-rights Confederacy created compulsory national military service first.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Confederate Conscription Acts 1862–1864"; Library of Congress, "Civil War Conscription Laws" (In Custodia Legis blog)

- **Why:** By spring 1862 the Confederacy faced a manpower crisis. Most initial volunteers had signed 12-month enlistments in 1861; those terms were expiring and re-enlistment was far below what was hoped, just as the Union was mounting major offensives. Conscription was the fix.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Confederate Conscription Acts 1862–1864"

- **Who was liable (First Act):** all white men aged **18 to 35**, made liable for **three years** of service. Later acts widened the band: the Second Conscription Act (September 1862) raised the upper age to 45; by February 1864 the range was extended to **17 to 50**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Confederate Conscription Acts 1862–1864"; Encyclopedia of Arkansas, "Conscription (Civil War)"

- **Substitution:** A drafted man could hire a substitute to serve in his place. The practice was heavily criticized as a loophole for the well-off and was **abolished December 28, 1863**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Confederate Conscription Acts 1862–1864"

- **The Twenty-Negro Law (passed October 11, 1862):** A new exemption — soon nicknamed the "Twenty Negro Law" (also "Twenty-Slave Law" / "Overseer Law") — exempted from military service **one white man for every twenty enslaved people owned** on a plantation, or across two or more plantations within five miles of each other that together held twenty or more slaves. Its stated purpose was to keep an overseer on the land to maintain control of the enslaved (fear of insurrection was sharp after Lincoln's preliminary Emancipation Proclamation) and keep plantations producing.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Twenty Negro Law"; American Battlefield Trust, "The Twenty Negro Law"; Encyclopedia Virginia, "Twenty-Slave Law"

- **Timing made it explosive:** The Twenty-Negro Law passed October 11, 1862 — just weeks after Lincoln's **preliminary Emancipation Proclamation** (September 22, 1862). It read, to ordinary soldiers, as the planter class buying its sons out of the war the planters had started.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Twenty Negro Law"; American Battlefield Trust, "The Twenty Negro Law"

- **This is where "rich man's war, poor man's fight" was forged in the South.** The slogan recurs North and South wherever the draft appeared, but the Twenty-Negro Law gave it its sharpest Southern edge.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "The Twenty Negro Law"

- **1863 amendment (May 1863):** Tightened the exemption — the applicant had to have been an overseer before April 16, 1862, plantations couldn't be subdivided after October 11, 1862 to qualify more men, and a **$500 fee** was attached.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Twenty Negro Law"

- **1864 revision (February 1864, Third Conscription Act):** Lowered the threshold from 20 enslaved people to **15**, but required the exempted person to sell 100 pounds of bacon or beef per slave to the government at fixed government prices.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Twenty Negro Law"

- **The law's actual reach was tiny — the resentment was the point, not the numbers.** Exemptions granted were small: roughly 200 in Virginia, 120 in North Carolina, 201 in Georgia, 300 in South Carolina — a few hundred per state, out of an estimated ~38,000 overseers nationwide. The political damage to Confederate morale far exceeded the number of men actually exempted.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Twenty Negro Law" (citing Confederate exemption records)

## BEAT 2: The Union Draft — The Enrollment Act (March 3, 1863)

- **Signed March 3, 1863.** The Enrollment Act (a.k.a. the Civil War Military Draft Act / Conscription Act) was the **first genuine federal conscription** in U.S. history. (A militia draft existed in 1862 but the Enrollment Act was the first true national one.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Enrollment Act"; American History Central, "Conscription Act Facts"

- **Who was liable:** every male **citizen** and every immigrant who had filed a declaration of intent to become a citizen, aged **20 to 45**, unless exempted.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Enrollment Act"

- **The two escape hatches — the heart of the inequity:**
  1. **Commutation:** pay a **$300 fee** to the government and be excused from that draft call.
  2. **Substitution:** furnish an acceptable substitute to serve in your place.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Enrollment Act"

- **What $300 meant:** roughly the equivalent of a full year's wages for an unskilled laborer (Wikipedia frames it as ~$5,000 in 2018 dollars; in 1863 terms it was about a year's pay for a working man). For the working class it was unreachable; for the comfortable it was a check.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Enrollment Act"

- **"A rich man's war and a poor man's fight."** The commutation-and-substitution system led directly to this slogan in the North, exactly as the Twenty-Negro Law had in the South.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Enrollment Act"

- **The substitution racket — "bounty jumpers":** Career "jumpers" enlisted as paid substitutes (or for enlistment bounties), collected the money, deserted before reaching the front, and repeated the cycle under new names. Substitutes were widely regarded as lower-quality soldiers than volunteers.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Enrollment Act"

- **Bounties:** Federal, state, and local governments paid cash **bounties** to induce volunteering, in part to fill draft quotas without actually drafting men. The draft's main effect was as a *threat* that drove volunteering and bounty enlistment, not as a direct supplier of soldiers.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Enrollment Act"; "We Need Men: The Union Draft in the Civil War" (Geary, summarized)

- **The draft was changed over time:** commutation was progressively restricted. In the first draft, $300 bought a three-year exemption; the **1864 Enrollment Act amendment limited commutation to a single draft call (one year)**, and in **July 1864 Lincoln signed a bill effectively eliminating commutation except for conscientious objectors.** Draft calls came in **October 1863, March 1864, July 1864, and December 1864.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Enrollment Act"; conscription-statistics summaries (see Beat 3)

## BEAT 3: How Few Men the Union Draft Actually Conscripted

- **The draft produced very few actual conscripts — its power was coercive, not direct.** Of those who **served in the Union Army, only about 2% were draftees** (conscripts held to service). The draft mainly pressured men to volunteer or to hire substitutes.
  — Source: "We Need Men: The Union Draft in the Civil War" (Geary), summarized; corroborated across conscription literature

- **The 1863 draft, broken down (frequently cited figures):** Of roughly **292,441 names drawn in 1863**, about **190,000 were excused** for medical disability or hardship, about **52,000 paid the $300 commutation**, and about **26,000 furnished a substitute** — leaving only about **9,881 men (≈3%) who were actually held as conscripts.**
  — Source: conscription-statistics summary (widely cited; corroborates Geary, "We Need Men"). **[VERIFY exact sub-totals against a primary Provost Marshal General report before quoting any single number as gospel — see CONTESTED.]**

- **Substitutes across the whole war:** estimates vary — roughly **74,000** men hired substitutes by one count, with other accounts citing up to ~116,000 substitutes furnished overall. **[VERIFY — figure range; see CONTESTED.]**
  — Source: conscription-statistics summary

- **Author takeaway (well-supported):** The Northern draft is best understood as a *system to make volunteering and bounty-paying happen* under threat — a coercive lever — far more than as a machine that put conscripts in uniform. The $300 fee and substitution are why it was hated even though it directly drafted so few.

## BEAT 4: The New York City Draft Riots (July 13–16, 1863)

> FRAMING NOTE for the author: cover this as a home-front explosion at the intersection of the draft + the war economy + race — working-class fury at a draft the rich could buy out of, fused with labor and racial competition and resentment of the war's new emancipation aim. It is NOT the Copperhead political story (that's another section).

- **The worst civil insurrection in U.S. history to that point.** Known at the time as "Draft Week"; sometimes called the Manhattan draft riots. Violent disturbances in Lower/Midtown Manhattan, widely regarded as the culmination of working-class discontent with the new federal draft.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"; History.com, "New York Draft Riots"

- **The trigger:** The first draft lottery drawing in NYC was held **peacefully on Saturday, July 11, 1863**. The **second drawing began Monday, July 13** at the Ninth District provost marshal's office (Third Avenue and 47th Street); around 10 a.m. a crowd of ~500 attacked it. Volunteer firemen of Engine Company 33 — the "Black Joke" — led the initial assault. The riot exploded from there and ran four days, through **July 16**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"

- **Who:** Predominantly **Irish American working-class** rioters confronted police, soldiers, and pro-war figures. Underlying grievances: the $300 commutation they couldn't afford; the draft landing hardest on the poor; and — after the Emancipation Proclamation (Jan 1, 1863) reframed the war's aim — fear of labor and racial competition with Black workers (Black laborers had been used as strikebreakers on the NYC waterfront earlier that year).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"; History.com, "New York Draft Riots"

- **It turned into a racial pogrom.** The protest against the draft became a **race riot targeting Black New Yorkers** — beatings, lynchings, and the destruction of Black homes and businesses.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"

- **The Colored Orphan Asylum burned (Monday, July 13, ~4 p.m.).** A mob attacked the Colored Orphan Asylum at **Fifth Avenue and 43rd Street**, which housed roughly **230 Black children**. Police and staff held the mob off long enough for **all the children to escape out the back**; the building was then looted and burned to the ground.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"; Tenement Museum, "After the Riots"

- **Lynchings:** Over the four/five days, **eleven Black men and boys were hanged/murdered** by mobs (often beaten, then hung from lampposts or trees, sometimes burned). Among the victims was a seven-year-old Bermudian child, Joseph Reed.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"

- **Order restored by troops fresh from Gettysburg.** Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton authorized **five regiments** pulled from the Army of the Potomac after **Gettysburg** (fought July 1–3, 1863). Units including the **7th New York State Militia, 152nd New York, 26th Michigan, and 27th Indiana** force-marched to the city and helped suppress the riots by **July 16**; by the end **over 4,000 soldiers** garrisoned New York.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"; Lincoln Shrine, "New York City Draft Riots"

- **Officials to name (for the author):** Provost Marshal General (U.S.A.) **Col. James B. Fry**; Provost Marshal for NYC **Col. Robert Nugent**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"

- **Death toll:** the **official figure was ~119–120**, but estimates run much higher (see CONTESTED). Property damage was estimated at roughly **$1–5 million** (1863 dollars), with about 50 buildings burned, including the orphan asylum and two Protestant churches.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"; History.com, "New York Draft Riots"

- **Aftermath:** The draft was suspended in the city, then resumed in August under heavy military guard and went forward. The riots are remembered as the bloodiest civil disorder in U.S. history.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"

## BEAT 5: The Southern Home Front's Collapse — Inflation, Scarcity, the Blockade's Bite

- **Runaway inflation — the defining fact of Southern civilian life.** Across the war, **prices in the South rose by more than 9,000%** — a commodity price index went from 100 at the war's start to **more than 9,200 by April 1865**, averaging roughly **~10% per month** (and worse in the final stretch). One Confederate dollar, worth ~90¢ in gold at the start, was worth about **1.7¢ in gold by war's end** (≈3¢ in September 1864).
  — Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Inflation in the Confederacy"; Wikipedia, "Confederate States dollar"; EH.net, "Money and Finance in the Confederate States of America"

- **Cause — printing money.** The Confederacy financed the war chiefly by issuing currency: between 1862 and 1865, **more than 60% of total revenue came from printing money.** The Southern money supply increased roughly **twentyfold**; by comparison the Union only about doubled its money supply.
  — Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Inflation in the Confederacy"; EH.net, "Money and Finance in the Confederate States of America"

- **Annual inflation by year (illustrative):** roughly **60% (1861) → 300% (1863) → 600% (1864)** — accelerating toward collapse. **[VERIFY exact annual rates against EH.net / Lerner's price-index work — these are widely cited but the index, not a clean single %, is the authoritative form; see CONTESTED.]**
  — Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Inflation in the Confederacy" (citing Eugene Lerner's price-index studies)

- **The blockade's bite.** The Union naval blockade of Southern ports choked imports — coffee, salt, medicines, manufactured goods, shoes — turning everyday items into luxuries and feeding the price spiral. (Detailed blockade strategy belongs to a naval/Anaconda-Plan section; here it is a *cause of civilian scarcity*.)
  — Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Inflation in the Confederacy"; general blockade scholarship (cross-referenced)

- **Impressment (Impressment Act, March 26, 1863).** The Confederate government legalized **impressment** — military agents (with state impressment boards) could seize food, fuel, livestock, wagons, and enslaved labor, paying in depreciating Confederate money at fixed government prices below market. Farmers hated being paid worthless paper for real goods seized at gunpoint.
  — Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, "Confederate Impressment During the Civil War"; Florida Historical Society, "Floridians reacted angrily to Confederate Impressment Act"

- **The tax-in-kind (Tax Act of April 24, 1863).** Because depreciating paper was useless to the government, the Confederacy imposed a **10% "tax in kind"** — farmers had to hand over **one-tenth of their produce** (corn, wheat, sweet potatoes, etc.) directly to government collectors. It fell hardest on small rural farmers and is estimated to have generated about **$62 million in goods for the Confederacy between 1863 and 1865**, while deeply alienating the yeoman population.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Tax in kind"; Encyclopedia Virginia, "Confederate Impressment During the Civil War"; Encyclopedia.com, "Inflation in the Confederacy"

- **1864 currency reform (February 1864).** The Confederate Congress tried to shrink the money supply: bills over $5 were to be converted into 4%-interest bonds, and bills not converted by April 1 were exchanged at a ratio of 2-for-3. By May 1864 the money stock was cut about a third and the price index briefly dipped — but it was far too little, too late.
  — Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Inflation in the Confederacy"

## BEAT 6: The Bread Riots — The Richmond Bread Riot (April 2, 1863)

- **The flashpoint of Southern civilian hunger.** On **April 2, 1863**, in the Confederate capital of **Richmond, Virginia**, a crowd — overwhelmingly poor **women** — broke into shops and warehouses and seized food, and then clothing, shoes, and even jewelry, before authorities restored order. It was the largest of a wave of Southern bread riots.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Southern bread riots"; History.com, "What Happened at the Richmond Bread Riot?"; Encyclopedia Virginia, "Bread Riot (Richmond)"

- **The build-up:** The women had first sought a meeting with Virginia Governor **John Letcher** on **April 1, 1863** to plead for relief from food and fuel prices; denied, they took to the streets the next day. The action was organized in the **Oregon Hill** neighborhood, with recruiting begun in late March.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Southern bread riots"; Encyclopedia Virginia, "Bread Riot (Richmond)"

- **Organizers:** **Mary Jackson**, a market huckster/peddler and mother of a soldier, and **Minerva Meredith** (a butcher's apprentice). Jackson began recruiting women on **March 22, 1863**, openly, telling market-women there would be "a meeting of the women in relation to the high prices," and reaching into a government clothing factory and surrounding rural counties (Henrico, Hanover, New Kent).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Mary Jackson (Richmond bread riot)"; Encyclopedia Virginia, "Bread Riot (Richmond)"

- **Scale:** The street crowd swelled — contemporary estimates of those who gathered run to the thousands, with a core of perhaps **400–500 active looters** plundering warehouses and stores of bacon, flour, and other goods worth tens of thousands of dollars. **[The "~5,000 gathered" vs. "400–500 looted" distinction matters — see CONTESTED.]**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Southern bread riots"; Britannica, "Richmond Bread Riot"

- **Jefferson Davis confronts the crowd (the iconic scene):** Confederate President **Jefferson Davis** climbed atop a wagon (or cart) and addressed the rioters. By the well-known account, he emptied his pockets and **threw the money to the crowd**, then pulled out his watch and gave them **five minutes to disperse** before the assembled troops/public guard would open fire. The crowd dispersed.
  — Source: History.com, "What Happened at the Richmond Bread Riot?"; Encyclopedia Virginia, "Bread Riot (Richmond)" — see KEY QUOTES for verbatim and a [VERIFY] flag

- **Arrests:** **More than 60 rioters**, including Mary Jackson, were arrested for theft and rioting. Jackson was reportedly found waving a Bowie knife and shouting "Bread or blood!" **[the "Bread or blood" cry is widely reported; flag for verification — see KEY QUOTES]**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Mary Jackson (Richmond bread riot)"

- **Not alone:** Richmond was the biggest, but **bread riots broke out across the Confederacy in spring 1863** — Atlanta, Salisbury (North Carolina), Mobile, Macon, Columbus, Petersburg, and other towns saw women-led food riots. The Confederate government tried to suppress newspaper coverage of Richmond to avoid encouraging copycats.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Southern bread riots"

## BEAT 7: Women and the War

> Cross-reference notes: (a) the *explosion of women as nurses* and the medical detail belong to "Medicine & Disease"; (b) *enslaved women's labor and self-emancipation* belong to the USCT / Freedom section. Both are mentioned here only as part of the home-front picture.

- **Women ran the farms and plantations alone.** With men at the front, women across North and South took over farm and plantation management — plowing, planting, harvesting, selling crops, managing livestock and (in the South) enslaved laborers and overseers. In the South this often meant managing plantations as enslaved labor grew unstable and, near Union lines, fled.
  — Source: NPS, "Women Amidst War"; New Georgia Encyclopedia, "Women during the Civil War"; EBSCO, "Women in the Civil War"

- **Women entered government and wage work.** In both capitals, women took **government clerkships** for the first time at scale — clerks in the **U.S. Treasury Department** and Post Office in the North; clerks in Confederate government offices (including the Treasury, signing/cutting currency) in Richmond. Women also worked as teachers and store clerks as men left those jobs.
  — Source: Tulane, "From Domesticity to Industry: Working Women in the Civil War"; EBSCO, "Women in the Civil War"

- **Women in the war industries — and dying in them.** Arsenals and munitions factories hired large numbers of women and girls to assemble cartridges and ordnance (small hands prized for the fiddly, dangerous work). Richmond, the Confederacy's industrial hub, employed hundreds of women in ammunition work.
  — Source: Tulane, "From Domesticity to Industry"; NPS, "Women Amidst War"

- **Two deadly arsenal explosions illustrate the danger:**
  - **Allegheny Arsenal, Lawrenceville (Pittsburgh), PA — September 17, 1862:** a series of explosions killed **78 workers**, most of them young women/girls. It was the **largest industrial disaster of the Civil War** (and coincided with the Battle of Antietam the same day).
    — Source: National Museum of Civil War Medicine, "Disaster at the Allegheny Arsenal"; Wikipedia, "Allegheny Arsenal"
  - **Washington Arsenal, Washington, D.C. — June 17, 1864:** an explosion killed **21 women**, mostly young Irish immigrant workers making cartridges. President **Lincoln and Secretary Stanton attended the funeral**, and the cortege processed down Pennsylvania Avenue to Congressional Cemetery.
    — Source: U.S. Army, "The Washington Arsenal Explosion"; Wikipedia, "1864 Washington Arsenal explosion"

- **The U.S. Sanitary Commission (founded June 1861) — civilian relief at national scale.** Authorized by the War Department and created by an order President Lincoln signed in **June 1861** (officially established **June 18, 1861**), the Commission coordinated soldier relief — sanitation, supplies, food, nursing support. **Frederick Law Olmsted** (then on leave from designing Central Park) served as its first executive secretary. It ran on a vast volunteer base of women in **thousands of local soldiers'-aid / branch societies**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "United States Sanitary Commission"; NPS, "Olmsted and the United States Sanitary Commission"; American Battlefield Trust, "The United States Sanitary Commission"

- **Sanitary Fairs — women-organized civilian fundraising.** Beginning with the **Northwestern Soldiers' Fair in Chicago in 1863** (a two-week event that raised about **$80,000**), women across the North organized massive **Sanitary Fairs** — bazaars selling donated goods, art, and curiosities to fund the Commission. Over the war the Commission raised in the range of **$25 million** in cash and supplies (some accounts: ~$5 million cash + ~$15 million in goods through the branch network and fairs), making it the most successful relief organization of the war.
  — Source: Women & the American Story (NY Historical), "Sanitary Fairs"; Pritzker Military Museum, "The Great Northwest Sanitary Fair of 1863"; American Battlefield Trust, "The United States Sanitary Commission"; Wikipedia, "United States Sanitary Commission" **[the $25M vs. $5M-cash-plus-$15M-goods framings should be reconciled — see CONTESTED]**

- **Cross-ref (nurses):** The war drove an explosion of women into nursing (Dorothea Dix as Union Superintendent of Army Nurses, Clara Barton, etc.). **Detail belongs to "Medicine & Disease" — do not develop it here beyond noting the home-front shift of women into paid/organized wartime work.**

- **Cross-ref (enslaved women):** Enslaved women's labor sustained the Southern war economy and home front, and many self-emancipated by fleeing to Union lines. **Detail belongs to the USCT / Freedom section.**

## BEAT 8: The Northern War Economy — Boom While the South Disintegrated

> The throughline for the author: a Congress with the Southern delegations gone passed a sweeping nationalist economic program, and the Northern economy *boomed* (war contracts, new money, new banks, westward expansion) at the exact moment the Southern economy was eating itself with inflation and scarcity. The contrast IS the section's payoff.

- **The first federal income tax — Revenue Act of 1861 (August 5, 1861).** To fund the war, Congress passed the first federal income tax: a **flat 3% on incomes above $800**. (It was reworked by the Revenue Act of 1862 into a graduated tax and created the office that became the IRS.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Revenue Act of 1861"; Historic Geneva, "Currency, Finance and the Civil War"

- **The Legal Tender Act (February 25, 1862) — "greenbacks."** Authorized the federal government to issue paper money — **"greenbacks"** — not backed by gold or silver but declared legal tender. The first act authorized **$150 million** in U.S. Notes (in denominations as small as $5); subsequent acts raised the authorized total to **$450 million**. These were the **first U.S. currency issued as legal tender**, a foundational shift toward a national paper currency.
  — Source: History.com, "Legal Tender Act passed"; Wikipedia, "United States Note"; U.S. Capitol Visitor Center, "HR 240, Legal Tender Act"

- **War bonds — Salmon P. Chase / Jay Cooke.** Treasury Secretary **Salmon P. Chase** launched the first mass **war-bond** program in U.S. history; financier **Jay Cooke** marketed bonds not just to bankers but to ordinary citizens — a popular-finance innovation that funded much of the Union war effort.
  — Source: Historic Geneva, "Currency, Finance and the Civil War"

- **The National Banking Acts (1863 and 1864).** Created a system of federally chartered **national banks** that had to buy U.S. government bonds to back the **uniform national banknotes** they issued — financing the government, creating a stable national currency, and undercutting the patchwork of unstable state-bank notes. A landmark in building a national financial system.
  — Source: Federal Reserve History, "National Banking Acts of 1863 and 1864"; Wikipedia, "National Bank Act"

- **The Congress-with-the-South-gone program of 1862.** With Southern members departed, the Republican Congress passed the nationalist economic agenda the South had long blocked:
  - **Homestead Act — signed May 20, 1862:** granted **160 acres** of surveyed public land to an adult head of household for a small filing fee plus **five years' continuous residence/improvement**, accelerating western settlement.
    — Source: National Archives, "Homestead Act (1862)"; Britannica, "Homestead Act of 1862"
  - **Pacific Railway Act — signed July 1, 1862:** authorized the **transcontinental railroad** with federal land grants and bond subsidies to the Union Pacific and Central Pacific (joined at Promontory, Utah, May 10, 1869).
    — Source: National Archives, "Pacific Railway Act (1862)"; U.S. Senate, "The Pacific Railway Act of 1862"
  - (Also in this cluster, for context: the **Morrill Land-Grant College Act, July 2, 1862** — land grants to fund public colleges. Mention only if useful as a third example of the "South-gone Congress builds the nation" theme.)
    — Source: National Archives milestone documents

- **War contracts, profiteering, and "shoddy."** The Union's enormous demand for uniforms, weapons, food, and supplies generated fortunes — and graft. Early-war contractors sold the Army cheap, falling-apart uniforms made of reprocessed wool dust pressed into cloth, giving the era its word **"shoddy"** for cheap goods and, by extension, the war-profiteering "shoddy aristocracy." **[VERIFY the etymological/"shoddy aristocracy" detail against a solid source (e.g., a period press or a standard economic-history account) before asserting it as fact.]**
  — Source: general Civil War economic-history accounts (to be pinned) **[VERIFY]**

- **The contrast — the section's thesis, well supported:** While Confederate prices rose ~9,000% and its money supply ballooned ~20×, the Union roughly **doubled** its money supply, built a national currency and banking system, funded itself with bonds sold to ordinary people, and used a wartime Congress to pass homesteading and a transcontinental railroad. **The North's economy grew and modernized through the war; the South's came apart.**
  — Source: EH.net, "Money and Finance in the Confederate States of America"; Encyclopedia.com, "Inflation in the Confederacy"; Federal Reserve History, "National Banking Acts"

## CONTESTED / ESTIMATES

### 1. NYC Draft Riots — Death Toll (HIGH PRIORITY — USE A RANGE)
- **Official count:** ~**119–120** deaths.
- **Higher estimates:** A contemporary estimate put killed-and-wounded at ~1,000 (mostly rioters). Herbert Asbury, in *The Gangs of New York* (1928), claimed **2,000 killed and 8,000 wounded** — figures most modern historians reject as wildly inflated. Some careful tallies of *documented* deaths (e.g., Black men killed by mobs) put the number of *known, named* victims much lower (~18 known killed, 11 of them Black).
- **Author guidance:** Use **"about 120 confirmed dead, with some estimates running higher"** — flag the official ~119–120 as the anchor, note Asbury's 2,000 as a long-discredited high. Do **not** state 2,000 as fact. Eleven Black men/boys lynched is well documented and can be stated plainly.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots"; History.com

### 2. Confederate Inflation — Figure Form (USE THE INDEX, NOT A CLEAN %)
- The authoritative form is a **price index rising from 100 to ~9,200** (≈9,000%+ increase) over the war, drawn from Eugene Lerner's studies. The "annual rates" (60% / 300% / 600%) and "averaged ~10%/month" are *summaries* of that index, not independently surveyed rates.
- **Author guidance:** Safest phrasings — "Southern prices rose more than 9,000% over the war"; "by 1864 a Confederate dollar was worth a few cents in gold"; "prices roughly doubled every few months by 1864." Treat any single annual % as illustrative, not precise.
  — Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Inflation in the Confederacy"; EH.net, "Money and Finance in the Confederate States of America"

### 3. Union Draft Sub-Totals (1863) — VERIFY BEFORE QUOTING EXACT NUMBERS
- The breakdown (292,441 drawn → ~190,000 excused, ~52,000 commuted, ~26,000 substitutes, ~9,881 held) is widely repeated but should be checked against the **Provost Marshal General's official report** before any single figure is quoted as exact. The *shape* is solid (the overwhelming majority were excused, paid, or substituted; only ~2–3% actually conscripted); the precise sub-totals are the soft part.
- **Author guidance:** Lead with the **2% of Union soldiers were draftees** / "only a few percent were actually held" framing, which is robust. Use the sub-totals as "roughly."

### 4. Richmond Bread Riot — Crowd Size
- Estimates of the gathering run up to ~5,000; the active looting core is put at ~400–500. Numbers are contemporary estimates, not counts.
- **Author guidance:** "a crowd of hundreds — some accounts say thousands — mostly women" is safe.

### 5. Sanitary Commission Money Raised
- Figures vary: "~$25 million raised" vs. "~$5 million in cash + ~$15 million in goods." These are different scopes (cash vs. cash+goods) and partly different sources.
- **Author guidance:** "raised millions of dollars in cash and supplies — on the order of $25 million in value over the war" is defensible; don't present a single precise dollar figure as gospel.

### 6. Substitutes Hired (Union, whole war)
- Range from ~74,000 to ~116,000 depending on source/definition. Use "tens of thousands" or "well over 100,000 substitutions across the war" only with the caveat. **[VERIFY]**

### 7. "Shoddy" / War-Profiteering Etymology
- The story that "shoddy" (reprocessed-wool cloth sold to the Army) gave the era its word for cheap goods and the "shoddy aristocracy" of profiteers is commonly told but was **not pinned to a hard source in this pass.** **[VERIFY before asserting as fact.]**
