# Fort Pillow & the Guerrilla War — Verified Image Manifest

Section source: `audits/war-pipeline/guerrilla-war-final.md`.
Proposal: **1 hero + 8 inline figures**, all verified **public domain**, distributed across
eight different section headings.

**Verification method.** Every image was resolved through the Wikimedia Commons MediaWiki
API (`commons.wikimedia.org/w/api.php?action=query&prop=imageinfo&iiprop=url|size|mime|extmetadata`),
which returned each file's canonical `upload.wikimedia.org` original URL, exact pixel
dimensions, MIME type, and the `LicenseShortName` field. **Every chosen URL was then
HTTP-fetched and returned `200 image/jpeg`** (no Wikimedia 429 blocked any fetch this pass).
**No CC-BY / CC-BY-SA image is used** — every entry's Commons license reads "Public domain,"
and every underlying work is a period photograph, engraving, or painting/print made
1862–1872 and published well before 1929.

**PD basis legend.** The two Harper's Weekly wood engravings (Fort Pillow massacre, Lawrence
ruins) were published in 1863–1864 → PD. The wartime portrait photographs of Forrest,
Quantrill, Anderson, Mosby, Ferguson, and Jesse James were all made c. 1860–1865 and
published pre-1929 → PD. Bingham's *Martial Law / Order No. 11* is the artist's own 1872
print of his protest painting → PD. All carry the Commons "Public domain" tag.

**Tone note.** These are atrocities, not feats of arms. Captions name the dead plainly,
attribute massacre where the record attributes it, and do not lionize any of the men
pictured. No post-mortem corpse image is used: "Bloody Bill" Anderson is shown in a living
1860s portrait, not the notorious death photograph; Champ Ferguson is shown seated in
Union custody, not on the gallows.

## HERO — The Fort Pillow massacre

- **filename:** `guerrilla-war-fort-pillow-massacre.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/cf/The_Massacre_at_Fort_Pillow_-_unsigned_-_Harper%27s_Weekly_-_issue_of_April_30%2C_1864_-_page_284.jpg`
- **orientation:** landscape (3336 × 2275)
- **caption:** "The Massacre at Fort Pillow," as the North saw it eighteen days after the killing — Confederate soldiers shooting and bayoneting Union men, many of them Black, on the bluff above the Mississippi as they tried to surrender. Of roughly 600 defenders, close to 300 were killed; about 35 percent of the Black soldiers survived the day against about 70 percent of the white. The asymmetry is the massacre.
- **credit:** Unsigned · wood engraving · 1864 · *Harper's Weekly* (Apr 30, 1864), via Wikimedia Commons · public domain
- **placement:** `hero`
- **subject-verification:** Commons "File:The Massacre at Fort Pillow - unsigned - Harper's Weekly - issue of April 30, 1864 - page 284.jpg"; the canonical period image of the Fort Pillow killings, published in *Harper's Weekly* two and a half weeks after the April 12, 1864 massacre this section is built around. PD: 1864 engraving, Commons license "Public domain."

## FIGURE 1 — Nathan Bedford Forrest

- **filename:** `guerrilla-war-forrest.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/43/Nathan_Bedford_Forrest.jpg`
- **orientation:** portrait (417 × 699)
- **caption:** Major General Nathan Bedford Forrest — a Memphis slave trader before the war, a commissioned Confederate cavalry general during it. The man who commanded at Fort Pillow was no outlaw in the brush; he was regular army, which is exactly what makes the killing there so hard to explain away. A witness from his own command said Forrest gave the order to shoot the surrendering men down; Forrest denied it; the question has never closed. What is not in question is what he became after the war.
- **credit:** Unidentified photographer · photograph · before 1877 · Wikimedia Commons · public domain
- **placement:** `after **Fort Pillow: surrender by the color of your skin**`
- **subject-verification:** Commons "File:Nathan Bedford Forrest.jpg," extmetadata artist "Unidentified photographer," date "before 1877"; standard wartime/period portrait of the Confederate general named throughout this section. PD: pre-1877 photo of a man dead in 1877, Commons "Public domain."

## FIGURE 2 — William Clarke Quantrill

- **filename:** `guerrilla-war-quantrill.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c7/William_Clarke_Quantrill_in_military_uniform.jpg`
- **orientation:** portrait (1000 × 1683)
- **caption:** William Clarke Quantrill — an Ohio-born schoolteacher before the border remade him into the most infamous bushwhacker of the Missouri–Kansas war. By the war's start he had traded the schoolhouse for horse theft and then for a band of un-uniformed pro-Confederate guerrillas accountable to no one but himself. Cole Younger, "Bloody Bill" Anderson, and the teenage James brothers all rode under him.
- **credit:** Kansas Historical Society · photograph · between 1861 and 1865 · Wikimedia Commons · public domain
- **placement:** `after **Bleeding Kansas never stopped bleeding**`
- **subject-verification:** Commons "File:William Clarke Quantrill in military uniform.jpg," credited to the Kansas Historical Society, dated 1861–1865; the standard wartime portrait of Quantrill, the section's named raider. PD: wartime photo, Commons "Public domain."

## FIGURE 3 — The ruins of Lawrence, Kansas

- **filename:** `guerrilla-war-lawrence-ruins.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b5/The_ruins_of_Lawrence%2C_Kansas_-_sketched_by_a_correspondent._LCCN2003663009.jpg`
- **orientation:** landscape (5118 × 4130)
- **caption:** The ruins of Lawrence, Kansas, sketched by a correspondent in the weeks after Quantrill's raiders rode in at dawn on August 21, 1863 and murdered somewhere between 150 and 190 men and boys — nearly all of them unarmed civilians, many shot on their own doorsteps in front of their families. About a quarter of the town was burned. The raiders lost exactly one man.

## FIGURE 4 — "Bloody Bill" Anderson

- **filename:** `guerrilla-war-bloody-bill-anderson.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/86/Bloody_Bill_1860s.jpg`
- **orientation:** portrait (420 × 500)
- **caption:** William T. "Bloody Bill" Anderson, photographed in the early 1860s — alive, and worth saying so, since the image usually attached to his name is the one taken after a Union ambush killed him in October 1864. He scalped and mutilated the men he killed and reportedly kept a knotted cord that tallied them; at his death it is said to have held some fifty-four knots. At Centralia, his band stripped and shot 22 unarmed Union soldiers pulled off a train, then rode down and killed about 123 more men the same afternoon.
- **credit:** Unknown photographer · photograph · early 1860s · Wikimedia Commons · public domain
- **placement:** `after **"Bloody Bill" Anderson and Centralia**`
- **subject-verification:** Commons "File:Bloody Bill 1860s.jpg," described "Bloody Bill in the early 1860s," dated early 1860s; a living portrait of Anderson, the section's named guerrilla — deliberately chosen over the well-known post-mortem photograph. PD: early-1860s photo, Commons "Public domain."

## FIGURE 5 — Bingham's *Martial Law (Order No. 11)*

- **filename:** `guerrilla-war-order-no-11.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/71/%22Martial_Law_As_Exemplified_in_the_Desolation_of_Border_Counties_Of_Missouri%2C_During_The_Enforcement_Of_Military_Orders%2C_Issued_By_Brigadier_General_Ewing%2C_Of_the_Federal_Army%2C_From_His_Head_Quarters%2C_Kansas_City%2C_Augt._25th_1863.%22.jpg`
- **orientation:** landscape (9276 × 6504)
- **caption:** George Caleb Bingham's *Martial Law*, better known as *Order No. 11* — a Missouri Unionist's furious protest against his own side, showing families turned out of their homes at gunpoint under the Union order that emptied four counties. General Order No. 11 expelled roughly 25,000 people in fifteen days and left a burned-over "Burnt District" of standing chimneys. A massacre and a military order are not the same thing, but they are the two ends of the same collapse.
- **credit:** George Caleb Bingham · engraving/print after his painting · 1872 · Wikimedia Commons · public domain
- **placement:** `after **The army empties four counties**`
- **subject-verification:** Commons file titled "Martial Law As Exemplified in the Desolation of Border Counties Of Missouri … Issued By Brigadier General Ewing … Augt. 25th 1863," artist "George Caleb Bingham," date 1872; Bingham's own print of his *Order No. 11* protest image, the work named in the section's closing paragraph on the expulsion. PD: 1872 work, Commons "Public domain."

## FIGURE 6 — John Singleton Mosby

- **filename:** `guerrilla-war-mosby.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a1/John_S._Mosby_-_Brady-Handy.jpg`
- **orientation:** portrait (2882 × 3377)
- **caption:** John Singleton Mosby, "the Gray Ghost," who fought the same hit-and-run war as the Missouri bushwhackers and still kept it inside the rules — his men wore Confederate uniforms, answered to a chain of command, and took prisoners alive. In November 1864, after a cycle of tit-for-tat executions began in the Shenandoah Valley, Mosby wrote to the enemy general and proposed both sides go back to treating captives as prisoners of war. It was the rare moment in this whole story when someone deliberately stopped the killing.
- **credit:** Mathew B. Brady · photograph · between 1860 and 1865 · Wikimedia Commons (Brady-Handy Collection) · public domain
- **placement:** `after **The Gray Ghost: how it could have been fought**`
- **subject-verification:** Commons "File:John S. Mosby - Brady-Handy.jpg," artist Mathew Brady, dated 1860–1865; a wartime Brady portrait of Mosby, the section's named partisan ranger and moral counterweight. PD: wartime photo, Commons "Public domain."

## FIGURE 7 — Champ Ferguson in Union custody

- **filename:** `guerrilla-war-champ-ferguson.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/38/Champ_Ferguson_sitting.jpg`
- **orientation:** portrait (381 × 600)
- **caption:** Samuel "Champ" Ferguson, seated in Union custody in 1865 before his trial. A backwoods guerrilla who claimed to have killed over a hundred men in the settling of neighborhood scores, he took part in murdering wounded Union soldiers — including Black troopers — in their hospital beds after Saltville. He was hanged on October 20, 1865, one of the very few Confederates ever executed for war crimes. Nathan Bedford Forrest, who commanded an army that killed surrendering men by the hundreds, was never charged, and died in his bed.
- **credit:** Federal government photographer · photograph · 1865 · Wikimedia Commons · public domain
- **placement:** `after **The thin reckoning**`
- **subject-verification:** Commons "File:Champ Ferguson sitting.jpg," described as Ferguson after his capture by Union forces in 1865; the standard in-custody portrait of the guerrilla the section names — deliberately the seated image, not a gallows/post-mortem one. PD: 1865 federal photo, Commons "Public domain."

## FIGURE 8 — Jesse James, teenage bushwhacker

- **filename:** `guerrilla-war-jesse-james.jpg`
- **direct URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2a/Jesse_James_%28approx._16_yrs._of_age%29._Missouri_bushwhacker_riding_with_Bloody_Bill_Anderson.jpg`
- **orientation:** portrait (304 × 400)
- **caption:** Jesse James, about sixteen, photographed in 1864 as a Missouri bushwhacker riding with "Bloody Bill" Anderson — years before the outlaw legend. Frank and Jesse James carried the bushwhacker's habits — the raid, the ambush, the grievance against Union authority — straight out of the war and into a seventeen-year outlaw career, a legend half folk hero and half Lost Cause myth. The border's hatreds simply refused to demobilize.
- **credit:** Taylor Copying Co., St. Louis · photograph · 1864 · Wikimedia Commons · public domain
- **placement:** `after **A pattern, not a single day**`
- **subject-verification:** Commons "File:Jesse James (approx. 16 yrs. of age). Missouri bushwhacker riding with Bloody Bill Anderson.jpg," dated 1864, publisher Taylor Copying Co.; a wartime portrait of the young Jesse James as a bushwhacker, matching the section's closing beat on the James brothers' outlaw afterlife. PD: 1864 photo, Commons "Public domain."

## SUMMARY

**9 images proposed (1 hero + 8 inline figures), all verified public domain, all returning
`200 image/jpeg`.** Distributed across eight headings: Fort Pillow (hero + Forrest),
Bleeding Kansas (Quantrill), the Lawrence Massacre (ruins), Anderson/Centralia (Anderson),
the four-county expulsion (Bingham), the Gray Ghost (Mosby), the thin reckoning (Ferguson),
and the closing pattern section (Jesse James).

**Not illustrated** (no contemporaneous PD subject, or owned by another section): the Lieber
Code / Partisan Ranger Act (abstract legal documents); the casualty-asymmetry argument
(carried by the hero engraving); Poison Spring / Saltville / the Crater (touched only
briefly here and largely owned by the USCT and Prisons sections). The two small portraits
(Anderson 420×500, Jesse James 304×400) are the best **living, non-gory** images available
for those subjects; both are clean inline insets, not hero candidates.
