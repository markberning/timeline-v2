## Section-by-section

### the-thicket (May 4) — GOOD
- **GOOD — the hook.** "The Wilderness was not a place you would pick for a battle.
  It was a place you would pick to *get lost* in." This is the right opening: a
  paradox, in house voice, that makes the reader want the answer. The follow-through
  ("So why fight there? Because one man wanted exactly that") keeps the momentum.
- **GOOD — the central mechanism is made vivid, not asserted.** "cannon could not be
  aimed — there was nothing to aim at but leaves … The terrain erased the difference
  between a big army and a small one." That's the whole battle's logic in two
  sentences. Excellent.
- **GOOD — the MEANWHILE on Grant's new kind of war** is doing real work: it reframes
  the entire battle ("the opening move of the end") before a shot is fired, so the
  reader knows what's at stake beyond this one field.
- **GOOD — the Chancellorsville ghost** is planted with restraint at the end of the
  section ("The woods seemed to insist on it") and pays off twice later. Good setup.
- **SHOULD-IMPROVE — the date stamp says May 4 but the section is all
  scene/strategy, no May 4 action.** A first-timer scanning the date headers will
  expect something to *happen* on May 4 and find only table-setting. Either soften
  the reader's expectation (the section is explicitly the "why," which is fine) or
  add the one concrete May-4 beat if the pack supports it (Grant's army crossing the
  Rapidan happened May 4). Right now the crossing is mentioned in past tense ("had
  brought a very large army across the Rapidan") rather than dramatized as the day's
  action. Minor.

### the-two-roads (May 5) — GOOD
- **GOOD — "To understand this battle you only need two roads and the crossing
  between them."** This is exactly the orienting move a confusing battle needs. It
  tells the reader the geography is simple before it gets complicated, which lowers
  the anxiety and lets them follow.
- **GOOD — the crossroads stakes are stated cleanly:** "Hold that crossroads and
  your army stayed in one connected piece; lose it and Lee could split the Union
  force in two." Concrete consequence, not abstract importance.
- **GOOD — the day's flatness is acknowledged, not hidden.** "a confused, bloody
  draw" and "nobody able to land a decisive blow" — the draft is honest that May 5
  was inconclusive, and then turns that into forward momentum: "That mess would
  matter terribly at dawn." Ending a low-energy day on a hook into the next is the
  right call.
- **SHOULD-IMPROVE — the north road (Saunders Field / Ewell vs. Warren) reads as
  the lesser, almost dutiful half of the section.** It's accurate but a little
  list-like ("gaps opened between regiments, units lost all sense of direction, men
  fired on their own comrades"). The Plank Road half has stakes (the race for the
  crossroads, Getty *racing*, Hancock piling in, Hays killed mid-sentence to his
  regiment); the Turnpike half has none of that texture. Consider giving the
  Turnpike one human or sensory beat so it isn't just "and meanwhile, to the north,
  similar confusion." The Hays death is the strongest human beat in the section —
  good that it's there.

### longstreet-attacks (May 6) — GOOD, but the longest section and the most at risk
- **GOOD — the cold open is the best single line of pacing in the draft:** "The
  second day opened with the Union nearly winning the whole thing before breakfast."
  That's a hook AND a stakes-spike AND a time stamp in one sentence. It earns the
  reader's full attention for the long section that follows.
- **GOOD — the twelve guns at Tapp Field.** "What held it together first was a single
  open field and twelve guns." Naming the thing that saved Lee in concrete, almost
  fairy-tale terms ("a single open field and twelve guns") is great storytelling —
  it gives the reader a fixed image to hold while the chaos swirls.
- **GOOD — the "Lee to the rear" beat is handled with real craft.** The soldiers
  grabbing Traveller's reins, refusing to let their commander die, is the human core
  of the Confederate side and it's told with the right warmth and brevity. The draft
  also threads the [VERIFY] discipline invisibly ("the soldiers halting his horse is
  the verified part") without breaking the spell. Well done.
- **GOOD — the friendly-fire/Jackson echo** is the emotional peak of the whole
  article and it's earned by the Chancellorsville setup in section 1. "both corps
  commanders, both cut down by friendly fire on the brink of victory, in this same
  tangle of trees." The repetition ("same woods … same fate … same volley") is
  rhythmically right, not over-egged.
- **MUST-IMPROVE — the section is doing too much in too few paragraphs and the
  middle sags under proper nouns.** Between Longstreet's arrival and his wounding the
  reader is handed, in fast succession: Poague, Gregg, the Texas Brigade, Field,
  Kershaw, Wadsworth, Martin L. Smith, the unfinished railroad, Sorrel, Mahone,
  Gibbon's miscarried order, the 12th Virginia, Micah Jenkins. That's a LOT of names
  for the payoff each carries. The flank-march "masterstroke" — which the draft
  rightly flags as the masterstroke — gets less narrative room and rising tension
  than it deserves because it's wedged between the Texas Brigade's destruction and
  Longstreet's wounding. **Fix:** give the railroad-bed flank attack its own breath.
  It's the single cleverest tactical stroke of the battle (a hidden, unfinished
  railroad nobody guarded, leading straight around the enemy's open end). Right now
  it's narrated almost as logistics. Slow down on the *discovery* ("open, unguarded,
  and it led directly around the flank") and the *surprise* before collapsing
  Hancock's line — let the reader feel the trap close. This is a pacing fix, not a
  new-fact ask.
- **SHOULD-IMPROVE — the late-afternoon Brock Road assault + Gordon's evening attack
  are a second pile-up.** After the Longstreet climax, the section keeps going
  through two more attacks (the breastworks fire/Carroll counterattack, then
  Gordon's flank attack capturing Shaler and Seymour and nearly bagging Sedgwick).
  These are real and worth keeping, but coming after the emotional peak they read as
  a long denouement that re-floods the reader with names just as their attention is
  spent. Consider compressing the Brock Road repulse to its essence (flags on the
  works, the fire, the works held) and letting Gordon's dusk attack — which is
  genuinely dramatic, a flank crumpling in the dark, generals captured — be the
  section's closing image instead of a fourth equal beat. The "darkness and the
  impossible woods let the Union line re-form" is a good closer; lean on it.
- **GOOD — the fire MEANWHILE is the right place and the right intensity.** Holding
  the fire for the end of the heaviest day, after all the maneuver, makes it the
  thing the reader carries out of the battle — which is historically and emotionally
  correct. "Nobody planned it, neither side could stop it, and it is the image the
  Wilderness left burned into everyone who survived it." Strong close. (Note: the
  single best fire image — Horace Porter's eyewitness line — is OMITTED for [VERIFY].
  If the fact-checker can confirm Porter's wording verbatim, **route to fact pack:
  this sidebar would go from very good to unforgettable with one verified eyewitness
  sentence.** Do not invent it; flag it.)

### the-turn (May 7) — GOOD (the strongest-paced section)
- **GOOD — "Grant did not retreat." as its own one-line paragraph** is the best
  structural beat in the draft. The setup ("By every rule of the previous three
  years, this was a defeat, and the next move was a retreat") makes the four-word
  paragraph land like a hammer. This is exactly how to tell a turning point.
- **GOOD — the night-march cheer is the emotional payoff of the whole article** and
  it's told beautifully: "at a certain fork in the road the men found the column
  turning *south* — toward the enemy — instead of north toward the river and home …
  the army broke into cheering, and on the dark road they sang." The "they sang"
  detail is the kind of small human note that makes a battle stick. Keep every word.
- **GOOD — Lee reading Grant exactly** ("he guessed Grant would make for Spotsylvania
  … and is another story") closes the section with momentum into the next battle
  without over-explaining. Good restraint.

### what-it-was-for (The meaning) — GOOD, with one structural risk
- **GOOD — names the cause plainly and in its own prose.** "The Confederacy fought
  to preserve and extend slavery. That is the plain fact under everything else." This
  clears the ship-blocking NAME-THE-CAUSE gate cleanly and does it in house voice,
  not as a disclaimer. The slavery cause is woven into Grant's strategy
  ("It was fighting to break the slaveholders' army for good"), not bolted on.
- **GOOD — the 23rd USCT local hook is the strongest emotional close available** and
  the draft finds it: "Men who had been somebody's legal property in *these
  neighborhoods* … were now carrying Union rifles back across the land of their own
  bondage." "The thicket was the object. Slavery was the reason." That final couplet
  is the right last line for the whole piece.
- **GOOD — the date/place discipline is handled without deflating the hook.** The
  draft is honest that the USCT division guarded the wagon trains during the
  Wilderness and that the directed combat came days later toward Spotsylvania — and
  it makes that honesty *part* of the story ("the point would land just days later")
  rather than a hedge. Skillful.
- **MUST-IMPROVE — the section opens with a recap paragraph that repeats what the
  reader just read.** The first two paragraphs ("So what did all that blood buy? On
  the map, the Wilderness opened the Overland Campaign … It proved Grant's strategy
  … It also gutted Lee's high command … And it was the morale pivot") re-list the
  significance points the previous four sections already dramatized (the campaign,
  the attrition math, Longstreet down, the cheer). It reads like a summary slide
  before the real ending. The genuinely NEW and best material — the cause, the USCT
  hook — doesn't start until "But none of that is the deepest answer." **Fix:**
  compress the recap hard (one or two sentences max) and get to "what Grant's
  relentlessness was *for*" faster. The reader's reward for finishing should be the
  new revelation, not a review of the quiz. The bones of a great closing section are
  all here; they're just behind a recap.

## The arc, end to end
The spine — **Lee picks the blind woods to erase Grant's numbers → three days of
confused slaughter → Grant refuses to retreat and the army cheers → and the reason it
all mattered was the men marching back across their own bondage** — is a real arc with
a real payoff, not a chronology. The two woven echoes (the fire; the Jackson/Longstreet
friendly-fire mirror) give the middle its emotional weight. The opening hook and the
closing couplet are both strong. The article knows what it is about.

   Cut the recap to a sentence or two; lead with the cause + USCT revelation sooner.

5. **(SHOULD) May-4 header has no May-4 action** — either dramatize the Rapidan
   crossing as the day's beat or accept the section as the explicit "why."
