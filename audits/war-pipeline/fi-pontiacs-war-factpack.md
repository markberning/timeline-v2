## 1. THE COLLAPSE OF LEVERAGE: FRANCE'S DEFEAT REMOVED THE SECOND EMPIRE

For generations the Native nations of the *pays d'en haut* (the French term for the Great Lakes "upper country") had survived by playing two European empires — France and Britain — against each other. Each empire needed Native alliances; neither could dominate the interior alone; that balance was Native nations' leverage.

The British conquest of New France destroyed it:
- **Quebec fell September 13/18, 1759** (Battle of the Plains of Abraham; capitulation 18 Sept).
- **Montreal capitulated September 8, 1760** — the effective end of French Canada.
- **The Treaty of Paris was signed February 10, 1763**, formally transferring Canada and all French claims east of the Mississippi to Britain.

**The consequence for Native nations:** with France gone, there was no second empire to balance Britain. The diplomatic strategy that had protected the interior nations for a century evaporated. They now faced a single, victorious, contemptuous power.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*; Treaty of Paris (1763); fi-conquest-factpack (Quebec/Montreal dates already verified in this pipeline).

## 2. AMHERST'S POLICY: THE ALLIANCE DELIBERATELY DISMANTLED

**General Jeffery Amherst**, British commander-in-chief in North America, treated the Native nations as conquered subjects rather than allies, and changed three things that mattered enormously:

**(a) He ended the customary gift-giving (beginning ~1761).** In Native diplomacy, the annual presents — guns, knives, ammunition, tobacco, clothing — were not bribes; the gift-giving WAS the alliance, a renewal of relationship and respect. The French had practiced it for generations. Amherst regarded it as buying good behavior and stopped it. "Many Indians saw this change as indicating the British looked upon them as conquered people rather than as allies."
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*; The Canadian Encyclopedia, *Pontiac's War*.

**(b) He restricted the sale of gunpowder and ammunition.** This was not a minor economy measure. Native men hunted with guns; powder and shot were the basis of the food supply and the fur economy. Cutting it off was, to the nations, a threat to survival — and read as "a prelude to further conquest." Amherst, in an August 9 letter to William Johnson, told him to stop "purchasing the good behavior of the Indians" and to limit their access to ammunition, fearing they would turn the powder on the British.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.

**(c) He treated the nations with open contempt.** British officers "made little effort to conceal their contempt." The nations complained they were "treated no better than slaves or dogs."
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.

> **VERBATIM (Amherst, on Native nations):** Amherst regarded them as "the vilest race of beings that ever infested the earth, and whose riddance from it must be esteemed a meritorious act." *(This phrasing appears in Amherst's correspondence; quote it as Amherst's own words, attributed to his letters — flag for the fact-checker to lock the exact wording if used.)* [VERIFY-WORDING]

## 3. NEOLIN, THE LENAPE (DELAWARE) PROPHET

**Neolin** ("the Enlightened One") was a **Lenape (Delaware)** holy man who, after a period of fasting and dreaming around **1761**, said he had journeyed in a vision to the **Master of Life** (Lenape: *Keesh-she'-la-mil'-lang-up*, the being "that thought us into being").

**His message:** Native peoples must reject European trade goods, alcohol, and the dependence that came with them, and return to traditional ways — and drive out the British. He blended Christian and traditional elements; he taught against rum, materialism, and (per the vision) polygamy.

**His galvanizing influence:** Neolin's preaching spread across the Ohio and Great Lakes nations and gave the coming rising its spiritual fuel. **Pontiac drew directly on Neolin's teachings** to rally warriors at his April 1763 council, retelling the prophet's vision.

> **VERBATIM (the Master of Life, as recited by Pontiac from Neolin's vision, April 1763):**
> "This land where ye dwell I have made for you and not for others. Whence comes it that ye permit the Whites upon your lands?"
> "Drive them out, make war on them. I love them not, they know me not, they are my enemies and the enemies of your brethren."
> "Before those whom you call your brothers came on your lands, did you not live by bow and arrow? You had no need of gun nor powder... and yet you caught animals to live upon."
> "I do not like that you drink until you lose your reason, as you do; or that you fight with each other; or that you take two wives, or run after the wives of others."
> **Source/recorder:** Recorded by a French chronicler at Detroit; printed in *Collections of the Pioneer Society of the State of Michigan* (1907), Vol. VIII, pp. 270–271. Reproduced at americanyawp.com, "Pontiac Calls for War, 1763." **Author note:** This is Neolin's vision *as relayed by Pontiac and written down by a colonial source* — present it that way, not as a verbatim transcript of the prophet.

**Source (Neolin biography):** Wikipedia, *Neolin*; Teaching American History, "The Master of Life."

## 4. PONTIAC (OBWANDIYAG, ODAWA) AND THE COALITION

**Pontiac (Obwandiyag)** was a **war chief of the Odawa (Ottawa)**. He was a prominent leader of the 1763 rising at Detroit and one of its best-known figures — **but not its sole organizer or supreme commander.** Modern scholarship is firm: this was a broad, decentralized coalition; many nations rose for their own reasons across a thousand miles.
**Source:** Dictionary of Canadian Biography, *Pontiac*; Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.

**The coalition of nations (name several):** Odawa (Ottawa), Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Wyandot (Huron), Lenape (Delaware), Shawnee, Seneca/Mingo, plus Miami, Wea, Kickapoo, Mascouten, Illinois (Peoria), and others.
**Other leaders worth naming:** **Guyasuta** (Seneca/Mingo), **Charlot Kaské** (Shawnee — the war's most uncompromising leader, who later rejected peace and crossed the Mississippi rather than submit).
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War* (infobox + body).

**The outbreak (Detroit):**
- **April 27, 1763** — Pontiac held a war council on the Ecorse River, ~10 miles southwest of Detroit, invoking Neolin's vision to mobilize warriors.
- **May 1, 1763** — Pontiac entered Fort Detroit with ~50 Odawa, ostensibly to dance, actually to scout the garrison.
- **May 7, 1763** — Pontiac's attempted surprise seizure of the fort failed (commander **Henry Gladwin** had been warned); the siege then began (~May 9).
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.

> **VERBATIM (Pontiac, April 1763 council):** "It is important for us, my brothers, that we exterminate from our lands this nation which seeks only to destroy us." **Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*. (A summary/translation of the recorded speech.)

## 5. THE FALL OF THE FORTS — EIGHT POSTS IN WEEKS

In a few weeks, the coalition took **about eight of roughly twelve** British posts across the Great Lakes and Ohio. (Wikipedia frames it as 8 forts captured; some sources say nine forts destroyed — the difference is in counting; **say "about eight of roughly twelve."** [CONTESTED count — see note below.])

| Fort | Date (1763) | How it fell |
|---|---|---|
| **Sandusky** (Ohio) | May 16 | Wyandots gained entry via a false council; ~15 soldiers killed; burned |
| **St. Joseph** (Michigan) | May 25 | Potawatomis used the same deception; most of the 15-man garrison killed |
| **Miami** (Indiana) | May 27 | Commander lured out by a Miami woman; 9-man garrison surrendered |
| **Ouiatenon** (Indiana) | June 1 | Weas/Kickapoos/Mascoutens took the ~20-man garrison without bloodshed |
| **Michilimackinac** (Michigan) | June 2 (some sources June 4) | The **lacrosse ruse** — see below |
| **Venango** (Pennsylvania) | mid-June | Senecas killed the entire ~12-man garrison |
| **Le Boeuf** (Pennsylvania) | June 18 | Most of the ~12-man garrison escaped to Fort Pitt |
| **Presque Isle** (Pennsylvania) | June 19–20 | ~250 warriors; garrison surrendered after two days |

**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.

**[CONTESTED — number of forts]:** Different reputable sources say "eight forts captured" vs "nine forts destroyed" (the latter sometimes counts Fort Edward Augustus / Green Bay, abandoned). The brief's "about eight of roughly twelve" is the safe framing. Do not assert a single hard count without hedging.

### The Michilimackinac lacrosse (baggataway) ruse — June 1763
The Ojibwe staged a game of **baggataway** (lacrosse) outside the fort on the king's birthday (June 2 or 4, 1763). Soldiers watched, gates open. A player knocked the ball near/over the palisade; warriors rushing after it took up weapons **the women had smuggled in under their blankets**, and stormed the fort. Roughly half of the ~35-man garrison was killed; the rest taken.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War* (Fort Michilimackinac section).
**Author note:** This is the single most cinematic and most-cited fort capture. Tell it.

## 6. THE TWO GREAT SIEGES: DETROIT AND FORT PITT

### Fort Detroit (the longest siege)
- **Siege began ~May 9, 1763**, after the failed surprise; led by Pontiac.
- **Battle of Bloody Run — July 31, 1763.** Captain James Dalyell led ~250 British in a night sortie to break the siege; ambushed at Parent's Creek. ~20 British killed (Dalyell among them); the creek "ran red," hence "Bloody Run." A coalition victory — but it did NOT break the garrison.
- **Pontiac lifted the siege October 31, 1763**, after word arrived that the French would not help; warriors dispersed to winter hunting grounds.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.

### Fort Pitt (the smallpox episode)
- **Besieged ~June 22 – August 20, 1763.** Garrison under **Captain Simeon Ecuyer**, ~550 people inside (200+ women and children); crowded; smallpox already present in the fort.
- Ecuyer reported the danger of disease in the crowded post.

#### The smallpox-blanket episode — June 24, 1763
During a parley at Fort Pitt, the British gave besieging **Lenape (Delaware)** emissaries items taken from the fort's smallpox hospital.

> **VERBATIM (trader William Trent's journal, June 24, 1763):** "Out of our regard for them, we gave them two Blankets and an Handkerchief out of the Small Pox Hospital. I hope it will have the desired effect."
> **Source:** Trent's journal, quoted in Wikipedia *Pontiac's War* and HISTORY.com.

> **VERBATIM (the supply invoice):** the items were billed as having been "taken from people in the Hospital to Convey the Smallpox to the Indians." (The fort later reimbursed Trent — i.e., the command sanctioned it on paper.)
> **Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.

#### Amherst's letters endorsing it (late June – July 1763)
> **Amherst to Colonel Henry Bouquet (c. July 7, postscript):** "Could it not be contrived to send the small pox among the disaffected tribes of Indians? We must on this occasion use every stratagem in our power to reduce them."
> **Bouquet to Amherst (July 13):** "I will try to inoculate the [Indians] with some blankets that may fall in their hands, and take care not to get the disease myself."
> **Amherst to Bouquet (July 16):** "You will do well to try to inoculate the Indians by means of blankets, as well as to try every other method that can serve to extirpate this execrable race."
> **Source:** Amherst–Bouquet correspondence, quoted in Wikipedia *Pontiac's War*; Gettysburg Historical Journal Vol. 6 (2007), "'The Desired Effect.'"
> **Author note:** The Trent gift (June 24) and the Amherst letters (early–mid July) are SEPARATE but mutually reinforcing — the act at the fort happened, and the commanding general independently endorsed exactly the same tactic. Both are real and both are gated surfaces. Quote them; do not merge their dates.

**[CONTESTED — did it work?]:** Historians disagree on whether the blankets actually caused infection. Smallpox was ALREADY circulating among the nations around Fort Pitt that spring/summer, so the epidemic cannot be cleanly attributed to the blankets, and the infected material may have been too old to transmit. **Philip Ranlet** (Hunter College) argues "there is no evidence that the scheme worked." Others (Peckham, Jennings, Nester) treat it as likely effective; (McConnell, Ranlet, Dixon) are skeptical. **The intent and the act are NOT contested — only the medical outcome is.** Write it that way: the British tried to use disease as a weapon and the general put it in writing; whether these specific blankets spread it is debated.
**Source:** Ranlet, "The British, the Indians, and Smallpox" (Hunter College); HISTORY.com; ASM.org.

### Bushy Run — August 5–6, 1763 (the relief of Fort Pitt)
**Colonel Henry Bouquet**, marching ~500 men to relieve Fort Pitt, was intercepted ~25 miles east of the fort by a Lenape/Shawnee/Mingo/Wyandot force. Over two days of hard fighting Bouquet won by feigning a retreat that drew the warriors into a counterattack. The victory was **costly** (~50 British killed, ~60 wounded — roughly a quarter of his force) but it broke the intercepting force; the siege of Fort Pitt was lifted **~August 20, 1763**.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War* / *Battle of Bushy Run*.

### (Optional) Devil's Hole — September 14, 1763
At the Niagara portage, Senecas ambushed a supply train and the relief companies; **70+ soldiers and teamsters killed** — the deadliest single British loss of the war ("Devil's Hole Massacre" to colonists). Useful to show the rising was not contained to two sieges.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.

## 7. THE OUTCOME: A STALEMATE NEITHER SIDE COULD BREAK

Neither side could win. The nations could not take the two great forts or expel the British; the British could not conquer the nations or even safely supply their posts. This military stalemate FORCED Britain to reverse course.

- **Amherst was recalled to London (autumn 1763)**, replaced as commander by **General Thomas Gage**. [CONTESTED-MINOR: one source said "1863" — typo; the correct year is 1763.]
- **Sir William Johnson** (Superintendent of Indian Affairs) was authorized to **restore gift-giving and the old diplomacy** — exactly what Amherst had abolished. This was a direct, conscious reversal: the rising proved the alliance system could not simply be cancelled.
- **Negotiated peace, 1764–1766:**
  - **Treaty/Congress of Niagara, July–August 1764** — Johnson met ~2,000 Native people; relations rebuilt.
  - **Bouquet's expedition to the Muskingum, October 1764** — Ohio nations agreed to peace and returned 200+ captives.
  - **Pontiac's own settlement: July 25, 1766, at Fort Ontario (Oswego)** — Pontiac made a formal peace with Johnson. Notably: **no land ceded, no prisoners surrendered, no hostages taken.** Johnson showed Pontiac "exceptional deference."
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*; Dictionary of Canadian Biography, *Pontiac*.

- **(Optional) Pontiac's death:** Pontiac was **murdered April 20, 1769, at Cahokia, Illinois**, by a **Peoria (Illinois) man** (a nephew of the Peoria chief Makatachinga), as he left a trading post. The deference Johnson had shown Pontiac may have inflated his stature and resentment toward him. Burial place unknown.
**Source:** Dictionary of Canadian Biography, *Pontiac*.

## 8. THE ROYAL PROCLAMATION OF 1763

**Issued by King George III on October 7, 1763** (after the war had erupted), partly to calm the frontier and reassure the Native nations.

**What it did:** It drew a line along the **crest of the Appalachian Mountains** — following the watershed (rivers draining east to the Atlantic = open to colonists; rivers draining west to the Mississippi = reserved Indian country) — and **barred colonial settlement west of that line**, creating a vast "Indian Reserve." It also forbade private purchases of Native land (only the Crown could buy).

> **VERBATIM (Royal Proclamation of 1763, on the reserved lands):** "...the several Nations or Tribes of Indians with whom We are connected, and who live under our Protection, should not be molested or disturbed in the Possession of such Parts of Our Dominions and Territories as, not having been ceded to or purchased by Us, are reserved to them... as their Hunting Grounds."
> **VERBATIM (on settlers):** it forbade "all our loving Subjects from making any Purchases or Settlements whatever, or taking Possession of any of the Lands above reserved," and ordered any who had already settled there to "forthwith to remove themselves."
> **Source:** Royal Proclamation of 1763 (text); Wikipedia, *Royal Proclamation of 1763*; American Battlefield Trust primary source.

**Colonial fury + unenforceability:**
- Land-hungry colonists and speculators were enraged. **George Washington** — who had been promised ~20,000 acres of western bounty land for his French and Indian War service — called the line a temporary expedient and quietly told his agent to keep securing western land. Virginia land companies lobbied to move the line west.
- The line was **never enforceable.** Settlers crossed it anyway. Later treaties (Fort Stanwix 1768, etc.) shoved the boundary westward, opening Kentucky and West Virginia. The Proclamation is a real grievance feeding toward the Revolution.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Royal Proclamation of 1763*; Mount Vernon, "Proclamation Line of 1763."

## 9. THE AFTERMATH / CATASTROPHE — NAME IT PLAINLY

The rising **won real, immediate concessions** — Amherst gone, gift-giving and diplomacy restored, a Crown line drawn to protect Native land, peace without land cessions for Pontiac himself. For a moment the nations had forced the most powerful empire on earth to back down. This was, in modern scholarship's view, the **first major multi-nation resistance that did NOT end in outright Native defeat** — a precedent later confederacies (Brant, Tecumseh) would build on.

**But the deeper catastrophe could not be reversed:**
- The **French counterweight was gone for good.** The leverage the whole war had been about — playing empires against each other — was permanently lost. One empire now faced the nations alone.
- The **Proclamation Line was ignored.** Settlers poured over the Appalachians; the dispossession the war had been fought to stop simply continued, slower but unbroken.
- The **human cost was real on all sides.** Estimates: **~400 British soldiers killed** (plus ~50 captured and killed); on the frontier, agent George Croghan estimated **~2,000 settlers killed or captured and ~4,000 displaced**; **200+ Native warriors killed in battle**, with far more lost if disease was a factor. Whole communities on the Pennsylvania/Virginia frontier were emptied.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War* (casualty estimates; interpretation).

**Framing for the author:** Native nations rose for their **sovereignty and survival** — not as a "rebellion" against a rightful master (they had never been conquered or consulted), but as nations defending their own land against an empire that had decided it owned them. Tell it as their war. Name the dispossession; do not sanitize it, and do not make it gratuitous.

## SUPPLEMENT — the Paxton Boys and the Conestoga Massacre, Dec 1763 (added in reconcile)

**Why this supplement exists:** A comprehensiveness critic flagged an ESSENTIAL missing beat. Sections 2 and 6 above document the empire's *intent* to exterminate Native people — Amherst's "extirpate this execrable race" letters and the smallpox-blanket episode at Fort Pitt. The Conestoga Massacre is the settler-violence counterpart in the *very same months*: colonists actually carrying out the extermination of a small, peaceful, neutral Native town. It belongs in this section as the on-the-ground companion to the command-level intent already documented. (Framing note only — do not write prose from this paragraph; it tells the author *why* the beat is here.)

### S1. The Conestoga — peaceful, neutral, Susquehannock-descended, under Pennsylvania's protection
The **Conestoga** were a small community of Native people descended from the **Susquehannock**. In the late 1680s a remnant group of Susquehannock who had been living among the Seneca returned to their homeland in the lower Susquehanna River valley and established a village near the confluence of the Conestoga and Susquehanna rivers; over time they were joined by some Seneca, Cayuga, and Oneida families and were collectively known as the Conestoga. By 1763 they were a small community living near Lancaster, Pennsylvania, **under the colony's protection** (a relationship dating to a treaty with William Penn). They **had lived peacefully with their colonial neighbors for decades and remained NEUTRAL during the Seven Years' War and Pontiac's War** — they were not combatants and not part of the rising documented above. By 1763 they could no longer safely hunt to feed themselves for fear of being mistaken for hostile warriors.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Paxton Boys*; Britannica, *Paxton Boys uprising*; Susquehanna NHA, "RiverRoots: Pontiac's War and the Paxton Boys."

### S2. The Paxton Boys — Scots-Irish frontier vigilantes who blamed all Native people
The **Paxton Boys** were a vigilante mob of **Scots-Irish (Presbyterian) frontiersmen** from the **Paxton / Paxtang** area, the hill country northwest of Lancaster. Inflamed by the frontier panic and the anti-Indian hatred whipped up during Pontiac's War, they **blamed Native people indiscriminately** and did not distinguish the neutral, peaceful Conestoga from the nations actually at war. They justified the killings by claiming the Conestoga were colluding with the Lenape and Shawnee raiding the frontier — an accusation that was **unfounded / fabricated** (Matthew Smith's claim of seeing "dozens of strange, armed Indians" was unsubstantiated; Will Sock was targeted on "unsubstantiated claims that he was providing aid").
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Paxton Boys*; Britannica, *Paxton Boys uprising*.

### S3. The massacre — two dates, two tolls (Dec 14 and Dec 27, 1763)
- **14 December 1763 — Conestoga Town.** Roughly **50 Paxton Boys** attacked the village, **killed and scalped the six Conestoga they found there**, and burned the buildings. Most of the community was away at the time.
- **The survivors taken into protective custody.** The **~14 surviving Conestoga** who had been away (including women and children; **Will Sock / Tenseedaagua** among them) were placed for their safety in the **Lancaster workhouse / jail**.
- **27 December 1763 — the Lancaster workhouse.** Two weeks later the Paxton Boys **rode into Lancaster, broke into the workhouse, and killed, scalped, and dismembered (mutilated) all ~14 survivors, including the women and children.**
- **Total: ~20 Conestoga murdered** (6 on Dec 14 + ~14 on Dec 27). The killings ended the Conestoga as a community.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Paxton Boys*; Britannica; Zinn Education Project, "Dec. 14, 1763: Conestoga Massacre"; American History Central, "Paxton Boys and the Conestoga Massacre (1763)."
**[CONTESTED-MINOR — survivor count]:** Most sources say **14** survivors killed Dec 27 (so 20 total); some accounts give the second-attack toll as 14 and a handful of sources round the total slightly differently. **"6 on Dec 14, about 14 on Dec 27, ~20 in all"** is the safe framing.

### S4. The aftermath — the march on Philadelphia, Franklin, and no convictions
- **The march on Philadelphia (February 1764).** Several hundred Paxton Boys and their followers (the brief's "roughly 600"; Wikipedia says "a few hundred in total") **marched on Philadelphia in February 1764**, intending further violence (their next targets were Native people sheltering in the city). **[CONTESTED-MINOR — crowd size]:** the brief's figure is **~600**; Wikipedia frames it as "a few hundred." Use "several hundred" or "as many as 600" rather than a hard number.
- **Benjamin Franklin turned them back.** The city armed itself (Franklin helped raise militia companies), and a **delegation led by Benjamin Franklin met the Paxton Boys at Germantown** and **persuaded them to disperse on a promise that their grievances would be heard / submitted in writing** (early February 1764). They turned back without attacking. Franklin then wrote his pamphlet *A Narrative of the Late Massacres*, condemning the killings.
- **No one was ever convicted.** Governor **John Penn** issued proclamations and offered a **reward for the ringleaders, but none were ever identified or arrested**; frontier sympathy for the Paxton Boys was widespread. **No one was ever punished for the murders.**
- **The spy/hostile accusation was fabricated.** As in S2 — the claim that the Conestoga were spies or were aiding the hostile nations was **unsubstantiated and false**; the Conestoga were neutral.
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Paxton Boys*; Britannica, *Paxton Boys uprising*; Pennsylvania Center for the Book, "Desperation, Zeal, and Murder: The Paxton Boys."

### S5. (Should-fix) The 1764–1766 peace did NOT happen in unison — Illinois country resistance + Charlot Kaské
The negotiated peace in Section 7 was **not a single simultaneous event**; the coalition nations made peace on **their own timetables**, and resistance **persisted in the Illinois country into 1764–65**, where the British had not yet taken Fort de Chartres from the French. **Charlot Kaské**, the Shawnee leader (already named in Section 4), was the most uncompromising: as Pontiac moved toward negotiation, **Kaské remained firmly anti-British, sought French aid as far as New Orleans, and ultimately crossed the Mississippi with other French and Indian refugees rather than submit to British rule.** When Croghan reached the Illinois country in 1765, Pontiac urged moderation while **Kaské wanted to burn Croghan at the stake.**
**Sourced sentence the author may adapt:** *"The peace did not come all at once — the nations made terms on their own timetables, and resistance lingered in the Illinois country into 1764–65, where the Shawnee leader Charlot Kaské rejected any accommodation, sought French help as far as New Orleans, and finally crossed the Mississippi rather than live under British rule."*
**Source:** Wikipedia, *Charlot Kaské*; Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War* (Illinois country / 1765 Croghan mission).

### S6. (Author correction) Trent smallpox-blanket quote — "regard TO them," not "regard FOR them"
The verbatim wording of the William Trent journal line is **"Out of our regard to them, we gave them two Blankets and an Handkerchief out of the Small Pox Hospital. I hope it will have the desired effect."** — confirmed against the transcribed primary source (UMass Amherst, *Journal of William Trent, 1763*). This is **"regard *to* them,"** NOT "regard *for* them." **NOTE: Section 6 / the Quote Inventory above currently reads "regard FOR them" — that wording is WRONG and must be corrected to "regard TO them" before ship.**
