## the-race · May 7–8, 1864 · A foot-race for a crossroads
### Why Grant didn’t go home

To understand Spotsylvania you have to understand what *didn’t* happen the night of May 7, 1864, because for three years it always had.

For two days the Army of the Potomac had been chewed up in the Wilderness, a tangle of scrub oak and brush about ten miles to the northwest, in a fight so confused that the brush caught fire and burned the wounded where they lay. It ended in a bloody stalemate. And every Union commander who had ever taken a punch like that from Gen. Robert E. Lee (South) and his Army of Northern Virginia had done the same thing afterward: pulled back north, licked his wounds, and let Lee keep Virginia. The Confederate soldiers expected it. It was practically a ritual.

Lt. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant (North) (the new general-in-chief of all the United States armies, riding with the Army of the Potomac and running this campaign himself) did not pull back north. On the night of May 7 he turned the army the other way: *southeast*, toward a sleepy crossroads village called Spotsylvania Court House, about ten miles below the Wilderness, set in the farm country between the Rapidan River to the north and the North Anna River to the south. A “court house” here just means the county seat, a few buildings around the place where the local roads happened to meet. But those roads were the point. The crossroads sat astride the direct route south toward Richmond, the Confederate capital. Grant’s idea was simple and aggressive: get the army *around* Lee’s right flank (the end of his line) and plant it between Lee and Richmond, forcing the Confederates to either attack him on his terms or fall back. Whoever reached the crossroads first and dug in would own the road south.

So it became a race in the most literal sense, two armies marching through the dark for the same patch of ground, and the one that lost would have to attack the one that won.

Lee read it almost at once. He sent his First Corps (a corps is the largest building block of a Civil War army, two or three divisions, tens of thousands of men (the Confederates numbered theirs in words, First and Second and Third; the Union, in Roman numerals)) pelting for the crossroads. It was led now by Maj. Gen. Richard H. Anderson (South), who had just inherited the corps because its usual commander, Lt. Gen. James Longstreet (South), had been shot by his own men in the Wilderness two days earlier. Anderson’s infantry, choking on the smoke of the burning woods and unable to sleep anyway, set off around ten at night down a forest road that had been freshly cut for the purpose, and marched all night without a real halt.

Meanwhile the Union infantry got tangled up on the **Brock Road**, the most direct route from the Wilderness down to Spotsylvania. Union cavalry under Maj. Gen. Philip H. Sheridan (North) had been wrestling all day with the Confederate horsemen of Maj. Gen. J.E.B. Stuart (South) for control of that very road, and the friction jammed the column. Every hour the Union foot soldiers lost on the Brock Road was an hour handed to Anderson.

Anderson won. His men reached the high ground above the crossroads (a low rise called **Laurel Hill**, just south of the Brock Road, the last defensible ground north of the village) *minutes* ahead of the Union arrivals. When the Federals came up and attacked on May 8, the Confederates were already there, throwing up dirt. The race was over, and the Union had lost it by a margin you could measure on a watch.

That set the shape of everything that followed. For the next two weeks the pattern almost never varied: Grant attacks Lee’s entrenchments, Grant is bloodily repulsed, Grant slides southeast and tries again somewhere else. It was the template of the entire campaign, and it started here, with a few minutes’ difference on a forest road.

> **MEANWHILE — the cavalry rides off.** On May 9, Sheridan (North) — frustrated and feuding with his superiors — took roughly ten thousand troopers and rode *away* from Spotsylvania entirely, on a deep raid toward Richmond aimed squarely at drawing Stuart’s (South) cavalry into a stand-up fight. It worked, with consequences nobody intended (see the death of Stuart, below). But it also meant that during some of the most critical days of the battle, Grant’s army was fighting half-blind, without its cavalry screen — the mounted scouts who are an army’s eyes.

## laurel-hill · May 8–9, 1864 · The line goes in, and a general dies
### Spades, and a sharpshooter

Once Anderson held Laurel Hill, the Confederates did what the war had slowly taught both armies to do: they dug. Over May 8 and 9 Lee’s men threw up roughly four miles of **works**, the soldiers’ word for field fortifications, dirt and logs piled into a defensive rampart that a charging man has to cross in the open while the defenders shoot from cover. By 1864 a Civil War army could turn a stretch of farmland into a fortress in a few hours, and a frontal assault on a finished line had become close to suicide. Grant was about to relearn that lesson several times in two weeks.

Maj. Gen. Gouverneur K. Warren (North) and his V Corps had drawn Laurel Hill, and Warren spent the battle hurling his men at it. The first assault on May 8 was thrown back with heavy loss; Maj. Gen. John Sedgwick (North) brought up his VI Corps to help and failed too. Warren would attack Laurel Hill again and again over the coming days, and it never fell. It became the part of the line that simply could not be cracked from the front, no matter how many times the order came down.

The most famous death of the battle came on May 9, and it came with a grim little joke attached.

Sedgwick was one of the most loved men in the army, his soldiers called him “Uncle John.” That morning he rode out to site some artillery near the front, where Confederate sharpshooters with long-range rifles were sniping at anything that moved, the bullets buzzing in from perhaps a thousand yards off. His men kept flinching and ducking. Sedgwick, amused and a little exasperated, chided them for it. They couldn’t hit an elephant at this distance, he told them.

> “They couldn’t hit an elephant at this distance.” — Maj. Gen. John Sedgwick (North)

A moment later a sharpshooter’s bullet struck him below the left eye. His chief of staff, Lt. Col. Martin McMahon (North), was standing right there and caught him as he fell. Sedgwick never regained consciousness. (There’s a popular legend that the bullet cut him off mid-sentence, that he never finished the word “elephant.” It’s a good story and it isn’t true; the elephant line was simply among the last things he said, and he spoke after it.) Maj. Gen. Horatio Wright (North) took over the VI Corps. The army had lost one of its most beloved commanders to a single rifle shot, in a quiet moment, almost as an afterthought of the day.

The lines were now set, and they ran along a stretch of ground that was about to become one of the most notorious places in American history, though nobody knew it yet, because the trouble was baked into the shape of the dirt itself.

## the-mule-shoe · May 10–11, 1864 · Upton’s column and the fatal geometry
### A salient is a trap with high ground inside it

Here is the problem Lee’s engineers couldn’t solve. When Lt. Gen. Richard Ewell (South) and his Second Corps dug their part of the line on the night of May 8, the best defensible ground (a patch of higher elevation they needed in order to see and shoot) didn’t sit in a tidy straight line. It bulged out *northward*, ahead of everything on either side of it. To hold that high ground, the trench had to follow it, which meant the line poked out into a great horseshoe-shaped loop, jutting more than half a mile out in front of the main works and enclosing something like 250 acres of farmland. The soldiers looked at the curve of it on the ground and named it the **“Mule Shoe.”**

A bulge like that in a fortified line has a formal name (a *salient*) and it is a known death-trap, for a reason you can see by drawing it. A straight line of works can only be shot at from the front. A salient sticks out, so an enemy can wrap around it and fire into it from the front *and* both sides at once (three directions of bullets converging on the men inside (gunners call that converging side-fire **enfilade**, and it is exactly as bad as it sounds). Lee knew the Mule Shoe was weak. His artillerists knew it. They accepted it anyway, because the alternative was surrendering the high ground, and to make up for the danger they crammed the salient with around thirty cannon and built the trenches stout. The apex) the tip of the horseshoe, the part that stuck out farthest and was exposed on the most sides, was the worst spot of all. Within a few days it would have a new name: the Bloody Angle.

The Union spent May 10 testing the line, and one test very nearly changed the war.

In the early evening, around six, a young colonel named Emory Upton (North) tried something new against the west face of the salient. The standard way to assault works was to advance in a broad line, stop, trade volleys to soften the defenders, then charge (which gave the men behind the rampart plenty of time to shoot you to pieces on the way in. Upton threw that out. He took twelve hand-picked regiments, roughly five thousand men, and stacked them in a tight, deep column) four battle lines packed close one behind the other, like a battering ram of human beings, and sent them sprinting across about 150 yards of open ground with one inflexible order: **do not stop to fire.** Don’t shoot, don’t pause, just *run* and hit the trench before the defenders can reload after their first volley.

It worked beautifully. Upton’s column took the first volley, absorbed it, and crashed straight into the works, breaking clean through the line and capturing a stretch of trench and a battery of guns. For a few minutes there was a real hole in Lee’s army. And then nothing came to fill it. The supporting attack that was supposed to pour through behind Upton (a division under Brig. Gen. Gershom Mott (North)) wilted under Confederate artillery fire and fell back before it arrived. Left alone and unsupported in a captured trench with the enemy closing in from three sides, Upton had to give the ground back; Confederate counterattacks drove his men out of the works they had just bled to take.

But everyone who mattered saw what had happened. A dense, fast column, charging without firing, had cracked a modern entrenched line, something that was supposed to be impossible. Grant was watching, and Grant drew the obvious conclusion: do it again, but *bigger*. Do it with a whole corps, aimed straight at the apex. He promoted Upton brigadier general on the spot and started planning the assault for May 12.

And then Lee, by pure accident, took the cannon off the very ground Grant was about to attack. On May 11 Lee guessed wrong: watching the Union army shuffle, he convinced himself Grant was disengaging and sliding off to the southeast again (the now-familiar pattern) and, to be ready to march, he ordered most of the artillery, around twenty-nine guns, *pulled out of the Mule Shoe.* It was, in effect, stripping the most exposed point in the army of its teeth the night before the biggest assault of the campaign landed on it. The error compounded itself by the clock. Late that night Maj. Gen. Edward “Allegheny” Johnson (South), whose division held the apex, grew nervous and asked for his guns back. The order to return them went out, but it crawled, and the artillery didn’t start rolling back toward the salient until roughly half past three in the morning on May 12. Hancock’s assault would land about half an hour later, with the cannon literally on the road, headed the wrong way. The Confederate artillerist E. Porter Alexander (South) would later judge the decision to pull those guns the single worst Confederate mistake of the whole campaign, and he was right.

> **MEANWHILE — “fight it out on this line.”** Around half past eight on the morning of May 11, with the army stalled and the casualty lists already appalling, Grant (North) wrote a dispatch to the army’s chief of staff in Washington, Maj. Gen. Henry Halleck (North). Buried in the routine business about sending the wagons back to Belle Plain for fresh provisions and ammunition was the line that would define the entire campaign in the Northern press: that he meant to fight it out on this line if it took all summer. It was the opposite of every message Washington had gotten from this army after a hard fight. He was not coming home.

> **MEANWHILE — Yellow Tavern, and the death of Stuart.** That same May 11, six miles north of Richmond, the cavalry raid bore its bitter fruit. Sheridan’s (North) roughly twelve thousand troopers ran into Stuart’s (South) roughly forty-five hundred at a place called Yellow Tavern. In the swirling fight, Stuart — Lee’s dashing cavalry chief, the army’s eyes for three years — was shot at close range by a dismounted Union trooper, Pvt. John Huff (North). He was carried into Richmond and died there the next day, May 12, even as the Mule Shoe was filling with bodies a few hours’ ride to the north. Maj. Gen. Fitzhugh Lee (South) — Robert E. Lee’s nephew — took over the cavalry. In the same handful of days, each army lost a beloved senior commander — Sedgwick for the Union, Stuart for the Confederacy — to a single well-aimed shot.

## the-bloody-angle · May 12, 1864 · Twenty hours in the rain
### The worst hand-to-hand fighting of the war

It had to be done in the dark. Around half past four in the morning on May 12 (delayed from four by darkness, mist, and a cold drizzle) Maj. Gen. Winfield S. Hancock (North) sent his entire II Corps, somewhere between fifteen and twenty thousand men packed into a massive column, straight at the apex of the Mule Shoe. This was Upton’s tactic blown up to corps scale: a huge dense mass of men surging across the wet ground in the half-light, not stopping to shoot, aimed at the single most exposed point in Lee’s line.

With the cannon gone, there was almost nothing to stop them. Johnson’s (South) roughly four thousand defenders at the apex got off what fire they could in the rain and then the wave was on top of them. The division of Brig. Gen. Francis Barlow (North) (the formation that would overrun the apex) poured over the works and the defense simply came apart. The Federals didn’t just take the trench, they took the *people in it*, scooping up most of Johnson’s division as prisoners in one of the largest mass captures of the war. Maj. Gen. Edward “Allegheny” Johnson (South) himself was taken, and so was one of his brigade commanders, Brig. Gen. George “Maryland” Steuart (South), along with thousands of their men and the guns that had arrived too late to fire. In the span of a few minutes a huge gap had been torn in the Army of Northern Virginia, and Union troops were pouring through it into the heart of Lee’s line.

This was the crisis of the battle, and possibly of Lee’s army. If the breach widened, the whole army could be cut in two.

What sealed it was speed and desperation. Maj. Gen. John B. Gordon (South) flung counterattacking brigades into the gap to plug it, feeding in everything within reach (the South Carolinians of Brig. Gen. Samuel McGowan (South), the men of Brig. Gen. Stephen Ramseur (South), brigade after brigade thrown straight into the breach. Lee himself rode forward to lead the counterattack in person, and his soldiers wouldn’t have it) they crowded around him and turned his horse back, shouting *“Lee to the rear!”* until he gave way. (It was nearly a replay of a moment in the Wilderness days earlier; the army would not spend its commander to buy back a trench.) The counterattacks didn’t drive the Federals out so much as *stop* them, jamming them up along the inside of the west shoulder of the salient. And there, on that west-face ground just below the apex, the two armies locked together and could not let go.

What happened next is the reason this place has a name people still flinch at.

For something on the order of twenty hours (many accounts say twenty to twenty-four) the two sides fought hand-to-hand across the same log parapet, in the rain, without either being able to break the other. This was not a battle line trading volleys at a hundred yards. This was men jammed muzzle-to-muzzle on opposite sides of the same wall of dirt and logs, firing point-blank into each other through the chinks, stabbing over the top with bayonets, swinging clubbed muskets like axes when there was no time to load. The rain that had delayed the dawn assault now soaked the gunpowder and ruined it, so that loaded weapons wouldn’t fire and the fight collapsed into the oldest kind of killing there is, close enough to grab the other man. Fresh men climbed up onto the parapet to shoot down into the trench and were shot down themselves and fell into it, and more climbed up over them. The ditch on the Confederate side filled with rain, mud, and the dead, layered where they fell. Soldiers on both sides who survived the war and saw everything else it had to offer still said they had never seen anything like the Bloody Angle, and never wanted to again.

> **MEANWHILE — Warren attacks Laurel Hill. Again.** While the apex bled, Grant needed Lee pinned everywhere so he couldn’t shift troops to the breach. The order went to Warren (North) to throw his V Corps at Laurel Hill once more — Meade told him to go in at once at all hazards. It failed once more, as it had every time, and worse, it drew off no Confederate reserves the way Grant had hoped. Some of Warren’s units were now making their fourth or fifth doomed charge at the same hill.

Sometime around three or four in the morning on May 13, it ended the only way it could. Lee’s men had spent the night building a fresh line of works straight across the *base* of the salient (a chord drawn across the bottom of the horseshoe, perhaps half a mile to three-quarters of a mile behind the apex) and now, regiment by regiment in the dark, the Confederates slipped back to it and abandoned the bloody tip of the Mule Shoe entirely. By dawn the apex belonged to the Union, and there was almost nothing there worth having.

Grant’s aide Lt. Col. Horace Porter (North) walked the ground afterward and tried to describe it. By his account, the Union dead were scattered all across the area around the angle, while in front of the captured works the Confederate dead lay vastly more numerous than the Union’s, piled on top of one another several layers deep. In something like twenty-four hours, the fighting in and around the salient had cost on the order of nine thousand Union and eight thousand Confederate casualties, though the Confederate figure includes the roughly three thousand of Johnson’s division marched off as prisoners. Whichever way you count it, it was the bloodiest single day of the costliest battle of the campaign.

There is one object that carries the Bloody Angle better than any casualty number, and it is sitting in a museum right now.

The volume of rifle fire along that contested stretch was so unrelenting, so dense and so continuous over those hours, that it did something hard to believe until you see the proof: it cut down a living oak tree. Not knocked it over with a cannonball (*cut it down with rifle bullets*, the way a saw would, the trunk gnawed clean through by an uncountable storm of small-arms fire until a tree nearly two feet thick simply fell. Conical minié balls) the soft lead rifle bullets of the war, are still embedded in the wood. The stump was dug up and sent to the Smithsonian in 1888, and it is on display today at the National Museum of American History, in an exhibition about the price of American freedom. You can stand in front of it. It is the single most concrete thing the Bloody Angle left behind: a tree killed by gunfire, because that is how much gunfire there was, in one spot, for the better part of a day.

## the-23rd · May 13–21, 1864 · The army that came back in blue
### The crossroads was the object; slavery was the reason

The fighting didn’t stop after the Bloody Angle, but it never reached that pitch again. From May 13 through 16, Grant (North) shifted the whole army eastward through heavy rain and bottomless mud, probing Lee’s (South) line for a soft spot and never quite finding one. On May 18 Hancock (North) tried the old Mule Shoe ground one more time at dawn, gambling that Lee had thinned it (but Ewell’s (South) Second Corps held the improved works with massed artillery and threw the assault back hard. On May 19, Lee sent Ewell out on a reconnaissance toward the Union right that ran into green Union regiments) “heavy artillery” units, men trained to man the big guns in the Washington forts and only just converted into infantry, at the Harris Farm; it was a costly, pointless fight that cost the Confederates around nine hundred men they could not spare, and Lee called them back. Then, on May 21, Grant did exactly what he had done after the Wilderness: he disengaged and slid the army *southeast* again, this time toward the North Anna River, keeping Lee in continuous contact and refusing to let go.

That is the strategic story, and it can be summed up in one cold sentence: Grant could replace his roughly eighteen thousand casualties, and Lee could not replace his roughly twelve to thirteen thousand. Spotsylvania was a tactical draw (both sides claimed it) but it was a strategic disaster for the South, because it proved the new arithmetic. The Union had the men. The Confederacy did not. Lee’s army would never again take the strategic offensive in the East; from here it could only defend, and bleed, and fall back. The road from this crossroads ran through the North Anna and Cold Harbor to the trenches of Petersburg by mid-June, and Petersburg was where the war finally ground to its end.

But it is worth stopping, the way this campaign was always really about something underneath the maps, and saying plainly what these two armies were fighting *for*, because at Spotsylvania the answer didn’t stay an abstraction. It walked onto the battlefield in person.

The Confederacy existed to preserve and extend slavery. That is not a framing or an interpretation; it is the thing itself. Eleven states had broken from the United States rather than accept any limit on holding Black people as property, and the Army of Northern Virginia was the shield of that slave society (the force that kept the slaveholders’ republic alive. Grant’s grinding attrition strategy, all the awful arithmetic of it, was at bottom a plan to destroy the army that defended slavery faster than the South could rebuild it. The crossroads was the *object*. Slavery was the *reason*. And on May 15, 1864) in the middle of this battle, in Spotsylvania County itself, the reason and the object stood on the same ground at the same time, and you could not miss it.

That day, at the **Alrich Farm**, where the Catharpin Road met the Orange Plank Road on the edge of the battlefield, Confederate cavalry under Brig. Gen. Thomas Rosser (South) pushed forward and shoved back the 2nd Ohio Cavalry (North). Up in support came the **23rd United States Colored Infantry (North)** (a regiment of Black soldiers. They skirmished, then charged, and they drove Rosser back. It was a small action by the standards of a battle that had cut down an oak tree with bullets three days before. But it was the first time United States Colored Troops fought Lee’s Army of Northern Virginia in directed combat) the first such clash north of the James River, and a marker of what the war had become.

Think about who those men were. The 23rd USCT had been organized just months earlier, mustered beginning in late 1863, out of formerly enslaved and escaped people (and many of them came from Virginia, from the very counties clustered around Fredericksburg: Spotsylvania, Stafford, Orange, Culpeper, Caroline, and the city of Fredericksburg itself. These were the places they had *fled*, walking to Union lines to free themselves. And now, a year or two later, they had come back to this exact county wearing Union blue, with rifles in their hands, and charged the army that was fighting to keep them and their families enslaved. Men who had been listed as property in the records of these towns drove off Confederate cavalry on the soil they had been enslaved on. As the Battlefield Trust and Park Service material puts it, the fight proved to the white Union soldiers watching that Black soldiers would stand and fight the Confederate army) but it proved something larger and simpler than that, too. Their own freedom was the cause of the war, and here they were fighting for it, in arms, on the ground where they had been owned.

That is the whole argument of the Civil War standing in one place: two great armies ground each other to pieces over a crossroads for two weeks, and a few miles from the apex of the Mule Shoe, formerly enslaved men from these very fields settled the question of what it was all *for* by picking up a rifle and walking back toward the slaveholders’ army, this time as free soldiers of the United States. The thing the South was fighting to keep was, at that moment, marching against it in uniform.

existed to preserve and extend slavery. That is not a framing or an interpretation; it is
the thing itself.”), develops the 23rd USCT at the Alrich Farm as the human proof
(formerly enslaved men from these very counties fighting Lee’s army), keeps the enslaved
people as the grammatical subject of their own liberation, and closes on it (“The thing
the South was fighting to keep was, at that moment, marching against it in uniform”). The
orphaned Corinth cross-reference was trimmed out so the close stands self-contained for a
cold reader. NAME-THE-CAUSE satisfied concretely and locally.
