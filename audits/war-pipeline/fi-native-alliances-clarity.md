# CLARITY / NEWCOMER critic — "The Contest for Native Alliances"

**Section kind:** theme (SOCIETY), French & Indian War off-the-battlefield.
**Read posture:** sharp 15-year-old, zero prior knowledge, reading cold.
**Verdict: PASS** — no comprehension-breaker (LOST) remains. A handful of small
NEEDS-GLOSS / NEEDS-TAG nits below are SHOULD-FIX polish, not ship-blockers.

Marquee names treated as gloss-exempt (introduceable without a scene-set):
George Washington, Edward Braddock, the Marquis de Montcalm, Jeffery Amherst,
Sir William Johnson, Pontiac. All are either tagged or scene-set anyway.

## Section-by-section

### Opening — "The Contest for Native Alliances" — CLEAR
The "the war is named after the enemy / the name is a point of view" hook lands
without prior knowledge. "Indigenous nations," "the interior," and the Ohio
Country are all inline-defined the first time they matter ("the upper Ohio River
valley, roughly today's western Pennsylvania..."). New France is defined on first
use ("a thin ribbon up the St. Lawrence River and a scatter of forts"). "Redcoats"
is recoverable from context. No leap. The two paragraphs are long but each carries
one idea and breaks cleanly; not a wall.

### "What France had built" — CLEAR
- *voyageurs* and *coureurs de bois* — both glossed in-line the moment they appear
  ("who paddled the canoe routes," "the unlicensed 'runners of the woods'"). Good.
- *pays d'en haut* — glossed ("the 'upper country'"). Good.
- The roster of nations (Ottawa/Odawa, Ojibwe/Chippewa, Potawatomi, etc.) gives the
  alternate names in parens on first use, which is exactly what a cold reader needs.
- *Seven Nations of Canada* — glossed ("seven Catholic Native villages allied to
  New France"). *Kahnawake* glossed ("near the rapids"). CLEAR.
- The "Mohawk would fight Mohawk" irony is set up cleanly with the 1660s backstory
  first. A newcomer can follow it.
- Gift-giving and the missions: the *mechanism* is explained, not just named
  ("accepting a gift created a real obligation"). This is the single best clarity
  move in the section.

### *Onontio* / "middle ground" — NEEDS-GLOSS (SHOULD-FIX, minor)
*Onontio* is glossed ("a father figure... one who gave gifts, settled quarrels...
rather than one who gave orders") — CLEAR. "A relationship of mutual accommodation,
a middle ground where neither side simply imposed its will" is also self-glossing.
The one soft spot: "Historians have a name for how this worked" promises a named
concept and then the named concept (*the middle ground*) is buried mid-sentence and
never flagged as the term of art. A 15-year-old won't be lost — the plain-English
restatement carries them — but the sentence under-delivers on its own setup. Optional
tightening only.

### "What Britain had, and what it didn't" — CLEAR
- *Covenant Chain* — glossed ("a partnership between the British colonies... and the
  Iroquois Confederacy"). Six Nations / Haudenosaunee both given with the member-nation
  list. The "silver chain that had to be polished" metaphor is explained, not assumed.
- "brothers to the English, not sons" — the meaning is unpacked. CLEAR.
- *land speculators* — glossed ("investors who buy up land cheaply hoping to sell it
  later at a profit"). Exactly the right call for a cold reader.
- Ohio Delaware / Lenape and Shawnee — introduced with the Lenape alias inline. CLEAR.

### Sir William Johnson para — CLEAR, one tag nit
- Johnson is tagged "(British)" and richly scene-set (Irish-born trader, learned
  Mohawk, adopted). CLEAR.
- *sachem* — glossed ("a civil leader"). *Warraghiyagey* — glossed. CLEAR.
- *Superintendent of Indian Affairs* — the role is explained ("revive the Covenant
  Chain and win the wary Iroquois"). CLEAR.
- Molly Brant (Konwatsi'tsiaienni) — "a Mohawk clan mother" + "power broker in her own
  right" scene-sets her; not on a war "side" in the North/South sense, so no side-tag is
  the right call. CLEAR.
- Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea) — explicitly de-scoped ("a boy in these years... his fame
  would come later, in another war"), which heads off the reader wondering why a famous
  name is walked past. Good.

### "The Iroquois play their own game" — CLEAR
The neutrality-as-leverage idea is stated in plain terms ("A nation everyone needs and
no one owns can play the empires against each other"). No jargon. CLEAR.

### "Hendrick at Albany" — CLEAR
- Theyanoguin / Hendrick — introduced with both names, clan, and town; the
  same-name-different-man caution is handled in a parenthetical that *helps* rather than
  confuses ("they were two different men"). CLEAR.
- *Bear Clan*, *Canajoharie* — proper nouns, not jargon; no gloss needed.
- *Albany* gathering — scene-set ("a gathering of colonial delegates and Iroquois leaders
  in Albany called to shore up the alliance"). CLEAR.
- The "all like women, bare and open, without any fortifications" quote is decoded right
  after the figure caption ("unguarded as a house with no walls"). CLEAR.
- Baron Dieskau — tagged "(French)". Lake George ambush is scene-set (detachment sent
  south, ambushed in a ravine). CLEAR. The "too few / too many" saying is framed as a
  remembered saying. CLEAR.

### "How Native allies won France its early war" (Monongahela) — CLEAR
- Fort Duquesne glossed ("the French post at the forks of the Ohio (where Pittsburgh now
  stands)"). Braddock is a marquee name and is scene-set anyway.
- *Canadian militia*, *troops*, the force-composition numbers are all in plain English; the
  640-vs-250 point is made so a newcomer sees *why* "Native allies won it." CLEAR.
- George Washington introduced as "a young Virginia officer" — perfect for a cold reader who
  may only know the later president. CLEAR.

### "The victory that cost France its allies" (Fort William Henry) — CLEAR
- *Marquis de Montcalm* — marquee, and scene-set by role. *George Monro* — tagged
  "(British)." *garrison* recoverable from context; *regulars* glossed ("full-time
  professional soldiers"). CLEAR.
- *honors of war* / the parole terms — explained in plain English ("march out under French
  escort... keeping their muskets (though no ammunition)... promise not to fight again for
  eighteen months"). CLEAR.
- The grievance mechanism (warriors promised plunder/scalps/captives, terms gave them
  nothing) is spelled out — the reader understands the betrayal logic. CLEAR.
- Ian K. Steele is tagged "the historian," so the modern-voice quote isn't mistaken for a
  period source. Good.
- Col. Joseph Frye — tagged "(British)," scene-set "a Massachusetts officer in the column."
  CLEAR.
- The legend-vs-modern-scholarship deflation (1,500 vs 69–184, disease over weapons) is
  handled in plain terms a newcomer can follow. CLEAR.

### "Easton: the Ohio nations change the war" — CLEAR
- The council, the parties, and the terms are all in plain language. Teedyuscung tagged
  "(Delaware)." John Forbes tagged "(British)." *Appalachian Mountains* needs no gloss.
- "blew up Fort Duquesne... built Fort Pitt, the seed of Pittsburgh" — clean, concrete.
- The "they chose peace, weren't given it" framing is explicit. CLEAR.
- *Royal Proclamation of 1763* — named at the very end as a forward-pointer; not relied on
  for comprehension, so naming-without-deep-gloss is fine here. The sentence explains what it
  did ("halt settlement at the mountains"). CLEAR.

### "Allies, not pawns" — CLEAR, one tag nit
- Tanaghrisson — introduced here for the FIRST time in the body as "the Mingo headman the
  British called the Half King." *Mingo* and *headman* are glossed-by-apposition; "the Half
  King" is explained as a British nickname. A cold reader meets him cleanly. CLEAR — but see
  the SHOULD-FIX note below: he is named only in this recap, never in the earlier Ohio
  section where he acted, so a curious reader gets no "what did he actually do" beat. Not a
  comprehension-breaker (the sentence stands on its own), just a thin first appearance.

### "Winning the war, losing the peace" (Amherst / Pontiac's War) — CLEAR
- The "France gone → no second empire to balance against → leverage evaporated" chain is
  stated in plain terms. CLEAR.
- Jeffery Amherst — marquee; scene-set as "Britain's commander in North America." His three
  policy moves (end gifts, restrict powder, contempt) are each explained with their
  consequence. CLEAR.
- Pontiac (Obwandiyag) — "the Odawa war leader," scene-set; the rising's trigger is given.
  CLEAR.
- Henry Bouquet — tagged "(British)." The smallpox-blanket episode and the Amherst quote are
  framed with the intent-vs-effect hedge in plain English. CLEAR.
- *extirpate* — a hard word for a 15-year-old, but it sits inside a direct quote and the
  surrounding prose makes the meaning obvious ("spreading smallpox to 'extirpate' the
  nations"). The caption restates it as wiping them out in effect. Borderline NEEDS-GLOSS but
  the context carries it. CLEAR.

## SHOULD-FIX (polish, not ship-blocking)
1. *Onontio* paragraph: "Historians have a name for how this worked" sets up a named term
   but then states *the middle ground* mid-sentence without flagging it as the term. Either
   name it crisply ("...a *middle ground*") or drop the "have a name" setup. Comprehension is
   not at risk.
2. Tanaghrisson appears only in the closing "Allies, not pawns" recap, never in the Ohio
   section where he acted (Logstown / pushing back French expansion). A reader who notices the
   name gets no action beat. Consider one clause on what he did, or accept him as a recap-only
   name.
3. *extirpate* — sits in a quote and is context-carried; fine as-is, but a three-word
   in-prose gloss ("wipe out") would remove the last hard word for a 15-year-old.

*coureurs de bois*, *pays d'en haut*, *sachem*, *Onontio*, *Covenant Chain*, *land
speculators*, *honors of war*, *regulars*, *garrison*) are all glossed inline or
context-carried.

## LOST (comprehension-breakers)
