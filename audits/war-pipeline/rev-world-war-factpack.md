# FACT PACK — "A World War" (1778–1780)

*American Revolution vertical, WAR-STORY SPINE, phase 4 of "How the War Happened" (route slug `world-war`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote verified against cited sources (founders.archives.gov, Library of Congress, NPS, American Battlefield Trust, Mount Vernon, Journal of the American Revolution, Yale University Art Gallery, Britannica, state encyclopedias); confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges for contested numbers.*

**Orientation.** SPINE chapter — phase 4 of six. The McPherson braid governs: 1778–1780 is ONE pulse, not parallel lanes. The French treaties turn a colonial rebellion into a world war; the British army walks out of the capital it spent 1777 winning; the last great battle in the north is fought in killing heat the same summer the Royal Navy starts recalculating around sugar islands; the war's center of gravity slides south through Savannah, Charleston, the Waxhaws, and Camden while the money dies, the army freezes and mutinies at Morristown, and the most celebrated fighting general of the cause sells West Point. The off-the-battlefield chapters own their arcs; this chapter carries the simultaneity beats and pills out: `french-alliance` (d'Estaing's arc, Rochambeau), `war-at-sea` (the Royal Navy's new math, the West Indies), `army-from-nothing` (Morristown, the mutiny, the currency's victims), `civil-war-among-themselves` (the Loyalist-manpower theory, Waxhaws' afterlife, Kings Mountain's all-American fight), `native-nations` (Wyoming/Cherry Valley/Sullivan — the theme OWNS the story; the spine places it in time), `women-of-the-revolution` (the Monmouth water-carrier legend lives there, NOT here; Peggy Shippen at evidence level only). ## 1. SPRING 1778 — EVERYTHING CHANGES AT ONCE

- **The treaties cross the ocean.** France and the United States signed two treaties in Paris on **6 February 1778** — a Treaty of Amity and Commerce and a **Treaty of Alliance** (operative if/when Britain and France went to war, which followed within months). The news reached Washington at **Valley Forge at the end of April/early May**; Congress ratified both treaties **4 May 1778**, and on **6 May** the army at Valley Forge celebrated with a *feu de joie* (a running fire of muskets down the whole line), cannon salutes, and cheers for the King of France. (HIGH on dates; the feu de joie is documented in general orders and participant letters.)
- **What the alliance means in one breath:** France brings a real navy — the one thing the Revolution cannot build — plus money, arms, and eventually an army. Britain's problem is no longer thirteen colonies; it is France (and from 1779 Spain, and from late 1780 the Dutch), the Channel, Gibraltar, the West Indies, India. **The American war becomes one front in a world war** — and not obviously the most important front. (HIGH as framing — the standard scholarly account; see §3.)
- **London moves first — peace terms and a new general in the same season.** Parliament, shaken by Saratoga and racing the French news, sent the **Carlisle Commission** (1778) to offer the Americans essentially everything demanded in 1775 — repeal of the offending acts, no parliamentary taxation — everything short of independence, three years too late. Congress refused to treat except on the basis of independence or withdrawal; the commission sailed home in failure by year's end. (HIGH on the commission and its failure; one beat.)
- **Clinton replaces Howe.** **Sir William Howe** had asked to be relieved in October 1777; **Sir Henry Clinton** was appointed his successor **4 February 1778** and took command at **Philadelphia in May 1778**; Howe sailed home **24–25 May** after the "Mischianza" (18 May) — an extravagant mock-tournament farewell fete his officers threw him in the occupied city, jousting knights and fireworks for a general going home with the war unwon (usable as one wry clause). (HIGH on the change of command, appointment date, Mischianza date; phrase the handover as "in May 1778," not a single day.)
- **Clinton's orders end the Philadelphia experiment.** With France in, the ministry's instructions (Germain, March 1778) required Clinton to detach ~5,000 men for an expedition against French **St. Lucia** in the West Indies and ~3,000 for **Florida**, abandon Philadelphia, and hold New York — and authorized evacuating even New York for Halifax if it came to that. The army that had spent the bloody 1777 campaign taking the rebel capital **marched out of it on 18 June 1778** without a shot — ~10,000–11,000 troops crossing New Jersey with a baggage train commonly described as stretching about **12 miles (19 km)**; some **3,000 Philadelphia Loyalists** went with the fleet rather than be left to their neighbors. Congress was back in the city within days. (HIGH on the evacuation date, detachment orders, and strategic logic; MED on the train length and Loyalist count — "about.")

## 2. JUNE 28, 1778 — MONMOUTH (what-happened scale; the last great northern battle)

- **The pursuit.** Washington left Valley Forge after Clinton with an army remade by the winter's drilling (Steuben's work — one clause; `army-from-nothing` owns the institution story). His council of war split over forcing a battle; **Maj. Gen. Charles Lee** — the army's senior major general, just exchanged after sixteen months as a British prisoner, openly skeptical that Americans could stand against British regulars in the open — argued against a general action, declined command of the advance force when it was small, then claimed it by seniority when it grew to ~4,500–5,000. (HIGH)
- **The day.** **28 June 1778, Monmouth Court House (Freehold), New Jersey** — fought in brutal heat, commonly given as **near or above 100°F (38°C)**; men died of heatstroke on both sides. Lee's advance force engaged Clinton's rearguard, then fell into a disordered, largely unordered retreat when British reinforcements turned on it. Washington, riding forward, met the retreating columns, confronted Lee (next bullet), rallied a line at the hedgerow and **Perrine's Ridge**, and through the afternoon the rebuilt Continental line — maneuvering under fire, artillery dueling, throwing back attacks by the British army's best troops — **stood toe-to-toe until dark: the Valley Forge payoff made visible**. Clinton broke contact that night and completed his march to Sandy Hook; the army crossed to New York by sea. Tactically a draw; Washington's army held the field. (HIGH on the shape.)
- **The Washington–Lee exchange — myth-hygiene mandatory.** The 19th-century version (Washington swearing "till the leaves shook on the trees," cursing Lee off the field) rests on secondhand recollections decades later — the famous swearing account traces to **Gen. Charles Scott, who was not present** at the meeting. Careful modern scholarship (Lender & Stone, *Fatal Sunday*, 2016, the standard study) concludes Washington was **furious but almost certainly not profane**, demanded an explanation — per the court-martial record, words to the effect of *"I desire to know, sir, what is the reason — whence arises this disorder and confusion?"* — got a stammering answer, and **did not strip Lee on the spot**: Lee was even given charge of a delaying position minutes later. Washington's documented rage level: hot, controlled, decisive — write exactly that. (HIGH on the corrected account; the exact words MED — from trial testimony, attribute as such.)
- **Lee's court-martial — handle the historiography fairly.** Lee, his honor stung, fired off two insolent letters and demanded a court-martial; he got one, charged with (1) disobedience of orders in not attacking, (2) misbehavior before the enemy in making an "unnecessary, disorderly, and shameful retreat," (3) disrespect to the commander-in-chief. Convicted on all three (the court softened "shameful"), **suspended from command for one year** — and finished; Congress dismissed him outright in 1780 after yet another insulting letter. **Modern scholarship has partly rehabilitated him:** many historians judge the retreat itself a defensible, even army-saving, withdrawal by an exposed advance force facing Clinton's counterstroke, and read the verdict as substantially political — the army could keep Lee or Washington, not both. State both halves: his battlefield decision was far better than the legend; his insubordination afterward was real and self-destructive. (HIGH on charges/verdict/suspension/dismissal; the rehabilitation framed as "many modern historians," never as settled fact.)
- **Casualties.** American return ~**106 killed, 161 wounded, 95 missing** (~360); British return ~**65 killed in action plus ~59–60 dead of heat and fatigue, ~170 wounded, ~64 missing** (~358) — with American burial parties claiming more British dead than the return admits. **Heat killed dozens on both sides.** Phrase as "each side lost something over 350." (HIGH that returns exist and are close; MED on exact splits — ranges.)
- **NO Molly Pitcher.** The water-carrier story and the composite "Molly Pitcher" figure belong to `women-of-the-revolution` — at most one pointing clause here. (Directive)
- **What Monmouth was:** the last great battle in the north. For the rest of the war the main armies sit watching each other around New York while the war is decided everywhere else — this chapter's hinge. (HIGH)

## 3. THE CENTER OF GRAVITY LEAVES — BRITAIN'S NEW MATH (one beat; `war-at-sea` owns the arc)

- With France in, the Royal Navy could no longer concentrate on America: **home waters against invasion** (a Franco-Spanish armada actually entered the Channel in 1779), **Gibraltar** (besieged from 1779), and above all the **West Indies**, where the sugar islands were among the most valuable real estate in the empire and a fleet action could decide a war in an afternoon. The standard framing, verified across the scholarship: **after 1778, the thirteen colonies were no longer Britain's first strategic priority — home defense and the sugar islands came first.** (HIGH as the standard account; phrase as Britain's calculus, not a precise ledger.)
- **The West Indies front opens immediately** — one beat: the ~5,000 men Clinton detached took **St. Lucia in December 1778** and held it against d'Estaing's fleet and a counter-landing; for the rest of the war islands changed hands (Dominica, Grenada, St. Vincent to France) in a theater Americans rarely saw but which governed when French ships could come north at all. (HIGH on St. Lucia/Dec 1778 and the island exchanges; one-beat scale — `war-at-sea` owns it.)
- The braid to use: **the same navy that decides whether Washington can ever trap a British army is, in any given month, off Martinique chasing sugar islands** — every French appearance on the American coast is a loan against the Caribbean calendar (fleets ran north in hurricane season, roughly July–October). (HIGH as framing.)

## 4. D'ESTAING'S TWO INTERLUDES (one braided beat each; `french-alliance` owns the arc)

- **Newport, August 1778.** Vice Adm. the **comte d'Estaing** arrived from Toulon (July 1778) with twelve ships of the line — the alliance's first physical payoff. The first joint operation, against **Newport, Rhode Island**, collapsed: d'Estaing sailed out to fight Lord Howe's fleet, a **two-day storm (11–13 Aug)** scattered and damaged both fleets before they could engage, and d'Estaing took his ships to Boston for repairs over furious American protest, leaving Gen. John Sullivan's besieging army exposed — it fought a creditable rear-guard action (**Battle of Rhode Island, 29 Aug 1778**) and withdrew. Sullivan publicly insinuated desertion; the alliance's first test ended in bruised feelings and nothing gained. (HIGH; one beat.)
- **Savannah, October 1779.** D'Estaing came back north from the Caribbean with ~4,000 troops to retake **Savannah** with Gen. Benjamin Lincoln's southern army. After a siege and bombardment, the allied **assault of 9 October 1779** on the Spring Hill redoubt was a bloody repulse: allied casualties commonly given **~800–1,000+** (French and American together) against a few dozen British defenders' losses; **Casimir Pulaski**, the Polish cavalry commander, was mortally wounded; d'Estaing was wounded twice; the fleet sailed for France. One of the war's bloodiest failures — and it left Charleston uncovered for 1780. (HIGH on date/outcome/Pulaski/d'Estaing's wounds; casualty totals as a range — returns conflict.)
- Frame both as **interludes**: the alliance's promise twice visibly real (a French battle fleet on the American coast) and twice unredeemed — until 1781. No editorializing beyond that; `french-alliance` owns the arc. (Directive)

## 5. THE WAR GOES SOUTH (Dec 1778 – Aug 1780)

### Savannah falls — the southern strategy opens
- **29 December 1778**: Lt. Col. **Archibald Campbell**, detached from New York with ~3,000–3,500 men, took **Savannah, Georgia**, nearly bloodlessly — an enslaved man (recorded as Quamino "Quash" Dolly; MED on spelling) showed a path through the swamp that flanked the ~700–850 American defenders. Georgia's royal government was restored — the only state brought fully back under the crown. (HIGH on date/outcome; numbers as ranges.)
- **The Loyalist-manpower theory** — the strategy's engine, one beat (`civil-war-among-themselves` owns the society story): London believed the South was thick with Loyalists who would rise, hold reconquered ground behind a moving British army, and free regulars for the world war. The flaw — that "holding ground" meant protecting Loyalists from their neighbors forever — plays out over the next two years. (HIGH as the standard account of British strategy.)

### Charleston — the war's worst American defeat
- Clinton came south himself (sailing from New York late Dec 1779 with ~8,700 men, reinforced toward ~13,000–14,000) and besieged **Charleston** by land and sea. Gen. **Benjamin Lincoln** — sealed inside the city, the civil government pressing him to defend it — surrendered on **12 May 1780**: about **5,500 men** (sources range ~5,000–6,000; roughly 2,500–3,000 of them Continentals), plus the city, ships, and hundreds of guns — **the largest American surrender of the war** (often called the worst American capitulation until Bataan in 1942 — attribute as "often called"). (HIGH on the date and ~5,500 as the standard figure; give the range.)
- Clinton sailed back to New York (a French expedition was expected northward), leaving **Lord Cornwallis** ~8,300 men to hold and extend the conquest. Clinton's parting proclamation (**3 June 1780**) voided paroles and required active loyalty — converting neutrals into enemies at a stroke; one clause, large consequences. (HIGH)

### Waxhaws — one beat, the established framing
- **29 May 1780**, the Waxhaws settlements near the North Carolina line: Lt. Col. **Banastre Tarleton**'s British Legion (~270, mostly Loyalist provincials, after a 100-plus-mile forced ride) caught Col. **Abraham Buford**'s ~350–420 Virginia Continentals — the last organized American force in South Carolina. The surrender went wrong in the smoke: Tarleton's horse went down (he later wrote his men, believing him killed, fought with "a vindictive asperity not easily restrained"), and the Legion kept killing after resistance stopped — ~**113 killed, ~150 wounded** (most too mangled to move), ~50 prisoners, against a handful of Legion losses. Whether massacre-by-intent or command breakdown is genuinely debated among historians; **what is certain is the wound evidence (multiple saber and bayonet wounds on fallen men) and what the story became**: "Tarleton's quarter!" — kill them all — a backcountry battle cry that fueled the partisan war. One beat; don't adjudicate past "the killing went on after the white flag." (HIGH on date/outcome/casualty scale/propaganda career; intent DISPUTED — keep it open.)

### Camden — Gates's catastrophe
- Congress (over Washington's preference for Greene) sent **Horatio Gates**, the victor of Saratoga, south. Near **Camden, South Carolina**, before dawn on **16 August 1780**, his half-starved army (~3,000–4,000 present and fit, roughly two-thirds militia) collided with **Cornwallis** (~2,200). At first light the Virginia and North Carolina militia on the American left **broke at the first British volley and bayonet charge — many without firing**; the Continentals under **Maj. Gen. Johann de Kalb** (the Bavarian-born veteran who had come over with Lafayette) stood almost alone and were destroyed. De Kalb, unhorsed, fighting on foot, took some **eleven wounds** and died three days later (**19 Aug**). American losses are chaotic in the returns — commonly **~600–900 killed and wounded and ~1,000 captured**; the army effectively ceased to exist. (HIGH on date/shape/de Kalb; numbers as ranges.)
- **Gates's flight:** swept off in the militia rout, he rode to **Charlotte that night — about 60 miles (97 km)** — and reached **Hillsborough, NC, roughly 180 miles (290 km), in three days**. Hamilton's contemporary jeer is the documented voice for it (Quotes, #3). It ended Gates's career. (HIGH — contemporary-rooted figures.)
- **The ledger, stated plainly (no melodrama):** within three months the southern army surrendered at Charleston, its replacement was annihilated at Camden, South Carolina was garrisoned, and Georgia was royal again. (HIGH)

## 6. THE FRONTIER, BRAIDED (one beat each — `native-nations` OWNS the story; the spine places it in time)

- **1778, the valleys.** **Wyoming Valley, Pennsylvania (3 July 1778)**: Loyalist rangers under John Butler with Seneca and other Iroquois allies destroyed the valley's militia (~300+ killed) and burned the settlements; atrocity stories — some true, some embroidered — ran through the American press for months. **Cherry Valley, New York (11 November 1778)**: a Loyalist–Iroquois force (Walter Butler, Joseph Brant) overran the settlement; ~30 noncombatants were killed along with soldiers of the garrison. One beat each, placed in time; the full story — what the raids answered, who the nations were, what came next — belongs to `native-nations`. (HIGH on dates/scale; keep casualty detail coarse here.)
- **1779, the answer.** Washington sent **Maj. Gen. John Sullivan** with ~3,200–4,500 men into Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) country; in late summer 1779 the expedition burned **about 40 towns** with their orchards and stored winter corn (the one stand-up fight, Newtown, 29 Aug 1779). Washington's own orders are the documented voice: "the total destruction and devastation of their settlements" (Quotes, #2). Thousands of Haudenosaunee wintered as refugees at British Niagara through the Hard Winter; the frontier raids did not stop. **One beat; `native-nations` owns the story and its meaning.** (HIGH; "about 40" towns.)
- **Clark at Vincennes, February 1779** — one beat: **George Rogers Clark** marched ~170 men some 180 miles (290 km) across the flooded Illinois country in midwinter — days of wading shoulder-deep icewater — and retook **Vincennes (Fort Sackville), 23–25 February 1779**, capturing the British lieutenant governor **Henry Hamilton**. A tiny action with a long shadow (the American claim to the trans-Appalachian west in 1783 leaned on it). The tomahawk-execution scene outside the fort is documented but needs space this chapter doesn't have — omit here. (HIGH on dates/march/outcome.)

## 7. THE HOME FRONT NEARLY BREAKS (one beat each — `army-from-nothing` owns the institution story)

- **The money dies.** The Continental dollar, near par in 1776, fell to about **40 paper dollars to 1 in specie by early 1780** — Congress itself ratified the collapse on **18 March 1780**, officially revaluing at **40:1** (writing off roughly $200 million at face); by 1781 the paper passed at 100+:1 and then stopped passing at all ("not worth a Continental"). The documented voice is Washington's: "a waggon load of money will scarcely purchase a waggon load of provision" (Quotes, #1). The mechanism in one sentence: Congress had no power to tax, so it printed; the states printed more; the army was paid in paper that melted in soldiers' pockets. (HIGH on 40:1 and 18 March 1780; the depreciation arc HIGH in shape, monthly rates vary by table — ranges.)
- **Morristown, 1779–80 — the worst winter of the war.** The main army's encampment at **Jockey Hollow, Morristown, New Jersey** (Dec 1779–June 1780) sat through the **"Hard Winter"** — by the standard accounts the coldest winter in recorded 18th-century northeastern America, with **more than two dozen snowstorms** and salt water frozen so hard that **New York Harbor could be crossed on the ice with heavy loads**. Supply failed for weeks at a time (snow-buried roads plus dead currency); by Washington's account the soldiers at the worst of it ate "every kind of horse food but hay" (MED verbatim — widely quoted from his correspondence; re-verify exact source or paraphrase). Hungrier and colder than Valley Forge, and far less famous. One beat. (HIGH on encampment/severity/harbor ice; storm count MED — "more than two dozen.")
- **25 May 1780 — the Connecticut Line mutinies.** At dusk at Jockey Hollow, regiments of the Connecticut Line (the protest began with the 8th and spread) **paraded under arms without orders**, threatening to march home or take food at bayonet point — months unpaid, days without meat. Respected officers (Col. Return Jonathan Meigs among them) and Pennsylvania troops contained it; punishment was light because every officer knew the grievance was just. One beat — the institution bending, not yet breaking; the great mutinies of January 1781 belong to the NEXT chapter (do not narrate ahead). (HIGH)
- **Britain's home front cracks the same season — the Gordon Riots, 2–9 June 1780.** USABLE as one beat, verified, with the trigger stated honestly: London's worst riots of the century began as **Lord George Gordon's Protestant Association march** against the **Catholic Relief Act of 1778** (a measure easing anti-Catholic laws, partly to widen recruiting for the world war) and swelled into nearly a week of burning — Newgate Prison broken open and torched, Catholic chapels and houses destroyed, the Bank of England attacked — put down only by the army: commonly **~285 shot dead, ~200 wounded, ~20–30 later hanged** (phrase "around 300 killed," ranges). The braid: in the same weeks London celebrated Charleston, the capital of the empire was burning from the inside — the strain of a world war showing on BOTH home fronts. Do NOT sand the anti-Catholic trigger into generic "war-weariness": it was a religious riot, with war strain and economic distress underneath. (HIGH on dates/events; casualty figures MED — contemporary counts, ranges.)

## 8. SEPTEMBER 1780 — ARNOLD (scene scale; THE SPINE OWNS THIS STORY)

### The grievances, told straight (documented, no mustache-twirling)
- **Benedict Arnold by 1780**: arguably the cause's best fighting general — Ticonderoga, the Maine march, Valcour Island, Ridgefield, Saratoga twice (the second time storming forward against orders, his leg shattered at the moment of victory). And repeatedly burned: Congress promoted **five junior brigadiers to major general over him (Feb 1777)**; he kept resigning and being talked back into service. As military governor of Philadelphia (from June 1778) he lived far beyond his means, married **Peggy Shippen (8 April 1779** — she 18, he 38; her family neutral-to-Loyalist-leaning), was court-martialed over money-making schemes, and in **April 1780** received Washington's formal **reprimand** — mild in wording, wounding to Arnold. Deeply in debt, convinced the Revolution had fallen to smaller men, and professing (his stated reason) outrage at the French alliance. All documented; present them as HIS ledger of grievances without endorsing the arithmetic. (HIGH)
- **The correspondence:** Arnold opened a ciphered channel to the British in **May 1779**, through **Maj. John André** — Clinton's adjutant general and intelligence chief, who had known Peggy Shippen socially from the Philadelphia occupation — and haggled for sixteen months over price and product. **Peggy Shippen at evidence level:** correspondence moved partly through her circle and her hands; after the war the crown paid her a **£350 annual pension**; contemporaries (Washington and Hamilton included) believed her wholly innocent. How much she initiated or steered is still argued — scholars split on whether her famous fit on exposure day (below) was breakdown or performance. State the documented conduit + pension + contemporaries' belief, and stop. (HIGH on channel/dates/pension; her agency DISPUTED.)
- **The price:** Arnold asked **£20,000** for West Point and its garrison; Clinton's side committed to a fraction of that if the plan failed. What he ultimately got: **£6,315 plus an annual pension of about £360**, a **brigadier general's commission** in the British army, and later pensions/commissions for his wife and sons. (HIGH on £6,315/commission/£360-scale pension; MED on the precise failure-guarantee — phrase as "he had asked for £20,000.")

### The plot and the unraveling (the documented clock)
- **3 August 1780**: Arnold — who had angled for it for months while pleading his ruined leg — received command of **West Point**, the Hudson chokepoint fortress. He then quietly ran it down (garrison dispersed, repairs neglected, the great river chain's weak links left weak) while feeding André its particulars. (HIGH)
- **Night of 21–22 September**: André came up the Hudson aboard the sloop-of-war **HMS Vulture** and met Arnold on the west bank below Stony Point — the only face-to-face meeting; the talk ran past dawn. On the morning of the 22nd, American shore guns **drove the Vulture downriver**, stranding André, who had to return overland: **in civilian clothes, under the name "John Anderson," with six papers in Arnold's handwriting hidden in his stockings beneath his boots** and Arnold's pass in his pocket. Each choice — clothes, alias, papers — converted a uniformed staff officer into a spy under the laws of war. (HIGH; "stockings under the boots" is the testimony — "in his boot" is the loose tradition.)
- **23 September, ~9 a.m., near Tarrytown**: three armed Westchester militiamen — **John Paulding, Isaac Van Wart, David Williams** — stopped him. André fatally misread them (Paulding wore a Hessian-style coat he had escaped British custody in) and declared himself a British officer; searched to the boots, the papers surfaced. By the participants' later accounts he offered his horse, watch, and money to go free; **they refused and brought him in.** The duty officer they delivered him to, in the affair's black-comic beat, **sent word to ARNOLD that "John Anderson" was taken**; only Maj. Benjamin Tallmadge's suspicion kept the prisoner held, and the captured papers went separately toward Washington, then returning from a Hartford conference. Congress voted the three captors medals and pensions; whether they were patriot sentinels or road bandits who would have robbed anyone was argued even then — one honest clause, don't resolve it. (HIGH on names/date/sequence/medals; the captors' motives DISPUTED.)
- **25 September, morning, the Beverley Robinson house (Arnold's HQ opposite West Point):** the letter announcing André's capture reached Arnold **at breakfast, with Washington's aides already at the table** and Washington himself due within the hour. Arnold went upstairs, told Peggy, came down, rode to the river, and had his barge crew row him downriver **to the Vulture** — clearing the net by something like an hour. Washington arrived, toured West Point puzzled by its disarray, and understood only that afternoon when the papers caught up with him. That same day, from the Vulture, Arnold wrote Washington asking protection for Peggy: "the same principle of Love to my Country Actuates my present Conduct" (Quotes, #4 — his self-justification in his own hand, the best answer to any temptation to psychologize). (HIGH — the hour-scale near-miss is documented and IS the scene.)
- **"Whom can we trust now?" — the documentation chain, stated honestly:** the famous outburst ("Arnold has betrayed us! Whom can we trust now?") descends from **Lafayette's recollection, recorded much later** and canonized by 19th–20th-century biographers (Flexner's telling is the modern vector). **Hamilton, writing that same week, described Washington receiving the news with outward composure.** Use the line ONLY as "by Lafayette's later account," and let the contemporary letters carry the documented temperature. (MED on the quote as verbatim — attribution mandatory; HIGH that the chain is late-recollection.)
- **Peggy's scene:** while Washington was in the house, Peggy Shippen Arnold raved — a hot iron on her head, that Washington meant to kill her baby. Hamilton and Washington, fully persuaded of her innocence, treated her with elaborate gentleness and passed her through the lines to Philadelphia. Whether breakdown or theater: evidence-level only (see above). (HIGH that the scene occurred and persuaded them; her interior state DISPUTED.)

### André's end — and the mourning on both sides
- A board of **fourteen general officers** (Greene presiding; Lafayette and Steuben among the members) examined André at **Tappan, New York, 29 September**. On his own candid written account — no coercion; he conceded the clothes and the alias — it found him **a spy, to suffer death**. Clinton's negotiation failed: Washington's unstated but unmistakable price was **Arnold for André**, which Clinton could not pay (no defector would ever come over again). André petitioned to be **shot as a soldier rather than hanged as a spy**; the request was refused — hanging was the legal death of a spy, and to shoot him conceded he wasn't one — and by most accounts Washington never answered the letter, sparing him the written no. (HIGH)
- **2 October 1780, Tappan:** André walked out composed and recoiled only at the sight of the gibbet — he had hoped to the last for the firing squad ("I am reconciled to my death, but I detest the mode," as the eyewitness accounts render it); he helped fix the noose himself and asked the crowd to **"bear me witness that I meet my fate like a brave man"** (per eyewitnesses incl. the army surgeon James Thacher — variants exist; attribute). American officers who had guarded him, Tallmadge above all, wrote of him with open grief; Hamilton: **"never perhaps did any man suffer death with more justice, or deserve it less"** (Quotes, #5). **The mourning was real on both sides — and it is also where the romanticizing began** (the gallant doomed officer against the gross traitor who lived). Report the mourning as fact; do NOT join it: André was a professional intelligence officer running the war's biggest betrayal, and the board's law was sound. Britain put a monument in **Westminster Abbey** and entombed his remains there in **1821**; Arnold got honors from no one. (HIGH on the scene/verdict/Abbey; the quoted lines MED-HIGH — eyewitness variants, attribute.)
- **The self-portrait:** on **1 October 1780**, the day before he hanged, André — a genuinely gifted amateur artist — drew himself in **pen and ink, seated at a table, composed**; the sketch survives at the **Yale University Art Gallery** (acc. 1832.103). THE image for this section (Images, #1). (HIGH — Yale holds it.)
- **What it amounted to:** West Point never changed hands; the plot's exposure set off a spasm of loyalty-testing politics; and the Revolution acquired its permanent synonym for traitor. Arnold's British-uniform war (Virginia, New London) is the NEXT chapter — don't narrate ahead. (HIGH)

## 9. OCTOBER 7, 1780 — KINGS MOUNTAIN (strategy scale; `civil-war-among-themselves` owns the story)

- Cornwallis pushed into North Carolina (Charlotte, late September); screening his left was Maj. **Patrick Ferguson** with ~**1,000–1,100 Loyalist provincials and militia** — **Ferguson the only British-born man on the field; every other man, both sides, was American.** Ferguson's proclamation-threat to march over the mountains and lay waste the settlements with "fire and sword" summoned exactly what it threatened: ~**900–1,000 "over-mountain men"** (frontier riflemen from today's Tennessee, Virginia, and the Carolinas) ran him down at **Kings Mountain, South Carolina, 7 October 1780**. About an hour of fighting up the wooded slopes; Ferguson shot dead; his command annihilated — commonly **~157 killed, ~163 badly wounded (left on the field), ~698 captured**, against ~28 Patriot dead and ~62 wounded. "Tarleton's quarter!" was shouted as some surrendering Loyalists were shot down, and nine prisoners were hanged days later — Waxhaws coming home. (HIGH on date/Ferguson/all-American composition/outcome; casualty sets vary across good sources — use the noted figures as "commonly given"; the killing-after-surrender attested.)
- **Strategy scale — why it's the turn:** Cornwallis halted his invasion of North Carolina and fell back to winter in South Carolina; **the Loyalist-manpower theory (§5) died on that hill** — backcountry Loyalists never again rallied in force to a British column. Clinton later called the battle "the first link of a chain of evils" ending in "the total loss of America" (his postwar narrative — MED verbatim; paraphrase or re-verify). The STORY — neighbors killing neighbors by name — belongs to `civil-war-among-themselves`; the spine takes the hinge only. (HIGH on the strategic consequence as the standard account.)

## 10. CLOSE — END OF 1780: THE LOWEST POINT, WITH THE TURNAROUND INVISIBLY IN PLACE

- The honest ledger, no violins: the money at 40:1 and falling; the main army unpaid and already once in mutiny; the southern army destroyed twice in one summer; Georgia royal, South Carolina garrisoned; the alliance two failed expeditions old; the hero of Saratoga a British brigadier general recruiting a Loyalist legion in New York. Let the participants state it — Washington, that May, to his old aide Joseph Reed: "we have almost ceased to hope" (Quotes, #6) — and stop. (HIGH)
- **The pieces already on the board** (the reader can see them; almost nobody then could):
  - **Rochambeau at Newport — 10–11 July 1780**: the **comte de Rochambeau** landed ~**5,500 French regulars** at Newport, Rhode Island, with Adm. de Ternay's squadron — a French ARMY wintering on American soil, blockaded and idle for now. (HIGH)
  - **Greene goes south** — this time Washington's own choice (Congress, chastened by Camden, let him name the man): **Nathanael Greene**, appointed **14 October 1780**, took command of the southern remnant at **Charlotte on 2–3 December 1780** — roughly 2,300 men on the rolls, ~1,500 fit for duty. What he does with them is the NEXT chapter. (HIGH)
  - Kings Mountain has already broken the southern theory; Daniel Morgan is with Greene (one clause at most — Cowpens is January 1781, next chapter; don't narrate ahead). (Directive)
- Close on the freeze-frame: a frozen French army at Newport, a beaten remnant mustering at Charlotte under a new general, a traitor in a red coat in New York, the money dead in men's pockets — the war visibly lost at home, and already turning where no one could see it. (Directive)

2. **Washington's instructions to Maj. Gen. 3. **Alexander Hamilton to James Duane, 6 September 1780** (on Gates after Camden): the passage mocking "a General running away … from his whole army," the flight of "one hundred and eighty miles in three days and a half," and its "admirable credit to the activity of a man at his time of life." 4. **Benedict Arnold to George Washington, from HMS Vulture, 25 September 1780**: "I have ever acted from a Principle of Love to my Country, since the Commencement of the present unhappy Contest between Great Britain and the Colonies, the same principle of Love to my Country Actuates my present Conduct." 5. **Alexander Hamilton to Lt. Col. John Laurens, 11 October 1780** (on André): "Never perhaps did any man suffer death with more justice, or deserve it less." (HIGH — founders.archives.gov, Hamilton Papers 01-02-02-0896.)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **Charles Lee's partial rehabilitation — present BOTH halves.** Modern scholarship (Lender & Stone's *Fatal Sunday* is the benchmark) holds that Lee's retreat at Monmouth was a defensible, possibly army-saving decision by an exposed advance force, and that his conviction was substantially political; it equally holds that his post-battle letters were genuinely insubordinate and self-destroying. Never write the old coward-or-traitor Lee; never write him a simple martyr. And Washington at the meeting: furious, controlled, almost certainly NOT the leaf-shaking profanity legend (that account traces to Charles Scott, who wasn't there) — he did not order Lee off the field.
2. **NO Molly Pitcher at Monmouth.** The women theme owns the water-carrier legend and the composite figure. One pointing clause maximum.
3. **Waxhaws — keep the intent question open.** Massacre-by-order vs. command breakdown after Tarleton's horse fell is genuinely debated (JAR's "Debating Waxhaws" lays out the spread). Certain: the killing continued after resistance stopped; the wound evidence; Tarleton's own "vindictive asperity" sentence; and the propaganda career ("Tarleton's quarter") that did as much strategic damage to Britain as a lost battle. Tell the certain parts; don't adjudicate the rest.
4. **The Gordon Riots are usable but must be told honestly:** an anti-Catholic riot (against the Catholic Relief Act of 1778, itself a war-manpower measure) with war strain and economic distress underneath — NOT generic "war-weariness." Casualties ~285 shot dead plus executions afterward — give as "around 300," ranges. The braid (London burning the month Charleston's fall arrived) is legitimate and documented.
5. **Arnold documented, never psychologized.** His grievances (the 1777 supersession, the reprimand, the debts) are in the record and get told straight; his stated motive is in his own Vulture letter (Quote #4). No interior monologue, no mustache-twirling, no "always a traitor at heart" teleology — the same man WAS the hero of Saratoga; that's the story's whole weight.
6. **"Whom can we trust now?" — attribution mandatory.** The line descends from Lafayette's much-later recollection via 19th–20th-century biography (Flexner's vector popularized it); Hamilton's same-week letters describe Washington's outward composure. Use only as "by Lafayette's later account," with the composure note nearby. Never present as a verbatim contemporary record.
7. **Peggy Shippen at evidence level.** Documented: the correspondence channel through her circle and hands; the later £350 crown pension; contemporaries' total belief in her innocence; the exposure-day fit that persuaded Washington and Hamilton. Disputed: how much she knew, initiated, steered; whether the fit was breakdown or performance. State the documented, name the dispute, stop.
8. **André — report the mourning, don't join it.** The grief of his American captors/guards is documented and belongs in the chapter; the gallant-doomed-officer romance (already running in 1780, institutionalized by the Abbey monument) is a historiographical fact to be NAMED, not a register to write in. The board's verdict was sound law and should read as such.
9. **No nadir-melodrama at the close.** End-of-1780 is genuinely the war's lowest point — let the ledger and Quote #6 carry it. No "all seemed lost" voice-over; and the turnaround pieces (Rochambeau, Greene, Morgan) are placed without narrating ahead to Cowpens/Yorktown.
10. **Casualty/strength figures as ranges, always:** Monmouth ~360/side (heat deaths inside both); Savannah assault allies ~800–1,000+; Charleston surrendered ~5,000–6,000 (standard "about 5,500"); Waxhaws ~113 killed/~150 wounded; Camden ~600–900 + ~1,000 captured; Kings Mountain Loyalists ~157/~163/~698 ("commonly given"); Gordon Riots ~285 shot + executions; Rochambeau ~5,500. Where sources differ, the pack's range governs — never invent precision.
11. **Anachronism/legend bans:** "Molly Pitcher" as a person in this chapter; the leaf-shaking Washington profanity; "not worth a Continental" presented as a 1780 coinage (it's fine as the proverbial summary, not as a dated quote); André's "papers in his boot" stated loosely (testimony: in his stockings, under the boots); Clinton's "first link in a chain of evils" quoted verbatim without re-verification.
12. **Theme-ownership discipline:** Sullivan/Wyoming/Cherry Valley = one beat each, placed in time, `native-nations` owns the story (including the Haudenosaunee perspective and aftermath — do NOT compress it into a victory or an atrocity headline here); the Loyalist-war society story = `civil-war-among-themselves`; the alliance arc = `french-alliance`; mutiny/pay/supply = `army-from-nothing`.
