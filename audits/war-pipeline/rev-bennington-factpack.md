# FACT PACK — Battle of Bennington, August 16, 1777

*American Revolution (Saratoga campaign). ONLY source for the battle page. Two linked fights on the same afternoon; battlefield actually in NEW YORK (Walloomsac), not Bennington, Vermont. Stark is a RETURNING character (Bunker Hill page carries his rail-fence bio + /war-img/rev-bunkerhill-stark.jpg portrait).*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- [HIGH] Date: August 16, 1777 (afternoon–dusk; two distinct engagements). Preliminaries Aug 14 (first contact at Van Schaick's/Sancoick mill, bridge destroyed) and Aug 15 (all-day rain, no fighting).
- [HIGH] Location: along the Walloomsac River near the hamlet of Walloomsac, Rensselaer County, NEW YORK — about 10 miles NW of Bennington, Vermont (the supply depot Baum was marching for). Main hilltop fight at ~42.9386 N, -73.3044 W. The battle is named for its objective, not its ground; the actual field is ~2.5 mi west of the NY–VT line. Battlefield = Bennington Battlefield State Historic Site (NY), National Historic Landmark 1961.
- [HIGH] Significance: a detached wing of Burgoyne's invasion army (~800 under Lt. Col. Friedrich Baum, then a ~550–650-man relief column under Lt. Col. Heinrich von Breymann) was destroyed by New England militia under Brig. Gen. John Stark, with Seth Warner's Green Mountain Boys finishing the second fight. Burgoyne lost ~900–1,000 men he could not replace, plus 4 cannon; most of his Native allies left him afterward. Directly shortened the road to Saratoga (surrender Oct 17, 1777).
- [HIGH] Winner line: decisive American victory — militia, not Continentals, destroying regular troops in open battle.
- [HIGH] August 16 is still a legal holiday in Vermont (Bennington Battle Day).

## 2. BACKGROUND — WHY BURGOYNE SENT THE EXPEDITION
- [HIGH] By late July 1777 Burgoyne's army (advancing from Canada via Ticonderoga toward Albany) had outrun its supply line: ~150 miles of wilderness back to Canada. His deputy quartermaster Capt. John Money estimated that under the best conditions only ~4 days' provisions could be brought forward to Fort Edward at a time (as little as 1 day's worth at worst). Moving the army's depots forward 13 miles from Fort Edward toward Saratoga was estimated at ~15 days of hauling. The army was short of carts, draft animals, and bateaux. (JAR, "Bennington Fatally Delays Burgoyne.")
- [HIGH] The Brunswick dragoon regiment (Prinz Ludwig) with the army had NO horses — heavy cavalrymen marching on foot in their riding boots. Mounting them was an explicit goal of the raid, alongside seizing draft horses, cattle, carts, and flour.
- [HIGH] Burgoyne's own words on the goal (letter to Lord George Germain, Aug 20, 1777): "The possession of the cattle and carriages would certainly have enabled the army to leave their distant magazines, and to have acted with energy and dispatch."
- [MED] The original plan (associated with Riedesel, the Brunswick division commander) aimed at the Connecticut River valley for horses; Burgoyne redirected the strike at Bennington after intelligence that the depot there was lightly held — reportedly only ~400 demoralized militia. The intelligence was wrong.
- [HIGH] The target: the Continental supply depot at Bennington in the Hampshire Grants (present Vermont) — corn, flour, cattle, horses gathered for the American army.
- [HIGH] The intelligence failure had a second layer: Baum was told the countryside was thick with friendly Loyalists who would rise and join him. Locals in arms did stream toward his column — many were Stark's scouts and militia pretending to be Loyalists, and the real strength at Bennington was Stark's ~1,500 New Hampshire militia (raised in roughly a week in July), not 400 dispirited men.

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS

### American
- [HIGH] Brig. Gen. **John Stark** (New Hampshire militia), ~2,000–2,500 engaged across the day: his ~1,500 New Hampshire militia brigade, plus Vermont/"Grants" rangers under Col. Samuel Herrick, Massachusetts (Berkshire) militia who arrived the night of Aug 15 (with the fighting parson Thomas Allen), and some Stockbridge Mohican allies. Col. **Seth Warner's** Continental regiment (the "Green Mountain Boys," ~330–350) marched from Manchester and decided the second fight.
- [HIGH] STARK BIO MATERIAL (returning character — Bunker Hill page bio exists; bio here should pick up the commission grievance): b. 1728, Londonderry, NH. French and Indian War veteran of Rogers' Rangers; at Bunker Hill (June 17, 1775) his New Hampshire regiment held the rail fence and Mystic beach and wrecked the British flank attack. In early 1777 the Continental Congress promoted junior officers over him to brigadier general; Stark resigned his Continental colonelcy (March 1777) and went home. When New Hampshire raised a militia brigade in July 1777 he took command ONLY on the condition that he answer to New Hampshire alone, not to Congress or Continental officers — which is why, days before the battle, he refused orders to march his brigade to the Hudson and instead stayed to cover Bennington. Congress made him a Continental brigadier general on Oct 4, 1777, after the victory. Died 1822, the last surviving Revolutionary general officer of his rank-cohort [verify phrasing — see Uncertainties]; his 1809 toast gave New Hampshire "Live free or die."
- [HIGH] WARNER BIO MATERIAL: Seth Warner, b. 1743, Roxbury, Connecticut; moved to the Grants (Bennington) 1763. A leader of the original Green Mountain Boys (the land-dispute militia against New York authority); helped take Ticonderoga May 1775; captured Crown Point; elected over Ethan Allen to command the Continental regiment raised in the Grants. Fought the rearguard action at Hubbardton (July 7, 1777). At Bennington his ~330-man regiment arrived from Manchester in time to stop Breymann's relief column; Warner himself was on the field with Stark earlier in the day [MED — see Uncertainties]. Health broke during the war; died 1784, age 41.

### British / German
- [HIGH] Lt. Col. **Friedrich Baum**, commander of the Brunswick dragoon regiment Prinz Ludwig — led the expedition. Spoke no English [MED — standard account]. Mortally wounded in the first fight; died two days later (Aug 18) [MED on exact day]. NO verified portrait exists (see Images).
- [HIGH] Baum's polyglot column, ~800 at the start (grew with Loyalist joiners): ~170–205 dismounted Brunswick dragoons (the men the horses were FOR, marching in heavy boots with broadswords); ~50 German light infantry (von Barner's) and a handful of jägers; small drafts of Brunswick/Hesse-Hanau musketeers and grenadiers; ~48 British select marksmen (Capt. Fraser's company); Hesse-Hanau artillerymen with 2 light 3-pounder cannon; ~150+ Loyalists (incl. Queen's Loyal Rangers under Lt. Col. John Peters); ~50–60 Canadians; ~100 Native warriors. Roughly half the column did not speak English; orders moved through translation.
- [HIGH] Lt. Col. **Heinrich von Breymann**, commander of the Brunswick grenadier/light battalion advanced corps — led the relief column: ~550–650 Brunswick grenadiers and light infantry with 2 six-pounder cannon. March was notoriously slow (~25 miles in ~30+ hours through Aug 15 rain and mud, ammunition wagons bogging, by some accounts pausing to dress ranks). Killed seven weeks later at Bemis Heights (Oct 7, 1777) defending the redoubt that bears his name; a persistent account says one of his own men shot him after he sabered soldiers to hold them in line [DISPUTED — tag if used]. NO verified portrait exists (see Images).
- [HIGH] Burgoyne context: Lt. Gen. John Burgoyne commanded the ~7,500–8,000-man invasion army on the Hudson; Maj. Gen. Friedrich Adolf Riedesel commanded its Brunswick (German) division and had proposed the horse-raid concept. Neither was on the Bennington field.

## 4. THE BATTLE

### The approach (Aug 9–15)
- [HIGH] Baum left the army's camp on the Hudson (Fort Miller area, below Fort Edward) around Aug 9–11 and marched east via Cambridge, NY. On Aug 14 at Van Schaick's (Sancoick) mill on a branch of the Walloomsac his advance bumped Stark's scouting parties, who fired, fell back, and broke down the bridge. Realizing the force ahead was far bigger than 400 demoralized militia, Baum sent to Burgoyne for reinforcements — but kept advancing, then dug in to wait.
- [HIGH] Aug 15: all-day heavy rain. No fighting. Baum entrenched: the elite of his force (dismounted dragoons + British marksmen, with one cannon) built a redoubt on a steep hill ~300 ft above the Walloomsac bridge (the "dragoon redoubt," now Hessian Hill at the NY state historic site); Loyalists built a breastwork on the other side of the river covering the bridge (the "Tory redoubt"); Canadians, Natives, and German detachments held scattered posts between. The position was spread thin — strongpoints too far apart to support each other [HIGH — standard analysis].
- [HIGH] The same rain slowed Breymann's relief column to a crawl: ~550–650 men with two 6-pounders made roughly 25 miles in over a day and a half on mud roads, ammunition carts bogging and overturning. [MED] Accounts add halts to dress ranks; use carefully — the rain and the guns are the verified causes.
- [MED, HANDLE-WITH-CARE] During the lull, locals presenting themselves as Loyalists came into Baum's lines and were taken at face value; many were Stark's men reconnoitering. The often-told detail that militiamen wore slips of white paper in their hats (the Loyalist field sign) to pass as friends and even take posts near the redoubts is a tradition repeated in standard accounts (it appears in Wikipedia and much battle literature) — treat as tradition, not documented fact.

### First fight (~3:00 pm – ~5:00 pm)
- [HIGH] Aug 16 dawned clear. Stark spent the morning positioning a double envelopment: Col. Moses Nichols with ~200–250 New Hampshire men swung wide around Baum's left/rear; Col. Samuel Herrick with ~300 Vermont rangers and Bennington militia circled to the right/rear; Cols. David Hobart and Thomas Stickney (~200–300) took the Tory breastwork frontage; Stark led the center reserve up the front. Militiamen moved through woods in shirtsleeves in the August heat [MED — widely repeated descriptive detail].
- [MED, HANDLE-WITH-CARE] Baum reportedly read the encircling parties as the promised Loyalist countryside rising to join him, and let them get into position — the cleanest single statement of the intelligence failure. Standard in the literature; no Baum document survives saying it (he left no account; he was dead in two days).
- [HIGH] The attack opened around 3:00 pm, signaled by Nichols's fire from the rear. Stark's own report: the action "lasted 2 hours the hottest I ever saw in my life." The Native warriors and Canadians broke out and fled early (the Natives between the closing pincers); the Tory breastwork was stormed; every post collapsed inward onto the dragoon redoubt.
- [HIGH] The dragoons held the hilltop until their ammunition gave out — their reserve ammunition wagon blew up / was lost [MED on the exact mechanism; "ammunition wagon caught fire/exploded" is the standard account]. Out of cartridges, Baum's dragoons drew their heavy straight broadswords and tried to cut their way out downhill; the charge was shot apart at close range. Baum fell mortally wounded (shot through the body); his survivors surrendered. He died two days later [MED on exact day — Aug 18 in most accounts] and was buried near the field; the grave site is unknown.

### Second fight (~4:30–5:00 pm to dusk)
- [HIGH] As Stark's militia scattered to guard ~600+ prisoners and to plunder the camp, Breymann's relief column finally arrived from the west (it had passed Sancoick mill mid-afternoon, ~4:30 pm by most reckonings) and ran into the disordered Americans. Stark's line was pushed back; for a stretch the day hung in the balance.
- [HIGH] Seth Warner's Continental regiment (~330–350, the Green Mountain Boys), marching from Manchester (some accounts: after a pause to dry muskets/powder from the storm), came up at the critical moment and counterattacked alongside Stark's rallied militia. Fighting "continued obstinate on both sides till sunset" (Stark). At dark Breymann broke off, abandoning both his cannon, his wounded, and most of his baggage; he was himself wounded [MED — leg wounds in standard accounts] and escaped with roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of his men only because night ended the pursuit.
- [HIGH] Stark on the near-run ending: had day lasted an hour longer, he reckoned, he would have taken the whole force. [Paraphrase of his report — see Quotes for exact verified lines; do not invent wording.]
- [HIGH] Americans captured 4 cannon total (Baum's 2 three-pounders + Breymann's 2 six-pounders), ~700 prisoners, hundreds of muskets, swords, and drums. One captured 3-pounder, the "Molly Stark cannon," survives at New Boston, NH and is still fired ceremonially [MED on its claims; the gun's identity is local tradition with documentation — HANDLE-WITH-CARE if used].

## 5. CASUALTIES
- [HIGH] American: ~70 total — commonly given as ~30 killed, ~40 wounded (Stark's report's order of magnitude; some accounts 40 killed/30 wounded). Use "about 70" or "roughly 30 killed and 40 wounded."
- [HIGH] British/German/Loyalist/Native (both fights combined): ~200–230 killed and wounded left on the field plus ~700 captured ≈ ~900–1,000 total lost to Burgoyne. American Battlefield Trust: 907 (200+ killed/seriously wounded; 700+ prisoners or missing). Wikipedia: 207 killed, ~700 captured. JAR: "nearly 900 men and all four cannon." Give as ranges; the ~700 prisoners figure is the firmest single number.
- [HIGH] Among the dead: Baum (mortally wounded, died of wounds ~Aug 18). The captured included most of the surviving Brunswick dragoons — the regiment effectively ceased to exist as a field unit.
- [MED] Loyalist prisoners were treated as traitors rather than POWs — marched to Bennington (tradition says tied in pairs, driven like cattle [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — local tradition]); German prisoners were marched through Bennington and on into Massachusetts captivity. Many Brunswick prisoners never went home — a substantial number eventually stayed/settled in America after the war [MED — standard for the Brunswick contingent as a whole; don't over-specify Bennington's share].

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- [HIGH] Burgoyne lost ~900–1,000 men (≈1 in 7 of his field force), 4 cannon, and got NOTHING — no horses, no cattle, no flour, no carts. The dragoons still had no horses; the army still had to drag its supplies forward. The "energy and dispatch" he said the captured cattle and carriages would have bought (his Aug 20 letter) never came.
- [HIGH] The delay was the price: the army halted weeks at the Hudson reorganizing and accumulating ~30 days' provisions; it did not cross the Hudson until Sept 13. In those weeks Gates replaced Schuyler, Morgan's riflemen and thousands of militia joined the American army, and the Bemis Heights fortifications went up. Bennington materially shortened the road to Saratoga (Burgoyne surrendered Oct 17, exactly 2 months after the battle).
- [HIGH] Most of Burgoyne's Native allies abandoned the expedition after Bennington (their confidence in the campaign broken; Burgoyne's Aug 20 letter to Germain is sour on his Native and Loyalist support generally). His intelligence screen and foraging reach shrank accordingly.
- [HIGH] The proof-of-concept effect: militia, raised in days, had destroyed two detachments of European regulars in the open field. The victory electrified New England recruiting at exactly the moment Burgoyne needed the countryside cowed; instead, in his own words, the Grants "hangs like a gathering storm upon my left."
- [HIGH] Stark's redemption arc closed: Congress, which had passed him over in February, voted him a Continental brigadier general's commission Oct 4, 1777.
- [HIGH] Memory: the 306-ft Bennington Battle Monument (completed 1889, dedicated 1891) stands at the DEPOT site in Old Bennington, VT — i.e., at the thing Baum never reached, not on the battlefield; the battlefield itself is the NY state historic site at Walloomsac. Statues of Stark and Warner stand near the monument. Aug 16 is Vermont's Bennington Battle Day holiday.

## 7. VERIFIED QUOTES
1. [HIGH] **Burgoyne to Lord George Germain, Aug 20, 1777**: "The Hampshire Grants in particular, a country unpeopled and almost unknown in the last war, now abounds in the most active and most rebellious race of the continent, and hangs like a gathering storm upon my left." (historycentral.com transcription of the letter; widely reproduced.)
2. [HIGH] **Same letter**: "Wherever the King's forces point, militia, to the amount of three or four thousand, assemble in twenty-four hours; they bring with them their subsistence, etc." — and on the supply problem: "In all parts the industry and management in driving cattle and removing corn are indefatigable and certain; and it becomes impracticable to move without portable magazines."
3. [HIGH] **Burgoyne (same letter), on what the raid was for**: "The possession of the cattle and carriages would certainly have enabled the army to leave their distant magazines, and to have acted with energy and dispatch." (Quoted in JAR, "Bennington Fatally Delays Burgoyne.")
4. [HIGH] **Stark to Gates, Aug 22, 1777 (his battle report)**: "The remainder of my little army I push up in the front, and in a few minutes the action began. It lasted 2 hours the hottest I ever saw in my life. It represented one continued clap of thunder." (Champlain Valley NHP transcription; ABT paraphrases the same line.)
5. [HIGH] **Same report**: "The enemy was obliged to give way and leave their field pieces and all their baggage behind them." And on the second fight: "The battle continued obstinate on both sides till sunset."
6. [HIGH] **Stark on his commission grievance (1777)**: "an officer who would not stand up for his own rights ought not to stand for the rights of his country" (quoted by Champlain Valley NHP — reported speech in early biographies; tag as "he declared" not as a letter).
7. [DISPUTED — CENTERPIECE, tell it AS tradition] **The Molly Stark line.** No contemporary source records it. The earliest written reference is from 1819 — four decades after the battle — in the form: "My boys, you see those red coats yonder, they must fall into our hands in 15 minutes, or Molly is a widow." The famous polished versions are later and many: "There are your enemies, the Red Coats and the Tories. They are ours, or this night Molly Stark sleeps a widow!" / "There boys are our enemies; to-night they must be ours or Mollie Stark's a widow" / "Tonight the American flag floats from yonder hill, or Molly Stark sleeps a widow." Per the Molly Stark literature, there are so many variants "it is impossible to determine the original words spoken." THE PAGE MUST present this as tradition (e.g., "the story New England told ever after has Stark pointing at the redcoats and saying...") — never as a documented quotation. Molly = Elizabeth "Molly" Page Stark (1737–1814), his wife, who that summer was nursing smallpox patients with their home as a hospital [HIGH on her identity/dates].
8. [DISPUTED — DO NOT USE AS FACT] "They fought more like hell-hounds than soldiers" — floats around the Bennington literature attributed to Baum (who died without leaving an account) or to an anonymous British officer. Attribution chain not establishable; skip or flag hard.
9. [MED] Stark is widely quoted summarizing the day as a militia triumph in his report to the New Hampshire authorities (a separate Aug 18 letter to the NH Committee of Safety exists). If the author wants a second Stark line beyond #4–5, verify wording against the printed Stark papers first — do NOT improvise.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. **The Molly Stark quote is TRADITION, not record** — earliest written version 1819 ("...or Molly is a widow"), polished variants multiply afterward; original words unrecoverable. Tell as legend, never quote as documented speech. (Centerpiece HANDLE-WITH-CARE.)
2. Casualty totals: Crown losses ~900–1,000 (ABT 907; Wikipedia 207 killed + ~700 captured; JAR "nearly 900") — use ranges, ~700 prisoners is firmest. American ~70 (killed/wounded split varies 30/40 vs 40/30).
3. Force sizes: Baum ~600–800 (grew with Loyalist joiners; JAR says 600 at departure, standard accounts ~800 engaged); Breymann ~550–650; Stark ~1,500 NH militia / ~2,000–2,500 total engaged; Warner ~330–350. Departure date also varies by source (left Fort Edward Aug 9, marched on Bennington Aug 11–13) — use "in the second week of August."
4. The paper-in-hat Loyalist ruse and "Baum mistook the encircling militia for friendly Loyalists" — standard accounts, but tradition-flavored; Baum left no record (dead Aug 18). HANDLE-WITH-CARE phrasing ("the story goes," "by most accounts").
5. Baum's death day (Aug 17 vs 18 — use "two days later"/"died of his wounds within two days"); his burial spot near the field is unmarked/unknown.
6. The ammunition-wagon fire/explosion that emptied the dragoons' cartridge boxes — standard but mechanically fuzzy; the saber breakout charge itself is solid in the literature.
7. Breymann's slow march: rain + mud + guns verified; the "halted to dress ranks" detail is an old characterization — use cautiously. His Bennington wounds (leg) MED; his death at Bemis Heights Oct 7 is HIGH but "shot by his own men" is DISPUTED.
8. Warner's personal whereabouts during the first fight (with Stark on the field vs only arriving with his regiment) — accounts differ; safest: his REGIMENT arrived from Manchester for the second fight, Warner himself advised Stark during the day [MED].
9. "Hell-hounds" quote — attribution unestablishable; skip.
10. Stockbridge Mohican presence with Stark [MED]; ~100 Natives with Baum [MED — round number].
11. The Bennington Flag ("76" flag) did NOT fly at the battle (long-standing misconception — the flag is 19th-century); if the page mentions a flag, it must be to debunk.
12. The "Molly Stark cannon" at New Boston, NH — captured-at-Bennington identity is local tradition with documentation; HANDLE-WITH-CARE.
13. Stark's NH brigade "raised in six days" (with John Langdon's funding pledge) — beloved story, broadly supported; keep the timeframe soft ("in about a week") and Langdon's speech is itself tradition-flavored.
14. Stark "last surviving general of the Revolution" claims at his 1822 death — phrasing varies (last general officer vs last of his grade); verify before using; the 1809 "Live free or die" toast is HIGH.
