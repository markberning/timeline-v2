# FACT PACK — "Women of the Revolution" (1775–1783)

*American Revolution vertical, war-years OFF-THE-BATTLEFIELD arc (route slug `women-of-the-revolution`). SOURCE-OF-TRUTH document, NOT reader-facing. Accuracy + completeness only; zero-hallucination bar absolute. Every date/number/name/quote below is verified against the cited sources (founders.archives.gov, Massachusetts Historical Society, Library of Congress, National Archives/NPS, Museum of the American Revolution, Journal of the American Revolution/allthingsliberty, Encyclopedia Virginia, John U. Rees's ration-return scholarship, Holly Mayer's *Belonging to the Army*); confidence tagged HIGH / MED / DISPUTED. Use ranges and "flag" guidance for contested specifics. This chapter OWNS the camp-follower topic (the army chapter deferred it). It does NOT own Black Americans' war broadly (rev-slavery does — Wheatley is one beat here, Dunmore nothing) or the Loyalist machinery broadly (rev-civil-war does — Grace Galloway is the woman-scale thread only). No battle narratives.*

**Orientation.** When the men marched off in 1775, the war arrived at the household. The legal frame waiting there was **coverture**: under the common law, a married woman had no separate legal existence — her property, earnings, and contracts belonged to her husband. That is the chapter's structural fact, and every story in it plays against it. Wives left behind became "deputy husbands," running farms, shops, and account books for years (Abigail Adams ran the family farm and finances for the better part of a decade and made the investments that preserved the Adams fortune). Poor women who could not survive at home alone followed the armies as laundresses, nurses, and sutlers — roughly one woman for every thirty Continental soldiers, far more with the British. A handful ended up at the guns (Margaret Corbin, documented, pensioned by Congress) or in the ranks (Deborah Sampson, seventeen months as "Robert Shurtliff"). Philadelphia's Ladies Association raised a staggering paper sum and was overruled by Washington into making shirts. Women wrote the propaganda (Mercy Otis Warren), printed the Declaration with a woman's name on it (Mary Katharine Goddard), carried intelligence (Molly Brant, for the British), and lost everything (Grace Galloway, evicted under her husband's attainder). And when it was over: a new ideology ("republican motherhood"), one accidental state-level experiment in women's voting (New Jersey, to 1807) — and coverture intact. The honest landing: the Revolution changed women's work and women's arguments far more than their legal condition.

## 1. THE WAR ARRIVES AT THE HOUSEHOLD — COVERTURE & "DEPUTY HUSBANDS"

- **Coverture — define precisely.** Under English common law (carried into every colony), marriage merged a woman's legal identity into her husband's. Blackstone's *Commentaries* (1765): **"By marriage, the husband and wife are one person in law: that is, the very being or legal existence of the woman is suspended during the marriage, or at least is incorporated and consolidated into that of the husband: under whose wing, protection, and cover, she performs every thing; and is therefore called in our law-French a feme-covert."** A married woman (*feme covert*) could not own property, keep her own wages, sign contracts, or sue in her own name; an unmarried woman or widow (*feme sole*) could. (HIGH — Blackstone quote verified.)
- **"Deputy husbands."** Historian Laurel Thatcher Ulrich's term (*Good Wives*, 1980/1982) for a long-standing colonial role: a wife acting as her husband's stand-in for the family's external affairs — bargaining, managing accounts, even legal matters — when he was absent. The role predates the war; the war made it near-universal in patriot households and stretched it over *years*, not seasons. Key nuance (Ulrich): the role shows premodern gender norms had a practical "elasticity" — nothing stopped a woman from driving a hard bargain — but it was a *deputy's* power, exercised in the husband's name, not her own. (HIGH on the term and meaning; attribute to Ulrich.)
- **What absence meant in practice:** running farms through harvests and epidemics, managing tenants and hired labor, paying wartime taxes in collapsing paper currency, keeping shops and presses open, deciding when to flee an approaching army. With husbands away, these decisions were made by women who, on paper, could not own the property they were managing. (HIGH — frame; the Abigail and Galloway sections instantiate it.)
- **Class is the chapter's other axis.** A merchant's or lawyer's wife "deputied" an estate; a propertyless soldier's wife had no estate to run — her option was the army's baggage train (§3). Name this plainly: Abigail Adams's war and a camp follower's war were different wars. (HIGH — framing per scholarship; not a single-source claim.)

## 2. ABIGAIL ADAMS — "REMEMBER THE LADIES"

- **The letter:** Abigail Adams to John Adams, **Braintree, 31 March 1776** (John was at the Continental Congress in Philadelphia). Verbatim, original spelling: **"I long to hear that you have declared an independancy — and by the way in the new Code of Laws which I suppose it will be necessary for you to make I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors. Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands. Remember all Men would be tyrants if they could. If perticuliar care and attention is not paid to the Laidies we are determined to foment a Rebelion, and will not hold ourselves bound by any Laws in which we have no voice, or Representation."** (HIGH — verified against the Adams Family Papers, Massachusetts Historical Society / Founders Online.)
- **John's reply, 14 April 1776** — dismissive, joking. Verbatim: **"As to your extraordinary Code of Laws, I cannot but laugh."** … **"Depend upon it, We know better than to repeal our Masculine systems. Altho they are in full Force, you know they are little more than Theory."** He adds that men "dare not exert our Power in its full Latitude," and that rather than give up the name of master — **"which would compleatly subject Us to the Despotism of the Peticoat"** — "I hope General Washington, and all our brave Heroes would fight." (HIGH — verified, MHS/Founders Online.)
- **What she was actually arguing — HANDLE WITH CARE.** Abigail's target is the **"unlimited power… of the Husbands"** — coverture's legal tyranny inside marriage (property, physical abuse, no legal recourse) — not the franchise. She nowhere asks for the vote. Do NOT inflate her into a proto-suffragist; the letter is radical enough as written, and its real force is that she aimed the Revolution's own logic ("no Laws in which we have no voice") at the household. (HIGH on the reading; this is the standard scholarly position.)
- **Her decade as deputy husband.** John was at Congress 1774–1777 and in Europe on diplomatic missions 1778–1784 (Abigail joined him abroad in 1784). Through those years she ran the Braintree farm and its tenants, raised and schooled the children through war and the 1775–76 epidemics, handled the money — and invested it, largely through her uncle **Cotton Tufts**, including in depreciated government securities that paid off handsomely after Hamilton's 1790 funding program. Economic historians credit her management with preserving and growing the Adams family fortune. (HIGH on the spine — absences, farm/finances, Tufts, securities; MED on any specific return figure — give none.)
- About **1,200 letters** between Abigail and John survive — the reason we can see this marriage and this war from the kitchen side at all. (HIGH)
- She signed many letters **"Portia"** (the persona she used in correspondence). "Mrs. Delegate" is a *framing* of her role while John sat in Congress — usable as the chapter's framing language, but do not present it as a documented contemporary nickname without a source. (HIGH on "Portia"; flag "Mrs. Delegate" as our framing, not a quote.)

## 3. CAMP FOLLOWERS — THE ARMY'S WORKING WOMEN (this chapter owns this topic)

- **Who they were.** Overwhelmingly **soldiers' wives, widows, and refugees** — poor women (often with children) who could not keep a household going alone once their man enlisted or died; also some hired nurses and sutlers (licensed vendors), plus some enslaved and indentured women brought along. The 1776 American Articles of War swept them into army jurisdiction as "all suttlers and retainers to a camp, and all persons whatsoever serving with the armies of the United States" — they *belonged to the army* (Holly Mayer's phrase and book title) and were subject to its discipline. (HIGH — Mayer, *Belonging to the Army*, 1996, the standard work; Encyclopedia Virginia.)
- **NOT prostitutes.** The old caricature is wrong and the scholarship is blunt about it: the women of the army were working members of the military community — laundresses, nurses, cooks, sutlers — mostly attached to specific soldiers and regiments. Prostitution existed at the margins of any 18th-century army, but it does not describe this population. Handle plainly, once. (HIGH — Mayer; standard scholarly consensus.)
- **The work:** **laundry** (the core trade — paid by the piece, and a disease-control function: lice/typhus), **nursing** (drafted or hired for hospital work), **cooking, mending, water-carrying, sutlery**. Unless formally employed as laundresses, nurses, or cooks, women got no pay — rations only. (HIGH)
- **Rations — verify-corrected policy.** Documented Continental practice (Rees, from ration returns): women drawing **one full ration** each, **children a half ration** (under the "late Regulations" phrasing in surviving returns). Allowances varied by period, commander, and unit — some orders put women at a half ration — so phrase as: *typically a full ration for a woman and half for a child, though allowances varied*. **Do NOT assert the half-for-women/quarter-for-children version as the standard policy** — the documented norm is fuller than that. (MED — returns are fragmentary; use the range language.)
- **Numbers — Continental.** John U. Rees (the standard count, from ration returns): on average about **one woman per thirty men, roughly 2–3% of army strength**, with the proportion drifting upward late in the war. Snapshots: three Virginia regiments at Middlebrook (spring 1779) averaged ~72 women each among them; about 50 women accompanied the (sampled) Yorktown-campaign units in 1781. Use "~3%" loosely with "one for every thirty men." (HIGH on the 2–3%/1:30 figure as Rees's; MED on the snapshots.)
- **Numbers — British (more).** The British army carried far more women: **Howe allowed six women per company** on campaign in 1776–77; an **August 1781 return for New York showed about one woman per 4.5 British soldiers** (1 per 15 for the German troops); roughly **2,000 women** trailed Burgoyne's army into captivity after Saratoga (Oct 1777). Safe comparative: *several times the American proportion*. (HIGH on Howe's 6/company and the 1781 NY return; MED on the Saratoga ~2,000.)
- **Washington's ambivalence — documented both ways.** He needed their work and resented their drag on the march:
  - General Orders, **4 August 1777**: **"the multitude of women in particular, especially those who are pregnant, or have children, are a clog upon every movement"** — commanders to "use every reasonable method in their power to get rid of all such as are not absolutely necessary." (HIGH — Founders Online, verified.)
  - Recurring orders that **women must not ride in the wagons** but march with the baggage — first issued July 1777 and repeated in some form every year through 1781; by **June 1781** Washington conceded some women would have to be permitted "to ride in waggons [or] walk in the ranks." Yet the women stayed, because the army could not function without the washing and nursing — and because turning them away meant destitution. (HIGH on the orders' existence and recurrence; the June 1781 concession quote MED — phrasing from Rees's transcriptions.)
- **Under fire by proximity.** Followers shared the army's marches, short rations, disease, and sometimes its battles — which is how women ended up serving guns at Fort Washington and Monmouth (§4). (HIGH — bridge.)

## 4. AT THE GUNS — MARGARET CORBIN (DOCUMENTED) vs "MOLLY PITCHER" (LEGEND)

### Margaret Corbin — the documented one
- **Margaret Cochran Corbin** (b. 1751, Pennsylvania frontier; her parents killed/captured in a 1756 raid — MED on the early-life details). Followed her husband **John Corbin**, a Pennsylvania artilleryman, to war. (HIGH on the spine.)
- **Fort Washington, northern Manhattan, 16 November 1776** (the British-Hessian assault that took the fort): John Corbin was killed serving his cannon; Margaret **stepped into his place at the gun** and served it until she was torn apart by grapeshot — wounds to her **left arm (nearly severed, permanently disabled), chest, and jaw**. (HIGH on killed-husband/took-his-place/grievously-wounded; MED on the exact wound list.)
- **First woman pensioned by the United States for military service.** Continental Congress, **6 July 1779**, resolved that Margaret Corbin, **"who was wounded and disabled in the attack on Fort Washington, whilst she heroically filled the post of her husband who was killed by her side serving a piece of artillery,"** receive **during her natural life one-half the monthly pay drawn by a soldier in the service of these states**, plus a suit of clothes (or its value in money). (HIGH — Journals of the Continental Congress; the "heroically filled the post of her husband" phrase verified via JAR. Note the honest sting: *half* a soldier's pay.)
- Afterward enrolled in the **Invalid Regiment** at West Point until the war's end; known around the post as **"Captain Molly"**; died near West Point ~1800, poor and difficult and largely forgotten. (HIGH on Invalid Regiment + "Captain Molly"; MED on death-year ~1800 and characterization.)
- **The grave postscript (optional, verified):** in **1926** the DAR exhumed what were believed to be her remains and reburied them with honors at West Point; a **2017** forensic study of the 1926 remains found they were **an unknown man's** — Corbin's actual resting place is unknown. The monument still stands. (HIGH — DAR and Army accounts of the 2017 finding.)

### "Molly Pitcher" — the myth-check (mandatory)
- **"Molly Pitcher" is a composite legend, not a documented person.** No contemporary record names any "Molly Pitcher." The legend — a woman carrying water at the **Battle of Monmouth (28 June 1778)** who takes her fallen husband's place at the cannon — crystallized in the 19th century out of multiple strands, Corbin's documented story ("Captain Molly") likely among them. (HIGH — Ray Raphael in JAR; Smithsonian; standard scholarship.)
- **The kernel that IS contemporary-adjacent:** Private **Joseph Plumb Martin's** memoir (published 1830, decades later) describes an unnamed woman serving a cannon with her husband at Monmouth: **"a cannon shot from the enemy passed directly between her legs without doing any other damage than carrying away all the lower part of her petticoat"** — and she carried on. A real woman (or women) almost certainly worked the Monmouth guns; the *name and the neat story* are the legend. (HIGH that Martin wrote it; the identification with any specific woman is unproven.)
- **Mary Ludwig Hays (McCauley)** (c. 1754–1832, Carlisle, Pennsylvania) is the woman the legend later attached to. What's documented: she was at Monmouth with her artilleryman husband William Hays; in **1822 the Pennsylvania legislature granted her an annual pension ($40) "for services rendered" during the war** — notably *not* naming Monmouth, a cannon, or any specific deed. Her 1832 obituaries praised her war service to the sick and wounded but said **nothing about a cannon or water-carrying**. The "Molly Pitcher" identification was attached to her **posthumously** (her son's 1856 obituary; the 1876 centennial grave-marking at Carlisle). (HIGH — Raphael, "Mary Hays McCauley's Claim to Fame," JAR 2013.)
- **Chapter handling:** tell **Corbin's documented story** as the real thing; badge "Molly Pitcher" explicitly as **legend** — useful as a window on how the 19th century preferred its Revolutionary women (one tidy heroine instead of thousands of working women). The Currier & Ives print is usable *as* legend-illustration, captioned as such. (Directive.)

## 5. DEBORAH SAMPSON — SEVENTEEN MONTHS IN THE RANKS

- **Deborah Sampson** (b. 17 December 1760, Plympton, Massachusetts; indentured servant in Middleborough until 18; then weaver and school-teacher). (HIGH)
- **Enlisted 20/23 May 1782** at Bellingham/Worcester as **"Robert Shurtliff"** (spelling varies across records: Shurtliff / Shurtleff / Shirtliff — use "Shurtliff," note variance), mustered into the **4th Massachusetts Regiment**; served with its light infantry company in the lower Hudson Valley — the war's main fighting was over (Yorktown, Oct 1781) but the "neutral ground" skirmish war around British-held New York was not. (HIGH on the enlistment, alias, regiment; date given as May 1782 — both 20th and 23rd appear in reputable sources, keep loose as "May 1782.")
- **Earlier failed attempt (documented, use carefully):** she had earlier taken an enlistment bounty in Middleborough under another name ("Timothy Thayer" per tradition), spent some of it, and was found out; the **First Baptist Church of Middleborough withdrew fellowship from her (September 1782)** for "dressing in men's clothes and enlisting as a Soldier." The church record is real; the "Timothy Thayer" name rests on later accounts. (MED — church record HIGH, alias name MED.)
- **Wounded near Tarrytown, New York (summer 1782):** in a skirmish she took **musket balls in the thigh** (and by most accounts a forehead/saber gash); she let the head wound be treated but **concealed the thigh wound and dug at least one ball out herself** rather than risk discovery; shot too deep remained in her leg for life. (MED — the spine "wounded near Tarrytown, concealed the thigh wound to avoid discovery" is consistent across sources including her pension materials; the penknife-and-needle self-surgery detail descends from Mann's account — see HANDLE-WITH-CARE.)
- **Discovered** in 1783 at Philadelphia, when she fell ill (fever) and a doctor (Barnabas Binney) found her out; he kept her secret to her benefit. (MED — Binney's role widely reported, details vary.)
- **Honorably discharged 25 October 1783 at West Point**, after ~17 months' service. (HIGH)
- **The pension fight (decades long):**
  - **1792** — Massachusetts granted her **£34 back pay** the army had withheld. (HIGH)
  - **1797** — **Herman Mann** published *The Female Review*, the as-told-to "memoir" — heavily embellished, including placing her at battles (Yorktown) she cannot have fought (she enlisted in 1782). **Mann is unreliable; treat nothing as fact on his word alone.** (HIGH on unreliability — standard scholarly judgment, Alfred Young's *Masquerade* the key work.)
  - **1802** — she went on a **lecture tour** (Massachusetts, Rhode Island, New York) — generally counted the **first lecture tour by an American woman** — performing her story, ending in full uniform with the manual of arms. The tour *itself* polished the legend; her stage narrative leaned on Mann's embellishments. (HIGH on tour + first-woman claim as "generally credited"; HIGH on the uniform/manual-of-arms finale.)
  - **1804** — **Paul Revere** (who had visited her struggling Sharon farm) wrote to Congressman **William Eustis** supporting her pension claim, calling her **"much more deserving than hundreds to whom Congress have been generous."** (HIGH — Revere's 20 Feb 1804 letter, MHS.)
  - **11 March 1805** — placed on the **Massachusetts Invalid Pension Roll at $4/month**; later moved to the general pension list (1818-act rate, $8/month, in 1821). Died **29 April 1827**, Sharon, Mass.; in **1838** Congress passed relief for her heirs — effectively a survivor's benefit on a *wife's* service, a first. (HIGH on 1805/$4 and death date; MED on the 1821 rate change and the 1838 act's characterization.)
- **Why she did it — resist the urge to know.** Her motives are undocumented (poverty, wages, restlessness, the bounty — all plausible). Don't assign one. (Directive.)

## 6. THE LADIES ASSOCIATION OF PHILADELPHIA — "THE OFFERING OF THE LADIES"

- **Context:** late May 1780 — Continental currency collapsing, Charleston falling, Washington reporting his troops near the end of clothing, pay, and patience. (HIGH)
- **"The Sentiments of an American Woman"** — a broadside published in Philadelphia, **10 June 1780**, written (per strong attribution) by **Esther De Berdt Reed** (London-born wife of Pennsylvania's chief executive Joseph Reed). It is a *manifesto for women's wartime action*: women are **"Born for liberty, disdaining to bear the irons of a tyrannic government"**; it invokes Joan of Arc (**"a French Maid kindled up amongst her fellow-citizens, the flame of patriotism"**); and it argues that **"if opinion and manners did not forbid us to march to glory by the same paths as the Men, we should at least equal, and sometimes surpass them"** — so women will renounce "vain ornaments" and give the money instead. (HIGH — broadside text verified, LOC Rare Book Division; attribution to Reed standard but technically anonymous → say "almost certainly Reed.")
- **The campaign:** within days, ~three dozen Philadelphia women organized a door-to-door canvass of the city and suburbs — treasurers per ward, a counting system, names recorded — the **first large-scale women's political fundraising organization in America**. (HIGH on the canvass structure; the "first" framing is standard but keep as "the first of its kind.")
- **The numbers:** Reed reported to Washington (**4 July 1780**) **more than $300,000 Continental (paper) dollars** raised, from roughly **1,600+ donors** (commonly itemized as ~$300,634 from 1,645 contributors). Caveat for honesty: in 1780's hyperinflation that paper sum was worth on the order of **a few thousand dollars in hard money** — the organizational feat is the story as much as the sum. (HIGH on >$300,000 and Reed's 4 July letter; MED on the precise $300,634/1,645 and on any specific specie equivalent — phrase as "a fraction of that in hard coin.")
- **The shirts fight — Washington overrules the women.** Reed proposed giving each soldier **hard cash in hand** ("an extraordinary bounty"), explicitly *not* routed through Congress's supply system. **Washington said no — make shirts**: cash, he argued, would breed discontent (and drink); linen shirts were what the men needed. The women pushed back once; Washington held; Reed yielded and began buying linen in August. (HIGH — the Reed–Washington correspondence, summer 1780.)
- **Esther Reed died 18 September 1780** (of dysentery/"fever"), age 33, mid-project. **Sarah Franklin Bache** (Benjamin Franklin's daughter) took over with the committee; the women sewed through the autumn, and on **26 December 1780 Bache forwarded over 2,000 shirts** (commonly 2,005; ~2,200 by the project's end) to Washington's army — many with **the maker's name sewn into the shirt**. (HIGH on Reed's death date and Bache finishing + 2,000+ shirts Dec 1780; MED on the exact 2,005/2,200 counts and the sewn-names detail — lovely if kept as "many, by tradition, with the maker's name stitched in.")
- **Spread:** the model was copied — women's associations raised money in **New Jersey, Maryland, and Virginia** (Martha Wayles Jefferson lent her name in Virginia). (HIGH on the spread to other states; MED on details per state — keep to one line.)

## 7. PEN, PRESS, AND INTELLIGENCE

- **Mercy Otis Warren** (1728–1814; Plymouth, Mass.; sister of James Otis, wife of James Warren, close friend and correspondent of Abigail Adams):
  - Anonymous **propaganda plays** read aloud and serialized in patriot papers: ***The Adulateur* (1772)** and ***The Defeat* (1773)** — skewering Governor Thomas Hutchinson as the tyrant "Rapatio" — and ***The Group* (1775)**. All her work appeared **anonymously until 1790**. (HIGH)
  - ***History of the Rise, Progress and Termination of the American Revolution*, three volumes, 1805**, under her own name — the **first major history of the Revolution by an American woman** (commonly: the first by an American woman, full stop). (HIGH; phrase the "first" carefully as above.)
  - The History's sharp portrait of John Adams ruptured the old friendship (a furious 1807 exchange; later patched). Adams's line **"History is not the Province of the Ladies"** (1813, to Elbridge Gerry) is real but post-war — usable as the era's verdict she defied. (HIGH on the rupture; MED on the exact quote sourcing — use only with the 1813 attribution, or drop.)
- **Mary Katharine Goddard** (1738–1816) — printer and publisher of the *Maryland Journal*, and **postmaster of Baltimore 1775–1789** (among the first women to hold federal office). In **January 1777** Congress, sitting in Baltimore, commissioned her press to print the **Goddard broadside** of the Declaration of Independence — the **first printing to list the signers' names** (55 of 56; McKean missing), and at the foot, in type: **"Baltimore, in Maryland: Printed by Mary Katharine Goddard."** A woman's name on the treason document. (HIGH — NARA/NPS; printed 18 January 1777 per standard accounts, copies sent to each state.)
- **Phillis Wheatley — one beat only (rev-slavery owns Black Americans' war broadly).** Enslaved in Boston, internationally published poet (London, 1773), **freed in 1773**. In **October 1775** she sent Washington a praise-poem ("To His Excellency General Washington"); Washington replied personally, **28 February 1776**, thanking her for "the elegant Lines" and inviting her: **"If you should ever come to Cambridge, or near Head Quarters, I shall be happy to see a person so favourd by the Muses."** Whether the Cambridge meeting actually happened is **traditional, not documented** — say "she may well have come; no record survives." (HIGH on poem, dates, reply quote — Founders Online/LOC; the meeting itself MED-to-undocumented.)
- **Lydia Darragh — badge as family tradition.** The story: a Philadelphia Quaker in whose requisitioned front room British officers conferred (occupied Philadelphia, **early December 1777**); she allegedly overheard plans for a surprise march on Washington at **Whitemarsh**, walked out of the city on a flour-mill pass, and got the warning to the American lines (2–4 December 1777; the British found the Americans ready and withdrew). **Evidence problem:** the full story was **first published in 1827** (and elaborated by her daughter Ann), ~50 years after the fact, with internal inconsistencies; **no contemporary record names her**. The genuinely contemporary anchor is American intelligence officer **Elias Boudinot's** memorandum of receiving a Whitemarsh warning from an unnamed woman who smuggled intelligence (in a "needlebook" by his account) — *consistent with* Darragh but not naming her. Tell it as: the army was warned, a woman likely carried it, family tradition says it was Lydia Darragh. (HIGH on the evidentiary state; the story itself rests on tradition → badge it.)
- **Sybil Ludington — heavily doubted; omit or myth-check in one line.** The famous "female Paul Revere" night ride (40 miles, 26 April 1777, rousing her father's militia toward Danbury) has **no contemporary evidence whatsoever** — no 1777 newspaper, dispatch, or letter mentions it; the earliest account is from a **nephew in 1854**, first printed in an **1880** local history. Paula Hunt's 2015 *New England Quarterly* study: "not a shred of contemporary evidence." Recommendation: **omit, or use only as a one-line example of how the 19th century manufactured Revolutionary heroines** (pairs with Molly Pitcher). (HIGH on the doubt.)

## 8. THE LOSERS' WAR — LOYALIST WOMEN & THE OTHER SIDE'S WOMEN

- **Confiscation hit women who stayed.** Loyalist men fled or were attainted; their wives, under coverture, had no separate claim to "his" property — including, bitterly, property the wife had brought into the marriage. The state seized the household out from under the woman left in it. Grace Galloway is the chapter's case. (HIGH — frame; the rev-civil-war chapter owns the Loyalist machinery broadly, this is the woman-scale thread only.)
- **Grace Growden Galloway** (Philadelphia) — wife of Joseph Galloway, the arch-Loyalist who administered occupied Philadelphia; daughter of **Lawrence Growden**, one of Pennsylvania's richest men (she brought vast Bucks County property into the marriage — which coverture made Joseph's). (HIGH)
  - **June 1778:** Joseph fled with the British evacuation (with their daughter Betsy), leaving Grace behind **deliberately — to try to hold the property**. Pennsylvania attainted him; the estates were marked for confiscation. (HIGH)
  - **20 August 1778, after 10 p.m.:** **Charles Willson Peale** — yes, the painter, acting as commissioner of forfeited estates — carried out her eviction from the Market Street mansion. Her diary records the scene: she refused to leave voluntarily, pushed Peale's offered arm away, and walked out on her own terms. (HIGH — diary; minor source disagreement over whether the eviction scene was the Market Street house or Trevose — most say Market Street; keep to "her Philadelphia home.")
  - **Her diary (1778–79)** is the great record of Loyalist dispossession from the inside: humiliation, dropped friendships ("as I have no friends, they treat me as they please," Aug 1778), and defiance — the famous passage (spring 1779): though **"striped & Turn'd out of Doors,"** she was still Lawrence Growden's daughter and **"I shou'd be Grace Growdon Galloway to ye last."** (HIGH — diary quotes verified; date given variously 20/21 April 1779 — cite as "April 1779.")
  - She spent her last years fighting to recover *her own inherited* property for her daughter; **died 6 February 1782**, buried in an unmarked grave. (Pennsylvania ultimately restored the Growden inheritance to her daughter after the war — the law conceding, too late, that it had never really been Joseph's.) (HIGH on her death; MED on the postwar restoration detail — verify phrasing if used, or end on her death.)
- **Baroness Frederika von Riedesel** (1746–1808) — wife of Major General Friedrich Riedesel, commander of the Brunswick (German) troops with Burgoyne; crossed the Atlantic with **three small daughters** and kept a journal/letters later published — among the war's most vivid eyewitness accounts. (HIGH)
  - **The Marshall House cellar, Saratoga, October 1777:** sheltering with her children and the wounded in a farmhouse cellar under American bombardment: **"Eleven cannon balls went through the house, and we could plainly hear them rolling over our heads."** And the horror: **"One poor soldier, whose leg they were about to amputate, having been laid upon a table for this purpose, had the other leg taken off by another cannon ball."** She describes "a horrible stench, the cries of the children," organizing the cellar's cleaning and fumigating it with vinegar on hot coals, and nursing the wounded — "I made them tea and coffee." (HIGH — Stone translation, 1867; the Marshall House still stands and three of the cannonballs are displayed.)
  - After the surrender she went into the long captivity of the Convention Army with her family — her account follows the war's losers' road south. One beat; don't expand. (HIGH)
- **Note the symmetry plainly:** Galloway (Loyalist) and Riedesel (German) lost *their* war; the chapter's patriot women won theirs. Women's stakes ran on both sides. (Directive.)

## 9. NATIVE WOMEN — MOLLY BRANT (one honest beat)

- **Molly Brant / Mary Brant / Konwatsi'tsiaienni** (c. 1736–1796), Mohawk; long-time consort of Sir William Johnson (British superintendent of Indian affairs, d. 1774) and sister of war leader Joseph Brant; a woman of real standing in the Six Nations, where **clan matrons held formal political weight** (a system worth one contrast-sentence against coverture). (HIGH)
- **Documented intelligence, August 1777:** learning that Patriot militia were marching to relieve **Fort Stanwix**, she sent Mohawk runners to warn the British/Native force — enabling the **ambush at Oriskany (6 August 1777)**. This is her best-documented wartime act. (HIGH — Dictionary of Canadian Biography; NPS.)
- Through the war she sheltered and supplied Loyalists and worked to hold the Six Nations (most of them) to the British alliance; her influence among the Mohawk was rated by British officers above her brother's. After the war the Crown gave her **a house at Cataraqui (Kingston, Ontario) and a £100 annual pension** — among the largest paid to any Native person. Her war ended in exile from the Mohawk Valley forever. (HIGH on the pension/house and exile; MED on the "officers rated her above Joseph" line — attributable to Daniel Claus's assessment, keep soft.)
- **Do not pad** this into a survey of Native women in the war; one well-documented actor, told straight, with the matron-vs-coverture contrast. (Directive.)

## 10. WHAT THE WAR CHANGED — AND DIDN'T

- **"Republican motherhood"** — historian **Linda Kerber's term** (coined 1976; her book *Women of the Republic*, 1980) for the postwar ideology that gave women a *civic* assignment inside the domestic sphere: a republic needs virtuous citizens, mothers raise them, therefore **women's education acquired public purpose**. Real consequence: a measurable boom in (white, middling-and-up) **female academies and literacy** in the 1780s–90s. Honest framing: it dignified and educated women *as mothers of citizens* — it conceded no political rights, and it was the *containment* of Abigail's argument as much as its fruit. (HIGH — define it as a historian's term, postwar.)
- **New Jersey actually let women vote — the stunning verified fact, stated precisely:**
  - The **1776 New Jersey constitution** gave the vote to **"all inhabitants of this Colony, of full age, who are worth fifty pounds proclamation money, clear estate"** with a year's county residence — no sex or race qualification. Alone among the thirteen states. (HIGH — text verified.)
  - Because of coverture, **only single women and widows** could hold £50 in their own right — so it was **property-holding single women** (and, under the same clause, some free Black New Jerseyans) who voted. **Never say "women could vote" flat.** (HIGH)
  - Election statutes of **1790** (seven counties) and **1797** (statewide) made it explicit, referring to voters as **"he or she."** Women demonstrably voted, in numbers, into the early 1800s (the Museum of the American Revolution's "When Women Lost the Vote" project has recovered poll lists with women's names). (HIGH)
  - **1807:** after a fraud-soaked local election (and partisan calculation), the legislature restricted the franchise to **free, white, male** taxpaying citizens — stripping women and Black voters in one act, justified as fraud prevention. The American women who voted in 1800 would be the last until the next century (in NJ terms, until 1920). (HIGH)
  - Whether the 1776 wording was an *accident* of drafting or quietly deliberate (Quaker-influenced West Jersey politics) is **DISPUTED among historians** — write "whether by design or by accident of drafting." (DISPUTED)
- **Coverture survived intact.** No state abolished it; married women's property acts were a half-century-plus away (1830s–40s onward). **Divorce** became marginally more available in some states (Pennsylvania's 1785 statute; Massachusetts practice loosened slightly) — but the change was thin. John Adams's "Masculine systems," as he promised, were not repealed. (HIGH on coverture intact + the long delay; MED on the divorce-law specifics — keep to one soft sentence.)
- **The honest landing:** the Revolution put arguments in women's mouths ("no Laws in which we have no voice"), work in their hands (farms, funds, presses, armies), and a new ideology around their role — and left their legal condition nearly untouched. The women of 1780 handed the *argument*, not the victory, to the next century. **Do NOT write "women won the war" or imply the Revolution emancipated them.** (Directive.)

## VERIFIED QUOTES (verbatim, sourced)

1. **Abigail Adams to John Adams, 31 March 1776:** "…in the new Code of Laws which I suppose it will be necessary for you to make I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors. Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands. Remember all Men would be tyrants if they could. If perticuliar care and attention is not paid to the Laidies we are determined to foment a Rebelion, and will not hold ourselves bound by any Laws in which we have no voice, or Representation." *(Adams Family Papers, MHS / Founders Online.)*
2. **John Adams to Abigail Adams, 14 April 1776:** "As to your extraordinary Code of Laws, I cannot but laugh. … Depend upon it, We know better than to repeal our Masculine systems." (And: "…compleatly subject Us to the Despotism of the Peticoat…") *(MHS / Founders Online.)*
3. **Blackstone, *Commentaries* (1765), on coverture:** "By marriage, the husband and wife are one person in law: that is, the very being or legal existence of the woman is suspended during the marriage…" *(Commentaries, Book I, ch. 15.)*
4. **Washington, General Orders, 4 August 1777:** "…the multitude of women in particular, especially those who are pregnant, or have children, are a clog upon every movement." *(Founders Online.)*
5. **Continental Congress resolution, 6 July 1779 (Margaret Corbin):** "…who was wounded and disabled in the attack on Fort Washington, whilst she heroically filled the post of her husband who was killed by her side serving a piece of artillery…" — granting her, for life, one-half the monthly pay drawn by a soldier. *(Journals of the Continental Congress.)*
6. **"The Sentiments of an American Woman" (Philadelphia, 10 June 1780):** "Born for liberty, disdaining to bear the irons of a tyrannic government…" and "…if opinion and manners did not forbid us to march to glory by the same paths as the Men, we should at least equal, and sometimes surpass them…" *(LOC Rare Book Division broadside.)*
7. **Baroness Riedesel on the Marshall House cellar (Oct 1777; Stone trans. 1867):** "Eleven cannon balls went through the house, and we could plainly hear them rolling over our heads. One poor soldier, whose leg they were about to amputate, having been laid upon a table for this purpose, had the other leg taken off by another cannon ball." *(Letters and Journals, Stone translation.)*
8. **Grace Growden Galloway, diary, April 1779:** though "striped & Turn'd out of Doors… I shou'd be Grace Growdon Galloway to ye last." *(Galloway diary, Historical Society of Pennsylvania; quoted JAR 2018.)*
9. **Washington to Phillis Wheatley, 28 February 1776:** "If you should ever come to Cambridge, or near Head Quarters, I shall be happy to see a person so favourd by the Muses…" *(Founders Online / LOC.)*
10. **Paul Revere to William Eustis, 20 February 1804 (on Deborah Sampson):** "much more deserving than hundreds to whom Congress have been generous." *(MHS, Revere letter.)*
11. **Joseph Plumb Martin's memoir (1830), the Monmouth woman at the gun:** "a cannon shot from the enemy passed directly between her legs without doing any other damage than carrying away all the lower part of her petticoat…" *(Martin, Narrative; quoted JAR — note: written decades later, woman unnamed.)*

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **"Molly Pitcher" is a composite legend, not a person.** No contemporary source names her; the legend congealed in the 19th century and was attached to Mary Hays McCauley only after her death (1832 obituaries say nothing of a cannon; the grave-marking came in 1876). Margaret Corbin is the *documented* woman at a gun — tell hers as fact, Pitcher as legend, and use the gap itself as the story (how America replaced thousands of working camp followers with one tidy heroine). Never caption the Currier & Ives print as a depiction of a real event.
2. **Do NOT inflate Abigail Adams into a proto-suffragist.** "Remember the Ladies" attacks the *legal tyranny of husbands* (coverture), not the absence of the vote. The radicalism is real but specific. Her threatened "Rebelion" is wit with teeth — keep the wit.
3. **Camp followers were NOT prostitutes** — the old caricature is wrong; they were soldiers' wives, widows, and refugees doing the army's essential work for rations. State it plainly once and move on. Also keep the ration policy honest: documented norms ran ~a full ration per woman, half per child, *varying* by time and commander — don't assert the harsher half/quarter version as standard.
4. **Deborah Sampson's record is layered with her own legend.** The service, wound, discharge, and pensions are documented; the vivid particulars (the penknife self-surgery, several battle exploits) descend from **Herman Mann's 1797 *Female Review*** and her 1802 stage narrative — both unreliable. Mann put her at Yorktown, which is impossible (she enlisted May 1782). Keep documented spine vs. embellishment visibly distinct in the prose.
5. **Lydia Darragh rests on family tradition** (first published 1827, daughter's elaborations, no contemporary naming) — anchored only loosely by Boudinot's note of an unnamed woman's Whitemarsh warning. Badge it as tradition; never assert it flat.
6. **Sybil Ludington's ride is rejected by scholars** — zero contemporary evidence; earliest account 1854 (a nephew), first print 1880 (Hunt, *NEQ* 2015: "not a shred"). Omit, or use only as a one-line lesson in heroine-manufacture.
7. **New Jersey voting — precision mandatory.** It was **property-holding single women and widows** (£50 clear estate) under "all inhabitants," made explicit by "he or she" statutes (1790/1797), ended 1807 (with free Black voters stripped in the same act). Whether the 1776 wording was deliberate is DISPUTED — hedge it. Never write "New Jersey women could vote" unqualified.
8. **No "women won the war" overclaim — and no celebration reel.** Women appear here as actors with stakes, choices, and losses on **both sides** (Galloway and Riedesel lost; Brant won her war's battles and lost her homeland). Name class plainly: Abigail Adams's war (an estate to manage) and a camp follower's war (a ration to earn) were different wars.
9. **The Ladies Association sum needs its inflation caveat.** ">$300,000" is paper money in a hyperinflation year — worth a small fraction in coin. The organizational achievement (the first women's political fundraising machine, door-to-door, ~1,600 donors) is the real story; Washington redirecting the cash into shirts is the period's gender politics in miniature — tell it as a fight the women lost gracefully, not a happy collaboration.
10. **Wheatley is one beat** (poem, Washington's reply, freed 1773 — the meeting "may have happened," undocumented). The slavery chapter carries Black Americans' war; do not expand here, and do not assert the Cambridge meeting as fact.
11. **Corbin's pension is recognition AND half-pay** — hold both: first U.S. military pension to a woman, set at *half* a soldier's pay. The 2017 not-her-grave finding is verified and usable, but as a postscript, not the story.
12. **Loose numbers to keep as ranges:** Continental followers ~2–3% (1 per ~30 men); British ~6 women/company (Howe) and 1 per ~4.5 troops (NY, Aug 1781); ~2,000 women after Saratoga (MED); shirts "over 2,000"; donors "about 1,600"; Sampson's enlistment "May 1782."
