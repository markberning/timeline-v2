# Mobile Bay — STORYTELLING CRITIC GATE

> War content pipeline, step 4 (parallel critic). Judges the draft on hook,
> momentum, stakes, scene-setting, the human core, arc, and pacing. Accuracy is
> a separate gate; this gate never demands an invented fact. Where prose needs a
> fact it does not have, it is flagged for the fact pack, not asserted.
>
> **VERDICT: PASS (with 2 MUST-IMPROVE, 4 SHOULD-IMPROVE).** The two dramatic
> peaks — Tecumseh's 30-second death and the lone Tennessee against a fleet —
> both land hard. The held-at-arm's-length quote treatment reads as compelling
> honesty, not deflation: it keeps the thrill and is straight about the words.
> The principal storytelling weakness is front-loading — §1 and §2 over-lecture
> the "be precise about the port vs. the city" point before the reader has any
> stakes to care about it, and the same caveat is then repeated three more times.

## Beat-by-beat

### §1 the-last-door — "Why a fleet came for a bay nobody could see into"
**GOOD (hook).** "A house with most of its doors nailed shut" is a strong, concrete
opening image and immediately earns the "last door" title. The blockade is defined
in one clean clause ("a wall of warships parked off an enemy's harbors") without
stalling momentum. "Slam that door and tighten the noose one more notch" gives the
operation a stake a newcomer feels.

**GOOD (the planted promise).** "By the end of this story it will be standing on the
deck of the flagship, in human form" is the single best structural move in the draft.
It sets a hook that the closing section pays off precisely (Wilson Brown on the
shell-whip). That promise-and-payoff arc is what lifts this above a battle recap.

**WEAK (pacing — the lecture lands too early). SHOULD-IMPROVE.** The third and fourth
paragraphs ("It is worth being precise…" / "Keep that straight; the distinction
matters") are the author stopping the story cold to litigate a distinction the reader
has no reason to care about yet — we have not even seen the fleet move. The point is
real and worth making, but at this position it reads as throat-clearing before the
hook fully fires. **Recommend:** compress the port-vs-city caveat here to one tight
sentence and let the full "it corked the bottle, it did not drink it" payoff live in
§5 where it has earned weight. The draft already has the great line in §5 ("It did not
drink it") — front-loading the same idea in heavier, more hectoring form ("Keep that
straight") dulls it.

**WEAK (over-instruction tic). SHOULD-IMPROVE.** "Hold that thought" / "Keep that
straight" / "to be precise, the way we promised to be" (§5) — the narrator
repeatedly tells the reader to remember things. Once is a nice device; three-plus
times reads as a writer not trusting the structure to carry the callback. Cut at
least two.

### §2 running-the-forts — "Lashed in pairs, straight at the guns"
**GOOD (scene-setting + the nervy plan).** "Less clever than it was nervy" is a
perfect house-voice characterization of "running" a fort, and the run is defined by
action ("take your beating on the way through"). The lashed-pairs detail is rendered
as *character* — "something that tells you how much he respected the forts" — turning
a logistics fact into insight. That is exactly the move the voice wants.

**GOOD (the cliffhanger).** "Then the lead monitor died in half a minute, and the
whole attack nearly died with it." A genuinely good chapter-button. It ends the
section on a hard hook and pulls the reader straight into §3.

**WEAK (roster dump). SHOULD-IMPROVE.** "Brooklyn… then Richmond, Lackawanna,
Monongahela, Ossipee, Oneida — each with its small partner roped to its flank." The
fact pack explicitly authorizes naming "a representative few." Listing six sloops by
name (most never reappear) is the one passage where momentum sags into a manifest.
Keep Brooklyn, Hartford/Metacomet, and maybe one more for texture; let the rest be
"and the others behind them." The reader will not track six ship names and does not
need to.

### §3 damn-the-torpedoes — Tecumseh sinks; the order
**GOOD (the dramatic peak lands — hard).** This is the best-paced section. The setup
of the deliberately-mined gap is clear, Craven's fatal choice is concrete (steered
*west* of the buoys), and the sinking is rendered with restraint that makes it worse,
not less: "so fast there was no time to do anything… only twenty-one were saved." The
gut-punch line — "That single mine, in less than half a minute, killed more Union
sailors than the entire rest of the battle combined" — is the kind of counter-intuitive
stat that the fact pack flagged as the emotional core, and the prose delivers it as a
hammer, not a footnote. **This peak fully lands.**

**GOOD (the held-at-arm's-length quote reads as honesty, not deflation).** This is the
gate's specific worry, so judge it carefully. The draft handles "Damn the torpedoes"
by giving the legend its full romantic weight FIRST ("the version on the monuments and
in the schoolbooks"), confirming the *action* as flatly true and thrilling
("drove straight through the mined water… the mines… failed to go off. That is what
happened, and it won the battle"), and only THEN dismantling the words. Because the
thrill is banked before the caveat arrives, the honesty deepens the drama rather than
puncturing it. "The legend is a tidy compression of a real and reckless decision" is
the perfect landing — it keeps the recklessness, which is the actual thrill, and is
straight about the syllables. **This is the model for how to do shaky-quote lore.**

**GOOD (the rigging correction is *better* than the legend).** "The most famous sailor
in the country, fussed over by his own crew" turns a debunk into a warmer, more human
image. The draft's own line — "It is a better story than the legend, honestly" — is
correct, and the section earns it. Strong.

**SHOULD-IMPROVE (the two corrections stack).** §3 runs three big paragraphs of
"the famous thing is actually more complicated": the order, then the rigging. Both
are good individually, but back-to-back the *rhythm* of debunking repeats (legend →
"the truth is…" → warmer reality), and the section's energy, having peaked at the
sinking, now spends a long time in a reflective register. Consider moving the
rigging beat earlier (it belongs to the moment Hartford takes the lead) or trimming
it so the debunk-pattern doesn't run twice in a row.

### §4 the-tennessee — One ironclad against a fleet
**GOOD (the second peak — the lone stand — lands).** The framing is excellent:
"'squadron' flatters it… exactly four ships." The David-vs-Goliath math is made
vivid ("four monitors and more than a dozen wooden warships" against one slow
ironclad). The pivot is the money sentence: Buchanan "did the thing that turned a
one-sided fight into a legend on the losing side: he turned his single ironclad around
and steamed it back into the entire Union fleet, alone." That is the dramatic heart
of the naval action and the prose nails it — the reader roots for the doomed side,
which is the whole point.

**GOOD (the brutality is physical).** "Smokestack was shot away; her exposed steering
chains were parted… she drifted nearly motionless under the guns of a fleet."
Concrete, escalating, cinematic. "Battered into surrender" is the right note — defeat
with honor intact.

**WEAK (the gunboats are dispatched too fast to register). SHOULD-IMPROVE.** "Gaines
was wrecked, Selma was taken, and Morgan escaped" — this is one half-sentence, which
is structurally fine (they ARE a sideshow), but it's delivered so flatly it reads as
checklist. A single beat of texture would help the *contrast* land harder: the
gunboats folded instantly, which is exactly what makes Buchanan's refusal to fold so
striking. Right now the contrast is stated but not *felt*.

**NOTE — the "After you, pilot" MEANWHILE.** Good placement (it belongs near the
Tennessee climax as a tonal counterpoint, the human cost echoing under the won bay).
The arm's-length treatment again reads as honesty: "a survivor's account of a dead
man's last words, so it is told here the way it should be." Keeps the lump-in-throat
while being straight. Lands.

### §5 what-it-won — The meaning; the cause; the men on the deck
**GOOD (the arc closes).** The payoff of the §1 promise — Wilson Brown, born enslaved
near Natchez, working the flagship's shell-whip — is the strongest single passage in
the draft. "The war's cause and the war's instrument were standing on the same deck"
is a genuine thesis line, and it is *earned*, not asserted, because §1 planted it.
"The port was the object. Slavery was the reason" is clean, declarative, and exactly
the house voice. This is how to name the cause as story, not as a compliance
checkbox.

**GOOD (the political stakes get full weight).** The 1864-election framing ("a vote in
that election") gives the naval action a consequence far larger than a harbor, and
McPherson's one-two-punch (paraphrased, correctly) earns the scope. The "lowest ebb of
Northern morale" context makes the victory *matter* in a way the reader feels.

**MUST-IMPROVE (the cause is stated three times and the third weakens the first).**
Slavery-as-cause appears in §1 ("The Confederacy existed to preserve and extend
slavery"), then §5 author voice ("The Confederacy fought to preserve and extend
slavery… That is not editorializing"), then the closing MEANWHILE ("a republic founded
to keep four million people in bondage"). The *gate requires* naming the cause — that
is non-negotiable and correctly satisfied. But as *storytelling*, the §5 paragraph that
begins "And that brings us back…" and the paragraph that begins "The Confederacy fought
to preserve and extend slavery… That is not editorializing" make nearly the same
argument back-to-back. The "That is not editorializing; it is the plain reason eleven
states left the Union" beat is the writer arguing with an imagined skeptic — which
slightly undercuts the confident, novelistic register everything else lives in. The
Wilson Brown payoff is so strong it does NOT need the defensive throat-clearing in
front of it. **Recommend:** keep the §1 statement, keep the Brown payoff, and merge the
two middle abstract restatements into ONE tighter pass so the cause is asserted with
calm authority and then *shown* — not argued, re-argued, and defended. The closing
MEANWHILE then becomes a clean reprise rather than a fourth swing.

**MUST-IMPROVE (the closing MEANWHILE largely duplicates the body's final beats).**
The "what the running ships carried" MEANWHILE restates, in compressed form, the exact
images already delivered in the preceding three paragraphs (the blockade-runners
carrying a slave republic's cargo; the man born a slave on the flagship's shell-whip;
"the integrated crew is not a footnote… it is the point"). A MEANWHILE should add an
angle or a parallel, not echo the section it follows. Right now §5 ends on its
strongest possible line ("exactly where the story said it would be") and then the
MEANWHILE makes the reader hear the same thesis a fifth time, deflating the close.
**Recommend:** either cut this MEANWHILE entirely (the body already lands it), or
repoint it to genuinely new material — e.g., the *symmetry* with the §1 MEANWHILE
(Wilmington/the other door) so the two MEANWHILE bookends rhyme, rather than restating
the cause a final time.

## Cross-cutting

**Momentum overall: strong middle, draggy ends.** §2→§3→§4 is excellent, escalating,
hook-to-hook storytelling. The drag is at the bookends: §1 over-prepares (the precise
port-vs-city lecture before stakes exist) and §5 over-concludes (the cause stated/
restated/defended/reprised four times). The fix in both cases is *subtraction*, not
addition — trust the planted promise and the Wilson Brown payoff to carry the meaning,
and stop hammering "keep that straight."

**The narrator's "I'll tell you a thing, then tell you it's complicated" device** is the
draft's signature and its biggest single asset (it's the right response to a folklore-
soaked battle), but it fires four times (the order, the rigging, "after you pilot,"
the port-vs-city). It's a great move; at four repetitions the reader can feel the
machinery. Keep the two best uses at full length (the order; the rigging) and let the
others be lighter.

**Voice: on target.** Informal, confident, "the legend is a tidy compression of a real
and reckless decision" — this is the popular-history register the product wants. No
sanding-down needed.

**No invented facts demanded.** Every improvement above is achievable by cutting,
reordering, or compressing existing verified material. Nothing here asks the fact pack
for a new fact.
