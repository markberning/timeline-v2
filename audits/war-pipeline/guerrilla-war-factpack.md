## BEAT 1: What the Irregular War Was

- **Three structural categories of irregular warfare** (standard historiographic taxonomy):
  1. **"People's war" / bushwhacking** — mass, decentralized guerrilla violence in the border states (Missouri, Arkansas, Tennessee, Kentucky, northwestern Virginia/West Virginia): neighbors killing neighbors, ambush, murder, terror. Most fighters wore no uniform and held no formal military commission. These were the **bushwhackers.**
  2. **Partisan warfare** — smaller *organized* units under some conventional-army control for behind-the-lines raids. Given legitimacy by the **Partisan Ranger Act**. Mosby's 43rd Battalion is the type case.
  3. **Raiding warfare** — deep cavalry raids by *regular* commanders (Nathan Bedford Forrest, John Hunt Morgan) against railroads, supply hubs, bridges. (Forrest belongs to this category as a soldier — important context for Fort Pillow: he was a regular Confederate cavalry general, not a bushwhacker.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Guerrilla warfare in the American Civil War" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guerrilla_warfare_in_the_American_Civil_War

- **Bushwhackers vs. jayhawkers (the two sides of the border war):**
  - **Bushwhackers** = pro-Confederate irregulars (the Missouri-side guerrillas: Quantrill, Anderson).
  - **Jayhawkers** = pro-Union irregulars, the term originating in the pre-war "Bleeding Kansas" period; free-state militia/vigilantes who raided Missouri plantations and farms. Associated leaders: Charles "Doc" Jennison, James Montgomery, and (politically) Senator James H. Lane. A related Union irregular group on the Kansas side was the **"Red Legs."**
  - Bushwhackers were un-uniformed civilian resisters with no formal army affiliation; **partisan rangers**, by contrast, wore Confederate uniforms, had commissioned leaders, and reported up a chain of command.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Bushwhackers and Jayhawks" — https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/bushwhackers-and-jayhawks ; Wikipedia, "Lawrence Massacre" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Massacre

- **The Partisan Ranger Act:** Passed by the Confederate Congress **April 21, 1862**, to recruit irregulars into Confederate service and give sanctioned partisan units legitimacy. **Repealed February 17, 1864**, under pressure from Robert E. Lee and other regular-army officers who saw partisan units as undisciplined and corrosive of regular discipline. Only two partisan commands were *exempted* from the repeal and allowed to keep operating: **Mosby's Rangers and McNeill's Rangers.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Partisan Ranger Act" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partisan_Ranger_Act ; American Battlefield Trust, "Bushwhackers and Jayhawks"

- **The Lieber Code (General Orders No. 100), April 24, 1863:** The Union's code of land-warfare law, written by Francis (Franz) Lieber — German-born lawyer and Napoleonic-wars veteran — and issued by Lincoln. 157 articles. **Crucially for this section:** it denied prisoner-of-war protection to guerrillas / "armed prowlers." The three functional disqualifications it applied: guerrillas (1) wear no uniform of a belligerent, (2) have no formal chain of command, and (3) cannot themselves take prisoners. In effect, it made captured bushwhackers legally killable — which fed the no-quarter spiral on the border.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lieber Code" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieber_Code ; Avalon Project (Yale), "General Orders No. 100: The Lieber Code" — https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/lieber.asp

- **Why the border states bred it:** Missouri, Kansas, Kentucky, western Virginia, and Tennessee were the regions where Union and Confederate loyalties were *mixed at the neighborhood level*. There was no front line; there were just neighbors on opposite sides. Conventional armies passed through, but daily control belonged to whoever held the local woods. Missouri's case was the most acute because it inherited the "Bleeding Kansas" hatreds of 1854–1859 directly into the war.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Guerrilla warfare in the American Civil War"; American Battlefield Trust, "Guerrilla Warfare" — https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/guerrilla-warfare

## BEAT 2: Fort Pillow — April 12, 1864 (THE EMBLEM)

### The setting and forces
- **Date / place:** April 12, 1864, at **Fort Pillow**, on a bluff above the Mississippi River, Lauderdale County, Tennessee — about **40 miles north of Memphis**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Fort_Pillow ; NPS Civil War battle detail (TN030) — https://www.nps.gov/civilwar/search-battles-detail.htm?battleCode=TN030
- **Confederate commander:** Major General **Nathan Bedford Forrest** — a regular Confederate cavalry general (slave trader and planter before the war). Force: **~1,500–2,500** cavalry (sources vary; use "roughly 1,500–2,500").
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"
- **Union garrison:** **~600 men** (sources: ~585–605). Two-part composition, roughly half Black:
  - **6th U.S. Colored Heavy Artillery** (Black) — one battalion
  - **Battery D / 2nd U.S. Colored Light Artillery** (Black) — a section
  - **13th (West) Tennessee Cavalry (U.S.)** — white Unionist Tennesseans, "Bradford's Battalion," many of them Confederate deserters / Southern Unionists (hence especially hated by Forrest's men)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"; American Battlefield Trust, "The Most Terrible Ordeal of My Life" — https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/most-terrible-ordeal-my-life-battle-fort-pillow
- **Union commanders:** Major **Lionel F. Booth** (killed early by a sharpshooter); command passed to Major **William F. Bradford**, who led the white Tennessee battalion.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"

### The surrender demand and the assault
- Forrest's men took the surrounding high ground; by mid-afternoon the garrison was effectively trapped against the river. At **~3:30 PM** Forrest sent in a surrender demand promising prisoner-of-war treatment, and gave Bradford **20 minutes** to comply. Bradford refused.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"; American Battlefield Trust, "Most Terrible Ordeal"
- **Surrender-demand wording** (paraphrase, well-attested): Forrest demanded "the unconditional surrender of the entire garrison, promising that you shall be treated as prisoners of war," and warned that if refused he "cannot be responsible for the fate of your command." (The "cannot be responsible" phrasing is central to the later controversy.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"; American Battlefield Trust, "Most Terrible Ordeal" [VERIFY exact wording against the *Official Records* before quoting verbatim — see CONTESTED.]

### The massacre
- Confederate troops stormed the fort and overran it quickly. **Survivors testified that men who threw down arms and tried to surrender — Black soldiers especially — were shot, bayoneted, and in some accounts burned or buried alive.** Soldiers reported Confederates shouting **"No quarter! No quarter!"**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"
- Major Bradford was apparently shot **after** surrendering.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"

### Casualties and the color line (the load-bearing numbers)
- **Union dead:** roughly **277–297 killed** (round to **"nearly 300"**); ~60 wounded; ~164–168 captured/missing.
- **Of the Union dead, close to 200 were Black** (African American soldiers).
- **Confederate losses:** only **14 killed**, ~86 wounded.
- **The survival color line (the single most damning statistic):**
  - **About 70% of white soldiers survived; only about 35% of Black soldiers survived.**
  - Put another way: of the Black soldiers, only ~20% were taken prisoner (≈58 of ~290); of white soldiers, ~60% were taken prisoner (≈168 of ~280).
  - The Black units lost roughly **64% killed outright** — more than 30 points higher than the white units.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"; History.com, "Fort Pillow Massacre" — https://www.history.com/articles/fort-pillow-massacre ; Britannica, "Fort Pillow Massacre" — https://www.britannica.com/event/Fort-Pillow-Massacre
- **AUTHOR GUIDANCE:** Use ranges. "Nearly 300 Union dead, of whom close to 200 were Black"; "about 70% of white defenders survived, but only about 35% of the Black defenders" — that asymmetry IS the proof of a massacre. The Confederate death toll of 14 next to ~300 Union dead is the second tell.

### The Congressional investigation
- The **Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War** (the standing radical-Republican oversight committee) investigated almost immediately. Co-chaired/led by **Senator Benjamin Wade** (R-Ohio) and **Rep. Daniel W. Gooch** (R-Mass.). They interviewed survivors and witnesses; their report **labeled the event a massacre** ("a massacre without parallel") and was published in May 1864 (reports already in Northern papers by April 16, 1864).
  — Source: U.S. Capitol Visitor Center, "Fort Pillow Massacre, by the Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War, May 5, 1864" — https://www.visitthecapitol.gov/artifact/fort-pillow-massacre-joint-committee-conduct-war-may-5-1864 ; Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"
- **Honesty note for the author:** the Committee's account was real testimony AND propaganda — Wade and Gooch had a partisan stake, and modern historians note a propagandist slant in the framing. The *core fact* of a massacre is sound (the casualty asymmetry confirms it independently); the *committee's rhetoric* should be cited as a wartime political document, not neutral forensics.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"

### The Forrest-responsibility controversy
- Historians still debate whether Forrest **ordered**, **permitted**, or merely **failed to stop** the killings. There is no surviving order from Forrest to give no quarter; his defenders argue the massacre was his men's rage (especially against the white Tennessee Unionists and Black soldiers) breaking loose after a refused surrender, not a directive. His critics note he was on the field, in command, and did little to stop it — and that his own subsequent report (below) reveals his attitude.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow"; History.com, "Fort Pillow Controversy Lingers, 150 Years Later" — https://www.history.com/articles/fort-pillow-controversy-lingers-150-years-later
- **Modern scholarly consensus:** a massacre occurred; premeditation remains disputed. (Andrew Ward's *River Run Red* and John Cimprich's *Fort Pillow, a Civil War Massacre, and Public Memory* are the standard book-length treatments — both conclude a massacre happened.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Pillow" (citing Ward, Cimprich)

### Forrest after the war: the Ku Klux Klan (the throughline)
- The KKK was founded by six Confederate veterans in **Pulaski, Tennessee**, in the spring of **1866** (some sources date the first meeting to December 24, 1865). **Forrest joined in the fall of 1866 and was elected its first Grand Wizard in the spring of 1867** (the Maxwell House Hotel meeting in Nashville is the traditional setting, ~April 1867).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Nathan Bedford Forrest" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Bedford_Forrest ; Britannica, "Nathan Bedford Forrest — Postwar life and the Ku Klux Klan" — https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nathan-Bedford-Forrest/Postwar-life-and-the-Ku-Klux-Klan
- During his tenure the Klan used violence and intimidation to suppress Black political participation, especially around the **1868 elections** — though historians dispute how much *direct* operational control Forrest had over local "dens."
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Nathan Bedford Forrest"
- In **1869** Forrest, expressing disillusionment with the Klan's uncontrolled terror and lack of discipline, issued a letter **ordering the Klan dissolved** and its costumes destroyed, then withdrew. (Later in life he made some public conciliatory gestures toward Black audiences — note carefully, do not overstate as a "change of heart"; the record is contested.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Nathan Bedford Forrest"; Britannica
- **THROUGHLINE FOR THE AUTHOR:** Fort Pillow → first Grand Wizard is the section's spine: the man who presided over the war's emblematic racial atrocity became the first national face of postwar racial terror. State it as fact, soberly; don't editorialize past the record (the premeditation-at-Fort-Pillow question stays open; the Klan role does not).

## BEAT 3: The Missouri–Kansas Border War

### Roots: Bleeding Kansas
- The Missouri–Kansas guerrilla war inherited the hatreds of **"Bleeding Kansas"** (1854–1859), the pre-war violence over whether Kansas would enter the Union slave or free, triggered by the Kansas-Nebraska Act's "popular sovereignty." (The territorial crisis itself belongs to the "Road to War" civ/war framing — reference it briefly as the *seedbed*, don't re-narrate.) By 1861 the border already had armed free-state "jayhawkers" and pro-slavery "border ruffians" who simply continued, now under war colors.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Bushwhackers and Jayhawks"; Wikipedia, "Lawrence Massacre"

### William Quantrill
- **William Clarke Quantrill** (July 31, 1837 – June 6, 1865). Born **Canal Dover, Ohio**. Schoolteacher in Ohio and Illinois; moved to Kansas in 1857; by 1860, living near Lawrence, he drifted into theft and was charged with horse stealing before the war.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "William Quantrill" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Quantrill ; Britannica, "William C. Quantrill" — https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-C-Quantrill
- Formed his own band of pro-Confederate guerrillas (**Quantrill's Raiders**) raiding Union-sympathizing towns and farms. Recruits over time included **Cole Younger, William T. "Bloody Bill" Anderson, and the James brothers.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "William Quantrill"
- **Death:** ambushed by Union forces at Wakefield, Kentucky, **May 10, 1865**; shot in the back and paralyzed; died **June 6, 1865** — after the war's main armies had surrendered.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "William Quantrill"

### The Lawrence Massacre — August 21, 1863
- At dawn on **August 21, 1863**, Quantrill led roughly **300–400 raiders** (≈450 reported on the outskirts) into **Lawrence, Kansas**, the abolitionist stronghold and jayhawker base.
- They murdered **between ~150 and ~190 men and boys** — figures vary by source: ~150 is the conservative count, 183–190 the higher end; the Wikipedia infobox lists **164 deaths.** **Use "roughly 150 to 190 men and boys."** Nearly all victims were unarmed civilians; among the dead were 18 of 23 unmustered army recruits.
- **Only one raider** was killed.
- **Property damage:** roughly **$1 million–$1.5 million** (1863 dollars); about a quarter of the town burned, nearly every business destroyed.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lawrence Massacre"; Civil War on the Western Border, "Quantrill's Raid on Lawrence" — https://civilwaronthewesternborder.org/encyclopedia/quantrills-raid-lawrence ; BlackPast, "The Lawrence Massacre" — https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/quantrills-raid-on-lawrence-kansas-1863/
- **Why Lawrence:** (1) longtime abolitionist center; (2) base of jayhawker operations into Missouri; (3) revenge for jayhawker raids on Missouri (e.g., the 1861 sacking of Osceola, Missouri, associated with Senator **James H. Lane**); (4) immediate trigger — see the prison-collapse below. Lane himself escaped the raid by fleeing through a cornfield in his nightshirt.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lawrence Massacre"
- **The trigger — the Kansas City women's prison collapse (Aug 13, 1863):** Union authorities had jailed female relatives of bushwhackers in a Kansas City building; on **August 13, 1863** it **collapsed**, killing four young women — including **Josephine Anderson, age ~15, sister of "Bloody Bill" Anderson** (others: Charity McCorkle Kerr, Susan Crawford Vandever, Armenia Crawford Selvey; Nannie Harris died of injuries days later). Bushwhackers believed the collapse was deliberate. It enraged Anderson and fed directly into the savagery at Lawrence eight days later.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Lawrence Massacre"

### "Bloody Bill" Anderson
- **William T. Anderson** (c. 1840 – October 26, 1864), "**Bloody Bill**." Among the deadliest border guerrillas. Joined Quantrill's Raiders in early 1863; the two split in late 1863 (winter in Sherman, Texas) after Anderson's accusations got Quantrill briefly arrested by Confederate authorities. Anderson then led his own band.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "William T. Anderson" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_T._Anderson ; SHSMO Historic Missourians, "'Bloody Bill' Anderson" — https://historicmissourians.shsmo.org/bill-anderson/
- Notorious for **scalping and mutilating** victims; reportedly kept a knotted cord recording his kills — **~54 knots** at his death.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "William T. Anderson"
- **Death:** killed in a Union ambush near **Albany, Missouri, October 26, 1864**, shot in the head by men of the 33rd (or 51st) Missouri militia under Lt. Col. Samuel P. Cox.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "William T. Anderson"; History.com, "'Bloody Bill' Anderson killed" — https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/october-26/bloody-bill-anderson-killed

### The Centralia Massacre — September 27, 1864
- **September 27, 1864, Centralia, Missouri** (~9:00 AM). Anderson led ~80 bushwhackers. They stopped a **North Missouri Railroad train** (~125 passengers), pulled off **24 unarmed Union soldiers** on furlough (returning from the Atlanta campaign), ordered them to strip, and **shot them** — sparing one, Sgt. Thomas Goodman, kept as a hostage. The bodies were mutilated and scalped; the train and depot were burned.
- **The same afternoon (~3:00 PM):** Union Major A.V.E. Johnston pursued with ~147 mounted infantry of the 39th Missouri. Anderson's mounted, revolver-armed guerrillas overwhelmed them: **~123 of the 147 Union pursuers were killed.** Combined with the 24 massacred at the depot, the day cost roughly **150 Union dead** against a handful of guerrillas.
- **Frank and Jesse James** rode with Anderson that day; by family/Frank James's account, **Jesse James fired the shot that killed Major Johnston.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Centralia massacre (Missouri)" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralia_massacre_(Missouri)

### Frank and Jesse James as young bushwhackers (the legacy hook)
- **Frank James** rode with Quantrill (present at Lawrence) and later with Anderson; **Jesse James** (b. 1847, a teenager during the war) rode with Anderson and was at Centralia. The border guerrilla war was the **school for the postwar James-Younger outlaw gang** — the bushwhacker ethos (raids, ambush, grievance against Union authority) carried straight into their 1866–1882 outlaw career.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "William Quantrill"; Wikipedia, "Centralia massacre (Missouri)"
- **AUTHOR NOTE:** this is the section's "the war never really ended on the border" beat — pair it with Forrest/KKK as the two afterlives of the irregular war's violence.

## BEAT 4: General Order No. 11 (Union Reprisal) — August 25, 1863

- Issued by Union General **Thomas Ewing Jr.** on **August 25, 1863** — **four days after the Lawrence Massacre** — to drain the bushwhackers of their civilian support base.
- **What it ordered:** **all persons** living in four Missouri counties — **Jackson, Cass, Bates, and northern Vernon** — to **leave their homes within 15 days**, unless they could prove loyalty to a Union post (in which case they could relocate to designated towns/posts).
- **Effect:** forced exodus of roughly **20,000 residents.** Cass County's population fell from ~10,000 (1860) to a few hundred by late 1863. The emptied, burned-over region became known as the **"Burnt District."** Only about a third of residents ever returned.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "General Order No. 11 (1863)" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Order_No._11_(1863) ; Civil War on the Western Border, "General Order No. 11" — https://civilwaronthewesternborder.org/encyclopedia/general-order-no-11
- **THE POINT FOR THE AUTHOR:** Order No. 11 is the section's clearest proof that the irregular war **erased the line between soldier and civilian on both sides** — the Union answer to bushwhacker terror was the collective punishment of an entire civilian population. Atrocity (Lawrence) bred reprisal (Order No. 11) bred more bushwhacking. The cycle, not any single villain, is the story.

## BEAT 5: The "More Honorable" Partisan — John S. Mosby (the contrast case)

- **John Singleton Mosby** (1833–1916), "**the Gray Ghost**." Commanded the **43rd Battalion, Virginia Cavalry** — "Mosby's Rangers" / "Mosby's Raiders" — a *sanctioned partisan ranger* unit (one of the two exempted from the 1864 repeal of the Partisan Ranger Act).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John S. Mosby" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_S._Mosby
- **Operated in Northern Virginia** — Loudoun, Fauquier, and Prince William counties, a region so thoroughly his that Union forces called it **"Mosby's Confederacy."** Tactic: lightning raids on Union outposts, supply lines, and pickets, then dissolve back into the farm population. ~1,900 men served across Jan 1863 – Apr 1865.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John S. Mosby"
- **The contrast that makes him this section's foil:** unlike the Missouri bushwhackers, Mosby's men **wore Confederate uniforms, operated under official authority, and took prisoners.** Mosby is the example of irregular war fought (relatively) within the laws of war — which is exactly why his name is honored where Quantrill's and Anderson's are not.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John S. Mosby"; Wikipedia, "Guerrilla warfare in the American Civil War"
- **The 1864 reprisal cycle (the limit case):**
  - **September 22/23, 1864:** Union forces (under Custer's/Sheridan's command in the Shenandoah) **executed six (some sources seven) of Mosby's captured men at Front Royal, Virginia** as guerrillas, plus a related hanging at Rappahannock.
  - **November 6, 1864:** Mosby ordered retaliation — **seven Union prisoners chosen by lottery** to be hanged; in the event only about **three were actually executed** (others escaped, or were spared — one through Masonic intervention).
  - **November 11, 1864:** Mosby **wrote to General Philip Sheridan** proposing both sides return to treating captives as prisoners of war. **Sheridan/Union complied, and the tit-for-tat executions stopped.** This is the rare moment where the no-quarter spiral was deliberately *halted* — the opposite of Fort Pillow and the border.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John S. Mosby"
- **Postwar:** Mosby became a **Republican**, supported and befriended **Ulysses S. Grant**, and held federal posts (U.S. consul to Hong Kong, 1878–1885) — making him a Confederate hero who reconciled, the inverse of Forrest's trajectory.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "John S. Mosby"
- **AUTHOR GUIDANCE:** Mosby is the deliberate contrast — "irregular war did not *have* to mean no quarter." Use him to throw Fort Pillow and the border massacres into relief, and to set up the moral spectrum (Mosby ↔ Quantrill/Anderson ↔ Ferguson).

## BEAT 6: Champ Ferguson and the War-Crimes Reckoning

- **Samuel "Champ" Ferguson** — pro-Confederate guerrilla on the **Cumberland Plateau / Tennessee–Kentucky border** (White County, TN; Clinton County, KY). **Claimed to have killed over 100** Union soldiers and pro-Union civilians, often in personal-vendetta killings of neighbors.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Champ Ferguson" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champ_Ferguson
- **Saltville (Oct 1864):** Ferguson's band took part in **murdering wounded Union soldiers in their hospital beds after the First Battle of Saltville (Virginia)** — including wounded **Black troopers of the 5th U.S. Colored Cavalry** and white officers. This is his cross-link to the no-quarter-against-USCT throughline (see Beat 7).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Champ Ferguson"
- **Trial and execution:** convicted October 10, 1865; **hanged October 20, 1865.** At his trial he declared: *"I am yet and will die a Rebel… I killed a good many men, of course, but I never killed a man who I did not know was seeking my life."* (verbatim — see Quotes).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Champ Ferguson"
- **The two executions (the section's reckoning beat):** Ferguson and **Henry Wirz** (the Andersonville prison commandant) are the two Confederates most commonly cited as the **only two executed for war crimes** after the Civil War. Wirz was hanged **November 10, 1865**, at the Old Capitol Prison, Washington.
  - **PRECISION FLAG:** some sources name a **third** — guerrilla **Henry C. Magruder** — also executed (in Kentucky). So the accurate phrasing is: **Ferguson and Wirz are the two usually named; a third (Magruder) is cited by some sources.** Use "the only two men executed for war crimes — the guerrilla Champ Ferguson and the prison commandant Henry Wirz" but add a soft hedge, OR say "among the very few." Do NOT assert a flat "only two" without the caveat.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Henry Wirz" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wirz ; Wikipedia, "Champ Ferguson"; NPS Andersonville, "Myth: Henry Wirz was the only person tried for war crimes" — https://www.nps.gov/ande/learn/historyculture/wirztribunal.htm
- **CROSS-LINK, DON'T DUPLICATE:** Wirz/Andersonville belongs to the **prisons section.** Here, name Wirz only as the second of the two executions and **link out** ("Andersonville and Wirz are the subject of the Prisons section").

## BEAT 7: Other No-Quarter Flashpoints (KEEP BRIEF — throughline, not catalog)

The point of these is to show Fort Pillow was the **emblem of a pattern**, not a one-off. Use one tight paragraph, not a roll-call.

- **Poison Spring, Arkansas — April 18, 1864** (six days after Fort Pillow): Confederates massacred and mutilated Black soldiers of the **1st Kansas Colored Infantry** during/after the battle; of ~301 Union casualties, **117 dead and 65 wounded were from the 1st Kansas Colored.** Survivors vowed never to be taken alive; the Trans-Mississippi Black battle cry became **"Remember Poison Springs!"** (parallel to "Remember Fort Pillow!").
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Poison Spring" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Poison_Spring ; Encyclopedia of Arkansas — https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/engagement-at-poison-spring-37/
- **Saltville, Virginia — October 2–3, 1864:** Confederates (including Champ Ferguson's men) murdered wounded Black cavalrymen of the **5th (and 6th) U.S. Colored Cavalry** after the battle. Estimates range **from ~5 definitely murdered up to ~45–50** per National Archives analysis — **use "estimates range from a handful to roughly 45–50; the precise toll is disputed."**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "First Battle of Saltville" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Battle_of_Saltville ; NPS Camp Nelson, "Saltville Battle and Massacre" — https://www.nps.gov/cane/battle-of-saltville-and-massacre.htm
- **The Crater (Petersburg, July 30, 1864):** surrendering USCT soldiers were murdered after the failed assault — **REFERENCE ONLY**; this is owned by the USCT section. One sentence max, with a pointer.
  — Source: (USCT section; do not re-derive here.)
- **AUTHOR GUIDANCE:** these three together = "no quarter against Black troops was Confederate practice in multiple theaters in 1864, not an aberration at Fort Pillow." That's the whole job of this beat. Don't expand into battle narratives.

## BEAT 8: The Human Meaning (the closing throughline)

For the author's closing movement — facts to anchor it, not prose:
- The irregular war **erased the line between soldier and civilian**: Lawrence (civilians murdered by guerrillas) and Order No. 11 (civilians expelled by the Army) are the matched pair.
- It **previewed the war's ugliest legacy**: the racial no-quarter of Fort Pillow / Poison Spring / Saltville led directly into Reconstruction-era racial terror — embodied in **Forrest, the emblem of the war's worst atrocity becoming the first Grand Wizard of the Klan.**
- It **outlived the surrender**: Quantrill died after Appomattox; the **James brothers** carried the bushwhacker war into a 17-year outlaw career; the border's hatreds did not demobilize.
- The moral spectrum the section draws: **Mosby** (irregular war within the laws of war, reconciled afterward) at one pole; **Quantrill, Anderson, Ferguson, and Fort Pillow** at the other (no quarter, race-hate, vendetta) — and the war-crimes gallows (Ferguson, Wirz) as the thin, late, partial reckoning.

> "The slaughter was awful. Words cannot describe the scene. The poor deluded negroes would run up to our men, fall upon their knees and with uplifted hands scream for mercy but they were ordered to their feet and then shot down."
> *(A companion passage from the same letter: "Blood, human blood stood about in pools and brains could have been gathered up in any quantity.")*

> "The river was dyed with the blood of the slaughtered for two hundred yards."

## CONTESTED / FLAGGED (author must use ranges / hedges)

1. **Fort Pillow Union dead — USE RANGE.** ~277–297 killed; round to "nearly 300." Of these, "close to 200" were Black. Confederate dead: 14. The survival color line (≈70% white vs ≈35% Black survival) is the load-bearing, well-attested figure — lead with it. Don't assert a single exact dead-count.

2. **Forrest's surrender-demand exact wording — [VERIFY].** The "treated as prisoners of war" / "cannot be responsible for the fate of your command" phrasing is well-attested in secondary sources but should be confirmed against the *Official Records* (OR, Ser. I, Vol. 32) before quoting verbatim. The substance is solid; the exact words need a primary check.

3. **Forrest's responsibility — KEEP OPEN.** Whether Forrest ordered/permitted/failed-to-stop the massacre is genuinely disputed by historians; there is no surviving no-quarter order. State that a massacre occurred (the casualty asymmetry proves it) but that *premeditation by Forrest* remains contested. Do NOT flatly assert he ordered it; do NOT exonerate him either. His own "negro soldiers cannot cope" dispatch is fair to quote as evidence of his attitude.

4. **Lawrence death toll — USE RANGE.** ~150 to ~190 men and boys; Wikipedia infobox says 164; "roughly 150–190" is safest. All victims male civilians; one raider killed.

5. **"Only two executed for war crimes" — HEDGE.** Ferguson and Wirz are the two usually named; some sources add a third, guerrilla **Henry C. Magruder.** Use "the only two usually named" or "among the very few," not a bare "only two."

6. **Saltville massacre toll — USE RANGE.** From ~5 definitely murdered up to ~45–50 (National Archives analysis); "the precise toll is disputed."

7. **Mosby Front Royal executions — count varies (6 vs 7).** Union executed "six (some sources seven)" of Mosby's men at Front Royal, Sept 22–23, 1864; Mosby's lottery selected seven but executed about three. Use the ranges.

8. **Anderson's birth year — approximate (c. 1840).** Sources give c. 1839–1840.

9. **KKK founding date — note the 1865-vs-1866 split.** Most sources: founded spring 1866 in Pulaski, TN; some date the first meeting to Dec 24, 1865. Forrest's election as first Grand Wizard (spring 1867) is firm. Use "1866" for founding, "1867" for Forrest.

10. **Jesse James firing the fatal shot at Centralia — family attribution.** "By Frank James's account, Jesse fired the shot that killed Major Johnston." Attribute, don't assert as established fact.
