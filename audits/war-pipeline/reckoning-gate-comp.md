## 1. INDEPENDENTLY-BUILT MUST-COVER CHECKLIST

A strong capstone "Reckoning" for the American Civil War — tying the toll, the end of the Confederacy, what the war settled and what it didn't, Reconstruction and its violent overthrow, Jim Crow, the Lost Cause — should cover:

**THE COST**
- [A] The human toll: traditional ~620,000 vs. revised ~750,000 (Hacker 2011); the range; why the old figure was soft; scale comparisons
- [B] The wounded and maimed: ~476,000 wounded, ~70,000 amputees, widows/orphans
- [C] USCT's disproportionate death rate; freedpeople in contraband camps (uncountable dead)
- [D] Burned cities and wrecked Southern countryside (Richmond, Atlanta, Columbia)
- [E] The economic destruction, including emancipation as the obliteration of the South's largest capital asset; dollar cost of the war; the South's century-long poverty

**THE ACTUAL END OF THE CONFEDERACY**
- [F] Appomattox handed off to battle layer; the war did NOT end there — the Confederate state collapsed over months
- [G] Bennett Place (Apr 26, 1865) — Johnston / Sherman; the largest actual surrender
- [H] Jefferson Davis's capture (May 10, 1865); the myth debunked; indicted-but-never-tried
- [I] Kirby Smith (May 26 / Jun 2, 1865) — the trans-Mississippi end
- [J] CSS Shenandoah (Nov 6, 1865) — the last Confederate flag, in Liverpool

**EMANCIPATION MADE PERMANENT**
- [K] 13th Amendment: text, House vote (Jan 31, 1865), ratification (Dec 6, 1865), ~4 million freed
- [L] Legal freedom ≠ real freedom: the open questions citizenship/vote/land/contract/wages
- [M] The "punishment for crime" loophole — opened the door to convict leasing (should be named as what the loophole *produced*, even briefly, not just its existence)

**RECONSTRUCTION**
- [N] Andrew Johnson vs. Radical Republicans — the governing collision
- [O] Black Codes (1865–66): purpose, mechanism, Northern reaction
- [P] Freedmen's Bureau: scope, achievements (schools/HBCUs), limits; "forty acres and a mule" promise broken
- [Q] 14th Amendment (Jul 9, 1868): citizenship, equal protection, due process; overturning Dred Scott
- [R] 15th Amendment (Feb 3, 1870): Black male suffrage; the loopholes left open
- [S] Black political power: ~2,000 officeholders; 16 in Congress; Revels; Bruce; SC Black majority legislature
- [T] The violent overthrow: KKK / White League / Red Shirts; terror as explicit strategy
- [U] Federal response: Enforcement Acts; Grant's prosecutions; the KKK broken and reforming
- [V] Colfax Massacre (Apr 13, 1873): the worst single act of racial violence; ~60–150 dead; bodies in the river
- [W] *U.S. v. Cruikshank* (1876): courts strip federal protection — terrorists win via the Supreme Court
- [X] Johnson's impeachment: Tenure of Office Act; 35–19 acquittal by one vote; first presidential impeachment

**THE END OF RECONSTRUCTION**
- [Y] 1876 election: Hayes/Tilden; 20 disputed electoral votes; the Electoral Commission; 185–184
- [Z] Compromise of 1877: troops withdrawn; "Redeemer" Democrats take every Southern state
- [AA] Disenfranchisement mechanics: poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, understanding clauses
- [BB] Jim Crow: the name, the scope (schools/transit/restaurants/water fountains), the legal architecture
- [CC] Sharecropping: denied land → debt trap → near-bondage in fact
- [DD] Convict leasing: the 13th Amendment loophole weaponized; forced labor via mass criminalization of Black men through vagrancy laws — "slavery by another name" (Blackmon/Douglas Blackmon; EJI framing); lasted until the 1930s-40s
- [EE] *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896): "separate but equal"; 58-year span; Harlan's lone dissent
- [FF] Lynching as systematic racial terror during and after Reconstruction (~2,000 documented during Reconstruction alone per EJI)

**WHAT WAS SETTLED / WHAT WASN'T**
- [GG] The Union is permanent: secession tested and answered
- [HH] Chattel slavery dead: the war's primary moral achievement, permanent
- [II] Racial equality: NOT settled — the explicit third question; the "deferred not resolved" frame
- [JJ] The "Second Reconstruction" framing: Foner's "unfinished revolution" / the civil rights movement as the return engagement

**THE LOST CAUSE**
- [KK] The Lost Cause mythology: states' rights, noble Confederacy, "faithful" enslaved, Reconstruction-as-tragedy
- [LL] Refuted by Confederate own words: secession declarations + Stephens "Cornerstone Speech" (reference earlier sections)
- [MM] The UDC (founded 1894): the institutional engine; monuments, textbooks, essay contests
- [NN] Monument timing tells the truth: early 1900s (Jim Crow entrenchment) + 1950s–60s (civil rights backlash)
- [OO] The South lost the war and won the argument over its meaning — for a century

**CLOSING SYNTHESIS**
- [PP] Lincoln Second Inaugural — both the moral-accounting passage and the "malice toward none" passage
- [QQ] Grant's memoirs — the Appomattox passage (real respect for the men; no illusions about the cause)
- [RR] Douglass's "justice, not charity" as the closing question and the 1877 answer
- [SS] Tie the arc back to the whole "off the battlefield" vertical

**NOT REQUIRED FOR THIS SECTION (SCOPE LIMITS)**
- Disease/medicine mechanics → Medicine section (cross-ref only)
- Appomattox scene → battle layer
- Emancipation Proclamation mechanics → earlier section
- Secession causes / Cornerstone Speech deep dive → earlier section
- The Great Migration (1910s–1970s) — downstream of this section's temporal scope; belongs to a potential future section or is a SHOULD-CONSIDER mention only

## 3. FACT-PACK BLIND SPOTS

The fact-pack is thorough and well-organized. Two gaps:

**BLIND SPOT 1 — Convict leasing is not in the fact-pack at all.**
The pack names the 13th Amendment's "punishment for crime" loophole (Beat 4) and notes it "became a loophole later exploited through convict leasing." But there is no Beat or sub-section on convict leasing as a system: no dates (peaked 1880s–1920s, largely abolished 1930s–40s), no mechanism (vagrancy arrest → Black conviction → state leases prisoner to private employer → unpaid labor in mines/plantations/railroads), no scale, no sources. Since the draft correctly mirrors the fact-pack's level of treatment, the fact-pack is where this needs to be sourced and loaded before the draft can cover it properly. This is a fact-pack gap that cascades into a draft gap.

**BLIND SPOT 2 — Lynching as a post-Reconstruction systematic practice is underweighted in the fact-pack.**
Beat 5 covers the Klan's terror violence and Colfax, which is correct. Beat 6's descent-into-Jim-Crow beat mentions disenfranchisement, sharecropping, and Plessy — but lynching as a post-1877 phenomenon (the roughly 4,000 EJI-documented racial terror lynchings, 1877–1950; the failure of federal anti-lynching legislation; Ida B. Wells's campaign) has no dedicated fact-pack treatment. The draft accordingly lists "lynching" in a single-word phrase in the "not settled" accounting. The fact-pack should carry at minimum a lynching-as-terror-system beat for Beat 6 — with sourcing — so the draft can name it with specificity.

**No other fact-pack blind spots identified.** All contested items are flagged with appropriate author guidance. The three verified quotes are sourced to primary documents. The Hacker toll, Foner officeholder count, Colfax range, and amendment dates are all correctly contested/sourced.

## 4. VERDICT AND FINDINGS

### VERDICT: **FAIL** (conditional — two items blocking, one minor)

---

### MUST-ADD (blocking — section cannot ship without these)

**MUST-ADD 1: Convict leasing — name and briefly describe the system.**
The draft says the 13th Amendment's "punishment for crime" clause "became a loophole the South would later pry wide open to force Black men back into unpaid labor through the prison system." That one sentence is all. For a capstone section built around unflinching accounting, the mechanism deserves naming: vagrancy laws (themselves named earlier in Black Codes) were used to arrest Black men en masse; they were convicted and sentenced; the state then leased them to private companies — mines, plantations, railroads — for unpaid forced labor. This is *slavery by another name* (the phrase and the Blackmon book title), and it ran for decades after the war. The "punishment for crime" sentence in the draft gestures at it and stops. This is the single biggest content gap relative to what the section promises. Fix: add 2–4 sentences in the "Long Night" section that name the system and close the loop from the 13th Amendment loophole to its actual operation.

Note: the fact-pack must be updated with sourced convict-leasing beats before the draft revision can draw on them cleanly. Recommended source anchors: EJI "History of Racial Injustice: Convict Leasing"; Douglas Blackmon, *Slavery by Another Name* (2008); NMAAHC convict leasing materials.

**MUST-ADD 2: Lynching as a post-Reconstruction systematic practice — requires at least a sentence with scale.**
The word "lynching" appears once in the section, in a list: "disenfranchisement, segregation, sharecropping, and lynching." For a capstone section that is explicitly structured around naming what was *not* settled, this is insufficient. The EJI has documented roughly 4,000 racial terror lynchings in the South between 1877 and 1950 — more than two per week for seven decades. This was not a random social evil; it was systematic enforcement of the racial hierarchy, and the federal government repeatedly refused to pass anti-lynching legislation (a Senate filibuster blocked it; 200+ anti-lynching bills died in Congress). Ida B. Wells's campaign to document and publicize it is one of the era's defining figures and is entirely absent. The section does not need a full treatment — but "lynching" cannot remain a single word in a list. Fix: in "The Long Night" section, give lynching 2–3 sentences that name its scale, its function as deliberate enforcement terror, and the federal government's refusal to act. If the fact-pack is thin here, it must be updated before the revision.

---

### SHOULD-CONSIDER (important but not blocking)

**SHOULD-CONSIDER 1: Ida B. Wells.**
The section's closing gives the last word to Douglass (1865). Ida B. Wells is the most important figure in *documenting and fighting* the post-Reconstruction violence the section describes, and she is entirely absent. Her 1892 *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* and subsequent anti-lynching campaign are the era's definitive investigative and activist work. She would be an appropriate name to attach to the lynching discussion — not a full biography, but a name and a sentence. SHOULD-CONSIDER rather than MUST-ADD because the section already has three verified closing quotes and adding a fourth voice risks diluting the ending; this could instead appear within "The Long Night."

**SHOULD-CONSIDER 2: Convict leasing's duration (into the 20th century) as a bridge note.**
If convict leasing is added (MUST-ADD 1), a brief note that the system persisted into the 1930s–40s — long after this section's 1865–1896 temporal range — would reinforce the "deferred not resolved" thesis: the war's unfinished business wasn't just slow to start being addressed; some of its direct successors lasted nearly a century. One clause would do it.

**SHOULD-CONSIDER 3: The Civil Rights Act of 1866.**
The fact-pack (Beat 5) names it as the precursor to the 14th Amendment. The draft jumps from Black Codes → Freedmen's Bureau → 14th Amendment without mentioning it. This is genuinely optional — the amendments are the constitutionally durable form of what the Act established, and the section is already long. But if the author wants to show the Congress → Johnson veto → override → constitutionalize-it-anyway arc more clearly, the Act is the hinge. Low priority.

**SHOULD-CONSIDER 4: The Great Migration — one-sentence downstream gesture.**
The section ends its explicit temporal scope around 1896 (Plessy). The Great Migration (1910s–70s, roughly 6 million Black Americans leaving the South for the North) is the direct human consequence of what the section describes. A single sentence — noting that the people the war freed voted with their feet for the next half-century, leaving the South that had re-enslaved them in everything but name — would tie the long shadow forward without requiring a new section. This is genuinely optional; the "Second Reconstruction" reference already gestures at the 20th century's return engagement. But the Migration is a powerful concrete expression of what the failure of Reconstruction meant in lived human terms.
