## FIRST CHECK — Does the section name SLAVERY as the cause in its OWN prose?

**YES. PASS — and emphatically.** Slavery is not a buried mention; it is the spine of the
section, named repeatedly and in the section's own voice (not deflected to a quote, a
caption, or a "some say").

- Title of section 1: **"Bragg's Bid for a Slave State."**
- §1 ¶1: "Kentucky was a **slave state** that had stayed loyal to the Union, and Bragg meant
  to flip it… A Confederate Kentucky would be a **slave state pried out of the Union and
  bolted onto the South.**"
- §1 ¶2 names the **preliminary Emancipation Proclamation (Sept 22, 1862)** and draws the
  collision explicitly: "one army moving to widen slavery's reach, a federal government
  openly moving to end it."
- §1 ¶3 covers the **Hawes Confederate-governor inauguration at Frankfort (Oct 4)** — all
  three hook elements the brief required are present.
- §5 (the-reckoning) restates it as the "deepest meaning": Bragg "had marched into Kentucky
  to take a **slave state** out of the Union," and Kentucky "stayed there as the Emancipation
  Proclamation… moved from threat to law… Kentucky would not be a Confederate slave state."

The cause is named in the section's own prose, in two separate sections, as the organizing
idea. This clears the ship-blocking floor with room to spare.

## OVERALL VERDICT: **FAIR**

This draft is, on the framing axis, a model of how to handle a battle the Confederacy
"won." The central tension every Lost Cause narrative exploits — *a gallant outnumbered
Southern army taking the field* — is exactly the situation here, and the draft refuses to
romanticize it. It repeatedly grounds the tactical "victory" in (a) Union failure (acoustic
shadow + Gilbert's restraint) rather than Confederate prowess, and (b) the slave-state war
aim that makes the campaign's purpose unmistakable. No MUST-FIX.

## FINDINGS

### 1. "Gallant Confederate tactical victory" framing — does it tip into glory? **FAIR.**
The brief flagged this as the live risk. The draft handles it cleanly. The tactical win is
named ("On paper it was a tactical victory"; "they had won at Perryville by every measure a
soldier counts") but is immediately and structurally undercut:
- The win is explained as the product of **Union dysfunction**, not Confederate skill:
  "not because the South had the numbers, but because most of the North's numbers never got
  into the fight" (§3); "between the acoustic shadow keeping Buell ignorant and Gilbert
  holding back the men in the center, the Union never brought its real weight to bear" (§3).
- Section 4's title — **"Winning the Field, Losing the State"** — and "the thing that turned
  his victory to ash" deflate any triumph.
- The Maney "high-water mark" line is correctly attributed to a historian (Noe) as a
  paraphrase, NOT asserted in house voice as glory. Good — this is the spot where a Lost
  Cause draft would let the narrator cheer; this one quotes the historian and moves on.
There is no admiring adjective attached to Bragg, Polk, Maney, or the Confederate soldier.
Courage under fire (Cleburne wounded and "kept going," Maney's three charges) is reported as
combat fact, not lionized. **FAIR.**

### 2. Slavery soft-pedaling. **FAIR — none found.** See FIRST CHECK. The draft does the
opposite of soft-pedaling: it makes the slave-state aim the headline. The word "slavery" /
"slave state" appears ~7 times across the section, always in plain causal language ("widen
slavery's reach," "pried out of the Union"). No euphemism ("states' rights," "Southern way
of life," "the peculiar institution") substitutes for it.

### 3. False both-sides balance. **FAIR.** The "both halves are real" construction in §5
refers narrowly and correctly to the *tactical-vs-strategic* military outcome — a genuine
both-true fact — NOT to the war's morality or cause. The draft never balances slavery
against an equivalent Northern aim, never implies the sides were morally symmetric. The one
asymmetry it draws is explicitly moral: "one army moving to widen slavery's reach, a federal
government openly moving to end it."

### 4. Confederate romanticization. **FAIR.** The Polk dusk-bluff anecdote (§4) is the one
"colorful Confederate" set piece, and it is handled correctly: rendered as "is said to" /
"as reported," flagged in the ledger as unverified, and not used to make Polk dashing — it's
framed as the last gasp of a doomed attack ("calling off the attack on account of the
darkness"). No "Lost Cause" cast of noble cavaliers; Bragg in particular is treated coolly
(his feud with subordinates, his abandonment of ~900 wounded named without flinching).

### 5. Euphemism / sanitizing the human cost. **FAIR.** The cost is named bluntly and
proportionately — "roughly one in five of all the troops engaged became a casualty," "two
Union generals… died within sight of each other," town of 300 became "one vast field
hospital," "laid out by the thousand." The abandonment of the Confederate wounded ("around
nine hundred of his own wounded") is stated plainly, not minimized. Not gratuitous either —
no lingering gore for effect. Human reality is neither sanitized nor exploited. **FAIR.**

### 6. Unfair sympathy / civilians. **FAIR.** The Boyle County civilians (§2 sidebar) are
treated with measured sympathy ("had not chosen this fight over a national question") that
does not become a Lost Cause "innocent invaded South" move — note the invading army here is
Confederate, and the draft is clear about that ("Bragg had brought a government to Kentucky
on the points of his bayonets"). Sympathy is for noncombatants caught between armies, not for
the Confederate cause.

### 7. Presentism. **FAIR.** No anachronistic moralizing voice; emancipation is dated to the
contemporaneous Sept 22 proclamation, not projected backward. The judgment that the campaign
was about extending slavery is grounded in 1862 fact (a slave state, a Confederate governor,
the EP), not imposed from the present.

## WEB VERIFICATION (framing-load-bearing claims only)

- **Campaign goal = bring the slave state Kentucky into the Confederacy.** CONFIRMED.
  Wikipedia (Confederate Heartland Offensive) and American Battlefield Trust both state the
  goal was to bring Kentucky into the Confederacy; Davis viewed the "Union slave-owning
  border state of Kentucky" as winnable. The draft's slave-state framing is historically
  accurate, not editorial overreach.
- **Strategic Union victory / Kentucky held for the war.** CONFIRMED (Battlefield Trust,
  NPS, Britannica). Draft's outcome verdict is standard.
- **Hawes inauguration / preliminary EP Sept 22.** Consistent with the standard record; the
  draft already renders the Hawes speech as paraphrase and flags it for the fact-checker
  (correctly out of this gate's scope).

Optional context surfaced in verification (NOT a framing defect): the recruitment hope of
25,000–30,000 produced only ~2,500 actual Kentucky enlistments — Kentuckians largely did NOT
rally to the Confederacy. The draft states the *goal* (25,000–30,000) without the *failure to
meet it*. Including the shortfall would, if anything, further deflate the Lost Cause "Kentucky
yearned to be Southern" myth. This is a SHOULD-FIX-adjacent storytelling opportunity, not a
framing distortion — handing to the storytelling/comprehensiveness gate, not blocking here.
