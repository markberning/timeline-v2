## 1. STRATEGIC CONTEXT: Why Britain and France Collided in the Ohio Country

### The Ohio Country Defined
The Ohio Country was a loosely defined region of colonial North America west of the Appalachian Mountains and south of Lake Erie — the upper Ohio River basin, roughly what is today western Pennsylvania, eastern Ohio, and northern West Virginia.

**Source:** Wikipedia, "Ohio Country."

### The Fur Trade Stakes
Both France and Britain coveted the region for the fur trade and for strategic control linking their North American empires. The French position: control of the Ohio Country would connect New France (Canada) to Louisiana and the French Illinois Country, forming an arc that would hem British colonists east of the Appalachians indefinitely. The British position (particularly Virginia): the Ohio Valley was legitimately part of Virginia's charter claim and would anchor westward expansion.

The critical difference in colonial practice: the French preferred hunting and trade over permanent agricultural settlement, which made them less immediately threatening to Indigenous lifeways. British colonists actively sought to settle and farm, which meant displacing Native peoples.

**Source:** Wikipedia, "Ohio Country"; Heinz History Center, "Constructing the French Forts of the Ohio Country."

### The Ohio Company of Virginia
- **Founded:** 1748
- **Key members:** Thomas Lee, Lawrence Washington (George's older half-brother), Augustine Washington Jr., Robert Dinwiddie (Virginia's Lieutenant Governor — see below), George Mason, John Mercer, Robert Carter III, John Tayloe II.
- **Land grant:** The British Crown granted **500,000 acres** in the Ohio Valley between the Kanawha River and the Monongahela, issued 1749. Structure: 200,000 acres initially, with 300,000 more contingent on settling 100 families within seven years.
- **Goal:** Settle the Ohio Country, trade with Native Americans, and block French expansion.
- **Washington connection:** George Washington was both an investor in the company and later the military officer deployed to defend its interests. His older half-brothers were on the board.

⚠️ **Contested/note:** Dinwiddie's dual role — colonial governor AND Ohio Company stockholder — is a significant conflict of interest that historians note as a driver of Virginia's aggressive posture in the Ohio Country. The French and Indian War connection is direct: Governor Dinwiddie sent Washington on his 1753 mission partly to defend the company's interests.

**Source:** Wikipedia, "Ohio Company"; NPS, "Robert Dinwiddie."

## 2. FRENCH FORT CONSTRUCTION, 1753–1754

In spring 1753, the Marquis Duquesne de Menneville (Governor General of New France) ordered a major military expedition to assert French sovereignty over the Ohio Country. The expedition was commanded by Paul Marin de la Malgue (rank: ensign/officer; French colonial; died October 29, 1753 during the campaign). Approximately **2,000 French troops** marched south from Fort Niagara to build a chain of fortifications.

### The Fort Chain (Lake Erie to the Forks of the Ohio)

| Fort | Modern Location | Construction Start | Notes |
|------|----------------|-------------------|-------|
| **Fort Presque Isle** | Erie, PA | May 15, 1753 | First depot; rectangle 75×105 ft with corner bastions; 12-ft horizontal log walls |
| **Fort Le Boeuf** (Fort de la Rivière au Bœuf) | Waterford, PA | July 11, 1753 | Eight 6-lb cannon per bastion; stockade bastions 12+ ft tall |
| **Fort Machault** (also called Venango) | Franklin, PA | August 1753 (completed late 1757) | French seized/occupied existing British trading post at Lenape village of Venango, at junction of French Creek and Allegheny River; 13-ft picketed walls |
| **Fort Duquesne** | Pittsburgh, PA (Point State Park) | April 1754 | Built on the Forks of the Ohio (confluence of Allegheny + Monongahela → Ohio); named for Governor Duquesne; small square fort with bastions and ravelins; known weakness: "low, swampy, and prone to flooding" |

**Note on Fort Duquesne's construction:** The French had to capture an unfinished British post first (see Section 4 below).

**Sources:** Heinz History Center, "Constructing the French Forts of the Ohio Country"; Wikipedia, "Fort Presque Isle"; Wikipedia, "Fort Le Boeuf"; Wikipedia, "Fort Duquesne."

## 3. WASHINGTON'S 1753 MISSION TO FORT LE BOEUF

### Authorization
In October 1753, Lieutenant Governor Robert Dinwiddie (British colonial official; Virginia's de facto governor, acting as royal agent; personal Ohio Company investor) dispatched 21-year-old Major George Washington to deliver a formal ultimatum to the French commander: leave the Ohio Valley.

Washington was approved by the Virginia Council on October 27, 1753. On October 31, Dinwiddie delivered Washington his commission, instructions, and £150 to cover expenses.

**Source:** Founders Online, "Commission from Robert Dinwiddie, 30 October 1753"; NPS, "Robert Dinwiddie."

### Washington's Party
- George Washington (Virginia militia, age 21) — **NOTE for author: no side-tag in prose per house rules**
- Jacob Van Braam — Dutch-born former Dutch army lieutenant, Washington's fencing instructor and French language teacher in Virginia. French speaker (though not native). Would later play a critical role at Fort Necessity.
- Christopher Gist — frontiersman, Ohio Company trader and guide
- Tanaghrisson (the Half-King) — joined the party at Logstown; provided escort and indigenous diplomatic legitimacy

**Source:** American Battlefield Trust, "Washington in the Backcountry."

### The Journey
- October 31, 1753: Departed Williamsburg, VA
- Late November 1753: Reached Logstown (modern Ambridge, PA); met with Native leaders; Tanaghrisson joined the party
- December 4, 1753: Arrived at Fort Machault / Venango. Met French Captain Philippe-Thomas Chabert de Joncaire (French colonial officer), who stated: **"It was their absolute Design to take Possession of the Ohio."** [direct quote, source: American Battlefield Trust]
- December 11, 1753: Arrived at Fort Le Boeuf (modern Waterford, PA)
- December 12, 1753: Washington met Captain Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre (French colonial commander at Fort Le Boeuf), who received him politely but rejected the ultimatum firmly. Saint-Pierre's reply asserted France's "incontestable" claim to the Ohio Valley. His key line: **"As to the summons you send me to retire, I do not think myself obliged to obey it."** He forwarded Dinwiddie's letter to Marquis Duquesne in Quebec and said he would maintain his post awaiting orders.
- December 16: Washington departed Fort Le Boeuf
- December 29: Nearly drowned crossing the ice-choked Allegheny River
- January 16, 1754: Reported to Dinwiddie in Williamsburg

**Outcome:** Washington's detailed journal of the journey was immediately published by Dinwiddie to publicize the French threat to London and to the other colonies.

**Sources:** American Battlefield Trust, "Washington in the Backcountry"; Wikipedia, "Fort Le Boeuf"; Fort LeBoeuf Historical Society.

## 4. THE FORKS OF THE OHIO: BRITAIN LOSES THE FOOTHOLD

### Fort Prince George / Trent's Fort (January–April 1754)
After Washington's report, Dinwiddie ordered construction of a British fort at the strategic Forks of the Ohio. Construction began in **January 1754**, commanded by Captain William Trent (Virginia militia) with approximately 41 Virginians.

The fort was called "Trent's Fort" in common usage; the name "Fort Prince George" was not formally proposed until September 1754, when Dinwiddie used it in a letter to London.

**On April 17, 1754**, before construction was complete, approximately **600 French soldiers and Native allies** under Claude-Pierre Pécaudy de Contrecœur (French colonial commander) arrived with at least four cannons and demanded surrender. The small garrison surrendered and was allowed to leave. The French immediately dismantled Trent's Fort and began building Fort Duquesne on the site, burning Trent's Fort in July 1754.

**Sources:** Wikipedia, "Fort Prince George"; Wikipedia, "Fort Duquesne"; Heinz History Center.

### Washington's 1754 Expedition
After his 1753 mission report, Washington was promoted to Lieutenant Colonel and ordered to march troops westward to support Trent's fort construction. He departed in April 1754 with approximately 159 men. He learned of the fort's fall en route.

**Source:** American Battlefield Trust, "Washington in the Backcountry."

## 5. JUMONVILLE GLEN — MAY 28, 1754

### Strategic Context Immediately Before
Washington established a base camp at the Great Meadows (Fayette County, PA) — a natural clearing offering water and grazing. He received word from Tanaghrisson that a French scouting party was camped in a ravine about 7 miles away.

### The Ambush
- **Date:** May 28, 1754
- **Location:** A rocky glen near present-day Hopwood and Uniontown, Fayette County, PA (site is now part of Jumonville Glen, maintained by the NPS)
- **Duration:** Approximately 15 minutes

**Forces:**
- British/Colonial: ~40 Virginia provincial troops under Lieutenant Colonel George Washington + 12 Mingo warriors under Tanaghrisson
- French: 35 soldiers (some sources say ~29–35; see note) under Ensign Joseph Coulon de Villiers, Sieur de Jumonville

On the night of May 27, Washington's force marched through darkness to locate the French camp. At dawn on May 28, Washington consulted with Tanaghrisson before approaching. The French had posted no sentries.

⚠️ **Contested — who fired first:** French survivors claimed the Virginians fired without warning. Washington reported that a Frenchman fired first from the camp. The origin of the first shot is unresolvable from surviving evidence.

Washington's men delivered volleys into the French camp. The 15-minute engagement ended with a French surrender — then Tanaghrisson intervened (see below).

### Jumonville's Status: Diplomat or Spy?

This is a central contested question with genuine historical stakes:

**French position:** Jumonville was on a peaceful diplomatic mission to deliver a summons/message to Washington demanding British withdrawal from French-claimed territory. The French argued he was a protected envoy; his killing was therefore an assassination.

**British/Washington position:** The party "concealed themselves rather than openly approaching" Washington's nearby camp, which suggested espionage or military reconnaissance. Why would a diplomatic envoy hide in a ravine?

⚠️ **Scholarly note:** Neither side's account is self-serving-free. The ambiguity was almost certainly intentional on the French side: Jumonville's orders likely gave him flexibility to act as either envoy or scout depending on circumstances, a standard practice of 18th-c. frontier command. The "diplomatic" framing was immediately weaponized by Louis Coulon de Villiers (Jumonville's brother) to shape the Fort Necessity capitulation document.

**Source:** Wikipedia, "Joseph Coulon de Jumonville"; American Battlefield Trust, "Jumonville Glen."

### Casualties

| Side | Killed | Wounded | Captured | Escaped |
|------|--------|---------|----------|---------|
| French | ~10–14 (including Jumonville) | — | 21 | 1 |
| British/Colonial | 1 | 2–3 | 0 | — |

⚠️ **Numbers note:** 18th-century casualty figures are contested. Most reliable scholarly range for French dead: 10–14 (Wikipedia gives 14 killed/wounded combined; NPS Fort Necessity gives 17 dead or wounded total; Battlefields.org gives 14 killed/wounded). The most consistent figure is 10 killed + 21 captured + 1 escaped. One survivor made his way back to Fort Duquesne and reported the attack.

**Sources:** NPS, "Jumonville Glen"; Wikipedia, "Battle of Jumonville Glen"; American Battlefield Trust.

### Tanaghrisson's Role and Motives

This is arguably the most historically important dimension of the engagement — Tanaghrisson was not Washington's subordinate ally but an independent actor with his own agenda.

**Background:** Tanaghrisson (c. 1700–October 4, 1754) was born likely Catawba, captured young and adopted by the Seneca. He emerged as a leader among the Mingo (Iroquois settlers who had migrated to the Ohio Country). He was titled "the Half-King" — a role the British established to give him formal standing as a spokesman/viceroy for Ohio Country Indigenous peoples.

⚠️ **The Half-King title is contested:** The Dictionary of Canadian Biography calls it "obviously of English origin," distinguishing it from the French equivalent. Modern historians argue the title was likely a "British invention" — the Grand Council at Onondaga viewed Ohio Mingos as warriors without formal delegated political authority, not as official viceroy-class representatives.

**His position in Ohio Country politics:**
- The Iroquois Confederacy (Six Nations / Haudenosaunee) at Onondaga claimed nominal authority over the Ohio Country "by right of conquest" (the Beaver Wars), but Ohio peoples (Mingo, Shawnee, Delaware/Lenape) operated with significant de facto independence.
- The Mingo position was precarious: neither fully aligned with the Six Nations in the east nor with the Ohio nations (Shawnee, Delaware) whose interests often diverged.
- Tanaghrisson had lived at Logstown (modern Ambridge, PA), maintaining influence over regional tribes. By 1754, however, his power base had eroded — he was "living as a refugee near the Forks with a small group of relatives and followers" (American Battlefield Trust / Smithsonian).

**Why Tanaghrisson killed Jumonville:**
Multiple scholars converge on the same analysis — Tanaghrisson had independent motives:
1. **Personal grievance:** He claimed French soldiers had killed, boiled, and eaten his father. His anti-French feeling was visceral and personal.
2. **Strategic provocation:** By killing Jumonville and then sending a messenger to the French claiming Washington was responsible, he was deliberately forcing a crisis between the two European powers to compel British military action against France in the Ohio Country — without being blamed himself.
3. **Power restoration:** He had lost influence with his own people. Getting Britain to expel the French was his path back to relevance.
4. **Diplomatic humiliation:** The French had dealt with Tanaghrisson dismissively, exposed his lack of real influence, and threatened his band with death. The killing was partly revenge.

**What Tanaghrisson actually did at Jumonville Glen:**
After the initial French surrender, Tanaghrisson approached the wounded Jumonville. According to multiple accounts (with variations):
- Denis Kaninguen (a French deserter) reported Tanaghrisson said: **"Tu n'es pas encore mort, mon père!"** ("Thou art not yet dead, my father!") — then delivered hatchet blows that killed him.
- Another account (Private John Shaw) describes Tanaghrisson approaching the wounded French commander and killing him with a tomahawk, then scalping him.
- A third (widely cited) account adds that Tanaghrisson washed his hands in Jumonville's brains — a symbolic act that would have carried specific Indigenous ritual meaning (the cleansing of a deeply personal grievance).

⚠️ **All accounts are disputed and contradictory.** The sources for the most graphic details are French-derived (the deserter account) or retrospective. The core fact — that Tanaghrisson personally killed Jumonville after the surrender — is accepted by most historians. The precise manner is uncertain.

**Tanaghrisson's warriors then killed and scalped all but one of the French wounded.**

Washington's criticism of Tanaghrisson after Fort Necessity: Washington later criticized him for withdrawing his warriors before the July battle. Tanaghrisson, for his part, called Fort Necessity **"that little thing upon the meadow"** and complained that Washington "would not take advice" from his Native allies. [Source: NPS, "Tanaghrisson"; Wikipedia, "Tanacharison."]

**Post-Jumonville fate of Tanaghrisson:** He withdrew his warriors before Fort Necessity (July 3) and retreated with approximately 80–100 Mingos first to Wills Creek, then to Aughwick, then to John Harris's farm near modern Harrisburg, PA. He died there of pneumonia on **October 4, 1754**. His successor as Half-King was Scarroyady.

**Sources:** NPS, "Tanaghrisson the Half-King"; Dictionary of Canadian Biography, "Tanaghrisson"; Wikipedia, "Tanacharison"; American Battlefield Trust, "Jumonville Glen."

### Washington's Immediate Reaction — A Famous Quote

Washington wrote to his brother John Augustine Washington on **May 31, 1754** (the letter in Founders Online is dated "31 May 1754"):

> **"I can with truth assure you, I heard Bulletts whistle and believe me there was something charming in the sound."**

[Primary source: Founders Online, National Archives, "George Washington to John Augustine Washington, 31 May 1754." Also quoted at Mount Vernon's digital history project.]

⚠️ **The King George II response:** It is widely reported that King George II, learning of Washington's remark, replied that Washington "would not find bullets so charming if he heard them more often" (or similar wording). This is usually attributed to secondary sources; the exact royal text has not been verified here from a primary document.

## 6. FORT NECESSITY (THE GREAT MEADOWS) — JULY 3, 1754

### Washington Builds the Fort
After Jumonville Glen, Washington retreated to Great Meadows and constructed a hasty stockade he called "Fort Necessity" — a circular palisade of upright split logs approximately **seven feet high and about fifty feet in diameter**, surrounded by earthen trenches. Construction was completed June 3, 1754.

⚠️ **Tactical problem:** The site was tactically indefensible. The surrounding hills commanded the fort; anyone on the wooded hillsides could fire down into the enclosure. Washington either did not recognize this or had no better option. The trenches flooded in heavy rain.

**Source:** American Battlefield Trust, "Assassins and Avengers: The Battle of Fort Necessity."

### Forces

**British/Colonial (Washington and Mackay):**
- ~293 Virginia Regiment troops (Lt. Col. George Washington) — **NOTE: no side-tag for Washington per house rules**
- ~100 South Carolina regulars (Captain James Mackay, British regular officer — this created a command tension: Mackay's regulars technically outranked provincial troops)
- Total: approximately 393 men

**French/Canadian (Louis Coulon de Villiers):**
- ~600 French regulars (troupes de la marine) and Canadian militia
- 100–300 Native American auxiliaries (Algonquin, Odawa, Huron sources noted; numbers vary widely by source)
- Total: approximately 700+ men
- Commander: Captain Louis Coulon de Villiers (French colonial officer) — **Jumonville's brother.** He specifically requested command of this punitive expedition.

**Sources:** American Battlefield Trust; Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Necessity."

### The Battle — July 3, 1754

- June 28: French force departs Fort Duquesne
- July 1: Washington's forces withdraw to Fort Necessity after an unsuccessful advance
- July 2: Heavy rain begins; trenches start flooding
- **July 3, ~11 AM:** French forces arrive and attack commences
- French troops took positions in surrounding woods and on the hillsides, firing down into the fort with near-impunity
- Heavy afternoon rain made the situation catastrophic: the trenches became "pools of slop and muddy water"; Washington's men's gunpowder was rendered wet and useless
- After approximately **9 hours** of fighting, Washington requested a truce around 8 PM

**Casualties:**

| Side | Killed | Wounded | Captured/Prisoners |
|------|--------|---------|-------------------|
| British/Colonial | 31 | 70 | 369 (paroled) |
| French/Allied | 3 | 19 | — |

⚠️ **Note:** The "369 captured" figure likely represents paroled soldiers allowed to leave under the surrender terms, not prisoners held. All sources agree British casualties were much heavier.

**Sources:** Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Necessity"; American Battlefield Trust.

### The Surrender and the "Assassination" Document

This is the defining political incident of Phase 1 and the diplomatic fuse that escalated the frontier clash into a casus belli.

Louis Coulon de Villiers, seeking revenge for his brother Jumonville's death, drafted the surrender terms in French. The document contained, in Article VII, the phrase **"l'assassinat du Sr. de Jumonville"** — "the assassination of Sieur de Jumonville."

**Jacob Van Braam** (Dutch-born, French language teacher, Washington's translator — see Cast below) translated the document for Washington, who could not read French. Van Braam was a native Dutch speaker translating French into English; his native language was neither. He reportedly rendered the critical term as "the death of" or "the killing of" Jumonville, not "the assassination."

Washington and Captain James Mackay signed the capitulation on July 3, 1754. Both Washington and Mackay were allowed to march out with honors — drums beating, flags flying, carrying one small cannon and personal baggage. Two hostages were left behind: Jacob Van Braam and Captain Robert Stobo, to be held until captured French soldiers were returned.

**Terms for the garrison:** Promise not to return to the Ohio Valley for one year.

**Washington's lifelong position:**
> "Every Officer, then present, is willing to declare, that there was no such Word as Assassination mentioned; the Terms expressed to us, were 'the Death of Jumonville.'"

[Source: Mount Vernon / Founders Online, "Articles of Capitulation, Fort Necessity," and Washington's July 19, 1754 statement quoted in American Battlefield Trust.]

Washington maintained for the rest of his life that it was an error of translation and that he never would have signed a document admitting to an assassination.

**The diplomatic impact:** To the French crown and French public, Washington's signature was an admission that he had ordered the murder of a peaceful diplomatic envoy. This gave France its justification for escalating the conflict to full-scale war. Louis Coulon de Villiers is believed to have inserted the language deliberately.

**Sources:** American Battlefield Trust, "Assassins and Avengers"; Mount Vernon, "Articles of Capitulation"; Wikipedia, "Battle of Fort Necessity"; Wikipedia, "Jacob Van Braam."

## 7. THE ALBANY CONGRESS AND FRANKLIN'S PLAN OF UNION (JUNE–JULY 1754)

The Albany Congress ran concurrently with the Ohio Country military crisis — a fact that underlines how poorly coordinated Britain's colonial response was.

### "Join, or Die"
On **May 9, 1754** — just 19 days before Jumonville Glen — Benjamin Franklin published his famous woodcut cartoon in *The Pennsylvania Gazette* (Philadelphia). The image: a snake cut into eight segments, each labeled with the abbreviation of a colony or colonial region. Caption: "JOIN, or DIE."

- Snake cut into **8 segments** (not 13): New England as one unit; Delaware omitted as part of Pennsylvania; Georgia excluded
- Published approximately one month before the Albany Congress convened
- Context: Franklin wrote that "The Confidence of the French in this Undertaking seems well-grounded on the present disunited state of the British Colonies..."
- Considered the first American political cartoon
- Later repurposed (c. 1765–66) for anti-British resistance during the Stamp Act era

**Source:** Wikipedia, "Join, or Die."

### The Albany Congress
- **Dates:** June 19 – July 11, 1754, City Hall, Albany, New York
- **Host:** James DeLancey (New York Governor), chairman
- **Attending colonies:** Connecticut, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, New York, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island (7 of 14 British North American colonies — notably absent: all southern colonies, New Jersey, Nova Scotia, Newfoundland)
- **Primary purposes:**
  1. Repair the relationship with the Iroquois Confederacy / Six Nations — the Mohawk had declared the Covenant Chain broken in 1753 after losing approximately 800,000 acres through fraudulent New York land dealings
  2. Coordinate common colonial defense against French expansion

### Franklin's Plan of Union
Franklin proposed unified colonial governance:
- A President-General appointed by the Crown
- A Grand Council with 2–7 delegates per colonial assembly, apportioned by tax contribution
- Federal jurisdiction over Indian affairs, military preparedness, and trade
- Coverage of all British North American colonies

The delegates **unanimously approved** the Plan and submitted it with recommendations to both the colonial legislatures and the British Crown.

**Why it failed:**
- Colonial legislatures rejected it, citing protection of their independent charters
- The British Colonial Office rejected it, preferring a military command structure to civilian colonial self-governance

**Long-term significance:** Elements of the Plan influenced the Articles of Confederation (1777) and the U.S. Constitution (1787).

**Sources:** Wikipedia, "Albany Congress"; Wikipedia, "Join, or Die."

## 8. THE QUOTE: "A VOLLEY FIRED BY A YOUNG VIRGINIAN..."

The famous quote commonly attributed to Horace Walpole:

> **"A volley fired by a young Virginian in the backwoods of America set the world on fire."**

⚠️ **SERIOUS ATTRIBUTION PROBLEM — FLAG FOR AUTHOR:**

Multiple independent investigations (including J.L. Bell's exhaustive 2020 research for the Boston 1775 blog, which searched Walpole's correspondence) have failed to find this quote in Walpole's actual writings. The quote is widely attributed to Walpole's *Memoirs of the Reign of King George II*, but the sourcing trail is murky — and the phrasing appears to originate with 19th-century American historian Francis Parkman, who may have invented it and falsely attributed it to Walpole.

**What is verified:** The sentiment that Washington's frontier skirmish ignited a global war is historically sound — the war that began in Ohio in 1754 became the Seven Years' War (1756–63), a genuinely global conflict fought in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and the Philippines.

**Recommendation for author:** Use the idea and credit the sentiment as "long attributed to Horace Walpole" and flag it as possibly misattributed, or paraphrase without direct quote. Do not present it as a verified Walpole quote.

**Sources:** Boston 1775 blog (J.L. Bell), "Source of 'the volley fired by a young Virginian'?" (May 2020); multiple web searches confirming the attribution dispute.

## 9. NATIVE NATIONS AS FIRST-CLASS ACTORS

### Nations in the Ohio Country (1754)
The Ohio Country was home to several Indigenous nations with distinct positions:

- **Mingo** (Ohio Iroquois): Iroquois-speaking people who had migrated from the northeast to the Ohio Valley in the 1720s–1740s. Tanaghrisson was their most prominent leader in 1754. Generally aligned with British interests but not reliably.
- **Shawnee:** Algonquian speakers; initially allied with the French in the early French and Indian War, switched to the British in 1758.
- **Delaware / Lenape:** Algonquian speakers; had moved from Susquehanna to the Allegheny River in the 1720s under Iroquois pressure. Largely sided with France after 1754 (seeing British settlers as the greater long-term threat).
- **Seneca** (part of Six Nations): Tanaghrisson had Seneca identity/affiliation. The Six Nations formally maintained neutrality in 1754 but individual leaders like Tanaghrisson did not.
- **Iroquois / Six Nations / Haudenosaunee (Onondaga council):** Formally claimed authority over Ohio Country peoples; actually had limited real power over them. The Council declared neutrality in 1754; this was partly why Tanaghrisson's alignment with the British was both valuable to Washington and difficult — he was operating outside Onondaga's official position.

### The Covenant Chain Crisis
The Covenant Chain — the series of alliances and treaties between the Haudenosaunee and British colonies dating to the 1680s — had been formally declared broken by the Mohawk in 1753. The Mohawk chief Hendrick told New York officials the alliance was severed after the colony allowed fraudulent land seizures of approximately 800,000 Mohawk acres.

This diplomatic rupture was a major reason why the Albany Congress convened in June 1754: restoring the Covenant Chain was as important as colonial defense coordination. (The Congress partially addressed Iroquois grievances but did not fully restore the alliance.)

### What Each Major Group Actually Wanted (1754)

**Tanaghrisson / Mingo:** Use British force to expel the French from the Ohio Country. Keep both European powers weak enough that Ohio peoples remained the essential middlemen in the fur trade and in regional diplomacy. Restore personal prestige and Mingo influence.

**Shawnee and Delaware:** Maintain their own independence. By 1754, many leaders in both nations leaned toward France specifically because British settlement (not just British trading) was the greater threat to their land base. The French built trading posts; the British built farms.

**Six Nations / Haudenosaunee (Onondaga):** Preserve Iroquois authority over Ohio Country peoples while playing Britain and France against each other — the strategy of "playing both ends" that had preserved Iroquois power for decades. Official neutrality was the instrument.

**Source:** Wikipedia, "Ohio Country"; NPS, "Tanaghrisson"; multiple web search results on Ohio Country Native alignment.

## 10. CONTESTED / UNCERTAIN — SUMMARY

1. **Who fired first at Jumonville Glen?** Unresolvable. French said unprovoked British volley; Washington said a Frenchman fired first. ⚠️

2. **Was Jumonville a diplomat or a spy?** Genuinely ambiguous. His orders were likely flexible. His concealment in a ravine undercuts the "peaceful envoy" claim; the French had strategic reasons to frame him as an envoy regardless. ⚠️

3. **The exact manner of Jumonville's death.** Most historians accept that Tanaghrisson personally killed him after the surrender. The "washed hands in his brains" detail comes from accounts friendly to the French and is disputed. ⚠️

4. **Tanaghrisson's tribal identity.** Born Catawba, adopted Seneca, leader of Mingo — all sources agree on this compound identity. The "Half-King" title's meaning and legitimacy are debated: likely a British invention, not an official Iroquois designation. ⚠️

5. **Force numbers throughout.** 18th-century numbers are frequently imprecise and politically shaped. Use ranges where possible (see Sections 5 and 6 above). ⚠️

6. **The Walpole quote.** Likely misattributed — possibly invented by Francis Parkman. Do not present as verified. ⚠️ (See Section 8.)

7. **The Van Braam translation.** Was it honest error or deliberate? Van Braam was later held hostage by the French for years. Most historians treat it as incompetence (Dutch → French → English mental translation). But Washington was never 100% sure, and Van Braam never gave a fully satisfying explanation. ⚠️

8. **Fort Necessity casualty figures.** The "369 captured" figure likely means paroled under surrender terms, not held prisoner. The 31 killed / 70 wounded is consistent across sources. ⚠️

### A. Tanaghrisson's Parting Words About Washington / Fort Necessity

**Verified form:** The complaints attributed to Tanaghrisson about Washington after the 1754 campaign survive in secondary-source paraphrase and in one cluster of direct-quoted phrases. The key quotes, as they circulate in the scholarly literature and are cited back to a single secondary source, are:

- **"that little thing upon the meadow"** — Tanaghrisson's scornful name for Fort Necessity (Great Meadows).
- Washington was described as a **"good-natured man but had no Experience."**
- Tanaghrisson complained that Washington **"took upon him to command the Indians as his Slaves"** and **"never consulted with us nor yet to take our Advice."**
- A separate account (recorded via NPS/secondary sources, attributed to Indian informants after Fort Necessity) states that Tanaghrisson and his warriors abandoned Washington before the battle **"because Col. Washington would never listen to them, but was always driving them to fight by his Directions."**

**Who recorded this:** The quotes are attributed in the scholarly apparatus to **George Croghan**, the Irish-born fur trader and Indian agent who debriefed Tanaghrisson and other Ohio Country leaders at Aughwick (Croghan's trading post, modern Shirleysburg, PA) in early September 1754 — approximately two months after Fort Necessity and a month before Tanaghrisson's death on October 4, 1754. The Aughwick council was a diplomatic gathering of Ohio Country refugees and leaders.

**Primary document:** The complaints were recorded in Croghan's papers / correspondence, later published in the colonial records. The specific document has not been independently accessed in this session, but the scholarly chain is clear.

**Citable secondary source:** **Nicholas B. Wainwright, *George Croghan: Wilderness Diplomat* (University of North Carolina Press, 1959), p. 65.** This is the footnoted citation in Wikipedia's "Tanacharison" article for the entire cluster of complaints (the "meadow" quote, the "slaves" charge, and the "no experience" characterization). Fred Anderson's *Crucible of War* (Vintage Books, 2001) also quotes Tanaghrisson's criticism of Washington (described at p. 6 of that work) and is independently citable.

**Exact verbatim status:** The phrases **"took upon him to command the Indians as his Slaves," "never consulted with us nor yet to take our Advice,"** and **"that little thing upon the meadow"** appear in secondary literature as direct quotes from Tanaghrisson (via Croghan's record), but the underlying primary document (Croghan's September 1754 account) has not been directly transcribed from an archive in this session. The phrases are cited verbatim in multiple scholarly and semi-scholarly sources all pointing to Wainwright 1959, p. 65.

**What cannot be confirmed here:** The specific phrase "would by no means take advice from the Indians" as an exact verbatim quote distinct from the above. That formulation is a paraphrase; the closest confirmed direct-quote versions are "never consulted with us nor yet to take our Advice" and "took upon him to command the Indians as his Slaves." Author should use those phrasing or flag as paraphrase.

**Note on Conrad Weiser:** Weiser (Pennsylvania's senior Indian agent and German-born interpreter) attended the same September 1754 Aughwick council and recorded Native grievances from that meeting. His account is in the Pennsylvania Colonial Records, vol. VI. Weiser is a plausible co-source for the Tanaghrisson complaints, and some secondary sources implicitly blend the Croghan and Weiser accounts from that period. The Wainwright p. 65 citation is the pin historians use.

**Author guidance:** Use the paraphrased substance confidently; flag the most dramatic phrasings as "as recorded by Croghan" or cite Wainwright. The phrases are multiply attested and the scholarly consensus treats them as genuine. Do NOT attribute the specific phrase "would by no means take advice" as a verbatim Tanaghrisson quote — that is a modern paraphrase of the above.

### B. The Treaty of Logstown (1752)

**Verified form:**

**What and where:** A diplomatic council held at Logstown (the Shawnee/Mingo village on the south bank of the Ohio River, modern Ambridge, Pennsylvania, approximately 18 miles below the Forks). The council convened **between 1 and 13 June 1752** (some sources give the council as running May 28 – June 13; the operative treaty proceedings are June 1–13).

**Parties:**
- **Virginia commissioners:** Colonel Joshua Fry, Lunsford Lomax, James Patton
- **Ohio Company representatives:** Christopher Gist, William Trent, William Beverley
- **Native leaders:** Tanaghrisson ("Thonariss, called by the English the Half King"), representing Six Nations/Iroquois interests in the Ohio Country; also Delaware (Lenape), Shawnee, and other Ohio nations present. Tanaghrisson was the principal Native negotiator and presided over internal Native proceedings, including appointing Shingas as Delaware king.

**What Virginia sought:** Two things: (1) Native confirmation that the 1744 Treaty of Lancaster had ceded Ohio Country lands to Virginia/Britain, clearing the legal path for the Ohio Company's 500,000-acre grant; (2) formal consent to build a British fort at the **Forks of the Ohio** (mouth of the Monongahela River, modern Pittsburgh).

**The Lancaster 1744 connection:** Tanaghrisson explicitly challenged the colonial interpretation at Logstown, stating that the Iroquois at Lancaster in 1744 "never understood...that the Lands then sold were to extend further to the Sun setting than the Hill on the other Side of the Allegany Hill" — i.e., the 1744 cession did not extend beyond the Allegheny Mountains. Despite this reservation, he was "eventually forced" (under pressure from the Virginia commissioners and Ohio Company agent Andrew Montour) to formally consent to British settlements on the "southern or eastern parts of the River Ohio" and, crucially, to allow fort construction.

**The fort consent:** Tanaghrisson himself formally requested the fort — in the treaty record he asked for "a strong House, at the Fork of the Mohongalio, to keep such Goods, Powder, Lead & necessaries." Virginia commissioners clarified this required permanent English settlement; the Half-King ultimately accepted. This consent is what gave Ohio Company operations their political legitimacy.

**Why this explains Tanaghrisson's view of the French fort chain:** When the French built their chain of forts in 1753 — Fort Presque Isle, Fort Le Boeuf, Fort Machault — and then seized the Forks to build Fort Duquesne in April 1754, they were not merely a European rival expanding into contested territory. From Tanaghrisson's perspective, they were violating a diplomatic agreement he had personally ratified at Logstown two years earlier: an agreement that called for a *British* fortified trading post at the very location the French now occupied. The French forts were a betrayal of an arrangement he had brokered; his urgency to provoke British military action was inseparable from that diplomatic humiliation.

**Legitimacy caveat:** Tanaghrisson held "figurehead" (half-king) authority and could not legally bind the Onondaga Grand Council. Both the Ohio Company and Virginia knew this; they used the Logstown agreement for political cover, not as a binding legal instrument. The Iroquois Grand Council at Onondaga never ratified the Logstown terms and maintained nominal neutrality in 1753–1754.

**The Ohio Company land grant context:** The Crown's 1749 grant to the Ohio Company was 500,000 acres between the Monongahela and the Kanawha, conditional on settling 100 families within seven years. The Logstown treaty was explicitly organized to open that land for settlement on schedule.

**Primary document:** "The Treaty of Logg's Town, 1752," published in *Virginia Magazine of History and Biography*, vol. 13 (1906), pp. 154–174. Digitized and available via the Treaties Portal at the University of Nebraska Lincoln (treatiesportal.unl.edu/earlytreaties/treaty.00004.html).

### C. The Escalation Mechanism — From 1754 Ohio Skirmish to Global War

**Verified chain with exact dates:**

**1. The Ohio spark (May–July 1754)**
Washington's ambush at Jumonville Glen (May 28, 1754) and defeat at Fort Necessity (July 3, 1754) were the opening shots. Fighting remained in the colonial Ohio Country; no formal war existed. Britain and France were nominally at peace.

**2. Britain sends regulars — Braddock's expedition (1755)**
In early 1755, Britain dispatched Major General Edward Braddock with approximately 2,000 regular army troops to North America. On **July 9, 1755** (some sources give July 13 as the date of Braddock's death), Braddock's force was ambushed near Fort Duquesne and destroyed; Braddock died of his wounds on **July 13, 1755**. This was a decisive escalation: regular British troops had now been bloodied in an undeclared war with France.

**3. Undeclared fighting expands (mid-1755)**
The British navy began harassing and seizing French merchant and military vessels **beginning August 1755** without a formal declaration of war, initiating an undeclared naval war. Admiral Boscawen had attacked French ships in the Strait of Belle Isle as early as June 1755. Hundreds of French ships were seized. Both sides escalated on land and sea while technically remaining at peace.

**4. The Diplomatic Revolution (January–May 1756)**
The colonial fighting triggered a realignment of the great European alliances — the "Diplomatic Revolution" or "reversal of alliances":

- **Convention of Westminster: 16 January 1756.** Britain and Prussia signed an alliance. Britain agreed not to aid Austria in a renewed conflict for Silesia; Prussia agreed to protect Hanover (George II's German possession) from French attack. This broke the existing British-Austrian alliance.
- **First Treaty of Versailles: 1 May 1756.** France and Austria, in direct response to the Westminster Convention, signed a defensive alliance. Both sides agreed to remain neutral and provide 24,000 troops if either got into conflict with a third party. This broke France's existing alignment and flipped the European alliance system: Austria switched from a British ally to a French ally; Prussia switched from a French ally to a British ally.

**5. Formal declarations of war (May–June 1756)**
- **Britain declared war on France: 17 May 1756.** This is the date given by the official declaration document ("Given at Our Court of Kensington, the Seventeenth Day of May, 1756"), confirmed by History.com, HistoryPod, and the Alabama Gazette. Note: some secondary sources give May 20 as a publication or proclamation date, but the declaration itself is dated May 17. Author should use **17 May 1756**.
- **France declared war on Britain: June 1756.** Multiple sources confirm France's formal declaration came in June 1756, after Britain's May declaration. The exact June date has not been pinned to a single day in the sources consulted; it is safely described as "June 1756." Author should not assign a specific June date without further archival confirmation.
- **Frederick the Great invaded Saxony: August 29, 1756**, beginning the European land war and formally opening what became the Seven Years' War (1756–1763) in Europe.

**6. The war spreads globally**
Once formal war was declared, fighting spread rapidly across all major theaters:
- **Europe:** Prussia vs. Austria + France (August 1756 onward); the core of the Seven Years' War proper
- **North America (French and Indian War):** Already underway since 1754; continued through 1763
- **India:** Anglo-French struggle for dominance in the Carnatic and Bengal; Battle of Plassey (1757); Britain secured dominance by 1760
- **Caribbean:** Britain vs. France for sugar islands (Guadeloupe, Martinique, Dominica); Britain captured most French Caribbean islands by 1759–1762
- **West Africa:** Anglo-French contest for Senegal and slave-trade posts; Britain captured French West African holdings
- **Philippines:** Britain captured Manila from Spain in 1762 (after Spain entered via the Family Compact, August 1761)

**What this chain means for the narrative:**
A frontier skirmish by a 22-year-old Virginia militia officer in May 1754 directly set in motion: a British military escalation (1755), a naval war (mid-1755), a complete realignment of European great-power alliances (January–May 1756), formal declarations of war (May–June 1756), and a genuinely global conflict across five continents. The "world on fire" claim is historically verifiable and not hyperbole.
