## Headline verdict

The draft is **unusually newcomer-friendly for a war section** — it self-defines most
of its hard terms inline (siege/investment, earthworks, running the guns, saps, mines,
USCT) the first time they appear. That is the house rule working. The remaining gaps
are concentrated in a handful of **military-unit words** that are used as if known
(corps/brigade/division/regiment/company, sloop-of-war/frigate/gunboat) and a few
**named actors and places dropped without a scene-set** (Milliken's Bend, Fort Wagner,
the 54th Massachusetts, Bayou Sara, Telegraph Road, Trans-Mississippi, "parole").
Nothing is so dense it overwhelms. **No section rates LOST overall**; the worst
individual items are NEEDS-GLOSS.

**MUST-FIX count: 6 · SHOULD-FIX count: 14**

## Cross-cutting jargon — the military-unit ladder (whole draft)

This is the single biggest systemic gap. The draft uses, across sections, an entire
hierarchy of unit and rank words **without ever once anchoring the scale**:

- **corps · division · brigade · regiment · company** — appear as
  "his divisions," "Brigadier Generals ... striking together," "the regiments Banks
  committed," "Leading Company E of the 1st Native Guard." A cold 15-yo has no idea
  which is bigger, how many men each holds, or how they nest. The prose mostly
  survives because raw numbers (30,000; 7,500) carry the scale — but every time a unit
  word appears, the reader is asked to nod at a concept never taught.
- **Major General vs. Brigadier General** — no rank ladder given (see Section 1).
- **sloop-of-war · frigate · gunboat** — ship-class ladder, never anchored
  (Section 2, already flagged MUST-FIX).

**Recommendation:** one short, plain anchoring on first contact would fix the whole
chain — e.g. when "Company E" or the first "division" appears, a parenthetical telling
the reader a company is ~100 men, a regiment ~1,000, a brigade/division progressively
bigger. This is the war vertical's standing newcomer trap; the draft glosses *fortification*
jargon impeccably (earthworks, saps, mines, parapet-by-imagery) but leaves *organizational*
jargon bare. **SHOULD-FIX** as a cluster (no single instance is blocking), but doing it
once early pays off across all five sections.

## MUST-FIX list (consolidated — 6)

1. **Define the two sides up front.** "Union/North" and "Confederacy/South" are used
   from sentence one of Section 1 with no statement that the country had split into two
   warring sides and who they are. Add one orienting clause early in `the-last-lock`.
   *(Section 1)*
2. **Define "siege/besieging" on first use.** The word appears in the Section-1
   *Meanwhile* block before its meaning ("surrounded, dug out, starved or stormed")
   arrives in Section 2. Pull a one-line gloss to first contact. *(Section 1)*
3. **Gloss the ship classes.** "sloops-of-war / frigate / gunboats" dropped together
   with no sense of the size hierarchy the prose then relies on. At minimum: "warships,
   from big sloops-of-war down to small gunboats." *(Section 2)*
4. **Gloss "paroled."** Civil-War parole (released on a promise not to fight until
   exchanged) is the *opposite* of the modern meaning and is set in direct contrast to
   "kept as prisoners," which actively confuses a newcomer. *(Section 4)*
5. **Tag/scene-set "Lee" + "Gettysburg."** Both appear only in *Meanwhile* blocks
   assuming the reader knows Lee is the South's top general and Gettysburg an Eastern
   battle. Add a side-tag "(the South's leading general)" and a half-clause placing
   Gettysburg. *(Sections 4 & 5)*
6. **Anchor the unit scale once.** The corps/division/brigade/regiment/company ladder is
   used throughout with no scale ever given. One early parenthetical (company ≈ 100,
   regiment ≈ 1,000, etc.) anchors the whole draft. *(cross-cutting; place in Section 3
   at first "division"/"Company E")*

## What the draft does RIGHT (so revisions don't break it)

- **Strategic stakes are taught, not assumed** — the river logic, the Red River artery,
  the two-fort lock, the Vicksburg dependency are all explained in plain cause-and-effect.
- **Fortification + siege-engineering jargon is self-glossed at first use** —
  earthworks, running-the-guns, saps, mines are textbook newcomer-onboarding.
- **The cause of the war (slavery) is stated in the draft's own voice** and doubles as
  newcomer orientation (Section 3 framing passage).
- **Cailloux is fully scene-set** before his death lands.
- **No passage is genuinely overwhelming**; the densest spot (four generals named at
  once, Section 3) is survivable as a blur. Keep these strengths; the fixes are
  additive glosses, not rewrites.
