## 1. JANUARY 1775 — THE STANDOFF

- **Boston, garrisoned and sealed.** Gen. **Thomas Gage** (commander-in-chief in North America AND royal governor of Massachusetts since May 1774) holds Boston with roughly **3,000–4,000 regulars** (commonly given as ~4,000 by spring 1775); the port is closed under the Coercive Acts; the town of ~**17,000** lives alongside its garrison. (HIGH on the situation; MED on the exact troop count — use "about four thousand" or a range.)
- **The Association enforcing.** The First Continental Congress's **Continental Association** (effective 1 Dec 1774) is being enforced by local **committees of inspection/observation** — non-importation policed town by town, violators published and shunned. Imports from Britain collapse in 1775 (the often-cited figure is a ~95–97% drop vs 1774). (HIGH on enforcement; MED on the percentage — phrase softly or omit the number.)
- **The Provincial Congress arming.** Massachusetts's royal government has effectively ceased outside Boston. The **Massachusetts Provincial Congress** (first met Oct 1774, John Hancock presiding) governs the countryside, with a **Committee of Safety** empowered to call out the militia. It is openly stockpiling arms, powder, and cannon — much of it at **Concord** and **Worcester** — and has directed towns to organize **minute companies**: roughly **a quarter to a third** of each town's militia enrolled to turn out "at a minute's warning." (HIGH on the Congress/stores/minute system; MED on the exact fraction — use the range.)
  - **Minuteman hygiene:** these were the **organized town militia**, a system with roots in 17th-century Massachusetts — officers elected, musters held, alarm networks drilled. NOT spontaneous farmers grabbing muskets. Explain the system tersely; no romance. (HIGH)
- **London answers the First Continental Congress — three documents in three months:**
  - **Lord North's Conciliatory Resolution** — proposed **20 Feb 1775**, dated **27 Feb 1775**: any colony that taxed *itself* for defense and the support of government (to Parliament's satisfaction) would be exempt from parliamentary taxation. Carrot with strings: Parliament keeps the right, the colonies do the paying, and it pointedly bypassed the Continental Congress to deal with colonies one at a time. Reached America after the shooting started; Congress formally rejected it **31 July 1775**. (HIGH)
  - **The New England Restraining Act** — royal assent **30 March 1775**: restricted New England's trade to Britain, Ireland, and the British West Indies, and **barred New Englanders from the North Atlantic fisheries**. Stick. (HIGH)
  - **Dartmouth's secret letter to Gage** — **Lord Dartmouth** (Secretary of State for the Colonies) drafted it **27 January 1775**; it reached Gage in Boston on **14 April 1775**. It declared the province in open rebellion in all but name and ordered decisive action: the "first & essential step" should be **to arrest and imprison the principal actors and abettors in the Provincial Congress** — and ventured that with secrecy and precaution it "can hardly fail of Success, and will perhaps be accomplished without bloodshed." Hancock and Samuel Adams were the obvious (unnamed) targets. (HIGH — Clements Library holds the letter; the quoted phrases are from it, MED on exact orthography — paraphrase unless re-verified verbatim.)
  - **Four days after the letter arrived**, Gage moved — not against the leaders, but against the **stores at Concord**. (HIGH)
- **Both sides convinced the other would fold.** In London, the dominant view was that the colonists would not stand: the **Earl of Sandwich** (First Lord of the Admiralty) told the Lords in March 1775 the Americans were "raw, undisciplined, cowardly men." In America, the pattern of 1766 and 1770 (resist → Britain retreats) taught colonists that firmness produced repeal. Both were wrong at once. (HIGH on the mutual miscalculation as framing; MED on the Sandwich quote verbatim — widely reproduced, paraphrase if not re-verified.)

## 2. APRIL 19 — LEXINGTON AND CONCORD (what-happened scale)

### The night ride(s) — myth-hygiene mandatory
- Night of **18 April 1775**: Gage orders ~**700 regulars** (grenadier and light-infantry companies) under **Lt. Col. Francis Smith**, with **Maj. John Pitcairn** of the marines leading the advance, to cross the Charles by boat and march to Concord to seize the stores. (HIGH)
- **Dr. Joseph Warren** (the patriot leader running Boston's intelligence network) dispatches **two riders by different routes**: **William Dawes** out over Boston Neck ("by land"), **Paul Revere** rowed across the Charles to Charlestown ("by sea"). (HIGH)
- **The lanterns were a backup, not the message.** Revere had pre-arranged with Charlestown patriots that the sexton of Old North Church (**Robert Newman**) would hang **one lantern if the regulars marched out by land, two if by water** — a contingency signal in case Revere himself couldn't get out of Boston. He did get out, and carried the warning in person; the lanterns were the redundancy. (HIGH — per the Paul Revere House.)
- **What Revere said:** NOT "the British are coming" — the colonists **were** British and called the troops "the regulars" or "the King's troops." At the Hancock-Clarke parsonage in Lexington (~12:30 a.m.), when Sgt. **William Munroe** told him to stop making noise, Revere shot back: **"Noise! You'll have noise enough before long. The regulars are coming out!"** (HIGH that "the British are coming" is anachronistic; MED on the Munroe exchange verbatim — it comes from the sentry's later account; attribute as "by one account.")
- Revere and Dawes warned Hancock and Adams at Lexington, then rode for Concord with a third rider, **Dr. Samuel Prescott** (a Concord physician heading home). A British patrol stopped them in **Lincoln**: **Prescott jumped a wall and escaped — he is the one who reached Concord**; Dawes escaped but fell from his horse and turned back; **Revere was captured**, questioned at gunpoint, and released without his horse. He walked back to Lexington and was on the green at dawn helping carry Hancock's trunk of papers to safety. (HIGH)
- Beyond the famous three, the alarm propagated through a **pre-built network** — dozens of riders, bells, and signal guns fanning the word across the countryside. That system, not one horseman, is what put thousands of militia on the road by morning. (HIGH)

### Lexington green, dawn
- ~**5:00 a.m.**, Lexington: Capt. **John Parker**'s company — about **77 militiamen** — stood on the green as Pitcairn's advance companies came up. Parker's own sworn deposition (25 April 1775): he ordered them to assemble "to consult what to do," concluded **"not to … meddle or make with said Regular Troops (if they should approach) unless they should insult or molest us,"** and on the regulars' "sudden Approach" **"immediately ordered our Militia to disperse and not to fire."** (HIGH — National Archives holds the deposition.)
- A British officer (commonly said to be Pitcairn) rode forward shouting at the militia to disperse — wording reported variously ("Disperse, you rebels!" / "Lay down your arms, you damned rebels"). The militia were beginning to file off — **not laying down arms** — when a shot was fired. (HIGH on the scene; the shouted words MED — report as "by colonial accounts.")
- **THE FIRST SHOT IS GENUINELY UNKNOWN — never resolve it.** No one knows who fired first: a regular, a militiaman, an onlooker, an accident. Both sides swore the other began it (and both sides' depositions were taken for exactly that propaganda purpose). The regulars then fired volleys and charged with bayonets. (DISPUTED — this is the chapter's honesty test.)
- Toll on the green: **8 Americans killed, 10 wounded**; one regular lightly wounded. The whole affair took minutes. (HIGH)

### Concord and the bridge
- The column reached **Concord** mid-morning. Most of the stores had already been moved or hidden (the colonists had had warning for weeks — Revere had ridden to Concord on April 8, and the Provincial Congress had dispersed supplies). The regulars found and burned gun carriages and other materiel; the **town house caught fire by accident and regulars helped douse it** — but militia massing on the high ground across the river saw the smoke and concluded the town was being torched. (HIGH)
- ~**9:30 a.m., the North Bridge**: roughly **400 militia** advanced on the ~**90–100 regulars** holding the bridge. The regulars fired first there (killing two, including Capt. Isaac Davis of Acton); Maj. **John Buttrick** ordered the return volley — by tradition crying "Fire, fellow soldiers, for God's sake, fire!" — and the militia's fire killed **3 regulars** and drove the rest back. The first time in the war Americans fired on the King's troops **in ranks, under orders**. (HIGH on the action and numbers; MED on Buttrick's exact words — "by tradition." "The shot heard round the world" is Emerson, 1837 — a later phrase, never contemporary.)

### The road back — where the day's battle actually happened
- The column left Concord around noon; the **16-mile (26 km) return to Boston** became a running fight as militia companies — eventually ~**3,500–4,000 men** arriving in relays from dozens of towns — fired from walls, woods, and houses, with pitched fights at Meriam's Corner, the "Bloody Angle," and Menotomy (now Arlington), where the worst house-to-house killing of the day happened. (HIGH; militia total as a range)
- At Lexington, Smith's exhausted column was met by a relief brigade — ~**1,000 men and two cannon under Lord Hugh Percy** — roughly doubling the force; Percy's disciplined withdrawal got the column to Charlestown and the protection of warships' guns by dark. (HIGH)
- **Casualties (full day):** British — **73 killed, 174 wounded, 26 missing** (273 of ~1,700–1,800 engaged). American — **49 killed, 39–41 wounded, ~5 missing** (~93–95). Give these as firm-ish totals; the wounded counts vary by a couple across sources. (HIGH, with the small variance noted)
- Atrocity claims ran both ways (scalping allegation at the bridge; civilians killed at Menotomy) — keep to one careful beat or omit; the depositions weaponized them immediately. (MED — handle with care if used.)

## 3. THE NEWS WAR — the Atlantic race

- Within days the Provincial Congress took **sworn depositions** from participants and eyewitnesses (Parker's among them, taken 25 April) — built to prove **the regulars fired first** — plus an address "To the Inhabitants of Great Britain." This was a deliberate, organized propaganda operation, days old when it sailed. (HIGH)
- **The Quero.** The Congress (Warren driving it) sent the packet to London aboard the **62-ton Salem schooner *Quero***, Capt. **John Derby**, sailing **in ballast** (no cargo — speed only) on the night of **28/29 April 1775** — **four days AFTER** Gage's official dispatches had left on the heavier packet ***Sukey***. Derby made the crossing in about four weeks, reached London **28–29 May**, and delivered the American account (to Arthur Lee, the Massachusetts agent — Franklin had just sailed home). The American version ran in the London press with **no official version to answer it**: the government had nothing. (HIGH)
- **The Sukey** — with **Gage's official account** — did not arrive until about **9–10 June**, nearly **two weeks behind**. By then the patriot framing (the King's troops fired first on the King's subjects) had had the field to itself. (HIGH)
- Use this as the chapter's signature simultaneity beat: the war was a **messaging war from day one** — both sides understood within hours that *who fired first* mattered as much as who won the road. (HIGH as framing)

## 4. THE SIEGE BEGINS — AND MAY 10, TWICE

- **An army materializes.** Within days of April 19, New England militia — Massachusetts, Connecticut, New Hampshire, Rhode Island — converged on Boston; the lines ran from Roxbury around to Chelsea. Strength fluctuated wildly as men came and went, but the besieging force is commonly given as **~15,000** (ranging in sources up to ~20,000) against Gage's garrison. Command settled (loosely) on Massachusetts Gen. **Artemas Ward** from 20 April. The **Siege of Boston** — April 19, 1775 to March 17, 1776 — had begun. (HIGH on the siege; the army size as a RANGE, ~15,000–20,000, never a hard number.)
- **25 May:** HMS *Cerberus* arrived with ~reinforcements and three major generals — **William Howe, Henry Clinton, John Burgoyne** — bringing the garrison to roughly **6,000–6,500**. (HIGH on the generals/date; MED on the exact garrison figure — range.)
- **May 10, in the dark before dawn — Fort Ticonderoga.** **Ethan Allen** and his Green Mountain Boys and **Benedict Arnold** (carrying a Massachusetts colonel's commission for exactly this mission, but with no troops of his own that Allen's men would obey) — **unable to agree who commanded, they went in side by side** — surprised the ~50-man British garrison at the decayed lake fort and took it **without a death on either side**. The real prize: the fort's **heavy artillery**, which mattered eight months later (§10). Allen's famous demand — that he took the fort **"in the name of the Great Jehovah and the Continental Congress!"** — comes from **his own 1779 memoir**; a witness remembered something closer to "Come out, you old rat!" Tell it with the wink. (HIGH on the capture/date/joint command friction; the Jehovah line DISPUTED-as-verbatim — always attribute to Allen's later telling.)
- **May 10, the same day, Philadelphia:** the **Second Continental Congress** convened — summoned months earlier as a follow-up congress, now opening **three weeks into a shooting war** nobody had voted for. This same-day coincidence (a congress gaveling in as a king's fort falls to men acting in its name, without its knowledge) is the spine beat — use it. (HIGH)

## 5. JUNE 17 — BUNKER HILL (what-happened scale)

- Learning the British planned to seize the Charlestown and Dorchester high ground, the besiegers moved first: night of **16–17 June**, Col. **William Prescott** with ~1,200 men was sent to fortify **Bunker Hill** on the Charlestown peninsula — and dug in instead on **Breed's Hill**, lower and closer to Boston (more provocative, harder to hold; whether by order, council, or error is unsettled). By dawn they had thrown up an earth **redoubt** roughly 130 feet on a side. The battle is named for the hill they were *told* to fortify. (HIGH; the "why Breed's" question MED — leave it loose.)
- **17 June, afternoon:** ~2,200–3,000 British under **Howe** crossed and assaulted; **Charlestown itself was burned** by heated shot from the batteries and ships during the battle, hundreds of buildings lost. The defenders (~2,400–3,000 engaged across the field, militia rotating in and out) threw back **two frontal assaults** with close-range volleys; the **third assault**, with bayonets, took the redoubt as the defenders' **ammunition ran out**. The Americans retreated off the peninsula; the British held the ground. (HIGH; engagement strengths as ranges)
- **"Don't fire until you see the whites of their eyes"** — attributed variously to Putnam, Prescott, Stark, or Gridley; the scholarly consensus is that it is **legend at this battle** (the formula is older than the Revolution, going back to European drill). Don't put it in anyone's mouth. (DISPUTED)
- **The butcher's bill — the shock that defined the battle:** British casualties **1,054** of ~2,400–3,000 engaged — **226 killed, 828 wounded** — including a devastating share of officers. American losses ~**420–450** (commonly: ~115–140 killed, ~270–300 wounded, ~30 captured), the worst single loss being **Dr. Joseph Warren** — the patriot leader of §2, newly a major general, who fought as a volunteer in the redoubt and was killed in the retreat. (HIGH on 1,054/226/828; American figures as ranges.)
- **What it taught.** **Henry Clinton**, the night of the battle: **"A dear bought victory, another such would have ruined us."** **Gage**, to the Secretary at War days later: "The loss we have sustained, is greater than we can bear" — adding that these people showed a spirit "they never shewed against the French." London's takeaway: this would be a real war, needing a real army. The American takeaway (dangerously seductive): militia behind earthworks could maul regulars. Both lessons shaped 1776. (HIGH on the Clinton line; MED-HIGH on the Gage line — letter to Lord Barrington, 26 June 1775, widely reproduced; verify verbatim before quoting in full.)
- Within Boston the siege then **stalemated** for the rest of the year: neither side strong enough to break the other, the garrison increasingly hungry and sick. (HIGH)

## 6. CONGRESS ADOPTS THE WAR WHILE PLEADING PEACE (the both-directions beat)

*(Terse — `army-from-nothing` owns the institution story; `idea-of-independence` owns the political arc. This chapter carries the simultaneity only.)*

- **14 June 1775** — Congress voted to **adopt the New England army around Boston as a continental force** and to raise rifle companies from Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia: the **Continental Army**. (HIGH)
- **15 June 1775** — Congress unanimously chose **George Washington** of Virginia as commander-in-chief (the political logic — binding the South to a New England war — in one clause, no more). He took command of the ~14,000–16,000 men at **Cambridge on 3 July 1775**. (HIGH on dates; the famous "under the elm" ceremony is 19th-century legend — omit.)
- **5 July 1775** — the same Congress adopted the **Olive Branch Petition** (John Dickinson's work; signed 8 July): a direct, loyal appeal to George III, blaming his ministers, professing attachment to his person, begging him to halt the war for reconciliation. (HIGH)
- **6 July 1775** — the **very next day** — it adopted the **Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms** (Jefferson and Dickinson): a justification of armed resistance, while still **explicitly disclaiming independence**. (HIGH)
- The honest frame: this is not hypocrisy but a genuinely divided body holding both positions at once — an army and an olive branch out of the same room in the same week. **The king refused to receive the petition** (it reached London in August; see §9 for what answered it instead). (HIGH)

## 7. THE WAR SPREADS NORTH — CANADA (Sept–Dec 1775)

- The strategic idea: Canada was Britain's open back door (and, Congress hoped, a potential **14th colony**); the route Ticonderoga had opened pointed straight up **Lake Champlain**. A two-prong invasion was authorized in late June and moved in September. (HIGH)
- **Prong 1 — Montgomery up the lake.** Brig. Gen. **Richard Montgomery** (Irish-born ex-British officer) led the main force north: a grinding siege of **Fort St. Jean** (mid-September to its surrender at the start of November) ate the campaigning season; **Montreal fell without a fight on 13 November 1775**; Gov. **Guy Carleton** escaped downriver to Quebec. (HIGH)
- **Prong 2 — Arnold through the Maine woods.** **Benedict Arnold** (Ticonderoga's other claimant, hungry for a real command) left Cambridge in mid-September with ~**1,100 men** to reach Quebec via the Kennebec and Chaudière rivers — a route far longer and crueler than the maps suggested. The march's honest horror at one-beat scale: leaking bateaux, drowned supplies, freezing bogs; **Lt. Col. Roger Enos turned back with roughly 300 men and most of the food**; the rest ate **boiled shoe leather, cartridge boxes, candles, and a dog**. About **600** scarecrow survivors reached the St. Lawrence opposite Quebec in mid-November — an astonishing feat that arrived too weak to do anything alone. (HIGH; keep the suffering inventory to attested items above)
- **31 December 1775 — Quebec, in a blizzard.** Montgomery (down to a few hundred effectives after enlistments and sickness) joined Arnold; with their men's **enlistments expiring at midnight on New Year's Eve**, they assaulted the walled city in a snowstorm. It failed completely: **Montgomery was killed** by grapeshot in the first minutes at his prong, **Arnold's leg was shattered** leading the other, **Daniel Morgan** fought deepest into the lower town and was captured with the survivors — **~400–430 Americans taken prisoner**, against trivial British losses. Montgomery became the cause's first martyred general. The remnant under Arnold kept up a ragged "siege" outside the walls through the winter, but the Canadian gamble was lost on the year's last night. (HIGH; prisoner count as a range. NO doom-foreshadowing excess — tell it as the gamble it was: it nearly worked on courage alone, and didn't.)

## 8. THE WAR SPREADS SOUTH — VIRGINIA (Nov 1775–Jan 1776)

- **Lord Dunmore** (John Murray, 4th Earl of Dunmore, royal governor of Virginia) had lost the capital and governed from Royal Navy ships in the Chesapeake. His proclamation — **dated 7 November 1775, published ~14–15 November** — declared martial law AND offered freedom to **"all indented servants, Negroes, or others (appertaining to rebels)"** who would bear arms for the King. Several hundred to ~2,000 enslaved people reached his lines (his Black soldiers formed the "Ethiopian Regiment"). **One beat only — `slavery-and-the-revolution` owns this story**; here it is the simultaneity: an emancipation offer from a royal governor in the same season as the Olive Branch's rejection. (HIGH; escapee numbers as the 800–2,000 range)
- **Great Bridge, 9 December 1775** — Dunmore's small force (regulars, loyalists, Ethiopian Regiment) attacked Virginia troops under Col. **William Woodford** across a narrow causeway south of Norfolk and was bloodily repulsed: British losses reported from **~62 (Dunmore's figure) up to ~102**; the Virginians' total casualties: **one man wounded in the hand**. Woodford called it "a second Bunker's Hill affair, in miniature." Dunmore abandoned Norfolk for his ships. (HIGH; British loss as the range)
- **Norfolk burns, 1 January 1776 — HANDLE THE ATTRIBUTION PRECISELY.** Dunmore's ships **began it**: a naval bombardment from about 3 p.m., with landing parties burning waterfront buildings. But the town's destruction was **mostly the work of the Virginia and North Carolina troops in it** — who looted and torched the largely loyalist port over the following days, and whose own government then ordered the remnant demolished (completed early February, to deny the British a base). A **1777 Virginia commission** found patriot forces destroyed the overwhelming share (its findings put it near **96%**, vs a few dozen buildings by the British) — and the report **was not made public for some 60 years**, by which time "Dunmore burned Norfolk" was settled patriot fact and atrocity propaganda. State both halves: he started it; they finished it; the blame was pinned on him. (HIGH — Encyclopedia Virginia, JAR, Smithsonian all carry the corrected account.)

## 9. LONDON CLOSES THE YEAR (one braided beat each — `idea-of-independence` owns the arc)

- **23 August 1775 — the Proclamation of Rebellion** ("A Proclamation for Suppressing Rebellion and Sedition"), George III's response to the news of Bunker Hill: the colonies "have at length proceeded to an open and avowed Rebellion," and all officers and subjects are ordered "to use their utmost endeavours to withstand and suppress such rebellion." Issued **before** the Olive Branch Petition was even presented; the king **refused to receive the petition at all**. (HIGH — both quoted phrases verified against the text.)
- **26 October 1775 — the King's speech** opening Parliament: the rebellion "is manifestly carried on for the Purpose of establishing an independent Empire," its leaders' professions of loyalty insincere — and he announced expanded forces, including foreign troops. The irony to surface: **George III asserted the Americans wanted independence months before most of them admitted it to themselves.** (HIGH on date and the "independent Empire" line)
- **22 December 1775 — the Prohibitory Act**: all trade with the thirteen colonies prohibited, a blockade declared, **American ships and cargoes made lawful prize** — economically, treating the colonies as a foreign enemy. (When the news arrived in 1776, even moderates read it as a de facto declaration of war — feeds the next chapter; don't narrate ahead.) (HIGH)
- Braid each of these against what's happening that same week in America (the siege lines, the Canada march, Dunmore's ships) — that's this chapter's whole method. (Directive)

## 10. CLOSE — NEW YEAR 1776, EVERYTHING POISED

- **The army dissolves and re-forms in plain sight.** The 1775 enlistments expired **31 December**; Washington had to recruit a new army while holding siege lines within cannon shot of the enemy. His own astonishment, to John Hancock, **4 January 1776**: "It is not in the pages of History perhaps, to furnish a case like ours; to maintain a post within Musket Shot of the Enemy for Six months together, without [powder], and at the same time to disband one Army and recruit another, within that distance, of Twenty odd British regiments." **One beat — `army-from-nothing` owns the institution story.** (HIGH — verified at founders.archives.gov; the bracketed gap is in the original.)
- **Knox's guns on the road.** **Henry Knox** (25-year-old Boston bookseller turned artillerist) reached Ticonderoga in early December; **5–9 December 1775** his party loaded **59 cannon, mortars, and howitzers — nearly 60 tons** — and started them on a ~**300-mile (480 km)** winter haul: by boat down Lake George, then sledge via Fort Edward–Saratoga–Albany, across the frozen Hudson, and east over the Berkshires, arriving at **Framingham, ~20 miles west of Cambridge, 24–25 January 1776**. Knox's phrase, from his letter ahead to Washington: a **"noble train of artillery."** (HIGH on dates/counts/route; note — the iconic ox-team imagery is partly later embroidery, horses did much of the hauling — keep the transport generic: "sled teams.") (MED on the ox/horse detail itself; just avoid asserting oxen.)
- **The freeze-frame close:** a new army re-enlisting in front of the enemy; the Ticonderoga guns creeping east; a failed invasion bleeding in the snow outside Quebec; Norfolk smoldering; a king's blockade signed into law; and in Philadelphia that same month a pamphlet called *Common Sense* coming off the press — **name it at most in one clause; the idea chapter owns it.** Everything 1776 will detonate is loaded by New Year. (HIGH on each fact; the close is the simultaneity payoff.)

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **The first shot at Lexington is GENUINELY UNKNOWN — never resolve it.** Both sides swore the other fired first, and both sides' sworn accounts were produced *as evidence for a propaganda war*. The honest statement: a shot came from somewhere — nobody has ever established from where — and then the regulars' volleys did the killing. This unknowability IS the story; resist any phrasing that tilts it.
2. **Revere myth-hygiene.** He was **one of several riders in an organized network**; Dawes rode the same night by another route; **Prescott, not Revere, reached Concord**; Revere was **captured** in Lincoln and released on foot. "The British are coming" is **anachronistic** — they were all British; the attested form is "the regulars are coming out." The lanterns were a **backup signal**, not the warning itself. Longfellow (1861) built the solo-hero myth; don't re-ship it. (Revere's real ride is impressive enough told straight.)
3. **No minuteman romance.** The men of April 19 were **organized, officered, repeatedly mustered town militia** with a designated rapid-response tier (minute companies, roughly a quarter to a third of the rolls) — a system, not spontaneity. Explain the system in a sentence; skip the embattled-farmer sentimentality.
4. **"Whites of their eyes" — legend at this battle.** Attributed after the fact to Putnam, Prescott, Stark, AND Gridley; the order formula predates the Revolution in European armies. Scholarly consensus: don't put it in anyone's mouth at Bunker Hill.
5. **Ethan Allen's "Great Jehovah" line is his own 1779 memoir** — four years later, in a book starring Ethan Allen. A witness remembered a saltier demand. Usable only as "Allen later claimed," ideally with the wink that he also nearly wrote Arnold out of the story entirely.
6. **Norfolk's burning attribution — precision is mandatory.** Dunmore's bombardment and landing parties **started** the fire (1 Jan 1776); **American forces did most of the burning** (looting/torching over days, then their own government's ordered demolition of the rest); the 1777 Virginia commission put the patriot share near **96%** and its report was suppressed for ~60 years while "Dunmore burned Norfolk" did propaganda service. Both sides' roles, plainly; the myth's career is itself part of the story.
7. **Quebec without doom-music.** The campaign was a near-run gamble (St. Jean ate the season; the assault was forced by expiring enlistments), not a foreordained folly. Arnold's march should read as the genuinely heroic horror it was — and Arnold in 1775 must be written as the brave, ambitious officer he then was, with **zero traitor-foreshadowing**.
8. **Casualty figures as ranges.** Use: Lexington/Concord British 273 (73/174/26), American ~93–95; Bunker Hill British 1,054 (226 killed/828 wounded), American ~420–450; besieging army ~15,000–20,000; Quebec prisoners ~400–430; Dunmore's Great Bridge losses ~62–102; escapees to Dunmore ~800–2,000. Where sources differ, the pack's range governs — never invent precision.
9. **Both sides as reasoning actors.** Gage was a restrained commander executing direct orders he'd warned were under-resourced; Dartmouth's letter explicitly hoped for success "without bloodshed"; North paired coercion with a real (if unacceptable) conciliation offer. No tyrant caricature — the tragedy is mutual miscalculation, both capitals sure the other side would fold.
10. **Anachronisms to ban:** "the shot heard round the world" (Emerson, 1837) as if contemporary; "the British are coming"; the Washington elm ceremony; treating "minutemen" as the generic word for all militia.
11. **Stay in 1775.** No narrating ahead to Dorchester Heights/evacuation (March 1776), the Declaration, or *Common Sense*'s impact — name-at-most-one-clause per the close; the idea chapter owns that arc.
