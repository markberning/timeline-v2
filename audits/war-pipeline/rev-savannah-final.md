note: The war went global in one harbor. A French battle fleet raced up from the Caribbean ahead of the hurricane season, landed the most diverse army of the whole war (French regulars, over 500 free men of color from the colony that is now Haiti, Continentals, militia, and a Polish general's international legion), and tried to take back a town Britain had captured almost for free the winter before. The British answer was a 24-hour stall: while their commander asked for a day to think it over, a relief column waded the coastal creeks and made the garrison defensible. The betrayed dawn assault that followed cost the attackers about five men for every defender, in under an hour, and locked in the southern strategy that took Charleston the next spring.

casualties:
- Allied: around 244 killed, roughly 584 to 600 wounded, and about 120 captured or missing, a total near 950 by the rounded modern count, of which the French bore the bulk (roughly 650 of about 800 killed and wounded). The dead and dying filled the ditch in front of the works before the second wave arrived.
- British: about 40 killed, 63 wounded, and 52 missing, roughly 155 in all. Captain Thomas Tawse, commanding the dismounted Loyalist dragoons in the redoubt itself, was among the dead.
- footnote: The allied figure is honest only as a range: about 800 killed and wounded plus over 100 prisoners is the consolidated modern count, the American Battlefield Trust rounds the total to 948, and Prevost's 1,000-to-1,200 is the winner's inflation. The Chasseurs-Volontaires' own losses are not reliably itemized beyond the September 24 raid (one killed, seven wounded); no separate figure for October 9 survives.

outcome:
- verdict: British victory · the southern strategy's green light
- text: The allies lifted the siege within days of the failed assault. D'Estaing re-embarked his guns and his men, the fleet dispersed, and Lincoln marched back to Charleston. Franco-American relations hit their wartime low, the second straight joint operation after Newport to end in recrimination, and the recrimination ran both ways: the Americans resented being summoned to fight in France's name without their counsel, and the French resented an army they had supplied most of the blood to defend. ## SECTION: the-fleet-and-the-clock
EYEBROW: Savannah · The southern turn
TITLE: A fleet against a deadline

Britain already held Savannah. A small British force had taken Georgia's colonial capital on December 29, 1778, almost for free, and the siege this page is about is the allied attempt to take it back. Hold that fact, because the rest follows from it: by the summer of 1779, Britain had decided to win the whole war the way it had won this one town, from the bottom up. The reasoning ran like this. Two years of fighting in the North had taken the rebel capital of Philadelphia and won set-piece battles and ended the war not at all; then France had come in (the treaties were signed in February 1778, and Britain and France were shooting at each other by that summer), and the war went global, with the Caribbean sugar islands, the fortress of Gibraltar, and India all now competing with America for the King's ships and soldiers. So London changed the plan: hold New York, and shift the offensive to the South. The Colonial Secretary, Lord George Germain, told the British commander in chief, Sir Henry Clinton, that recovering the southern provinces was "considered by [the King] as an object of great importance in the scale of the war."

PILL: /war-revolution/world-war | The world war: how France's entry sent the war's whole weight south

The theory behind it was tidy on paper. The southern colonies were believed to be thick with Loyalists (colonists still loyal to the Crown) who would rise the moment a British army arrived and hold reconquered ground behind it with few regulars; southern exports of rice and indigo funded the rebellion's purchases in Europe; and taking Savannah and then Charleston would roll the whole rebellion up from its bottom edge. Britain also offered freedom to enslaved African Americans who would serve, a policy formalized that June in Clinton's Philipsburg Proclamation. And the theory had already been tested once, right here. The winter before, on December 29, 1778, a small British force under Lieutenant Colonel Archibald Campbell had taken Savannah at almost no cost: an enslaved man showed the British a path through the swamp around the American line, 600 redcoats came out behind it, and the defenders were routed at a cost to Campbell of barely two dozen men. Augustine Prevost (British) marched up from British East Florida to take overall command, and the royal governor came home. Georgia became the only rebel state where royal government was actually restored. The strategy, ten months in, looked like it was working.

Then a French battle fleet appeared off the Georgia coast, and for a few weeks the war's whole weight swung onto one harbor.

The fleet belonged to Charles Henri, the comte d'Estaing (French), a soldier-noble turned admiral who had had a frustrating American debut the year before and then won real prizes in the Caribbean: he took St. Vincent and Grenada in the summer of 1779 and fought a British fleet off Grenada to a bloody draw. Appeals from Charleston, from the American general Benjamin Lincoln (American), from South Carolina's government, and from the French consul, begged him to strike the British in Georgia before he sailed home. He came north with more than 20 ships of the line and about 4,000 troops, having sailed roughly 1,200 miles (1,900 km) up from his Caribbean base at Cap-Français, the main port on the north coast of Saint-Domingue (today Haiti), and appeared off the coast in the first days of September. And he came with a clock running against him from the moment he arrived. It was hurricane season, and his fleet lay exposed on an open coast with no harbor it could safely use; scurvy and dysentery were spreading through the crews; supplies were short; and his orders were to bring the fleet home. He had budgeted only days ashore. Every decision in this battle, the early summons, the impatience with a proper siege, the final gamble on a frontal assault, runs back to that clock.

What came ashore at Beaulieu plantation, on the Vernon River about 13 miles (21 km) south of town, starting on September 12, was the most diverse army the war would produce. There were French line regiments drawn from the Caribbean garrisons, and Dillon's regiment, Irish Catholics in French service under Colonel Arthur Dillon (French). There were South Carolina and Georgia Continentals (full-time regulars of the Continental Army) and militia (part-time citizen soldiers). There was the legion of Casimir Pulaski, the Polish nobleman Benjamin Franklin had sent to Washington as "an officer famous throughout Europe for his bravery," whom Congress had made "Commander of the Horse": about 200 cavalry and infantry drawn from Poles, Frenchmen, Germans, Irishmen, and Americans. The column leaders alone included a Swedish count, an Irish colonel, and a Polish general. And there were the Chasseurs-Volontaires de Saint-Domingue: roughly 545 men raised that spring in the French sugar colony of Saint-Domingue (the colony that is now Haiti) from free men of color and enslaved men promised their freedom for service. By the standard accounts they were the largest unit of soldiers of African descent to serve on either side in the entire war. Officially the French order of battle assigned them to labor and reserve duty, the order said they "were only to be used to dig trenches," but they would serve under fire and take casualties all the same. Add it up and the allied army numbered about 7,500, more than twice the garrison it had come to break.

It should have been enough. It was not, and the reason was a single day's truce a garrison asked for and got. At Beaufort, about 50 miles up the coast, a Scottish colonel named John Maitland had 800 men, a fever, and a knowledge of the inside water passage that the French and the Americans had both failed to watch.

MEANWHILE: The Caribbean | Where the fleet and the men came from | The army that landed at Beaulieu had sailed from a world the war rarely looked at. D'Estaing's fleet had spent the summer fighting in the West Indies, and its staging port and the recruiting ground for the Chasseurs-Volontaires was Cap-Français, on the north coast of Saint-Domingue (today Cap-Haïtien, in Haiti). The free men of color who filled the ranks of the Chasseurs came from a colony built on enslaved sugar labor, where their own legal status sat below the white colonists they lived beside: free, but barred from full civic rights, shut out of many professions, and required by law to defer. They were marching now to help one set of colonists win a freedom their own home would have to fight for a decade later. The Caribbean was not a sideshow to this war. For a few weeks in 1779 it sent its fleet and its soldiers straight into the middle of it.

## SECTION: twenty-four-hours
EYEBROW: Savannah · The stall
TITLE: Twenty-four hours

On September 16, 1779, before Lincoln's army had fully joined him, the comte d'Estaing (French) summoned Savannah to surrender. He did it in a way that would cost the alliance for the rest of the campaign: he demanded the town give itself up "to the arms of the King of France." The wording landed badly with the Americans, whose war this presumably was, and became a lasting grievance. It was also premature. D'Estaing had summoned the town before his siege guns were ashore and before Benjamin Lincoln's (American) army had fully arrived, on nothing but the moral force of a fleet in the river and the clock in his own head.

Augustine Prevost (British), commanding a garrison too thin to be confident, asked for twenty-four hours to consider. D'Estaing granted it. Standard siege custom gave a garrison the right to ask for time to consult, and d'Estaing, certain the town would yield, saw no harm in it. This was the masterstroke of the siege, and it belongs to the British as competence, not trickery: a commander who needed time asked for it, and got it, and used every minute. While the truce held (the formal ceasefire both sides observed while a surrender demand was under consideration), the Royal Engineer James Moncrief (British) kept his crews digging, and the truce's real prize came in by water. Lieutenant Colonel John Maitland (British) had been at Beaufort, on Port Royal Island up the coast, with about 800 men of the 71st Highlanders and other troops. Sick with fever himself, he brought them out of Port Royal and threaded them through the half-charted tidal creeks of the inside passage, behind Daufuskie Island, by Wall's Cut and Skull Creek, a water route that ran between the barrier islands and the mainland, hidden from the open sea where the French fleet lay, with local fishermen as guides, dodging the French ships in the sounds and Lincoln's army on land. It took him roughly four days to cover the fifty-odd miles. An intercepted dispatch boat in Skull Creek had even warned the Americans he was coming, and it made no difference, because the allies had left the one inside-water door they knew about unguarded: responsibility for watching it fell between the French navy and the American army, and so nobody did. Maitland's column reached the town during the truce window, and with those 800 men in the lines the garrison went from doubtful to defensible. Prevost sent his answer. There would be no surrender; he would fire a cannon at sundown to signal the truce was over and the fighting had resumed.

The popular version makes the truce a simple blunder by d'Estaing. That reading undersells the allied failure to guard a water route they knew about, and the fact that d'Estaing had summoned the town before he was ready to take it. The twenty-four hours did not lose the battle by themselves. They were the hinge on which a thin garrison became a held one.

After that the siege settled into its slow work. The allies dug their approaches and hauled heavy guns and mortars ashore from the fleet, a mule-starved, grinding process that did not have the artillery in position until early October, and all the while d'Estaing's naval officers pressed him daily about the exposed fleet, the sick crews, and the hurricane season. A British sortie (a sudden attacking raid launched by defenders from inside the fortifications) on September 24 hit the allied working parties in the trenches; the Chasseurs-Volontaires de Saint-Domingue were among the troops engaged, and took casualties, one killed and seven wounded by the documented count, the best-recorded action of the unit's siege. When the guns were finally ready, on October 4, the allies opened a bombardment of the town that ran for about five days: some 53 cannon and 14 mortars. It smashed houses and killed civilians and did almost nothing to the earthworks. One British officer wrote that "there was hardly a house that had not been shot through." An American officer wrote that "the poor women and children have suffered beyond description." Prevost asked leave to send the women and children out of the town, his own wife and children among them, and the Georgia general Lachlan McIntosh's (American) family inside the bombarded town as well; the allies refused, suspecting a ploy for time. It was standard siege practice on both sides, and it was grim.

By October 8 the fleet had been on an open coast for five weeks, the crews were sicker, and the clock that had been running since the day d'Estaing arrived was now running out of weather and ships. His engineers told him a proper siege needed perhaps ten more days; his naval officers gave him none. Rather than lift the siege and sail with nothing, he chose to storm the works at dawn on October 9, with the main blow falling on the Spring Hill redoubt (a redoubt being a small earthwork fort) on the British right, where the defenders were believed to be mostly militia.

They were not, because the plan had already crossed the lines. By the standard account a sergeant major of the Charleston Grenadiers named James Curry, who had overheard officers discussing the assault, deserted to the British and told Prevost where the main attack would fall and when. Prevost stacked the Spring Hill sector accordingly: not militia, but a mix of Loyalist provincials (Captain Thomas Tawse's dismounted dragoons, South Carolina Royalists, Thomas Brown's King's Rangers) backed by regulars, grenadiers, marines, and the 71st under Maitland's command of the right. The allies were about to attack the one point on the line the defenders had spent a day reinforcing, at the hour the defenders expected them.

## SECTION: spring-hill
EYEBROW: Savannah · What it meant
TITLE: The hour at Spring Hill

The columns formed in the dark around one in the morning on October 9, 1779, to attack before first light, and almost nothing went the way it was drawn. The fog and the swamps wrecked the timetable. The column of Colonel Arthur Dillon (French) lost its way in the Yamacraw swamp northwest of town and came out into the open under the British guns. A feint (a fake attacking move meant to pull defenders away from the real blow) by several hundred militia on the far left was easily stopped. Instead of striking together in darkness, the main columns went in piecemeal, fragmented rather than together, at first light, around half past five, against works whose defenders were awake and waiting.

At the Spring Hill redoubt the assault broke and died almost at once. Grapeshot (a cannon round that sprays a cloud of iron balls) and musket fire poured out of the works into the white-coated French columns, and the dead and wounded of the first wave filled the ditch in front of the redoubt before the second wave could reach it. The Americans of John Laurens (American) cut into the abatis (a belt of felled trees with sharpened branches, laid as an obstacle in front of the works) and reached the parapet (the earth wall at the top of the redoubt). Two lieutenants of the 2nd South Carolina, Hume and Bush, planted the regiment's colors on the rampart and were killed; a third officer holding them was mortally hit. Sergeant William Jasper (American), the man who had remounted the colors under British fire at Fort Sullivan three years earlier, was mortally wounded keeping a color up, and carried one off the field as the attack collapsed. The Swedish count Curt von Stedingk (French) got an American flag onto the entrenchment for a moment before the cross-fire shot his men off it; he came back with about twenty men, every one of them wounded, and remembered the retreat, with the cries of his dying comrades in his ears, as the bitterest moment of his life. For a moment it was the highest the allies got. The comte d'Estaing (French), leading on foot, was shot twice and stayed up.

Casimir Pulaski (American) had been waiting with about 200 horse to charge through the breach the infantry was supposed to open, the same man whose charge at Brandywine had covered the army's escape, now waiting for a gap that never came. When no breach opened, he rode forward into the smoke between the redoubts, by most accounts trying to rally the wavering infantry and find a way through. Grapeshot cut him down. He was carried off the field and taken aboard the privateer brig Wasp (a privately owned armed merchant vessel, not a Continental Navy ship), and he died about two days later, generally dated October 11. The father of the American cavalry, the man Franklin had sent over as famous throughout Europe for his bravery, was gone, and where his body went is a question that has never been fully settled.

After about an hour of this, d'Estaing ordered the retreat. The cost is honest only as a range. Around 800 allied soldiers were killed or wounded, plus over a hundred taken prisoner, the great majority of them French, in less than an hour. The British lost about 155 in all, roughly 40 of them killed; Captain Thomas Tawse died defending the Spring Hill redoubt. It was close to five allied casualties for every British one, one of the bloodiest hours of the war for the attackers.

As the shattered columns came back, the Chasseurs-Volontaires de Saint-Domingue stood in the reserve that covered their withdrawal. What they did there is the most carefully bounded thing on this page. The documented record is this: the roughly 545 men of the unit were at the siege, were officially assigned to labor and reserve duty, served under fire, and took casualties. Tradition holds more. Nineteenth-century memory, most popular retellings, and the monument later raised in Savannah credit them with covering the allied retreat outright and blunting a British counterattack, saving much of the army. Contemporary French records of a dramatic rear-guard stand on October 9 are thin, and scholars note that the unit is systematically occluded by the colonial archive, which cuts both ways: it neither proves the heroic version nor lets anyone erase the men who stood in the reserve while the columns came apart. What is safe to say is that the Chasseurs held the reserve that covered the broken army's withdrawal, and that tradition credits them with blunting the British counterattack and saving lives in the retreat.

The siege lifted within days. D'Estaing, wounded, re-embarked his guns and his men; the works were abandoned on October 17 and 18, the French fleet sailed and dispersed, and Lincoln marched back up the road to Charleston. The second straight joint operation, after the wreck at Newport the year before, had ended in failure and mutual blame, with the French and Americans pointing fingers at each other: the Americans over being summoned to fight in France's name without their counsel, the French over an army they had supplied most of the blood to defend. Franco-American relations hit their wartime low. The bruised alliance held, but barely, and that mattered, because the same French fleet the alliance would need to seal the trap at Yorktown two years later was the fleet that had just failed twice in a row. The southern war the British had wanted now had its theater. Sir Henry Clinton called the news "the greatest event that has happened the whole war," and proved he meant it. With the French fleet gone he sailed south from New York in December, laid siege to Charleston, and took it on May 12, 1780: Lincoln surrendered the city and some 5,000 men, the largest American capitulation of the war. The full southern war, Camden and Kings Mountain and Cowpens and Guilford Courthouse, and a civil war burning through the backcountry, followed from there. Savannah itself stayed British until July 1782.

The men who came back out of the trenches scattered into very different stories. Maitland was dead in Savannah by October 26, of the fever he had carried from Beaufort. Pulaski's bones became an argument that outlived everyone who knew him. His contemporaries split at once: shipboard accounts said he died aboard the Wasp and was buried at sea near the mouth of the Savannah, while a parallel tradition said his body was landed and buried at Greenwich plantation, near Thunderbolt outside the town. In 1854 remains from Greenwich were placed under a new Pulaski monument in Savannah's Monterey Square; those remains were exhumed in 1996, and after years of stalled identification reinterred with full military honors in 2005. Then, in 2019, a Georgia Southern-led team reported a finding that was messier than it is often retold: the team ran two rounds of mitochondrial DNA extraction, and the first sample comparison produced no match before a second pairing succeeded, matching a grandniece's profile and excluding roughly 99 percent of the population. With that, and with the skeleton found consistent with Pulaski's known injuries, they concluded the Monterey Square remains are most likely Pulaski's, which, if so, would confirm the Greenwich burial over the burial-at-sea story. The same study found that the skeleton bears female-typical features, leading the researchers to suggest, as a hypothesis and not a proven finding, that Pulaski may have been intersex. A 2022 article challenged the identification itself, the two-attempt sequence among its grounds. Both the identification and the hypothesis still have scholarly challengers, and the burial that the dispute began with is, after two and a half centuries, only most-likely settled.

Jasper got the opposite fate: not an argument, but a legend that grew over the plain facts. The documented core is William Moultrie's account, that the colors went up on the rampart and their bearers were soon killed, and that Jasper supported one of the colors until he took his death wound, brought it off with him, and died a little time after. Everything richer than that, the deathbed speech bequeathing the colors to the woman who had presented them, the heroic print of a man shot down planting the flag on the parapet, is later embroidery in the mold of the storytellers who mythologized his earlier exploits. He was a brave man who died hard in a failed assault. The legend made him a tableau.

And the men of Saint-Domingue carried Savannah home. The Chasseurs-Volontaires went back to a French sugar colony where the contradiction they had marched into, free and enslaved men of color fighting for a liberty their own home denied them, would explode within a dozen years into the Haitian Revolution. Veterans of the unit appear in the standard scholarship among the revolution's leaders; men like André Rigaud are said to have served at Savannah, and the unit's recruiting ground around Cap-Français supplied much of the revolution's officer class. Tradition puts the future king of Haiti, Henri Christophe, in the ranks at Savannah as a boy of about twelve, a drummer or servant with the unit; no contemporary muster roll proves it, and it is best told as the tradition it is. The men's memory eventually got its monument: in 2007 a Haitian Monument was dedicated in Savannah's Franklin Square, sculpted by James Mastin, honoring the Chasseurs-Volontaires, with a young drummer figure for the boy Christophe is said to have been. Its placard calls their stand "the most brilliant feat of the day," which is the monument's tribute rather than history's settled verdict, and the gap between those two things, the documented reserve and the honored legend, is exactly the gap this whole battle keeps asking you to hold open and look at honestly.

MEANWHILE: Spring Hill, 2005 | The ground comes back | For more than two centuries the exact line of the Spring Hill fortifications was lost under the growing city. In 2005 archaeologists located the works, the first physical remains of the battlefield to be found, and the city's Battlefield Memorial Park now marks the sector where the assault died. Three modern Army National Guard units trace their lineage to the siege's participants, and Charles Dickens, of all people, left the battle a small literary headstone: in Barnaby Rudge he gave the character Joe Willet an arm lost "defending the Salwanners." The most international hour of the war cast a long, strange shadow.
