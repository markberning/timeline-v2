## FINDINGS

### 1. Confederate-victory romanticization risk — FAIR (well-managed)
This is the hardest framing trap in a Confederate-win battle: the natural narrative (Jackson's brilliant 50-mile march, Lee at "the height of his powers," Longstreet's avalanche) invites admiring-the-villain tone. The draft admires the *generalship* but never the *cause*, and it repeatedly re-tethers every Confederate success to what it served:
- "the height of Lee's ascendancy… his reputation as a tactical commander was cemented" is immediately followed by the slaveholding-republic stake.
- The "what the victory unleashed" framing converts Lee's triumph into the thing that *backfired* into emancipation — the win is narratively subordinated to the defeat-of-slavery arc.
This is the correct handling. **FAIR.**

### 2. The "wild feasting" / "ate their enemy's dinner" beat — FAIR, monitor tone (SHOULD-FIX-adjacent, not framing-blocking)
The Manassas Junction looting is told as "wild feasting," "gleeful," have-nots eating the haves' dinner. This is historically grounded color and is *not* Lost-Cause romanticization (it's about hunger and class, not noble Confederates). It does not soft-pedal slavery. **No framing violation.** One light note for the integrator: the "lean men in rags… for one day, the have-nots ate the haves' dinner" framing leans sympathetic-to-Confederate-soldiers; that's acceptable (suffering of common soldiers is fair game and the draft tags the army correctly), but keep it from tipping into charm. Currently it does not tip. **FAIR.**

### 3. Human reality — FAIR (neither sanitized nor gratuitous)
The dead are concrete and not abstracted into arrows: Brawner's Farm "one of every three men engaged was shot"; the 5th New York's ~300 of 500 down in ten minutes, explicitly named as the worst single-battle regimental loss of the war; the Chinn Ridge math; the closing "The arrows were made of men like these." The casualty totals (~22,000) are given weight. This is the right register — vivid but not wallowing, and it serves the point rather than gore-for-gore's-sake. **FAIR.**

### 4. No false both-sides balance — FAIR
The draft does not "both-sides" the cause. The Union's evolving aim (restore the Union → end slavery) is stated honestly without inflating early-war Northern motives into pure abolitionism, and the Confederate aim is stated flatly as slavery. The Porter court-martial digression is internal-to-the-Union and does not create false moral symmetry. **FAIR.**

### 5. Presentism — FAIR
No anachronistic moralizing imposed on the actors; the moral judgment is carried by naming what the war was *for*, which is accurate to the period, not presentist. The closing "the men who fell… did not know it, but the road out of their defeat ran straight to the end of slavery" is dramatic irony, not presentism. **FAIR.**

### 6. Unfair sympathy — FAIR
Sympathy is distributed to the dead of both armies and to the wrongly-scapegoated Porter; it is not extended to the Confederate cause. Lee/Jackson/Longstreet are treated as formidable, never as sympathetic or righteous. **FAIR.**
