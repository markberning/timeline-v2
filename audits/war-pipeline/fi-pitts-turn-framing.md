# Framing/Fairness Critic — "Pitt's Turn" (French and Indian War)
**Draft:** `fi-pitts-turn-draft.md` | **Fact pack:** `fi-pitts-turn-factpack.md`
**Date:** 2026-06-02

---

## FIRST-CHECK RESULTS

### 1. Native nations as first-class actors with their own aims
PASS — but PARTIAL. The draft does give the Ohio/Lenape nations genuine agency: "the reconsidering was theirs," "for their own reasons and on their own terms." The betrayal is named directly and Pontiac's War is correctly identified as the consequence. The draft earns the core of this check.

PARTIAL GAP — SHOULD-FIX: The draft says the Ohio nations' pre-war calculation was binary (French = trader, British = farmer) but does not name what was driving Teedyuscung specifically. The Walking Purchase (1737) — the fraudulent Penn survey that stripped the Delaware of lands they hadn't agreed to sell — is entirely absent. This is NOT a historical footnote; it's the specific grievance Teedyuscung had been pressing at Easton conferences since 1756, and it explains why "land promises" were the right instrument to flip the alliance. Without it, the nations' calculation reads as strategically rational but generically so. The fact pack explicitly flags this gap and calls for "a full beat." It is close to a MUST-FIX (see below).

### 2. Human cost named
PASS. Carillon's bloodbath is named in unambiguous terms: "slaughterhouse," 1,600–2,000 casualties, 500+ dead, "the bloodiest battle of the entire war in North America." The draft does not flinch from it. Grant's defeat is named. Forbes's death is named. The section carries real human weight.

MINOR: Louisbourg's cost to the French side is mentioned (Drucour accepts hard terms "to spare the town's civilians") but the fact that the Mi'kmaq who fought throughout the siege had no representation in the capitulation terms and faced dispossession thereafter goes unmentioned. The fact pack acknowledges this gap ("their fate after the surrender...would enrich the narrative"). SHOULD-FIX.

### 3. Avoids triumphal "tide turns for the good guys" frame
MIXED — one clean failure. The draft's closing line is: "The losing empire of 1757 had, in a single year, rebuilt its war and turned it around, and 1759 would be the year the bill came due for New France." "The bill came due for New France" is Britain-as-protagonist framing; there is no acknowledgment in that closing beat that 1759 was also the year the bill came due for the people who had just been promised their lands would be safe. The draft correctly names the betrayal inside the Easton section, but the chapter-close summary ("The Tide at Year's End") recaps British gains without a single line about Native stakes going forward. The reader ends on triumphalism with no shadow of Pontiac. MUST-FIX.

## FULL FINDINGS

### FAIR
- The draft's framing of Pitt is complex and accurate: brilliant, arrogant, indispensable, and never present. No hero-worship. The Pitt-Newcastle arrangement is explained accurately.
- Abercromby is not pure-villain: the draft notes he "managed the logistics of that vast force capably enough to get it there," which is the accurate and balanced note the fact pack explicitly requests.
- The draft names the Ohio nations' reasoning correctly as rooted in a real calculation about who threatened their land, not as alliance-switching or "choosing sides."
- French voice: Montcalm's near-impossible defensive victory is treated with genuine respect. Drucour's acceptance of hard terms to protect civilians is named. French figures are not cartoons.
- Carillon receives proportional weight — one full section, the deadliest battle of the war, framed as the strategic exception that changed nothing.

### TILTED — SHOULD-FIX

**1. Walking Purchase omission diminishes Native agency (borderline MUST-FIX)**
The nations at Easton were not making a generic land-rights calculation — Teedyuscung had been pressing a specific fraud grievance (the 1737 Walking Purchase) at Easton since 1756. That fraud is why the British land promises were the key instrument: the nations wanted to get back what had been taken, not just a general assurance. The draft's current framing ("the French came to trade and garrison while British colonists came to clear forest and stay") is accurate but thin. Add one sentence naming the Walking Purchase and why Teedyuscung had been at this table for two years before 1758. This elevates agency from "smart calculation" to "a specific people pressing a specific wrong."

**2. Mi'kmaq fate after Louisbourg**
The draft correctly names the Mi'kmaq as participants and French allies who "fought alongside the garrison throughout the siege." But the capitulation section says nothing about what happened to them — they got no terms; their longstanding alliance relationship with the French was severed by British conquest; their post-siege situation was part of the same colonial dispossession story. Even one sentence noting they received no terms and faced their own reckoning would prevent the section from closing as a purely bilateral British-French affair.

**3. "Stepped aside" phrasing slightly underplays what the nations gave up**
"They chose, for their own reasons and on their own terms, to step aside" — this is elegantly put, but "step aside" slightly undersells the magnitude. These nations had been actively at war alongside France for four years. Withdrawing that alliance, on the strength of promises that turned out to be worthless, was a consequential choice they would pay for in 1763. The phrasing is not wrong, but something slightly stronger — "withdrew from a four-year alliance, on the strength of promises that would not survive the war" — would better match the stakes the betrayal section then describes.

### MUST-FIX

**1. Chapter close erases Native stakes (the "1759 bill came due" problem)**
The "Tide at Year's End" closing recapitulates British gains — Louisbourg, Frontenac, Duquesne, Easton — and ends with "1759 would be the year the bill came due for New France." This is unambiguously triumphal. The reader has already been told about the betrayal inside the Easton section, but the chapter's final beat lands on British victory with no shadow. Add one closing sentence or clause that holds the contradiction: 1759 was Britain's year of victories AND the year the Ohio nations began watching the promises made at Easton dissolve. The betrayal is already in the chapter — the close just needs to carry it forward rather than setting it aside. The "meanwhile" block on the Conquest of Canada compounds this; it uses "dominoes" language with no counterweight.

**2. Walking Purchase (promoted from SHOULD-FIX if chapter close is the only MUST-FIX)**
If the framing team judges the chapter close the single most urgent fix, the Walking Purchase is the next in line. Without naming the specific fraud driving Teedyuscung's two-year negotiating effort, the Easton section — which is explicitly called "the hinge of the entire western campaign" — treats Native strategic calculation as generically land-motivated rather than anchored to a documented specific wrong. This is the difference between first-class actors with their own history and first-class actors performing a readable but somewhat generic role.
