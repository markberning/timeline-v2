# the-retreat | The Hill Above the James

For a week, Gen. Robert E. Lee (South) had been hitting Maj. Gen. George B. McClellan (North) every single day, and McClellan had spent every single day backing away. This was the end of it. By the morning of July 1, 1862, the great Union army that had come up the Virginia Peninsula to take the Confederate capital at Richmond was in full retreat, marching south across that finger of land between the rivers toward the one thing that felt safe: the James River, where the United States Navy's gunboats waited to cover them with their heavy guns. The army was running. And the thing it was running from — the thing the whole bloody week had been about — was the survival of a slaveholding republic. Richmond was the capital of a new country founded to keep four million people enslaved, and an entire campaign had been built to capture it and end the rebellion in a stroke. Now that campaign was collapsing backward toward the river, and Lee smelled a chance to finish it for good.

## The Seven Days' end | The Hill Above the James

To understand the day, you have to understand the week. The fighting was called the Seven Days Battles (June 25 to July 1, 1862), and it was Lee's offensive — six engagements in a row, launched to drive McClellan away from Richmond and save the Confederate capital. Mechanicsville, Gaines's Mill, Savage's Station, Glendale — the names piled up, and after Gaines's Mill on June 27, McClellan made his decision: he would not fight his way into Richmond. He would withdraw the whole Army of the Potomac south across the Peninsula (the strip of Virginia land between the York and James rivers that the army had marched up) and put his back against the James, where Union gunboats could shield him. Lee chased the whole way, swinging at the retreating column, trying to cut it apart before it reached the water. At Glendale, on June 30, he nearly did, and missed.

By July 1, the Union rearguard had backed up onto Malvern Hill — and for the first time in the whole week, the entire Army of the Potomac stood united on one field. It was a good place to make a stand. Malvern Hill is a broad, flat-topped plateau, only about 100 feet above the country around it, but its strength was never really its height. Its strength was what it let you see and shoot. The north face — the side the Confederates would have to come up — was open, gently sloping farm field, with almost no cover for hundreds of yards, in places more than a mile. Any attacker had to walk uphill across that bare ground in full view the entire way. The James River lay just one to two and a half miles to the south, securing the Union rear. Boggy creeks, ravines, and dense woods guarded the flanks (the exposed sides of the army's line). It was, in plain terms, a natural fortress, and the men in blue knew it.

What turned a strong position into a death trap was Col. Henry J. Hunt (North), the army's chief of artillery (the officer in charge of the cannon). Artillery in this war meant cannon grouped into batteries — a battery being a cluster of roughly six guns that worked and moved together. Hunt's job that day was to mass them: to gather batteries side by side and tier on tier along the crest of the hill, rank behind rank, so that gun stood above gun all looking down the open northern slope. There is real confusion in the records about exactly how many cannon he had — different counts measure different things, the forward line versus the reserve versus every piece on and behind the hill — but the totals run up toward 250 guns altogether, stacked across the high ground. Hunt also wedged a battery of heavy 32-pounder howitzers (short, big-bored cannon that lob a heavy shell) onto the right flank. Behind the hill, in close support, sat the army's Artillery Reserve, fresh batteries ready to roll forward as the front guns burned through their ammunition. Down on the James, three Union gunboats — including the USS Galena — added their fire from the river. Hunt had built a wall of iron on the high ground, and he aimed all of it at the empty field below.

_ITALIC_: Lee had spent a week trying to destroy this army on the run. Now it had stopped running and turned to face him from the best ground it had found yet.

### Meanwhile in Richmond | The capital they were fighting over

Ten or so miles north sat Richmond, the prize and the reason for all of it. A month earlier, Union soldiers had been close enough to the city to hear its church bells; civilians had packed wagons, the government had readied trains to flee, and the fall of the Confederate capital had seemed near. Lee's whole offensive was built to push that danger back across the Peninsula — and as McClellan's army backed toward the James, the bells of Richmond fell quiet with relief. The capital of the slaveholding republic was no longer in immediate reach. The fight at Malvern Hill was the last act of the campaign meant to take it.

# it-was-murder | It Was Not War

Lee thought he could still destroy this army. He had been chasing it for a week, and at Glendale the day before he had come close to splitting it open. One more hard blow, he believed, and McClellan's retreating force might break apart entirely before it reached the safety of the river. So on July 1, looking up at the worst piece of ground he could possibly attack, Lee decided to attack it anyway. What followed was less a battle than a slaughter, and the man who watched it happen gave it the only name it has ever deserved.

## July 1 | It Was Not War

The plan was the problem before a single man stepped off. Lee meant to soften the Union position first with his own cannon — to plant Confederate batteries at separated spots, have them throw converging fire onto Hunt's guns, and silence them. Only after the Union artillery was beaten down would the infantry go in. It was a sound idea on paper and a disaster in practice. The Confederate guns came into action one at a time instead of all together, never managed their crossfire, and Hunt's massed batteries — firing first and firing from above — simply cut them to pieces piecemeal. D.H. Hill (South), one of Lee's division commanders, called the whole Confederate artillery effort "most farcical."

It got worse. The orders for the infantry assault, drafted by Lee's staff, made the attack signal a *yell* — fifteen brigades were supposed to charge when they heard one general's men cheer. As a way to coordinate thousands of soldiers across miles of broken country, it was hopeless. On top of that, a bad map mislabeled one of the key roads through the area, and the confusion delayed Maj. Gen. John B. Magruder's (South) arrival by hours. So the assault, when it finally came, did not come as one great coordinated wave. It came in pieces — brigade after brigade, at different times across the whole afternoon and into the evening, each one walking up that bare open slope alone into the teeth of the guns.

A frontal assault — marching infantry straight at a strong, fortified position head-on — is the costliest attack in war even when it works. A frontal assault uphill, across open ground, against massed artillery, delivered one fragment at a time with no support, is not really an attack at all. From mid-afternoon on, the Confederate brigades came on and were torn apart. The guns fired canister — tin cans packed with iron balls that turned a cannon into a giant shotgun, spraying the slope at close range — and shell, and the attacking lines stalled well short of the crest and were driven back down. Then another brigade tried, and was driven back. Then another. Around twenty separate Confederate brigades charged at different moments across the afternoon; some divisions lost a quarter or more of their strength, individual brigades close to half. Not one Confederate soldier reached the top of the hill. A large share of those losses came from the artillery — Hunt's gunners fired hundreds of rounds per battery and broke charge after charge before it could ever close to musket range — though no one has a precise accounting of how many men the cannon killed versus the rifles. What is certain is the result: the open field below the crest filled up with the dead and wounded of an army that never got to grips with its enemy.

D.H. Hill led one of the largest of those charges himself — five brigades, more than eight thousand men, sent up through the killing ground in uncoordinated waves — and he watched them get shot down. More than twenty years later, writing his own account of the battle, he gave the day its verdict:

> It was not war—it was murder.

_ITALIC_: He had seen it from the front. Brigade after brigade walking up an open hill into massed guns that broke each one before it arrived. There is no better description, and it came from a man who was there.

### Meanwhile in the killing ground | The view from the slope

That same morning, before any of it began, D.H. Hill had ridden out to look at the Union position on Malvern Hill — the open fields, the cannon stacked tier on tier along the crest — and he had read it correctly. He told the others, in so many words, that if McClellan was up there in strength, the smart thing to do was to leave him alone. Through the long afternoon, his warning came true brigade by brigade, on the very slope he had looked up at. The man who named the slaughter had tried, hours earlier, to talk everyone out of it.

# richmond-saved | Richmond Saved

By dark on July 1, the field belonged to the North. Every Confederate charge had been broken; the dead and wounded covered the slope below the guns; Lee's army had thrown itself at Malvern Hill and bounced off with thousands of casualties and nothing to show. By every measure of the battlefield, the United States had won — clearly, and at far lower cost than the enemy. And then the winning army got up in the dark and continued its retreat. To understand why takes pulling back from the hill to the whole campaign, because the small story and the big story point in opposite directions.

## The aftermath | Richmond Saved

The first thing to settle is the cost, and here the count was lopsided in the North's favor. Union losses came to roughly 3,000 men, killed, wounded, and missing. Confederate losses ran far higher — somewhere in the range of 5,300 to 5,650, the price of sending brigade after brigade up an open hill at massed cannon. Lee had lost nearly twice as many men as McClellan and gained not one foot of the crest. On the field, Malvern Hill was a one-sided Union tactical victory, the kind of defensive win generals study for a century afterward.

And the victorious commander wasn't even there to see it. Maj. Gen. George B. McClellan (North) had left the immediate battlefield before the main fighting, going aboard the gunboat USS Galena on the James River and riding off to inspect Harrison's Landing, the spot downriver where he meant to plant the army. Command on the hill fell to Brig. Gen. Fitz John Porter (North), his V Corps commander, who ran the defense, lined up the infantry around Hunt's guns, and actually won the battle. McClellan's absence from the field of his own great victory became a thing his enemies never let him forget; years later, during his run for president, his critics were still mocking him for sitting on a gunboat while other men fought.

When McClellan's orders came down, they stunned the men who had just won. The army would not turn and drive back toward Richmond. It would keep retreating — abandon the hill it had just defended so well and march on through the night to Harrison's Landing on the James. Some of his subordinates objected; they had just shattered Lee's attacks and wanted to push the other way, back toward the Confederate capital. McClellan overruled them. Around eleven at night the army pulled off Malvern Hill and marched away in heavy rain, tearing down a bridge behind it, and reached Harrison's Landing the next day under the protection of the gunboats. The Seven Days were over. Lee thought about chasing and let it go, citing the rain and his own exhausted, bled-white army; Maj. Gen. Thomas J. Jackson (South) reportedly wanted to pursue and was overruled in turn.

Here is where the small story and the big story split apart, and the big story wins. Yes, McClellan had won the battle. But Lee had won the week. In one week of relentless fighting — six battles, the last of them a defeat — Lee had taken an army that started within sight of Richmond's church steeples and shoved it all the way back across the Peninsula to cower under naval guns on the James, ten miles from where it began. The threat to the Confederate capital was gone. The Peninsula Campaign, the North's grand effort to win the war by taking Richmond, was effectively finished; the Army of the Potomac would dig in at Harrison's Landing and never resume the advance. By the tally of blood, Malvern Hill went to the North. By the tally of the map — who saved his capital, who got driven away from it — the whole campaign went, decisively, to the South. It would be roughly two years before Union forces came that close to Richmond again.

The consequences rolled outward from there. President Lincoln (North) began losing faith in McClellan, the general who kept winning ground and giving it back; a new Union army under Maj. Gen. John Pope was already forming for a fresh overland push. Lee, meanwhile, reorganized his victorious army and turned north. The war in the East was about to leave the Peninsula entirely and swing toward a new collision in northern Virginia — the campaign that would end at Second Bull Run that August.

> Our success has not been as great or complete as we should have desired.

That was Lee's own verdict, written to his wife after the Seven Days. He had saved Richmond and driven the enemy from its gates, and still he wanted more — the destruction of the army that had just slipped away from him. He would spend the next two years chasing it.

_ITALIC_: McClellan won the last battle and lost the campaign. He saved his army and surrendered the prize it had come for. Both halves were true at once, and the South would take that trade every time.

### Meanwhile in Richmond | The captain who saved a capital

In Richmond, no one was tallying the slope below Malvern Hill. They were tallying the map — and on the map, the enemy that had nearly taken the city was now ten miles away and going nowhere. The Confederate press lionized Lee, the general who in one week had turned near-disaster into deliverance; the Richmond Dispatch went so far as to declare that "no captain that ever lived could have planned or executed a better plan." A month earlier the capital had been packing to flee. Now it was safe, and the man who had saved it was a hero — a tactical defeat at Malvern Hill swallowed whole inside a strategic triumph.
