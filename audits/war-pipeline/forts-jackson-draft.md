# the-great-port | The Mouth of the River

In the spring of 1862, the biggest city in the Confederacy sat almost undefended at the bottom of a river, and almost nobody on the Southern side believed it was in any danger. New Orleans was the South's great metropolis — about 168,000 people, the sixth-largest city in the United States, bigger than Charleston, Richmond, Mobile, Memphis, and Savannah put together. It was the place where more than half of all American cotton went to the sea, the outlet for goods from the entire Mississippi River valley, second only to Richmond in the value of what it manufactured. It was also the South's great slave market — the largest slave-trading center in a country that had gone to war to keep human beings as property. The whole grim machinery the Confederacy was built to protect ran, in large part, through New Orleans.

That is exactly why the United States wanted it. The Union's grand strategy for winning the war had a name, the Anaconda Plan, after the snake that kills by squeezing: General-in-Chief Winfield Scott's idea was to wrap the Confederacy in a blockade, choke off its trade, and seize the Mississippi River to cut the South in half. Take the mouth of the Mississippi and you put a hand around the Confederacy's throat. New Orleans was the mouth of the Mississippi. It was the single most valuable piece of real estate on the map, and the Confederacy had left it lightly guarded.

_ITALIC_: The South was about to lose the city it could least afford to lose, in defense of the thing it had gone to war to keep.

## The prize | Why the city lay open

The Confederacy had stripped New Orleans almost bare, and it had done so on purpose, out of a confident misreading of where the blow would land. Southern leaders simply did not believe a fleet could come at the city from the south, up the river from the Gulf of Mexico. They were sure the threat was from upstream, from the north, and they poured their men and ships into the river forts above the city — Island No. 10, Fort Pillow, Vicksburg.

Then the upstream war went badly and drained the city of even more. Union victories at Forts Henry and Donelson in February 1862, and the bloodbath at Shiloh in early April, pulled Confederate troops away to other theaters. The Confederate river fleet under Commodore George Hollins was sent upstream too. By April, the general in charge of New Orleans, Maj. Gen. Mansfield Lovell (South), had only about 3,000 ninety-day militia in the city itself — and only around 1,200 of them even had muskets. The forts below the city held roughly 1,100 more, many of them untrained and foreign-born. The South's greatest city was guarded, in the end, by two old forts and a chain across a river.

## The prize | The gauntlet

What stood between the Gulf and New Orleans was a stretch of river about 75 miles below the city, and a set of defenses that looked formidable on paper. Two forts faced each other across the water at a bend in the river. **Fort Jackson**, the bigger and stronger of the two, sat on the west bank — a five-sided brick fortress built decades earlier, mounting around 74 guns. Half a mile upstream on the east bank stood **Fort St. Philip**, an older brick-and-earth work with about 52 guns. Between them the two forts carried somewhere between roughly 126 and 177 guns, depending on how you count, all of them trained on any ship that tried to pass.

The forts were only the start of it. Stretched across the river between them was a barrier — a heavy chain cable threaded through eight derelict ships (hulks) moored in the current, a fence laid across the water to stop a fleet dead in the gun line where the forts could pound it to splinters. Floating fire rafts (barges piled with pine knots and tar, meant to be set alight and shoved downstream into wooden warships) were added to the obstacles. And lurking above the barrier was a small Confederate fleet: the converted gunboats *McRae* and *Jackson*, two state-navy vessels, and six **cottonclad rams** of the River Defense Fleet — ordinary river steamers with their bows reinforced for ramming and their sides packed with bales of cotton for cheap armor.

The two pieces the South was counting on most were the half-finished ones. The **CSS Louisiana** was meant to be a monster: a 264-foot **ironclad** (a warship sheathed in iron plate, nearly impervious to the cannon of the day), carrying sixteen guns. But she was a monster that couldn't move. Her engines didn't work properly, and she ended up moored against the bank as a floating gun battery, unable to maneuver. Her sister, the **CSS Mississippi**, was even less ready — launched only in late April, barely any machinery, two guns aboard, never finished. The third ironclad was the **CSS Manassas**, an early Confederate **ram** (a vessel built to sink enemies by driving an armored bow straight into their hulls), a low turtle-backed thing with a single heavy gun and a crew of about 104. The Confederacy had bet its river on iron ships, and the iron ships weren't ready.

### Meanwhile in the Gulf | The man coming up the river

The fleet gathering down at the river's mouth belonged to **Flag Officer David Glasgow Farragut (North)**, a sixty-year-old Tennessee-born sailor who had been at sea since boyhood — he had first gone aboard a warship at age nine, raised by the naval officer whose first name he later took. In February 1862, Washington had handed him secret orders and the West Gulf Blockading Squadron, and a single, enormous job: take New Orleans. He came up from the Gulf with about 17 warships and, trailing behind, an army of 18,000 men under Maj. Gen. Benjamin Butler (North) waiting to occupy whatever the navy could capture. The South thought the river couldn't be forced from below. Farragut had come to prove it could.

# running-the-forts | Running the Forts

For nearly a week, the United States tried to do this the careful way, and it didn't work. Before Farragut would risk his wooden ships under the guns of the forts, his foster brother, **Commander David Dixon Porter (North)**, was going to soften those forts up with the strangest-looking part of the whole fleet: a **mortar flotilla** (a squadron of small sailing schooners, each carrying one enormous 13-inch seacoast mortar — a stubby cannon that lobs a heavy shell high in a steep arc, to drop it down inside fortress walls rather than batter them from the front). Twenty of these mortar schooners, their masts dressed with branches to hide them against the trees of the riverbank, anchored below Fort Jackson and opened fire on April 18, 1862.

What followed was a bombardment on a scale that is hard to picture. Over five or six days, Porter's schooners threw something on the order of 7,500 to 13,000 shells at the forts — close to 1,400 of them on the very first day, by some counts far more. Inside Fort Jackson it was hell. General Johnson Duncan (South), commanding the forts, wrote in his report:

> The mortar fire was accurate and terrible, many of the shells falling everywhere within the fort and disabling some of our best guns.

And yet, when the smoke cleared each morning, the forts were still firing. The shelling smashed Fort Jackson's drawbridge, wrecked its hot-shot furnaces and cisterns, cracked its walls, flooded its lower floors, knocked a few guns off their mounts — but it did not break the fort. Fort St. Philip across the river was barely scratched. Day after day of the heaviest bombardment the war had yet seen, and the forts could still rake any ship that tried to pass. The careful way was failing.

## April 18–24 | Cutting the chain

There was still the matter of the chain. No fleet could run the gauntlet while a cable and eight moored hulks blocked the channel — the ships would simply pile up under the forts' guns. So on a dark night, two of the smaller Union gunboats, the *Pinola* and the *Itasca*, slipped up to the barrier under fire to break it. Working in the dark almost on top of Fort Jackson, they managed to open a gap in the chain near the east bank, under Fort St. Philip.

The date is genuinely uncertain — the breach seems to have been made on the night of around April 20 to 23, and the sources don't agree on the exact night. What matters is that by the time Farragut was ready to move, there was a hole in the fence. The door was open.

## April 18–24 | Farragut's gamble

Six days of bombardment had not reduced the forts, and Farragut had seen enough. He called his captains together. Porter, the mortar man, wanted to keep shelling — give the schooners more time, grind the forts down. Farragut overruled his own brother. He was not going to wait for the forts to fall. He was going to ignore them.

It was an enormous bet. Conventional wisdom said you had to silence the forts before you passed them — to "run the forts" (steam your fleet straight past the enemy's guns and accept the punishment, betting that most of your ships get through before the gunners can sink them) was to gamble your whole fleet on speed and darkness against a wall of cannon. Farragut chose to gamble. He ordered his captains to armor their wooden ships as best they could with whatever they had — anchor chains draped down the sides like chain mail, sandbags piled around the boilers and engines. He split the fleet into three divisions and aimed them at the gap in the chain. They would go in the dead of night.

## April 18–24 | The night they ran the gauntlet

In the pre-dawn dark of April 24, 1862 — somewhere around 2:00 to 3:30 in the morning — the Union fleet started up the river through the gap, and the river caught fire.

It was a battle fought almost blind, by muzzle flash and burning rafts. Both forts opened up the moment the ships came into range, hammering them from either bank as they crowded through the narrow break in the chain. The Confederates set their fire rafts ablaze and sent them down into the fleet. Farragut's own flagship, the **USS Hartford**, swerved to dodge one and ran aground; a small Confederate steamer shoved a burning raft against her hull, and the flagship caught fire — for a few minutes the admiral's own ship was in flames — before her crew beat the fire out and worked her free.

The Confederate vessels came at the fleet out of the dark and the smoke. The cottonclad ram *Governor Moore* drove again and again into the **USS Varuna**, ramming her until she sank in the shallows — the only Union warship lost in the passage. The ironclad ram *Manassas* came charging in and rammed the *Mississippi* and the *Brooklyn*, but her blows glanced off without crippling either; she was driven ashore, set afire, drifted off burning, and sank. The big ironclad *Louisiana*, still helpless with her dead engines, sat moored by Fort St. Philip and fired into the passing ships like a fortress, doing what she could without being able to move an inch. On the *McRae*, the Confederate commander was mortally wounded.

For all the fire, it didn't work. The chaotic Confederate flotilla — half-built ironclads, untrained crews, ships getting in each other's way — came apart. The historian John D. Winters later judged that the Confederate navy that night "made a sorry showing":

> Self-destruction, lack of co-operation, cowardice of untrained officers, and the murderous fire of the Federal gunboats reduced the fleet to a demoralized shambles.

When the sun came up, most of Farragut's fleet was above the forts. Thirteen or fourteen of his warships had made it through; three had been forced to turn back, and the *Varuna* was lost, but the bulk of the squadron was past. Twelve Confederate vessels had been destroyed. The casualties of that night were, for a battle this decisive, remarkably light — on the order of a few dozen Union sailors killed and a hundred-odd wounded. Farragut had done the thing everyone said couldn't be done. The forts were now behind him, still flying Confederate flags but suddenly pointless. There was nothing left between his guns and New Orleans.

### Meanwhile in the forts | Cut off and behind the line

The strangest thing about running the forts is that the forts were still there. Fort Jackson and Fort St. Philip had not surrendered — they had not even been seriously damaged. Their roughly 1,100 men stood in their gun emplacements with a hundred-odd cannon, fully able to fight. They had simply been rendered useless. The fleet they were built to stop had sailed straight past them in the dark, and now the city they guarded lay open behind their backs. A fort is only as good as the thing it blocks. Farragut had stopped letting them block anything.

# the-fall-of-new-orleans | The Fall of New Orleans

On April 25, 1862, the day after the run, Farragut steamed up the river and dropped anchor before New Orleans at noon — and found a great city with nothing to defend it. Maj. Gen. Lovell (South), with about 3,000 militia and no way to fight a fleet of warships in the streets, had made the only sane choice and pulled his troops out, marching them 78 miles north toward Camp Moore rather than turn New Orleans into a slaughterhouse he was certain to lose. The largest city in the Confederacy had been left to its fate.

What greeted Farragut was less a surrender than a tantrum. The waterfront was on fire — citizens were burning cotton bales, sugar, and supplies along the wharves so the Union couldn't take them, and the unfinished ironclad *Mississippi*, the dream-ship that never got her engines, was set ablaze and sent drifting downriver as a burning hulk. Mayor John Monroe refused to formally surrender the city, insisting it was undefended and that Farragut could take it himself if he wanted it. The crowds on the levee were furious and helpless at once.

## April 25–28 | The flag and the forts

The fury found a focus on April 26. Marines went ashore to raise the United States flag over the New Orleans Mint, and a group of citizens promptly tore it down. One of them, a gambler named **William Mumford (South)**, was part of the group that hauled the flag down and dragged it through the streets, where the crowd ripped it to shreds. It was a small act of defiance with an enormous price tag, though that bill would not come due for weeks.

Downriver, the forts were dying on the vine. Cut off, with the city already lost behind them, their boats destroyed and their supplies gone, the garrison of Fort Jackson finally broke. On the night of April 27–28, around 300 of its soldiers mutinied — spiking guns and walking out. Fort St. Philip never mutinied, but the position was hopeless. General Duncan (South), cut off and half-deserted, opened surrender talks, and both forts gave up on April 28. To the last, the South denied the Union its prizes: rather than let the great ironclad *Louisiana* be surrendered under the truce, the Confederate naval commander ordered her set afire — and she blew up as she drifted past Fort St. Philip, the blast killing one Confederate soldier in the fort.

## April 25–28 | Beast Butler takes the city

With the forts down, the army came up. On May 1, 1862, Maj. Gen. Benjamin Butler (North) marched his troops into New Orleans, and the occupation began — and it would be one of the most hated in American history. Butler ruled with a hard hand: martial law, forced loyalty oaths, confiscated weapons. When the women of New Orleans made a point of insulting his soldiers, he issued the notorious "Woman Order," declaring that any woman who showed contempt for Union troops would be treated "as a woman of the town plying her avocation" — that is, as a prostitute. The order caused an international uproar.

And then there was Mumford. Butler had the flag-tearer arrested, tried for treason, and hanged on June 7, 1862, from a flagstaff jutting out of the very Mint where he had pulled the flag down — a deliberate, public execution meant to make an example of him. Between the Woman Order, the hangings, and the iron rule, white New Orleans gave Butler the nickname history kept: "Beast" Butler.

_ITALIC_: The occupation was harsh, but the harshness was almost beside the point. The real blow had already landed.

## April 25–28 | The city gone, and with it the river

Step back from the flags and the executions and look at what the Union had actually won, because it was staggering. The Confederacy's largest city, its biggest port, the place where most of the South's cotton reached the world and where its second-greatest manufacturing base stood, was gone — taken after only about fifteen months of Confederate control, the first major Southern city to fall. With it went the entire lower Mississippi: there was now no real Confederate force, on land or water, between the Gulf of Mexico and Memphis. Within weeks Farragut had Baton Rouge and Natchez too, and the great river was Union from the sea up to Vicksburg.

The South understood at once what it had lost. The Confederate diarist Mary Chesnut, taking the news in her journal, captured the despair in a single line that has echoed ever since — that with New Orleans gone, the Confederacy itself felt gone. The Anaconda was tightening. The hand was around the throat. The South had built its war to defend a slave economy that ran through New Orleans, and now New Orleans, and the river it sat on, belonged to the men who had come to end that economy. It was, as one Union official put it, the great event of the war second only to the final surrender of Lee — and it had cost the Union, on the night that decided it, only a few dozen lives.

### Meanwhile in Europe | A door quietly closes

The fall of New Orleans was felt across the Atlantic as much as on the Mississippi. Britain and France had been weighing whether to recognize the Confederacy as a real nation — a step that could have brought money, trade, even intervention. The loss of the South's greatest city, taken so suddenly and held so completely, was exactly the kind of news that cooled that interest. The historian Allan Nevins judged that the disaster put the Confederacy's weaknesses on display for all of Europe to see: its "poverty, disorganization, lack of skilled engineers and craftsmen, friction between State authorities and Richmond, and want of foresight." A Confederacy that couldn't hold its own front door was a poor bet for a foreign government, and the foreign governments noticed.
