## MUST-ADD 1 — Nat Turner's Rebellion (the enslaved South acting — the thesis's sharpest proof)
- Night of **Aug 21–22, 1831, Southampton County, Virginia.** Led by **Nat Turner** — enslaved,
  literate/self-taught, deeply religious, believed he had divine visions authorizing the rising.
- ~**70 enslaved and free Black participants** at peak; killed **~55–60 white Virginians** across
  ~11 farms/plantations — the deadliest enslaved insurrection in U.S. history. `[VERIFY ~55–60]`
- Turner **evaded capture ~2 months**; caught, tried, **hanged Nov 11, 1831.**
- Aftermath (the political point): Virginia held an **1832 legislative debate on gradual
  emancipation — and rejected it** (the last such debate in any slave state); the South then
  **hardened slave codes, banned Black literacy in many states, restricted free Black movement.**
- It happened the **same year** Walker's *Appeal* was circulating and Garrison launched *The
  Liberator* — Southern whites read all three as connected.
- WHY IT BELONGS: the section argues the enslaved were authors of their own history; Turner is
  the man who decided — no newspaper, no convention, no conductor. Place as a compact paragraph
  around the Walker→Garrison transition (1830→1831). Do NOT make it a chapter; one focused beat.
- Sources: Britannica "Nat Turner's rebellion"; Encyclopedia Virginia; Gilder Lehrman.

## MUST-ADD 2 — The emigration / "stay and fight" debate (the movement's central fracture)
- The **American Colonization Society (ACS, founded 1816)** proposed sending free Black Americans
  to **Liberia**; backed by some sympathetic whites but **overwhelmingly rejected by Black
  activists as deportation, not liberation.**
- **1817:** ~**3,000 Black Philadelphians** met at Mother Bethel to protest the ACS — among the
  largest Black political gatherings to that point. `[VERIFY ~3,000]`
- **Freedom's Journal split over it:** co-editor **John Brown Russwurm** moved toward emigration/
  ACS support by 1829 and emigrated to Liberia; the paper folded. (Already in base pack — connect it.)
- **After the 1850 Fugitive Slave Act:** **Martin Delany** (who had co-edited *The North Star*
  with Douglass) broke with Douglass over this in 1852 — published *The Condition, Elevation,
  Emigration, and Destiny of the Colored People* (1852) arguing Black Americans should build a
  future elsewhere; **Douglass publicly rebutted — stay and fight, "this is our country."**
  Delany convened a **National Emigration Convention, Cleveland, 1854.**
- WHY IT BELONGS: shows the struggle was not a monolith — the very question "fight FOR America or
  leave it?" was live and serious. Walker's anti-colonization line (already in draft) is ONE side
  of a real argument. Place a clause/short paragraph in Beat 1 (Walker/Russwurm) and a beat in
  Beat 5 (Delany vs. Douglass after 1850).
- Sources: Wikipedia "Martin Delany"; Colored Conventions Project (emigration debate); BlackPast
  (Mother Bethel / ACS protest).

## SHOULD-ADD — Maria Stewart (cheap, strong; reinforces agency + offsets Douglass great-man tilt)
- **Maria W. Stewart**, free Black woman, Boston; published in *The Liberator*; **September 1832,
  Franklin Hall** — the **first documented American woman of ANY race to address a mixed (male +
  female) public audience** on political themes — **19 years before Truth's Akron speech.**
- Retired from public lecturing in **1833** under heavy backlash (including from parts of the Black
  community that disapproved of women speaking publicly).
- Sources: NPS "Maria W. Stewart"; BlackPast.

## SHOULD-ADD — texture facts the gates asked for
- **AME Church backbone:** Richard Allen founded it after leading a **walkout from St. George's
  Methodist (Philadelphia) over segregated seating**; **first independent Black denomination**;
  by 1830 the institutional spine of free Black Northern life (literacy schools, meeting space,
  UGRR stations). (Use to explain WHY the 1830 convention met at Mother Bethel.)
- **Colored Conventions agenda** was broader than abolition: labor, education, voting rights,
  temperance, emigration — a full Black political platform (62 conventions 1830–1864).
