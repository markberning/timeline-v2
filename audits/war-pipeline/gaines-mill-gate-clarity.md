The actual worst offender present in the text is the **division/brigade/corps term stack in the parenthetical at §The Seven Days**, which lands four military-structure terms in one sentence without enough daylight between them, immediately followed by a reader being asked to track *four separate division commanders* (D.H. ## MUST-FIX

### MF-1 | "V Corps" — first use, no gloss
**Passage:** "one piece sat exposed on the north bank — the V Corps under Maj. Gen. Fitz John Porter (North)."
**Problem:** "V Corps" uses a Roman numeral plus the word "Corps" — a cold reader either thinks this is a rank/title or skips it. The parenthetical explanation immediately follows but is about what a *corps* is generally, not what "V Corps" means (it's just the name of Porter's specific corps, the Fifth). A 15-year-old may wonder: is V a name? a rank? why Roman numeral?
**Suggested gloss:** Change "the V Corps" to "his Fifth Corps (numbered units were just how the army named its corps)" — or append a two-word note: "the V Corps — Porter's piece of the army —"

---

### MF-2 | Corps/division/brigade definition arrives *after* "V Corps" is used — reading order problem
**Passage:** "(A corps was an army's largest building block, on the order of 20,000 to 35,000 men, itself made of divisions of several thousand, those made of brigades of a few thousand.)"
**Problem:** The reader hits "V Corps" cold, then gets the definition. But the definition also introduces "divisions" and "brigades" back-to-back in one compressed parenthetical — a reader has to absorb three levels of military hierarchy simultaneously, mid-sentence, mid-paragraph. Worse, within one page they then meet "the Light Division," "three brigades," "Whiting's division," "the Texas Brigade" — all without a moment to breathe. The parenthetical is doing too much at once.
**Suggested fix:** Split the explanation. Gloss "corps" on first use. Hold the division/brigade breakdown for the first time those words appear as the subject of an action.

---

### MF-3 | "change of base" — scare-quoted but the plain-English explanation is buried mid-paragraph
**Passage (first use):** "began that very day to plan a 'change of base': army slang for picking up his whole supply line and shifting it somewhere safer"
**Problem:** This is actually well-done on first use. BUT the second use, in the final section, has no gloss at all: "He ordered his whole Army of the Potomac to 'change base' — to abandon the advance on Richmond." The second instance does add a plain-English synonym ("abandon the advance"), but a reader who skimmed the first definition may not connect them. **This is a MUST-FIX only if the sections are read out of order or as stand-alone articles** (which the war app supports). If sections are always read in order, downgrade to SHOULD-FIX.
**Suggested fix:** In the final "change base" appearance: "ordered his whole Army of the Potomac to 'change base' — the army's term for packing up and retreating — to abandon the advance on Richmond."

---

### MF-4 | "the Peninsula" never defined; "Virginia Peninsula" defined only in §1
**Passage (§1):** "the Virginia Peninsula — the long neck of land between the James and York Rivers, southeast of Richmond"
**Problem:** The full name with the gloss is fine in §1. But the section headers and the Fact Ledger reference "the Peninsula Campaign" and "the Peninsula" without the gloss, and the body text uses "the Peninsula" implicitly throughout. A cold reader who starts at §2 or later (boatswains-swamp) has lost the orientation that the whole battle is happening on a specific geographic peninsula. **The map anchor is critical.**
**Suggested fix:** At the first use of "Peninsula" in a non-§1 section (or in any section header), add a brief anchor: "the Virginia Peninsula" or add a parenthetical "(the Virginia Peninsula, southeast of Richmond)."

---

### MF-5 | Fitz John Porter — no scene-set on who he is beyond his rank and corps
**Passage:** "one piece sat exposed on the north bank — the V Corps under Maj. Gen. Fitz John Porter (North)."
**Problem:** Porter is the central figure of the entire battle — he picks the defensive position, he holds it, he retreats. But the reader's only introduction is his rank, his corps number, and his side tag. Compare to Lee, who gets "He had been a general for less than a month" and a strategic sketch. Porter gets nothing: who is he, why does it matter that *he* has the exposed corps?
**Suggested fix:** One appositive is enough: "Maj. Gen. Fitz John Porter (North), a career officer McClellan trusted with his most vulnerable flank" or similar. Doesn't need to be long.

---

### MF-6 | "the Shenandoah Valley" — assumed known geography
**Passage:** "Maj. Gen. Thomas J. 'Stonewall' Jackson (South), marching in from the Shenandoah Valley to the west."
**Problem:** The Shenandoah Valley is named but not anchored. A cold reader doesn't know where it is relative to Richmond or why Jackson would be coming from there. The "Valley campaign" is mentioned in the next sentence, also without explanation.
**Suggested fix:** Add a brief geographic anchor: "from the Shenandoah Valley — a long agricultural corridor about 100 miles (160 km) west of Richmond, where Jackson had spent the spring raiding Union supply lines."

---

### MF-7 | "his old regiment, the 4th Texas" — "old regiment" implies prior context the reader doesn't have
**Passage:** "Hood took his old regiment, the 4th Texas, plus companies of the 18th Georgia"
**Problem:** "His old regiment" implies Hood previously commanded the 4th Texas before leading the Texas Brigade — but the reader has no prior context for Hood's career. It reads as if the author expects us to know this.
**Suggested fix:** Drop "old" entirely, or explain: "the 4th Texas (the regiment he had commanded before taking the brigade)."

---

### MF-8 | "field-grade officers" — undefined military term
**Passage:** "The 4th Texas alone lost about 570, including every one of its field-grade officers."
**Problem:** "Field-grade officers" is pure military jargon. A cold reader does not know this means majors, lieutenant colonels, and colonels — the officers who command a regiment.
**Suggested fix:** "every one of its senior officers (the colonels and majors who led the regiment)."

---

### MF-9 | "Rebel Yell" — assumed cultural knowledge
**Passage:** "just fixed bayonets, the Rebel Yell, and a flat-out rush"
**Problem:** "Rebel Yell" is a famous phrase but a 15-year-old with zero Civil War background may not know it means the distinctive battle cry Confederate troops used when charging — a high-pitched howl meant to unnerve the enemy.
**Suggested fix:** "the Rebel Yell (the high howling war cry Confederate troops used when charging)" — or work it in as action: "screaming the Rebel Yell — the bone-chilling war cry that had already terrified Union troops from Bull Run to Shiloh."

---

### MF-10 | "Sykes's Regulars" — undefined; "Regulars" is jargon
**Passage:** "with Sykes's Regulars covering the rear, Porter pulled his battered corps south"
**Problem:** "Regulars" in Civil War context means the professional U.S. Army (as opposed to volunteer regiments), which is a meaningful distinction — they were better-drilled and often used as rearguards. But a cold reader just sees a proper noun and an unexplained modifier.
**Suggested fix:** "Sykes's Regulars — the professional U.S. Army troops, the steadiest men in Porter's corps —"

---

### MF-11 | "Sumner's II Corps" — unexplained unit with no side tag (though Union is implied)
**Passage:** "McClellan ... sent Porter two brigades from Sumner's II Corps."
**Problem:** Sumner is introduced with no side tag, no first name, no explanation of who he is or why his corps is relevant. The reader has to infer he's Union because McClellan (North) sent the brigades. But "Sumner's II Corps" reads as a new proper noun with no anchor.
**Suggested fix:** "two brigades from the II Corps under Maj. Gen. Edwin Sumner (North), stationed south of the river" — or just "two brigades from another Union corps stationed nearby."

---

### MF-12 | "Whiting's division" — Whiting never introduced
**Passage:** "leading the Texas Brigade as part of Whiting's division" ... later "Hood/Whiting's division — lost 1,018 men"
**Problem:** W.H.C. Whiting is never introduced. He appears twice, only as a possessive. A cold reader must infer he's a Confederate general. First mention needs at minimum a side tag.
**Suggested fix:** "as part of Maj. Gen. William Whiting's (South) division" — or restructure so Whiting's role is briefly named before Hood attacks.

---

### MF-13 | "J.E.B. Stuart" — named with no introduction or side tag
**Passage:** "Brig. Gen. Philip St. George Cooke (North), who happened to be the father-in-law of Confederate cavalryman J.E.B. Stuart"
**Problem:** Stuart is dropped in as a famous name the reader is expected to recognize. For a cold reader, "Confederate cavalryman J.E.B. Stuart" is technically sufficient on its own — but calling it a "footnote cameo" when Stuart is the South's most famous cavalry commander, mentioned here purely as a curiosity, creates a slight jarring effect. The reader has no sense of why Stuart's identity matters.
**This is BORDERLINE.** The parenthetical does say "Confederate cavalryman" which is enough identification. Downgrade to SHOULD-FIX if the team prefers to keep it light.
**Suggested fix (optional):** "Confederate cavalry commander J.E.B. Stuart — one of Lee's most famous officers, and notably, Cooke's own son-in-law."

---

### MF-14 | "Harrison's Landing" — new place name, no orientation
**Passage:** "fall back southeast to Harrison's Landing on the James River, under the protection of the navy's guns."
**Problem:** Harrison's Landing is introduced as the destination of the Union retreat but is given no orientation other than "on the James River." A cold reader doesn't know how far this is, whether it's deeper into Confederate territory or away from it, or why the navy's guns would help there.
**Suggested fix:** "Harrison's Landing, a plantation on the James River about 25 miles (40 km) southeast of Richmond, where Union gunboats could shell any Confederate approach."

## SHOULD-FIX

### SF-1 | "right flank" gloss is good but "left flank" used later without any reminder
**Passage:** "to turn Porter's right flank — the exposed northern end of his line, the side you hit to roll a position up from its edge"
**Problem:** The flank gloss here is clear and good. But later, "Morell's division held his left, on Turkey Hill; Sykes's division held the right" appears with no orientation cue — left/right of *what*, facing *which direction*? A compass direction would help (Porter's line faces west toward the Confederates, so his "left" is south and his "right" is north).
**Suggested fix:** "Morell's division held his southern (left) flank, on Turkey Hill; Sykes held the northern (right) flank."

---

### SF-2 | "D.H. Hill" vs "A.P. Hill" — two generals named Hill appear in the same passage with no disambiguation
**Passage:** "the division of Maj. Gen. D.H. Hill (South) reached the crossroads of Old Cold Harbor ... Around 2:30 p.m., A.P. Hill (South) sent his roughly 12,000-man division"
**Problem:** Two Confederate generals named Hill appear within two sentences. They are not related, but a cold reader may easily confuse them — especially since A.P. Hill was already introduced much earlier and D.H. Hill appears for the first time here.
**Suggested fix:** Introduce D.H. Hill at first mention: "the division of Maj. Gen. Daniel Harvey Hill (South) — no relation to A.P. Hill — reached the crossroads." Then use "D.H. Hill" and "A.P. Hill" consistently.

---

### SF-3 | "Light Division" nickname used without explanation
**Passage:** "A.P. Hill (South) sent his roughly 12,000-man division (grandly nicknamed the Light Division)"
**Problem:** "Grandly nicknamed the Light Division" is a nice voice touch but a cold reader doesn't know why "light" is ironic or grand — the Light Division was A.P. Hill's name for his own command, and the irony is that it was *not* light in any conventional sense, just fast-moving. The word "grandly" implies irony but doesn't explain it.
**Suggested fix:** Add a brief note: "(A.P. Hill's nickname for his own command — 'light' meaning fast, not small; at 12,000 men, it was anything but)."

---

### SF-4 | "Iron discipline" — rhetorical phrase that may obscure the tactical reason for not firing
**Passage:** "he did something that took iron discipline: he sent his men forward without firing a shot."
**Problem:** A cold reader might not understand *why* not firing takes discipline, or what the tactical logic is. In Civil War infantry tactics, soldiers instinctively stopped to load and fire when shot at — it was the trained reflex. Ordering men to suppress that reflex and just run was genuinely unusual and hard to execute.
**Suggested fix:** Add a brief explanation: "he sent his men forward without firing a shot — an almost unheard-of order, since the natural reflex under fire was to stop and shoot back; running without shooting meant getting hit without being able to answer."

---

### SF-5 | "counter march" — the word appears without explanation
**Passage:** "His column had been led down the wrong road, costing an hour-long countermarch"
**Problem:** "Countermarch" is not immediately obvious to a cold reader. It means turning the entire column around and retracing its steps.
**Suggested fix:** "an hour-long countermarch — turning the whole column around and retracing its steps."

---

### SF-6 | "sharpshooters" — used twice, first without definition
**Passage:** "a first line down at the creek bottom with sharpshooters firing from behind felled trees" and "dogged by burned bridges, roadblocks, and sharpshooters"
**Problem:** "Sharpshooters" is fairly transparent but technically refers to designated marksmen with longer-range rifles, different from regular infantry. It's probably fine for most readers but could use a one-word gloss on first use.
**Suggested fix (light):** "sharpshooters (expert marksmen with long-range rifles)" on first use.

---

### SF-7 | "batteries" — technical artillery term used without gloss
**Passage:** "with still more batteries firing in support from across the Chickahominy to the south"
**Problem:** "Batteries" here means artillery units (groups of cannon). A reader who already encountered the cannon count (96 guns) will likely figure this out, but "batteries" is not the same word as "cannon."
**Suggested fix:** "with still more artillery batteries — groups of cannon — firing in support."

---

### SF-8 | "regimental commanders" — "regiment" never explained as a unit size
**Passage:** "Nine Union regimental commanders were killed or mortally wounded here. The 9th Massachusetts alone lost 206 men, the most of any Northern regiment on the field."
**Problem:** A regiment was approximately 500–1,000 men, the basic tactical unit below a brigade. The reader has seen brigade and division explained but not regiment. The "9th Massachusetts" naming convention also may be opaque — a cold reader might not know that Union regiments were named by state and number.
**Suggested fix:** Either gloss "regiment" when it first appears, or add after "9th Massachusetts": "a regiment of roughly 900 Massachusetts volunteers."

---

### SF-9 | "the Peninsula Campaign" / "the Seven Days Battles" — titular phrases without explanation of their historiographic status
**Passage (various):** "what history would call the Seven Days Battles"; "the Seven Days" used in section headers
**Problem:** "What history would call" is a good hedging device for the Seven Days. But "the Peninsula Campaign" is used in the Fact Ledger without the same hedge. A cold reader might not know this is a named campaign with a specific historiographic identity — they might think "Peninsula" just refers to the geography.
**This is light.** The Fact Ledger is not reader-facing, so this may be moot if the ledger is internal only. Flag for completeness.

## Reading-order vulnerability

The three sections (lee-attacks, boatswains-swamp, hoods-breakthrough) are written as a linear story and each assumes the reader has read the previous one. The war app may serve sections individually or out of order. If so:

- **boatswains-swamp** loses the Peninsula geography, the Chickahominy orientation, the corps/division/brigade definition, the Pinkerton phantom-army context, and the Jackson setup — all defined only in lee-attacks.
- **hoods-breakthrough** loses Porter's identity, the three-tier defense, and the piecemeal-assault context.

If standalone reading is in scope, each section needs a one-paragraph "where we are" oriented for a reader who landed here first. If strictly linear, this is not a bug.
