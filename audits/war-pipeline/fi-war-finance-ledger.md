## The cost of winning
- National debt = borrowed money repaid with interest; bonds; yearly interest = fixed bill → §1 gloss.
- Debt roughly doubled, ~£75M (1756) → ~£130M (1763), £132.6M most precise → §1.
- Interest swallowed enormous share of budget, "by some accounts more than half" → §1 (CONTESTED; softened with "by some accounts," "more than half" given as high figure, exactly as pack permits).

## An army that wouldn't go home
- Bute ministry decided early 1763 to keep ~10,000 regulars in N. America; decision made before Pontiac's War → §2.
- Regulars = full-time professional soldiers → gloss per §2/§6 jargon rule.
- Cost "a few hundred thousand pounds a year… roughly £200,000" → §2 (CONTESTED range) — see [VERIFY].
- Colonial perception of peacetime standing army as instrument of tyranny → §2 (attested colonial perception; stated as perception, not sourced quote, per pack instruction).

## The people the army was for (Pontiac's War)
- Pontiac's War, April 1763 – July 1766, British name → §10.
- Loosely coordinated nations, not one confederacy under single command → §10 NOTE.
- Nation lists by region (Great Lakes / Ohio Country / Illinois Country) with correct names → §10 exact list.
- Their own aims: halt settler encroachment, force return of diplomatic gifts; Amherst cut off the customary gifts (powder/goods), read as contempt; gifts = diplomacy not charity; war for sovereignty → §10.
- Pontiac (Odawa war leader), siege of Fort Detroit from May 1763, several hundred warriors, ~six months → §10.
- Neolin "the Delaware Prophet" (Lenape); Master of Life displeased Native peoples took up white men's bad habits (goods/alcohol), return to older ways, push British back → §10.
- 8 forts fell mid-May to mid-June 1763; Forts Pitt and Detroit held → §10 (16 May–19 June).
- ~400 British soldiers killed; est. 2,000 settlers killed or captured; figures are estimates → §10 (CONTESTED, hedged).
- Read by historians as stalemate not Native defeat → §10.
- Royal Proclamation of 1763, line along crest of Appalachians, reserved west as Indian territory, forbade settlement + private land purchase → §9 / §10.
- Proclamation protected land only on paper / briefly; colonists ignored it; steadily eroded → §10 dispossession caveat.
- Proclamation closed the west to land-hungry colonists + confirmed need for the costly army → §9.

## Why not just tax England? (cider)
- British public among most heavily taxed in Europe → §3.
- Cyder Act 1763 (Bute), 4 shillings per hogshead of cider made; hogshead = large barrel → §3.
- Warrantless searches of private homes by excise officers (tax collectors); cider made domestically; that was the explosive part → §3.
- Riots across cider west, cut across classes, Bute hanged in effigy → §3.
- Grenville pushed it through, defeated repeal motion Feb 1764 → §3.
- Grenville turned to America because home taxes triggered riots → §3 connective point.

## Grenville's reasoning
- Grenville head of Treasury / PM April 1763 → §4.
- Reasoning: colonists chief beneficiaries (France swept off continent); army for their defense; should help pay → §4.
- Not asking them to retire debt/interest, only partial contribution to own garrison → §4.
- Army ~£200,000/yr; revenue program projected ~£78,000–79,000 → §4/§5.
- Colonies elected no members to Parliament → §4.
- Sugar Act preamble quote (VERBATIM) → §5.

## Sugar Act + Currency Act
- Sugar Act passed 5 April 1764 → §5.
- Lowered foreign molasses duty 6d → 3d/gallon; molasses = syrup from sugar refining, raw material of rum trade → §5 (rum-trade gloss = author connective, molasses-from-sugar is general; acceptable).
- Old 6d duty set 1733, widely smuggled, barely collected → §5 (Molasses Act 1733).
- Lower rate meant to be enforced/collected; "a tax for money, not for trade"; first time Parliament taxed colonies for revenue → §5.
- Detailed cargo manifests, expanded customs powers → §5.
- Vice-admiralty courts: single Crown-appointed judge, no local jury, presumed guilty, vs colonial juries that acquitted → §5.
- Currency Act royal assent 19 April 1764, in force September → §5.
- Did not ban paper money outright; forbade future paper money as legal tender for public/private debts; legal tender gloss → §5.
- Extended to all N. American colonies a restriction previously only on New England → §5.
- Specie/hard money chronically scarce; squeezed coin-starved economy as Britain demanded payment → §5.

## Stamp Act
- Passed 22 March 1765 → §6.
- First direct internal tax Parliament ever laid on colonies → §6.
- Internal vs external distinction (external = goods crossing border collected at port, grudgingly accepted; internal = transactions wholly inside a colony) → §6 gloss + §8 Franklin.
- Stamp required on newspapers, pamphlets, legal documents, magazines, playing cards, dice, other papers → §6.
- Hit lawyers, printers, merchants, tavern-keepers → §6.
- Payable in specie (gold/silver), the money Currency Act made scarcer → §6 (tie-back to §5).

## No taxation without representation
- Principle: free people taxed only by a body they vote for; no MPs; only own assemblies; property taken without consent → §7 gloss.
- Treated as the era's distilled cry, NOT put in quotation marks as a verbatim 1765 utterance → §7 IMPORTANT note honored (slogan never quoted as a sourced quote).
- James Otis Jr., 1764 pamphlet *The Rights of the British Colonies Asserted and Proved*; VERBATIM quote → §7.
- Virtual representation = British counter-argument; most Britons couldn't vote yet held represented → §7.
- Samuel Johnson "represented by the same virtual representation as the greater part of England" (VERBATIM); attributed to Johnson, NOT dated to 1765 in draft → §7 (Johnson's pamphlet is 1775; draft gives no date).
- Otis tagged (British); Johnson tagged (British) per side-tag rule.

## The colonies answer

- Declaration of Rights and Grievances; "with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives" (VERBATIM) → §8.
- "The only Representatives of the People…" (VERBATIM pull-quote) → §8.
- Otis a delegate, helped draft address to House of Commons → §8.

## One victory, two fuses
- Two fuses framing; Native catastrophe; contest ultimately over Native land; "protected" territory overrun → §9 + §10 (cost/dispossession named plainly, per ship-blocking framing rule).
- "French and Indian War" is colonists' name for their enemies; Native nations fought all sides and for themselves; paid steepest price → §10 + framing rule.
- Albany Plan 1754: Franklin proposed intercolonial government for common defense; "Join, or Die" snake cartoon (Pennsylvania Gazette 1754); colonies refused → §9.
