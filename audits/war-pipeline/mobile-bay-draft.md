## the-last-door · Mobile Bay · The last door on the Gulf
### Why a fleet came for a bay nobody could see into

By the summer of 1864, the Confederacy was a house with most of its doors nailed shut. The Union navy had spent three years choking off the Southern coast — a blockade, which is simply a wall of warships parked off an enemy's harbors to stop anything from sailing in or out. The point of it was economic strangulation: keep cotton from getting out to be sold, and keep guns, medicine, and manufactured goods from getting in. And by August 1864, the blockade had sealed nearly every important harbor on the Gulf of Mexico. Nearly.

The one big exception, east of the Mississippi River, was **Mobile, Alabama.** Mobile Bay was the last major Gulf port still in Confederate hands — and that made it the Gulf's surviving hub for the **blockade-runners**, the fast, low-slung ships that made a living slipping through the Union wall in the dark, carrying cotton out and contraband cargo back in. As long as Mobile stayed open, the South had a working back door on the Gulf. Closing it would slam that door and tighten the noose one more notch.

It is worth being precise, right at the start, about what "closing the port" was going to mean, because it is easy to overstate. The target on August 5, 1864, was the *mouth of the bay* — the channel that let ships in and out. Seal that, and you cork the bottle: no more blockade-runners, no more open Gulf harbor. But the **city of Mobile itself sat miles up the bay**, and the city was *not* the objective that day. It would not even be attacked until March of 1865, and it held out until the war's very last days. The 1864 battle this story tells corked the bay. It did not take the town. Keep that straight; the distinction matters.

And it is worth being clear about what the door was a door *for*. The Confederacy existed to preserve and extend slavery — that was the cause the whole war turned on, and the blockade-runners feeding through Mobile were the supply line of a republic built on enslaved labor. Hold that thought. By the end of this story it will be standing on the deck of the flagship, in human form.

Guarding the door were two forts and a minefield. On the east side of the channel — the right hand as you steam in from the Gulf — stood **Fort Morgan**, the strong one, mounting forty-six guns on Mobile Point. On the west side, on Dauphin Island, stood the weaker **Fort Gaines**, with twenty-six guns. A third, **Fort Powell**, sat off to the northwest covering a back channel, well away from the main passage. And strung across the channel between the forts were the torpedoes — which in 1864 did not mean a self-propelled weapon at all. A "torpedo" was a **moored naval mine**: a keg of gunpowder anchored under the surface, rigged to blow when a ship's hull touched it. The Confederates had planted roughly sixty-seven of them across the channel. The man who meant to run that gauntlet was Rear Admiral David G. Farragut.

> **MEANWHILE — the Gulf half of a two-door job.** Mobile Bay was one of the last two big blockade-running ports the Confederacy had left. The other was Wilmington, North Carolina, on the Atlantic side, guarded by Fort Fisher. Mobile Bay shut the Gulf door in August 1864; Fort Fisher would shut the Atlantic door in January 1865. After those two, the Confederacy had essentially no open harbor left on either coast. Mobile Bay was not a sideshow — it was half the job of finally sealing the South off from the sea.

## running-the-forts · Dawn, August 5 · Lashed in pairs, straight at the guns
### The fleet stands in

Farragut (North) — commander of the West Gulf Blockading Squadron, flying his flag in the steam sloop **USS Hartford** (a sloop-of-war being a single-deck warship, smaller than a frigate but still a serious gun platform) — had a plan that was less clever than it was nervy. He would *run* the forts. "Running" a fort means exactly what it sounds like: instead of stopping to slug it out with the fort's guns, you steam past at full speed, take your beating on the way through, and get into the water beyond before the fort can finish you. He would push his whole fleet through the channel, past Forts Morgan and Gaines, over the minefield, into the bay — then destroy the Confederate squadron waiting inside and seal the port. Major General Gordon Granger (North) had landed troops to besiege the forts from the land side once the fleet was through.

The fleet had two parts. Leading on the starboard side — the right, the Fort Morgan side, where the deep water ran — came a single column of four **monitors.** A monitor was a low, almost-submerged ironclad: a flat iron deck barely above the waterline, with the guns mounted in a revolving armored turret, presenting almost nothing for an enemy to hit. The four were **Tecumseh, Manhattan, Winnebago, and Chickasaw**, in that order, with **Commander Tunis A. M. Craven (North)** in the lead aboard Tecumseh.

To port came the wooden ships — the big steam sloops and frigates — and here Farragut did something that tells you how much he respected the forts. He **lashed them in pairs.** Each large wooden warship was tied alongside a smaller gunboat on its disengaged side (the port side, away from Fort Morgan), so the big ship's hull shielded the little one from the fort's fire, and if either was crippled the other could tow it clear. Hartford was lashed to the gunboat **Metacomet** under **Lieutenant Commander James E. Jouett (North).** At the head of the wooden column came **USS Brooklyn**, under **Captain James Alden (North)**, lashed to **Octorara**; behind Brooklyn came Farragut's Hartford, then Richmond, Lackawanna, Monongahela, Ossipee, Oneida — each with its small partner roped to its flank.

Just before dawn the fleet formed up, and around six to seven in the morning it stood in toward the channel, the monitors leading, Brooklyn at the head of the wooden line. Fort Morgan opened fire. The big wooden ships took the hammering on their starboard sides while the lashed gunboats sheltered to port, and the column pushed up the narrow lane — mines and their marker buoys on the left hand, Fort Morgan's forty-six guns on the right. The fleet had to thread between the two, because the safe water was a tight strip hard up under the fort. So far, so planned.

Then the lead monitor died in half a minute, and the whole attack nearly died with it.

## damn-the-torpedoes · ~7:30 a.m. · The minefield and the order
### Tecumseh sinks, Brooklyn stalls, and Farragut decides

The minefield had a gap in it. The Confederates had left a roughly five-hundred-yard opening on the eastern side of the channel — the Fort Morgan side — so that their own blockade-runners could still slip in and out, and they had marked the edge of the live mines with a line of buoys. The safe lane ran *between* that buoy line and the fort. Everyone in Farragut's fleet had been told: stay east of the buoys, no matter how close that put you to Fort Morgan's guns.

Craven (North), in the lead, did not stay east of the buoys. Trying to close on the Confederate ironclad waiting inside the bay, he steered Tecumseh **west of the buoy line — into the mined water.** Almost at once she struck a torpedo. The monitor went down bow-first in something like twenty-five to thirty seconds, so fast there was no time to do anything. Of her crew — sources differ on the exact complement, but it was something over a hundred men — only twenty-one were saved. Somewhere around ninety men went down with her. That single mine, in less than half a minute, killed more Union sailors than the entire rest of the battle combined.

And the attack stalled. With the lead monitor gone in front of everyone's eyes and the channel now visibly full of mines, **Brooklyn stopped** — Alden (North) began to back his ship and signaled the flagship for instructions. But a column of warships under a fort's guns cannot afford to stop. Brooklyn stalling fouled the whole line behind her, leaving the fleet piling up, motionless, directly beneath Fort Morgan. This was the moment the battle could have been lost.

Here is where the most famous order in American naval history is supposed to have been given. The version on the monuments and in the schoolbooks is **"Damn the torpedoes! Full speed ahead!"** — Farragut, refusing to stop, driving his flagship through the mines. And the *action* is exactly right and fully documented: Farragut ordered Hartford forward, swung her around the stalled Brooklyn into the lead, and drove straight through the mined water. The whole fleet followed Hartford through, and the mines they passed over — long submerged, fouled, defective — failed to go off. That is what happened, and it won the battle.

The *words*, though, are a different matter, and honesty about them is part of the story. The famous line did not appear in print until years after the battle — first attributed to Farragut something like fourteen years later — and some historians doubt he said it at all. A signal officer close enough to have heard it wrote a detailed account of the action that never mentions the exclamation, and men who were there doubted any shouted order could even have been heard over the guns. What he more plausibly said, if he said anything, was an order to his flag captain and to Jouett — something on the order of "Damn the torpedoes. Four bells, Captain Drayton," then "Go ahead, Jouett, full speed." The line we remember was stitched together and polished in the retelling. What is certain — and certain is enough — is that the lead ship sank, the column froze, and Farragut shoved his own flagship into the front and went through the mines anyway. The legend is a tidy compression of a real and reckless decision.

There is a second iconic image from this morning, and it gets the same gentle correction. We picture Farragut **lashed high in Hartford's rigging**, tied aloft so he could see over the gun smoke — a swashbuckler binding himself to his ship. The truth is a little less theatrical and a little more human. His flag captain, **Captain Percival Drayton (North)**, worried that the admiral might be hit and fall to the deck from where he stood up in the shrouds, and had a sailor secure him with a line. As the story is told, Farragut waved the man off — he was fine, he said — but the sailor had his orders and lashed him anyway. He was tied up there for his own safety at his captain's insistence, not out of bravado. It is a better story than the legend, honestly: the most famous sailor in the country, fussed over by his own crew.

> **MEANWHILE — most of the dead, in one ship.** The casualty math at Mobile Bay is lopsided in a way that's worth pausing on. The Union fleet lost roughly 150 killed and 170 wounded in the naval action — and the great majority of those deaths were the men of Tecumseh, gone in under thirty seconds when she struck the mine. The fleet fight that followed, ship against ship, was comparatively cheap in blood. One torpedo accounted for most of the Union dead in the whole battle. The forts, when they fell, were even cheaper: Granger's land troops besieging them lost about one man killed and seven wounded. The big Confederate "loss" was not battle dead either — it was prisoners, the fort garrisons that eventually surrendered.

## the-tennessee · Inside the bay · One ironclad against a fleet
### Buchanan turns and fights them all

The fleet was now inside Mobile Bay, north of the mines, in open water. Waiting for it was the Confederate squadron — and "squadron" flatters it. **Admiral Franklin Buchanan (South)**, the senior Confederate naval officer afloat, had exactly four ships: the ironclad ram **CSS Tennessee**, his flagship, and three small wooden gunboats, **Selma, Morgan, and Gaines.** An ironclad ram was a warship sheathed in iron armor with a reinforced bow meant to be driven straight into an enemy's hull like a battering ram. Tennessee was the strongest ironclad in Confederate service and carried six guns — but her engines were weak and she was painfully slow. Against her and three gunboats, Farragut had four monitors and more than a dozen wooden warships.

The three gunboats were overmatched the moment the fleet was through. They were quickly run down — Gaines was wrecked, Selma was taken, and Morgan escaped up toward the city — and that should have been the end of it. Buchanan should have run Tennessee under the guns of Fort Morgan and waited. Instead he did the thing that turned a one-sided fight into a legend on the losing side: he **turned his single ironclad around and steamed it back into the entire Union fleet, alone.**

What followed was close, brutal, and slow. The big Union sloops bore down and **rammed Tennessee** again and again, trying to run her under, while the whole fleet pounded her at point-blank, arm's-length range. The monitors **Manhattan and Chickasaw** closed in and battered her armor. For something like an hour she took it from every direction at once. Her smokestack was shot away; her exposed steering chains were parted, so she could no longer steer; she drifted nearly motionless under the guns of a fleet. Buchanan himself went down with a badly broken leg.

With the ship helpless and her commander wounded, **Commander James D. Johnston (South)**, Tennessee's flag captain, asked the wounded Buchanan's permission and ran up the white flag. The strongest ironclad in the Confederacy had fought the whole Union fleet single-handed until she was battered into surrender. By around ten in the morning, the bay was won.

> **MEANWHILE — Tecumseh's commander did not jump.** Back at the sunken monitor, the battle's most-told human moment had played out in the few seconds Tecumseh took to go down — and like the famous order, it comes to us as cherished naval lore rather than documented fact, because the only man who could tell it was the one who lived. As the story is handed down, Commander Craven (North) and the ship's pilot, John Collins, reached the narrow escape scuttle at the same instant, with time for only one man to climb out. Craven is said to have stepped back — "After you, pilot" — and let Collins go up first. Collins made it; Craven did not. Collins later described the end: the moment he reached the top of the ladder, the vessel seemed to drop out from under him. It is ranked alongside "Don't give up the ship" as a Navy touchstone, and it may well be exactly true — but it is a survivor's account of a dead man's last words, so it is told here the way it should be: as the thing we remember, gently held at arm's length.

## what-it-won · The meaning · The door, the reason, and the men on the deck
### What the fleet shut, and who shut it

**OUTCOME: Union victory.** Farragut's fleet forced the bay, destroyed the last significant Confederate squadron on the Gulf — Tennessee captured, the gunboats wrecked or taken, Buchanan a prisoner — and closed Mobile to blockade-runners. Over the following weeks the forts gave up: **Fort Powell**, cut off behind the fleet, had its guns spiked and its magazine blown by **Lieutenant Colonel James M. Williams (South)** the night after the battle; **Fort Gaines** surrendered on August 8 under **Colonel Charles D. Anderson (South)**; and the strong one, **Fort Morgan**, under **Brigadier General Richard L. Page (South)**, held out under bombardment until August 23. The last open Gulf door, east of the Mississippi, was shut.

But — to be precise, the way we promised to be — the *port* closed, not the *city*. Mobile the town, miles up the bay, was never attacked in 1864 and held out until April 1865. What happened on August 5 corked the bottle. It did not drink it.

The strategic payoff was large, but the political payoff was enormous, and it has to be given full weight. Mobile Bay came three months before the November 1864 election, at the lowest ebb of Northern morale. The war was grinding on, the casualty lists were endless, and Abraham Lincoln's re-election was genuinely in doubt — a war-weary North was within reach of voting in a peace candidate and letting the Confederacy go. Then, in a matter of weeks, the news turned: Farragut took Mobile Bay, and almost immediately Sherman captured Atlanta on September 2. The gloom broke. Historian James McPherson described Mobile Bay and Atlanta together as the first blow of a one-two punch that scattered Northern war-weariness and helped assure Lincoln's re-election — and thus the war's continuation to total Union victory and the end of slavery. The fleet action in the bay was, in the end, also a vote in that election.

And that brings us back to the thing this story opened with: the door was a door *for* something. The Confederacy fought to preserve and extend slavery. That is not editorializing; it is the plain reason eleven states left the Union and built armies. The blockade-runners feeding through Mobile were the supply line of a slaveholding republic, and the blockade that strangled them was, concretely, the instrument shutting down the economy of a society built on owning human beings. The blockade and emancipation were two halves of one policy — as Union ships took the coast, they began taking fugitive slaves aboard and enlisting the men. Closing the port was, in the most literal sense, an attack on slavery.

But the clearest proof of what the war was for at Mobile Bay was not the strategy. It was who was firing Farragut's guns. By 1864 the U.S. Navy was a substantially integrated force — nearly eighteen thousand men of African descent served in it during the war, about a fifth of its enlisted strength, and in Farragut's own West Gulf Blockading Squadron roughly one in four of the enlisted men aboard ship were Black. They were not in the back. They were on the gun decks of the flagship, in the fight.

Two of them, on Hartford's shell-whip — the crew that hauled ammunition up to the guns — earned the Medal of Honor that day, and their stories are the cause made flesh. **Landsman John Lawson (North)** was a free-born Black sailor from Philadelphia. When an enemy shell struck the berth deck, it wounded him in the leg and slammed him violently against the side of the ship; he was urged to go below for treatment and refused, and stayed at his post for the rest of the action. And **Landsman Wilson Brown (North)** — Brown had been **born enslaved on a plantation near Natchez, Mississippi.** A shellburst killed a man on the ladder above him and knocked Brown unconscious into the hold; he came to, climbed back to the shell-whip on the berth deck, and kept the ammunition moving though four of the six men at his station had been killed or wounded. Four African American sailors won the Medal of Honor at Mobile Bay, among an extraordinary number awarded for the battle.

Sit for one second with what that means. A man born a slave on a Mississippi plantation was, that August morning, working the guns of the fleet that was shutting the Confederacy's last Gulf door. The war's cause and the war's instrument were standing on the same deck. The port was the *object*. Slavery was the *reason*. And a formerly enslaved man, firing the flagship's guns toward his own freedom, was the proof of the reason — impossible to miss, exactly where the story said it would be.

> **MEANWHILE — what the running ships carried.** It is easy to file Mobile Bay as a battle "about a port," and the admirals on both sides did fight over the harbor. But sit a moment with what the harbor was *for*. The blockade-runners Farragut came to shut out carried the cotton and the cargo of a republic founded to keep four million people in bondage. That is the symmetry at the heart of this fight: the very port that fed a slaveholding war effort was sealed by a fleet whose own decks carried men that war effort had once claimed to own — a man born a slave in Mississippi among them, working the shell-whip of the flagship. The integrated crew is not a footnote to the battle of Mobile Bay. It is the point of it.
