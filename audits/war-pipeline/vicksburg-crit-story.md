# Vicksburg — STORYTELLING critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

Judged AS A STORY (the #1 goal). The bar is the shipped Shiloh sections
(`src/app/war-civil-war/western/shiloh/s/[section]/section-narrative.tsx`):
informal popular-history voice; hooks that land on a human or an idea before the
units arrive; play-by-play kept moving by stakes and character; italic asides
that say "treat this as legend, not gospel"; Meanwhile sidebars that widen the
lens. Selective coverage is a virtue, not a gap.

**Overall: GOOD (ship-worthy after the MUST-IMPROVE items).** This is genuinely
strong work — voice is on-model, the spine ("one army grows while the other
wastes away") is clear, and the emancipation section is the best thing in the
draft. The risk across 6 sections is the middle: §the-gamble compresses the most
famous 18 days of the war into a march-and-battle checklist, and §the-siege
buries its most-feltable material (the caves, the starvation) under setup. Fix
those two and this sustains.

---

## §the-key — GOOD

**Hook & stakes — STRONG.** "the Mississippi River was the spine of a continent,
and the Confederacy was sitting on the one vertebra that mattered" is exactly the
Shiloh register. The hairpin-bend / guns-looking-down-on-the-water image is
concrete and earns the "key" metaphor honestly.

**Cause-naming — STRONG.** Paragraph 4 names slavery in the section's own prose
and ties it to *this* valley ("Vicksburg's own valley… the river plantations
Grant's army would soon march past were worked by enslaved people"). It also
plants the through-line — "every mile Grant advanced… the institution the South
had seceded to protect began dissolving behind him" — which §emancipation pays
off. This is the framing gate done right, not a bolted-on sentence.

**Voice — GOOD.** "turn the key," "sawed the Confederacy clean in two,"
"corked the bottle" all land.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE — the Lincoln "Vicksburg is the key" quote is hedged in prose
> ("is supposed to have told his cabinet") AND flagged [VERIFY]. As a *story
> beat* the hedge slightly deflates the section's own title ("The Key to the
> War"). Either commit to the quote if the fact-checker clears it, or cut the
> quote and keep "Lincoln understood this exactly" — the idea is stronger
> unhedged than the quote is hedged.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE — two consecutive paragraphs (3 and the Meanwhile) restate "no
> straightforward attack can get through the swamp/bluff/guns." Slight echo; the
> OUTCOME box says it a third time. Trim one.

## §the-failed-approaches — GOOD

**Hook & stakes — STRONG.** "for four months the water beat him" is a clean,
human framing of what could have been a dry engineering recap. Naming the
through-line of the section up front ("the failure was the point") gives the
reader a reason to care about four dead ends.

**Play-by-play tedium — WATCH, currently OK.** This is the section most at risk
of becoming a checklist (Canal → Lake Providence → Yazoo Pass → Steele's Bayou),
and it mostly dodges it because each scheme gets a vivid one-line image (the
cotton-bale Fort Pemberton; Porter's gunboats "stuck in a flooded forest"). The
"Lake Providence Boondoggle" nickname and the felled-trees-front-and-back detail
keep it alive. **Do not expand this section** — four failures at this density is
the right call; one more sentence each would tip it into the checklist.

**Voice — STRONG.** "miserable, mud-caked, disease-ridden season" / "Sometimes
the value of a dead end is that it proves the road is dead" — the second is a
genuinely good house-voice aphorism.

**Grant-drinking handling — GOOD.** Framed exactly like Shiloh frames its legends
(italic-aside register, "rumor and political knife-work… not fact"). On-model.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE — Steele's Bayou is "the strangest of all" but gets the same
> word-count as the others. One more concrete beat of how absurd it was (warships
> lost in a *forest*, infantry hacking through swamp to rescue the navy) would
> let the section's funniest image breathe. Minor — don't break the no-expand
> rule for it; trade a sentence from Yazoo Pass.

## §the-gamble — NEEDS WORK

This is the campaign's legend and the draft knows it ("the thing that made the
whole campaign legend"). The problem is the back half reads like the checklist
the brief warns about.

**Hook & stakes — STRONG.** "the kind of plan that gets a general either
remembered or relieved" is a great opener — stakes are personal and immediate.

**Play-by-play tedium — MUST-IMPROVE.** Paragraph 4 is the failure mode named in
the brief. Five battles get marched through as a list: "won at Port Gibson on May
1… beat a Confederate brigade at Raymond on May 12… took Jackson… Champion Hill…
Big Black River." Dates, place, verb, next. The reader is told this is "the thing
that made the whole campaign legend" but is not *shown* why it felt legendary —
the audacity is asserted ("drove inland fast"), not dramatized. Compare Shiloh,
which never lists engagements; it picks Sherman's stand, Johnston's death, the
Hornet's Nest and *stays* in each long enough to feel it.

> MUST-IMPROVE — pick ONE battle to actually inhabit (Champion Hill is the
> obvious choice — it's named "the hardest-fought battle of the campaign" and it
> "sealed Vicksburg's fate") and give it a real beat: a moment, the ridges, the
> stakes of the two armies that must not combine. Let the other four stay as the
> fast montage — that contrast (four-in-a-blur, then *stop* on Champion Hill) is
> the storyteller's tool the section is missing. Right now Champion Hill gets one
> sentence more than Raymond and that is not enough for "the hardest-fought
> battle of the campaign."

**Human dimension — SHOULD-IMPROVE.** "lived off the country… taking the rest
from the Mississippi countryside" passes over the most felt-able thing in the
section: an army of tens of thousands feeding itself off the plantations it
marched through. That's both vivid AND the emancipation through-line (these are
the same plantations §the-key promised would dissolve). One concrete sentence —
what living off the country actually looked like — would do double duty.

**Voice — GOOD.** "cut himself loose," "the gamble had worked," the closing
inventory ("crossed a major river, cut his own supply line, won five battles,
taken a state capital") is a strong rhetorical button.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE — the Bruinsburg "largest amphibious landing… until D-Day 1944"
> is a great fact buried mid-paragraph between ferrying and stepping ashore. Let
> it land as its own beat; it's exactly the kind of scale-anchor Shiloh uses.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE — Grierson's 600-mile raid is one sentence. As a *story* it's
> the most cinematic thing in the section (a cavalry column slashing the length
> of a state as a decoy). It doesn't need a paragraph, but it's underplayed
> relative to its drama.

## §the-siege — NEEDS WORK

The material here (caves, mule meat, rats at $3.50, a 26-year-old diarist
underground) is the most *feltable* in the whole draft. The structure half-buries
it.

**Hook & stakes — GOOD, but mis-ordered.** The section opens correctly on "Grant
did not want a siege" (good — character, not logistics). But then it spends two
full paragraphs on the failed assaults and the engineering (15 miles of trench,
220 guns) before reaching the civilians in paragraph 3. The human heart of the
siege is structurally third.

> MUST-IMPROVE — the caves and the starvation must be FELT, and right now they're
> reported competently rather than dramatized. "They went underground" and "the
> food ran out. The beef went first" are the right instinct but too clipped.
> This is the one section where slowing DOWN is the fix. Stay in the cave with
> Loughborough. The rats-for-sale, the pea-and-cornmeal bread, the
> "shoe leather… last resort" — these are gut-punch details delivered as a list.
> Let one of them be a scene. Shiloh's standard is the boot quietly filling with
> blood; the siege has equivalent images and currently spends them too fast.

**Play-by-play — GOOD.** The two assaults (May 19, May 22) are handled well —
McClernand's false breakthrough that "fed more men into the killing ground" is a
real character beat, not a stat. No checklist problem here.

**The arithmetic — STRONG.** "one army growing while the other wasted away — was
the whole arithmetic of the siege" is the draft's best single line of analysis
and the spine of the whole piece. Keep it exactly.

**Human dimension — NEEDS WORK (the central note).** The "fewer than a dozen
civilians killed" out of 22,000 shells is a stunning statistic stated flatly. The
Loughborough quote is the section's one chance to put the reader *inside* a cave
during a bombardment — and it's both quarantined to one sentence and [VERIFY]-
flagged. If the fact-checker clears it, build the cave scene around it. If not,
the section still needs one sentence of sensory cave-life (the soft loess walls,
timing your dash above ground to the gaps in the shelling — that detail is
already in the draft and is the best thing in the paragraph; lead with it).

> SHOULD-IMPROVE — the two mines (June 25 crater, July 1) are introduced and then
> immediately dismissed ("almost beside the point… the city was being starved to
> death"). That's the right judgment, but blowing a mine under a fort and
> failing to hold the crater is a vivid image to raise and drop in two clauses.
> Either give the crater one real sentence or cut it to a half-line — the current
> middle ground name-drops without dramatizing.

**Voice — GOOD.** "out-camp the enemy," "strangle the city," the Gettysburg
Meanwhile ("the country did not yet know that both would break the same week") is
a strong cliffhanger into §the-surrender.

## §emancipation-made-real — STRONG

The best section in the draft, and the one that justifies the whole "causes-first"
direction. This is what the war app is *for*.

**Hook & stakes — STRONG.** "the part… that the maps and the casualty tables
miss, and it is the heart of it" earns the claim and then delivers on it.

**The reframe — STRONG.** "It did not come apart because Grant freed people,
exactly. It came apart because enslaved people freed themselves." This is the
section's thesis and it's both historically honest (NPS-sourced) and dramatically
alive. The agency is given to the right people.

**Human dimension — STRONG.** Milliken's Bend is FELT, which the brief
specifically asked for. "bayonets and clubbed muskets across the levee… men
killing each other at arm's length" is visceral; the 9th Louisiana's 68% loss
and "days into uniform, barely trained and poorly armed" give the hand-to-hand
stand real weight. The contested-casualty range is handled as story, not
hedging ("the figure is genuinely disputed").

**Voice — STRONG.** "The men the South had owned were now being handed rifles to
fight the South" / "an answer written in blood on a Louisiana levee." On-model
and earned, not purple.

**Lincoln close — STRONG.** Ending on the Conkling letter ("with silent tongue,
and clenched teeth… well-poised bayonet") and binding the open river to the Black
soldiers is the right last beat — it's the through-line from §the-key closing.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE (the only note) — the contraband-camps paragraph is the one soft
> spot. It's structurally a bridge (self-emancipation → camps → recruiting →
> Milliken's Bend) and reads slightly list-like ("disease ran through… prejudice
> and violence… many were put to wage labor"). The brutality of the camps is the
> hard-place half of "a lifeline and a hard place" and it's told, not felt. One
> concrete image of camp conditions would keep the section honest about its
> darker half without slowing the build to Milliken's Bend. Minor — do not
> expand much; the section's pace is otherwise ideal.

## §the-surrender — GOOD

A satisfying close that resists the temptation to over-celebrate.

**Hook & stakes — STRONG.** "Pemberton was out of road" is a clean, human opener
that carries the exhaustion of the whole siege.

**Human dimension — GOOD.** Bowen carrying the flag of truce while already dying
of dysentery, dead within days — "one more casualty of the disease that had
hollowed out the garrison" — is a quietly devastating detail that ties surrender
back to the starvation of §the-siege. The "29,495 marched out… gaunt from weeks
of mule meat and pea bread" callback works.

**Play-by-play — GOOD.** The parole arithmetic (shipping 30,000 prisoners would
tie up the boats → terms instead) is good "show the reasoning" writing, very much
the Shiloh standard of explaining mechanism.

**The twin-turning-point — STRONG.** Holding Gettysburg for the Meanwhile rather
than the body is the right structural call — it keeps the section's focus on the
river while still landing "the week the Confederacy lost the initiative for
good." The "reached for victory in the East and gambled on survival in the West…
lost at both" framing is excellent.

**Voice — GOOD.** "The key was in the pocket at last" closes the §the-key
metaphor cleanly.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE — the Pemberton-chose-the-Fourth-of-July story is the most
> human/ironic beat available at the surrender (a Pennsylvania-born Confederate
> banking on Union pride in the date), but it's wrapped in so much [VERIFY]
> hedging ("the exact words attributed to him are not reliable… the gist…
> reasonably well attested") that the *story* gets smothered by the caveat. If
> the fact-checker clears the gist, tell it cleanly in one confident sentence and
> move the hedge to a footnote-style italic aside (the Shiloh model: state the
> beat, then one italic line on its reliability). Right now the caveat is longer
> than the anecdote.

> SHOULD-IMPROVE — the casualty paragraph ("roughly 4,800… something over
> 10,000 each side") is necessary but lands as a number-dump right before the
> emotional close. Consider tightening to the one figure that matters (the
> draft even says so: "the figure that mattered was the surrender: nearly 30,000
> men… an entire field army gone") and trimming the rest.

## Pacing across 6 sections — does it sustain?

Mostly yes. The arc is well-shaped: setup (key) → frustration (failures) →
audacity (gamble) → grind (siege) → meaning (emancipation) → resolution
(surrender). The two soft spots are adjacent and in the middle — §the-gamble
(checklist) and §the-siege (buried heart) — which is the dangerous place for a
long campaign to sag. Fixing both MUST-IMPROVE items removes the only stretch
where a reader might feel the campaign becoming a march-and-battle recap.
Selective coverage is correctly used throughout (five battles compressed, four
failed approaches compressed) — the draft does NOT try to cover everything, which
is right. The Meanwhile sidebars consistently widen the lens (Johnston helpless
outside; the country waiting on the river; Gettysburg) and each one earns its
place. Voice is on-model start to finish.
