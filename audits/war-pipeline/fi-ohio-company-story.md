# STORYTELLING CRITIC REPORT — "The Ohio Country & the Ohio Company"

**Section:** French & Indian War, theme (CAUSE / off-the-battlefield)
**Draft:** `audits/war-pipeline/fi-ohio-company-draft.md`
**Fact pack:** `audits/war-pipeline/fi-ohio-company-factpack.md`
**Verdict: PASS**

This is a strong piece of cause-narrative. It has a real thesis (a world war began as a
real-estate deal), it keeps the dispossessed Native nations as actors rather than scenery,
and it earns its emotional payoff because the structure repeats one fact — empires claimed
land they did not live on — in escalating registers (lead plates, royal grant, Tanaghrisson's
speech) until it lands. The house voice holds throughout: no em-dashes in body prose, no
meta-narrator, no bold, full-rank-first, cross-refs as standalone pill lines.

---

## House-voice compliance scan

- **No em-dashes in prose: PASS.** The only em-dashes in the file are (a) the quote-to-speaker
  separator inside `> quote:` attribution lines, which is standard citation syntax and not
  reader-facing prose, and (b) one inside a `[VERIFY:]` author note that will not ship. Body
  prose uses commas where em-dashes would have gone ("a slab of lead about 11 inches by 7.5
  inches, and nailed the French royal arms..."). Clean.
- **No en-dashes in prose: PASS.** En-dashes appear only in eyebrow date ranges ("1748–1749"),
  which is correct numeric-range typography, not prose punctuation.
- **No meta-narrator: PASS, with two watch-items.** The draft mostly avoids "in this section."
  Two lines flirt with it: "the venture this section is about" (line 23) and "that one has its
  own story below" (line 81). Neither is a hard violation — they read as orientation, not as a
  narrator talking about the writing — but if a later voice pass is happening anyway, prefer
  "the venture at the center of this story" and just let the Fort Duquesne race arrive without
  the "below" signpost.
- **Little/no bold: PASS.** Zero bold markers in the file.
- **Full-rank-first: PASS.** "Major General Edward Braddock," "Captain William Trent," "Ensign
  Edward Ward," "Lieutenant Governor of Virginia" all introduce with full rank. Washington is
  introduced as "a major and adjutant in the Virginia militia," which is correct.
- **Cross-refs as standalone pill lines: PASS.** All three cross-refs are bare bracketed link
  lines on their own ("[The Contest for Native Alliances]", "[Jumonville Glen]", "[Fort
  Necessity]"), not woven into sentences.

No house-voice rule is violated.

## Heading-by-heading grades

### Opening (untitled hook) — STRONG
"A world war began as a property scheme. Not over a throne, not over a religion, but over a
stretch of rich river-bottom land..." This is the best sentence in the piece and exactly the
right hook for a CAUSE theme: it states the thesis as a small, almost sordid fact and lets the
scale do the work. The pivot to "The people who did live there... had already moved once to get
away from men exactly like these" plants the moral spine immediately. No notes.

### The land everyone wanted and no empire held — STRONG
The "land bridge" / "saw New France in half" image gives France a concrete motive instead of an
abstract one. The Beaver Wars → 1720s re-settlement arc is handled as story ("They were getting
away") rather than as a date dump. "Hold the shape of that in mind, because everything else turns
on it" is a legitimate storyteller's beat, not meta-narration — it's pointing at the world, not
at the prose. Strong.

### A syndicate of planters and a king's signature — GOOD
The content is right and the conflict-of-interest seeds (the teenage George Washington
shareholder; Dinwiddie holding shares) are well placed for later payoff. One pacing note: the
investor roster in the second-to-last paragraph (lines 41) is the closest the piece comes to a
list-of-facts lull — six names with parenthetical "(British)" tags in a row. It survives because
the paragraph ends on the load-bearing name (Dinwiddie) with a forward hook, but consider
trimming Robert Carter III / John Tayloe II to "and other tidewater shareholders" so the
genuinely load-bearing names (the Washingtons, Mason, Hanbury, Dinwiddie) carry weight. Not
blocking. "read it slowly" / "the load-bearing fact of this entire story" is an acceptable
emphasis beat.

### The land rush and the king of the traders — GOOD
"the king of the traders" is a good handle and the Croghan legal-wrinkle (Pennsylvania bars
the purchase, so he ends up working for Virginia) is told as a small human irony rather than a
procedural note. The reserved "two square miles at the Forks of the Ohio... for a British fort"
is a sharp foreshadow of the climax. The `[VERIFY: Loyal Company...]` inline marker is correctly
left as a marker and the prose honors the fact-pack GAP by not asserting an acreage. Good.

### Lead plates in the ground — STRONG
The strongest of the middle sections. "His method was strange and formal" sets up the lead-plate
ceremony as theater, and "the way it failed is the point" turns a curio into argument. The image
of the Iroquois in Céloron's own party peeling off and pulling the plates back down is vivid and
true to the sources. The closing "notice who is missing from the whole ceremony" reprises the
thesis without restating it flatly. Strong.

### A chain of forts toward the Forks — GOOD
Efficient connective tissue. The four-fort table-data is rendered as moving narrative ("Ceremony
having failed, France reached for stone and timber") rather than a spec sheet, which is the right
call for a theme section. "the man who would not let it stand was an Ohio Company shareholder" is
a clean handoff to the next beat. Good.

### An ultimatum carried by a 21-year-old — STRONG
The Dinwiddie conflict-of-interest is the spine of the section and it's stated with real force
("The empire's interest and the Company's interest had become, in Dinwiddie's person, the same
interest"). Saint-Pierre's "diplomatic French for: we are not leaving, and you cannot make us"
is a good house-voice gloss that earns its informality. The 21-year-old framing keeps the human
dimension front and center. Strong.

### The land does not belong to either of you — STRONG
This is the heart of the piece and it delivers. The contested-identity handling of Tanaghrisson
("Both readings can be held at once, and the gap between them is exactly the tension this story
runs on") respects the fact pack's `[CONTESTED]` flags without going limp or academic. The
Iroquois-claim-vs-resident-nations distinction is the most sophisticated idea in the section and
it's made legible. The two Tanaghrisson quotes are the emotional climax, well set up by "says
plainly what no empire wanted to hear." Strong.

### The race for the Forks — STRONG
The climax pays off every seed: the grant ordered a fort here, Washington saw why, Virginia
built first by weeks, France took it without a fight. "It was a modest thing, and it did not
last two months" is good compressed pacing. The Braddock forward-reference ("that is another
story") is a legitimate sequel hook. Strong.

### What was already being taken — GOOD
A risk section — closing CAUSE essays often sag into thesis-restatement — and this one mostly
avoids it by adding the genuinely new "naming" argument (the war's very name "centers their
point of view and flattens everyone else into one word"). The leverage inventory ("They could
refuse a land cession... They could expel a trader... They could tip the balance") is a touch
list-like but it's doing real analytical work, not padding. The final triplet ("The land grab
came first. The war came after. And the families on the ground at the Forks were about to pay
for both.") is a strong button. Good.

## Cross-section / pacing notes (non-blocking)

- The thesis sentence ("the real-estate venture and the war are not two stories. They are one")
  appears in close-to-identical form at the end of "The race for the Forks" (line 149) and is
  echoed again in the closing section. Three statements of the same payoff in the last third is
  one too many; consider letting the Forks section land it and trimming the echo so the closer
  carries the *naming* argument as its fresh note rather than re-ringing the same bell.
- Several sections open with the same move ("X having failed, ..." / "It all came down to ...").
  It works, but a future voice pass could vary two of the openers to avoid a rhythm the careful
  reader starts to hear.

None of the above is ship-blocking. No heading grades NEEDS WORK or REWRITE; no house-voice rule
is violated.
