# Framing / Lost Cause Gate — Glorieta Pass
**Section:** the-dream / the-battle / the-wagons (Confederate Dream of the Pacific)
**Date reviewed:** 2026-05-30
**Reviewer:** framing critic gate

---

## FIRST CHECK: Is slavery named as the cause in the section's own prose?

**PASS.** The word appears explicitly, the framing is direct, and it lands hard in paragraph 2 of the opening section:

> "And it was, at bottom, a bid to push slavery across the continent. Strip away the gold and the ports and the maps, and the Confederate dream of the Southwest was the same thing the Confederacy was everywhere else: an attempt to extend a slaveholding republic, by force, all the way to the Pacific. The men marching up the Rio Grande were carrying the institution of human bondage west with them. That is what the fight in New Mexico was finally about. The rest was real estate."

This is not buried or euphemized. It appears before any tactical narrative begins, and it is stated as the author's own declarative judgment, not hedged as "some historians argue." The framing also recurs at the section's close: "The dream of a slaveholding republic stretching to the ocean died in a burning wagon park at the mouth of a canyon." The cause-naming is strong and properly load-bearing.

## Finding 2 — False both-sides balance
**Verdict: FAIR**

The draft does not distribute moral weight evenly between the two sides. The Confederate objective is named as conquest in the service of slavery; the Union objective is named as denial of that conquest. Confederate tactical wins (Valverde, Pigeon's Ranch) are described accurately but are explicitly framed as pyrrhic and self-defeating, not celebrated. The Scurry quote is deployed to make the Confederates themselves testify to their own defeat. No false equivalence detected.

## Finding 3 — Confederate romanticization ("lost cause of the West" / dashing Texans)
**Verdict: FAIR with one minor note**

The draft describes the Confederate plan as "breathtaking" on paper (paragraph 1, "the-dream"), immediately followed by the causation paragraph stripping that romance away. That sequence is intentional and works: the audacity is acknowledged so the reader understands the strategic stakes, then immediately recontextualized as slavery expansion. The Texans are not described as gallant, dashing, or heroic; they are described as hungry, miscalculating, and ultimately beaten by their own supply failure. Confederates who die in battle (Shropshire, Raguet) are named without elegy. Scurry's tactical victory is given its due as a military fact, then immediately undercut. The retreat is described as "a slow catastrophe" with soldiers stripping food from civilians.

**Minor note (NOT a MUST-FIX, noted for author awareness):** The phrase "the wildest prize of the whole war" in the opening sentence is atmospheric but leans slightly into the adventure-register that romanticizes the Confederate enterprise. It is not harmful in context — the slaveholding-republic paragraph comes within the same section — but the author may want to consider whether "wildest" slightly glorifies the audacity in a way that a reader could quote out of context. SHOULD-FIX at author's discretion; does not distort the section.

## Finding 4 — Erasure of New Mexican Hispanic and Colorado volunteers
**Verdict: FAIR — no erasure; Hispanic role explicitly centered**

This is the sharpest historical risk in this section, and the draft handles it well. Manuel Chaves is:

- Named and identified as "Lt. Col. Manuel Chaves (North), a Hispanic New Mexican frontiersman of the 2nd New Mexico Mounted Volunteers"
- Identified as the essential guide whose local knowledge made the decisive flanking march possible: "chosen by Canby and Chivington precisely because he knew this terrain in a way no Coloradan or Texan ever could"
- The closing "Meanwhile" paragraph makes the distributional credit explicit: "Manuel Chaves and his New Mexico volunteers had supplied the one thing that made the decisive blow possible, the knowledge of how to get over the mesa and where the wagons would be"

The Colorado Pike's Peakers receive significant attention (their march is dramatically described), but their glory is explicitly conditioned on Chaves and the New Mexico volunteers making it possible. The framing is additive, not substitutional.

Historical context from NPS and secondary sources confirms that the standard historiographical failure for this battle is assigning all credit to the Colorado volunteers and erasing the New Mexicans (one source: "history books detailing the battle are filled with the names of Anglo leaders, Hispanic surnames rarely come up"). The draft resists this failure clearly.

**One small verification note:** The draft identifies Chaves as "2nd New Mexico Mounted Volunteers." Wikipedia and NPS sources list him variously as 2nd New Mexico Infantry or 1st New Mexico Infantry depending on the engagement; some sources give "2nd New Mexico Mounted Rifles." This is not a framing concern but a fact-check flag — the unit designation should be verified against the primary record. SHOULD-FIX (fact accuracy, not framing).

## Finding 5 — Chivington / Sand Creek forward reference
**Verdict: FAIR — handled correctly and at appropriate weight**

The draft includes the Chivington/Sand Creek forward reference in a dedicated final paragraph of "the-wagons":

> "One more thing has to be said about the man who lit that fire, because history did not leave him a hero. Just over two years later, in November 1864, the same John Chivington, by then a colonel commanding in Colorado, led the Sand Creek Massacre, in which his men attacked a peaceful Cheyenne and Arapaho encampment and killed scores of people, most of them women and children. The officer who saved the Union's hold on the Southwest became one of the worst perpetrators of anti-Native violence in the West. Both of those things are true, and the second does not erase the first, or the first the second."

Assessment:
- The encampment is correctly described as "peaceful" and under a flag of truce (confirmed by NPS and multiple secondary sources — "under a white flag of truce when they were attacked")
- "Scores" is an appropriately cautious death-count word given the disputed toll (estimates range from ~150 to over 230, with women and children constituting ~two-thirds); the draft's fact ledger notes this range and the deliberate vagueness
- "One of the worst perpetrators of anti-Native violence in the West" is defensible — Chivington was discredited by three separate federal investigations
- The "both things are true" formulation is structurally correct and avoids two failure modes: (a) using Sand Creek to retroactively erase his role at Glorieta, or (b) using Glorieta to rehabilitate Sand Creek. The section neither lionizes nor whitewashes

The human reality is not sanitized. The victims are named as Cheyenne and Arapaho, their status as a peaceful camp is stated, the sex and age of most victims is noted. This is adequate for a forward reference in a battle section (Sand Creek is not the subject here); a fuller reckoning belongs in a future section specifically covering Sand Creek or Indian policy.

**No flag.**

## Finding 6 — Presentism
**Verdict: FAIR**

The draft does not project 21st-century political categories backward, and it does not use anachronistic language to pass judgment on historical actors in ways that would distort the reader's understanding of period context. The cause-naming ("extend a slaveholding republic") uses the period's own vocabulary and logic, not a retrofit. The characterization of Confederate defeat as "the dream … died" is historical outcome description, not rhetorical presentism. No flag.

## Finding 7 — Unfair sympathy toward Confederate actors
**Verdict: FAIR**

Individual Confederate officers (Scurry, Pyron, Shropshire, Raguet, Sibley) are treated with historical matter-of-factness: their tactical decisions are described accurately, their failures are noted, their deaths are recorded without hagiography. The Scurry quote is allowed to do its job without editorial sympathy. Sibley's absence from the battlefield is noted with deliberate understatement (the draft explicitly flags in its fact ledger that intoxication was NOT asserted, consistent with responsible uncertainty). No Confederate figure is romanticized. No flag.

## Finding 8 — Sanitizing human cost / Confederate soldiers stripping civilians
**Verdict: FAIR**

The retreat passage includes: "Hungry soldiers stripped food from New Mexican civilians along the way, and some of those civilians fought back." This is brief but present. The violence and desperation of the retreat is named, the harm to civilians is named, and the civilian resistance is named. This is not sanitized. The section could go deeper on the civilian experience of Confederate occupation in Santa Fe, but for a battle-section forward reference this is adequate. No MUST-FIX.
