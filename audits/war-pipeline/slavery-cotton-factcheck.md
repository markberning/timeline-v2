# Fact-check report — "King Cotton, and the Wealth the South Would Kill to Keep"
Checker: Claude (Sonnet 4.6), 2026-05-22. Draft: `slavery-cotton-draft.md` v1.

## Per-claim verification

### PRESSURE — the world before the boom

1. ✅ CONFIRMED — Post-Revolution, tobacco was exhausting Virginia/Maryland soil; slavery looked to some like it might be fading in the Upper South.
   Source: American Yawp ch. 11 "The Cotton Revolution"; Wikipedia, *Slavery in the United States*.

2. ✅ CONFIRMED — Long-staple (Sea Island) cotton: smooth seeds, grew only on the coastal strip.
   Source: Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*.

3. ✅ CONFIRMED — Short-staple (green-seed) cotton: grew well across the Southern interior; sticky seeds made it enormously laborious to process.
   Source: Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*.

4. ✅ CONFIRMED — By hand, a single laborer needed about ten hours to separate one pound of fiber from the seeds.
   Source: Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*; American Yawp ch. 11.

5. ✅ CONFIRMED — "Gin" is short for "engine" (cotton engine).
   Source: Wikipedia, *Cotton gin* — the article states the term "gin" means "cotton engine," with "gin" being short for engine.

### TURNING POINT — the cotton gin

6. ✅ CONFIRMED — Eli Whitney was born in Massachusetts (specifically Westborough, MA, born December 8, 1765).
   Source: Wikipedia, *Eli Whitney*.

7. ✅ CONFIRMED — Whitney graduated from Yale (entered 1789, graduated Phi Beta Kappa 1792).
   Source: Wikipedia, *Eli Whitney*.

8. ✅ CONFIRMED — Whitney was visiting a Georgia plantation when he invented the gin (Mulberry Grove Plantation, owned by Catherine Littlefield Greene, widow of General Nathanael Greene).
   Source: Wikipedia, *Eli Whitney*.

9. ⚠️ UNSUPPORTED-OR-UNHEDGED — The draft describes the gin mechanism as "a hand-cranked drum studded with wire hooks that dragged the cotton fiber through a slotted screen too narrow for the seeds to follow." The actual mechanism per Wikipedia is a "wooden cylinder covered by rows of slender wires" that passed through "the bars of a comb-like grid" (not a "slotted screen"). The seeds were blocked by the comb-like bars, not a "slotted screen." The draft's description captures the function but the word "hooks" and the phrase "slotted screen" are imprecise renderings. Wikipedia also mentions a second rotating cylinder with brushes. "Wire hooks" is an oversimplification; sources say "slender wires." This is a minor mechanical inaccuracy, not a gross error, but it is an imprecise rendering of the mechanism.
   Source: Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*; ASME; HowStuffWorks.

10. ✅ CONFIRMED — Patent applied October 28, 1793; granted March 14, 1794.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*.

11. ✅ CONFIRMED — The gin could clean about 50 pounds of lint per day.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*; American Yawp ch. 11.

12. ✅ CONFIRMED — The paradox: a labor-saving machine multiplied the demand for enslaved labor (growing and picking still required human hands).
    Source: Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*; American Yawp ch. 11.

### THE BOOM — numbers

13. ✅ CONFIRMED — Cotton output: 156,000 bales (1800) → 750,000 bales (1830) → 2.85 million bales (1850) → more than 4 million bales (1860).
    Source: American Yawp ch. 11; Wikipedia, *Cotton gin*; fact pack §3.
    NOTE: The American Yawp gives 1860 production as "2 billion pounds" but expresses bale counts differently (it uses weight-based figures). The specific bale counts (156k/750k/2.85M/4M+) appear in the fact pack traced to American Yawp and Wikipedia. These are standard figures and well-established.

14. ✅ CONFIRMED — A bale weighed 400–500 pounds; 1860's crop was roughly two billion pounds. The draft hedges with "roughly."
    Source: American Yawp ch. 11.

15. ✅ CONFIRMED — By 1860 cotton was roughly 60 percent (more than 60 percent by some counts) of all US exports, worth on the order of $200 million a year.
    Source: Wikipedia, *King Cotton*; American Yawp ch. 11; multiple corroborating sources.

16. ✅ CONFIRMED — New Orleans moved approximately $220 million in goods, carried by ~3,500 vessels (steamboats).
    Source: American Yawp ch. 11 — states "nearly $220 million in trade" and "3,500 steamboats."
    NOTE: American Yawp says "3,500 steamboats" not "3,500 vessels" generically, but this is a trivial terminological softening.

17. ⚠️ UNSUPPORTED-OR-UNHEDGED — The draft states: "The United States grew roughly two-thirds of all the cotton on earth; across the decades from 1820 to 1860 it produced something like 80 percent of the world's supply, and fed about three-quarters of the cotton flowing into the mills of Britain."
    The King Cotton Wikipedia article says the US supplied "75% of the world's cotton" by 1860 — not "two-thirds." The two figures (two-thirds and 80 percent) appear to refer to different time windows, but the draft presents them somewhat conflated. The fact pack does give both figures (two-thirds as a general figure; 80% for 1820–1860), so this is not an error per se, but the phrasing is slightly confusing because these are presented as simultaneously true ("grew roughly two-thirds... produced something like 80 percent"). The figures are within range of the scholarly record and properly hedged. MINOR.
    Source: Wikipedia, *King Cotton*; fact pack §3.

18. ✅ CONFIRMED — "Black Belt" (originally named for its dark, rich soil). This is the primary and uncontested etymology. Booker T. Washington himself confirmed it referred to soil color. The secondary demographic meaning developed later but the soil origin is not disputed.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Black Belt in the American South*; Encyclopedia of Alabama.
    NOTE: The author's parenthetical "(originally for its dark, rich soil)" is accurate. The "Black Belt" name origin is NOT genuinely disputed — soil is the primary origin, confirmed by historical sources including Booker T. Washington's own 1901 autobiography. The draft's caution flag was overcautious; this claim stands.

### CONSEQUENCE — population and 1808

19. ✅ CONFIRMED — The Constitution barred Congress from banning the Atlantic slave trade before 1808; the Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves took effect January 1, 1808.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves*.

20. ⚠️ UNSUPPORTED-OR-UNHEDGED — The draft states "there were roughly 700,000 enslaved people in the United States in 1790." The actual 1790 census figure is 694,207 (also rendered in various sources as approximately 697,000 when rounding differently). The American Yawp gives 654,121 for the South alone in 1790, which is a South-only figure. The total US figure of ~694,000–700,000 is the correct framing when not South-limited. "Roughly 700,000" is an acceptable rounding of 694,207 and is within standard scholarly usage, but the fact pack says "~700,000" without citing a precise census figure, and some sources will cite the more exact 694,207. This is a minor hedge issue, not an error. The draft's use of "roughly" adequately covers the rounding.
    Source: US Census Bureau historical tables; Wikipedia, *1790 United States census*.

21. ✅ CONFIRMED — By the census of 1860 there were 3,953,760 enslaved people (some sources give 3,950,511). The draft correctly presents both figures.
    Source: Wikipedia, *1860 United States census*; fact pack §4.

22. ✅ CONFIRMED — The population nearly quadrupled between 1790 and 1860 despite the 1808 ban.
    Source: Math from confirmed figures above.

23. ✅ CONFIRMED — The American South was the only major slave society in the Americas to grow its enslaved population mainly by natural increase (births) rather than by continuously importing new captives. This is well-established in the scholarly record and not contested. The draft appropriately hedges with "appears to have been."
    Source: Gilder Lehrman Institute of American History ("Historical Context: American Slavery in Comparative Perspective"); Wikipedia, *Slavery in the United States*; multiple corroborating academic sources.
    KEY DETAIL FROM GILDER LEHRMAN: "Only 6 percent of African arrivals in the New World landed in what is now the United States. Yet by 1860, approximately two-thirds of all enslaved men, women, and children in the Western Hemisphere lived in the American South" — this underscores the natural-increase claim powerfully. The framing is well-established and the hedging in the draft is appropriate.

### THE SECOND MIDDLE PASSAGE

24. ✅ CONFIRMED — Upper South sold enslaved people south and west to the cotton frontier; historians call this the Second Middle Passage.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.

25. ✅ CONFIRMED — Estimates: about 835,000 forcibly relocated 1790–1860 by one count; roughly 1 million (or more) moved after 1808; commonly summarized as "about a million." The draft properly hedges this as contested.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*; fact pack §5.

26. ✅ CONFIRMED — Exporting states: Virginia and Maryland above all, also Kentucky, North Carolina, Tennessee, Missouri.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.

27. ✅ CONFIRMED — Importing states: Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Arkansas, Texas.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.

28. ✅ CONFIRMED — Franklin & Armfield was the largest slave-trading firm; Bolton, Dickens & Co. was another major trader.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.

29. ✅ CONFIRMED — New Orleans was the chief center of the lower-South trade by 1827; economists estimate more than 135,000 people were sold there between 1804 and 1862.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.

30. ✅ CONFIRMED — Natchez, Mississippi: "Forks of the Road" market. Also Richmond, Charleston, Savannah, Memphis.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.

31. ✅ CONFIRMED — Coffles: columns of people chained together, walking roughly 20 miles a day.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.

32. ✅ CONFIRMED — "Sold down the river" = being shipped via the Mississippi to the Deep South, usually a permanent severing from family.
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.

33. ✅ CONFIRMED — The trade routinely broke apart families (spouses, parents, children sold separately).
    Source: Wikipedia, *Slave trade in the United States*.

### CAPITAL — the $3 billion

34. ✅ CONFIRMED — By 1860 the estimated market value of the nation's enslaved people exceeded $3 billion; more than all capital invested in US railroads, factories, and banks combined; worth more than the South's farmland; the single largest capital asset in the American economy. The draft properly hedges this as an estimate.
    Source: National Museum of African American History & Culture; NPS; multiple economic histories; fact pack §6.
    NOTE: Some sources put the figure at $2.7 billion (one Brainly-sourced summary) or "over $3 billion" — the scholarly range is $2.7B–$3.5B depending on methodology. The draft's "$3 billion+" framing and explicit hedge ("the $3 billion figure is an estimate") correctly handles this.

### KING COTTON — Hammond

35. ✅ CONFIRMED — James Henry Hammond was a US Senator from South Carolina, serving December 7, 1857 to November 11, 1860.
    Source: Wikipedia, *James Henry Hammond*.

36. ✅ CONFIRMED — The "Cotton is King" / "mud-sill" speech was delivered to the US Senate on March 4, 1858.
    Source: Wikipedia, *James Henry Hammond*; Teaching American History; Raw History.

37. ❌ WRONG — **CRITICAL: The "Cotton is king" quote in the draft is wrong.** The draft reads:
    > "You dare not make war on cotton — no power on earth dares make war upon it. Cotton is king."

    The verbatim text of Hammond's speech is:
    > "No, you dare not make war on cotton. No power on earth dares **to** make war upon it. Cotton is king."

    Two errors:
    - The draft begins with "You dare not" but the actual speech begins with "**No, you dare not**" (the "No," is part of the quotation, not dropped context).
    - The draft reads "dares make war" but the actual speech reads "dares **to** make war."

    The draft's version is a paraphrase, not a verbatim quote. This is a MUST-FIX because the fact pack explicitly requires Hammond quotes be verbatim ("never altered").
    Sources: Teaching American History (teachingamericanhistory.org/document/cotton-is-king/); libquotes.com/james-henry-hammond; PBS; Raw History; multiple corroborating sources all agree on "No, you dare not" and "dares to make war."

38. ✅ CONFIRMED — The mud-sill quote in the draft reads:
    > "In all social systems there must be a class to do the menial duties, to perform the drudgery of life… It constitutes the very mud-sill of society."

    The verbatim text is: "In all social systems there must be a class to do the menial duties, to perform the drudgery of life. That is, a class requiring but a low order of intellect and but little skill. Its requisites are vigor, docility, fidelity. Such a class you must have, or you would not have that other class which leads progress, civilization, and refinement. It constitutes the very mud-sill of society and of political government."

    The draft's version uses an ellipsis to bridge from "...drudgery of life…" directly to "It constitutes the very mud-sill of society." The ellipsis is legitimate and no words inside the quotation marks are altered. The fact pack's version of this quote matches the draft. The omission of "and of political government" after "mud-sill of society" is an acceptable trim with ellipsis implicit at the end. CONFIRMED as acceptable.
    Source: Teaching American History; PBS; Raw History.

39. ✅ CONFIRMED — King Cotton diplomacy: conviction that Britain and France's dependence on Southern cotton would force them to recognize and aid the South; it failed (Britain turned to India, Egypt, and Brazil; refused to publicly side with a slaveholding rebellion).
    Source: Wikipedia, *King Cotton*; Wikipedia, *Cotton diplomacy*.

40. ✅ CONFIRMED — The definition of "mud-sill" (lowest foundation beam of a building, the timber laid right on the ground).
    Standard construction/architectural definition; consistent with Hammond's own usage.

## MUST-FIX list (blocks the section)

### ❌ MUST-FIX #1 — "Cotton is king" quote is not verbatim (Claim #37)

**The draft reads:**
> "You dare not make war on cotton — no power on earth dares make war upon it. Cotton is king."

**The correct verbatim text is:**
> "No, you dare not make war on cotton. No power on earth dares to make war upon it. Cotton is king."

Two specific errors inside the quotation marks:
1. The leading "No, " is missing — the quote begins "No, you dare not" not "You dare not."
2. "dares make war" must be "dares **to** make war."

The fact pack rule is explicit: Hammond quotes must be verbatim, never altered. This is a hard fail. Fix: replace the quoted text with the correct verbatim version. The prose context ("he argued, made it invincible:") will need a very minor adjustment since the three-sentence structure is slightly different.

## SHOULD-FIX list (minor precision / hedging issues)

### ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX A — Gin mechanism: "wire hooks" and "slotted screen" (Claim #9)

The draft describes the gin as "a hand-cranked drum studded with wire hooks that dragged the cotton fiber through a slotted screen too narrow for the seeds to follow." Wikipedia and engineering sources describe it as a "wooden cylinder covered by rows of slender wires" passing through "the bars of a comb-like grid." The seeds were blocked by the closely-spaced bars, not by a "slotted screen." "Wire hooks" is an oversimplification of "slender wires." The function is correctly described; the mechanical words are slightly off. Recommended fix: change "wire hooks" to "wire teeth" or "slender wires," and "slotted screen" to "a comb-like grid" or "a series of closely-spaced bars."

### ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX B — 1790 enslaved population precision (Claim #20)

"Roughly 700,000" is acceptable rounding of the census figure of 694,207, and the draft's "roughly" hedge covers it. No correction required, but note for the record that the exact figure is 694,207 if precision is needed in a future revision.

### ⚠️ SHOULD-FIX C — US world cotton share phrasing (Claim #17)

The draft presents "roughly two-thirds" and "something like 80 percent" as simultaneously true descriptions of US world cotton share. These figures refer to different time windows (the 80% figure is for the longer 1820–1860 period; by 1860 the US share is sometimes given as 75%). The prose is hedged and not technically wrong, but a reader could find it confusing. Recommended clarification: attribute the 80% figure explicitly to the 1820–1860 period (as the fact pack does), and leave "two-thirds" as the more general/conservative baseline figure.

---

## VERDICT

**FAIL** — One MUST-FIX blocks the section.

The "Cotton is king" quote as written is not verbatim; it omits "No," at the start and drops the word "to" from "dares to make war." The fact pack's own requirement ("verbatim, may be lightly trimmed for flow, never altered") makes this a hard blocker.

All other facts — every date, every production figure, every population figure, every firm name, every place name, the natural-increase framing, the $3 billion capital figure, the 1808 act, the coffle details, New Orleans figures, the mud-sill quote — are confirmed against authoritative sources. The two SHOULD-FIX items (gin mechanism wording, world-share phrasing) are minor precision issues that do not rise to MUST-FIX but should be corrected in the revision pass.
