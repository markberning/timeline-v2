# Vicksburg Campaign & Siege — CLARITY / NEWCOMER critic gate

War pipeline step 4 (critic gate). Reader profile: a sharp 15-year-old with ZERO prior Civil War knowledge, reading COLD. Verdict scale per item: **CLEAR** (a newcomer follows it) / **NEEDS-GLOSS** (term/leap goes by undefined; reader can limp on but loses meaning) / **LOST** (reader hits a wall — can't follow without outside knowledge).

Severity: **MUST-FIX** = a newcomer is genuinely blocked or seriously misled. **SHOULD-FIX** = comprehension dips but the reader survives; fix improves the read.

Bottom line up front: the draft is unusually good at scene-setting and inline definition — most of the "war jargon" the brief worried about (siege, running the batteries, parole, contraband, living off the country) is in fact defined in-line at first use. The real gaps are a cluster of **fort/earthwork vocabulary** ("redan / redoubt / lunette / Stockade Redan / Third Louisiana Redan"), a few **unit/structure words** ("corps," "division," "brigade," "battery," "garrison"), and one or two **geographic/strategic leaps** in the gamble section. Two MUST-FIX items only; the rest are SHOULD-FIX polish.

---

## MUST-FIX

### M1 — "redan / redoubt / lunette" + the named redans — LOST
**§the-siege, line 59 / line 65.** First strongpoint hit is the "**Stockade Redan**, one of the strongpoints in the roughly nine-mile arc of earthworks — **redans and redoubts and lunettes, the angled forts and bastions of the era** — that ringed Vicksburg's landward side." Later: "tunneled a mine under the **Third Louisiana Redan**."

The parenthetical "the angled forts and bastions of the era" is *meant* to be the gloss, but it defines four hard words ("redans and redoubts and lunettes... bastions") with two more hard words ("forts and bastions" — "bastion" is itself jargon). A newcomer ends the sentence knowing these are some kind of fort but with no mental picture, and "Stockade Redan" / "Third Louisiana Redan" then read as opaque proper nouns. This is exactly the "fort vocabulary" failure the brief asked to catch.
**Fix:** collapse to one plain image. Something like "...a nine-mile arc of dug-in earth-and-log forts — chest-deep trenches and angled dirt strongpoints with names like the Stockade Redan." Drop "redoubts/lunettes/bastions" as a list; let the reader picture a dirt fort and move on. The proper names (Stockade Redan, Third Louisiana Redan) are fine to keep once "redan = a dirt strongpoint" lands once.

### M2 — "corps" / "corps commander" — NEEDS-GLOSS (borderline LOST because it carries plot weight)
**§the-siege, line 59.** "Major General John A. McClernand (North), one of Grant's **corps commanders**, overstated a breakthrough..." Also **§the-gamble** structure implicitly. "Corps" is never defined anywhere in the draft. A newcomer does not know a corps is a big chunk of an army (tens of thousands of men) commanded by a general under Grant — and this matters, because the McClernand episode (a corps commander's bad call costs lives and gets him fired) only lands if the reader grasps he commanded a major fraction of the army, not a squad.
**Fix:** at first use, "one of Grant's corps commanders (a corps was one of the army's three big wings, tens of thousands of men)." One clause fixes it.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1 — "battery / batteries" — NEEDS-GLOSS
**§the-key line 9, §the-failed-approaches line 25, §the-gamble line 41+ ("run the batteries"), throughout.** "Battery" is used from the very first section ("the river batteries made a direct naval passage suicidal") and never defined. Context makes clear it means the city's big guns, and "running the batteries" is decently inferable from the April 16 passage ("the Vicksburg guns lit up the night and hammered them"). A newcomer probably guesses "battery = a cluster of cannons" correctly. But it's the load-bearing term of the whole campaign and is asserted before it's earned.
**Fix:** on first use in §the-key, gloss once: "the river batteries (the heavy gun emplacements dug into the bluff)." Then "run the batteries" later is self-explanatory.

### S2 — "garrison" — NEEDS-GLOSS
**§the-failed-approaches → §the-siege onward.** "Garrison" appears repeatedly (the defenders/the troops holding the city) and is never glossed. A newcomer can mostly infer "the soldiers stuck inside Vicksburg" from context, but the word does real work in the casualty/surrender math ("half the garrison was sick"). Low-cost to fix.
**Fix:** first use, "Pemberton's garrison (the troops holding the city)."

### S3 — "division" and "brigade" — NEEDS-GLOSS
**§the-gamble line 47 ("a Confederate brigade at Raymond"; "an entire Confederate division under... Loring... was cut off"), §the-siege.** Like "corps," these unit sizes are never anchored. The reader can tell a division/brigade is "a body of troops" but has no sense of scale (is a division 500 men or 15,000?). The Loring detail — "an entire division cut off and never made it back inside the works" — is meant to feel like a big deal, and the scale is what makes it big.
**Fix:** when "corps" is glossed (M2), one extra clause anchoring the nesting helps, e.g. note a division is several thousand men. Doesn't need repeating each time.

### S4 — "the works" / "earthworks" / "fortifications" — NEEDS-GLOSS
**§the-gamble line 49 ("inside the works"), §the-siege ("Confederate works," "earthworks").** Used interchangeably for Vicksburg's dug-in defensive line. "Earthworks" is semi-self-defining (works made of earth) but "the works" alone is insider shorthand. Tied to M1 — if M1's fix establishes "a nine-mile line of dug-in dirt forts and trenches," then "the works" reads cleanly afterward.
**Fix:** folded into M1.

### S5 — "sap / mine / crater" — actually handled, minor
**§the-siege line 65.** The brief flagged "sap/mine." Good news: the draft never uses "sap," and it *explains* the mine in plain language — "tunneled a mine under the Third Louisiana Redan and blew it up... opening a crater the storming party couldn't hold." A newcomer follows this (dig a tunnel, pack it with explosives, blow a hole). "The storming party" is mildly undefined but inferable (the troops who rush the gap). **CLEAR-ish.** Optional: "the storming party (the troops sent to rush the breach)."

### S6 — "investment" / "enfilade" — NOT PRESENT (brief items cleared)
The brief listed "investment" and "enfilade" as suspects. Neither word appears in the draft. No action. (The *concept* of investment — surrounding/strangling — is conveyed in plain words: "strangle the city," "the lines tightened.") **CLEAR.**

### S7 — "parole" — CLEAR (well handled)
**§the-surrender line 95.** "parole. The garrison would lay down its arms and sign an oath not to fight again until formally exchanged, and then go home." This is glossed perfectly in-line. The one residual: "**until formally exchanged**" introduces the prisoner-exchange concept without explaining it. A newcomer won't know what "exchanged" means here. **NEEDS-GLOSS (minor):** add "(until each side traded them back in a swap of prisoners)" or cut "until formally exchanged."

### S8 — "contraband" — CLEAR (well handled)
**§emancipation line 79.** "'contraband' camps, in the awkward legal language of the time, the word the Union used for enslaved people who reached its lines." Defined in-line and even flags its own awkwardness. No fix.

### S9 — "running the batteries" / "amphibious landing" / "feint" — mostly CLEAR
**§the-gamble.** "feint an attack" (line 47) — "feint" is undefined but the construction "to feint an attack up at Haynes' Bluff... where the Confederates still expected the blow to fall" + "two deceptions" makes the meaning (a fake attack) recoverable. "amphibious landing" (line 45) is glossed by context ("ferried the army across the river... the army stepped onto dry Mississippi soil"). **CLEAR**, but "feint" could get a one-word gloss ("feint — fake — an attack").

### S10 — "adjutant general" — NEEDS-GLOSS (minor)
**§emancipation line 79.** "The Union's adjutant general, Lorenzo Thomas, came down the valley to enlist freedmen." A newcomer has no idea what an adjutant general is (a senior administrative officer). It doesn't block the sentence — the reader gets that Thomas is a Union official who recruited soldiers — so low priority.
**Fix (optional):** "a senior Union officer, Lorenzo Thomas, came down the valley to enlist freedmen" — or leave the title and trust context.

## Geographic / strategic LEAPS (the brief's "campaign covers a lot of ground fast")

### L1 — the geography of the gamble — mostly CLEAR, one soft spot
**§the-gamble lines 41–49.** This is the section most at risk of losing a newcomer, and the author largely defuses it: the plan is stated in plain terms up front ("get *below* it — march down the Louisiana side, past the city, cross back to the eastern bank south of the fortress, come at it from the rear"). The five battles are walked in order with a one-line purpose for each. **Largely CLEAR.**

One soft spot — **the Jackson detour (line 47).** "instead of turning straight for Vicksburg, he wheeled northeast and on May 14 took Jackson... Only then did he turn west toward Vicksburg." The *why* is given (wreck the railroads/relief force; stay planted between Johnston and Pemberton), which is good — but a newcomer has no map and may not register that Jackson is east/inland and Vicksburg is west on the river, so "wheeled northeast then turned west" is spatially fuzzy. **NEEDS-GLOSS (minor):** one orienting clause — "Jackson, the state capital, lay inland to the east" — anchors the zig-zag. (Note line 157 already asks reviewer to confirm no pronunciations needed; the issue here is direction, not pronunciation.)

### L2 — De Soto Point / the hairpin bend — CLEAR
**§the-key line 9 + §the-failed-approaches line 27.** The hairpin-bend geography and De Soto Point ("the low, swampy peninsula on the Louisiana side, inside the hairpin bend opposite the city") are set up carefully and re-anchored. A newcomer can picture it. **CLEAR.**

### L3 — "Trans-Mississippi" — CLEAR (well handled)
**§the-key line 11.** Defined in-line: "west of it lay Texas, Arkansas, and most of Louisiana, the so-called Trans-Mississippi." Re-used cleanly at the end. **CLEAR.**

### L4 — Port Hudson — NEEDS-GLOSS (minor)
**§the-key line 9 ("together with Port Hudson, Louisiana, downstream") → §the-surrender line 101.** Port Hudson is named in §the-key as the *other* Confederate river fort, then drops out for four sections and reappears at the end ("Port Hudson... the last Confederate post downstream, gave up too"). A newcomer who read §the-key carefully will connect them; a casual reader may have forgotten it. The end-of-draft reappearance does re-explain it ("the last Confederate post downstream"), so it self-heals. **CLEAR-to-NEEDS-GLOSS**, low priority.

### L5 — the Gettysburg parallel — CLEAR
**§the-siege line 69 + §the-surrender lines 103/107.** Gettysburg is introduced as "the Confederacy's great gamble in the East, General Robert E. Lee's invasion of Pennsylvania" and the twin-turning-point idea is spelled out plainly. A newcomer with zero knowledge gets enough ("the other half of the turning point"). **CLEAR.**

## Named actors — side-tag / scene-set check (newcomer lens)

Every battlefield actor is side-tagged on first mention and given a one-line who-they-are, which is exactly right for a cold reader:
- Grant — "the Union's hardest-driving western general, the victor of Forts Henry and Donelson" — **CLEAR.**
- Pemberton — "the Confederate commander charged with holding Vicksburg," later "Northern-born, a Pennsylvanian who had cast his lot with the South" — **CLEAR** (the Northern-birth reveal is a nice late touch and well placed).
- Porter — "Rear Admiral... commanding the river fleet of gunboats and ironclads" — **CLEAR.**
- Sherman, McClernand, Johnston, Loring, Bowen, McCulloch — all tagged + role-stated — **CLEAR.**
- Grierson (line 47) — "a cavalry raid under Colonel Benjamin Grierson" — given a role but **NO side tag**. A newcomer infers he's Grant's man from context, so not LOST, but it's an inconsistency the reader of a side-tagged draft might trip on. (The draft's own ledger, line 149, already self-flags this.) **SHOULD-FIX: tag "Grierson (North)."**
- Lee (Meanwhile, line 107) — named without side tag. Context ("the Confederate army," "Lee's invasion") makes the side obvious, so a newcomer is **not** lost. Ledger line 152 self-flags. **SHOULD-FIX (consistency only).**
- Lincoln, Stanton, Lorenzo Thomas, Conkling — administration/political figures, untagged. For a newcomer this is fine — Lincoln is introduced as the president-figure implicitly; Stanton as "The Secretary of War"; Thomas's title (see S10) is the only soft spot. **CLEAR.**

## Overwhelming passages (density / wall-of-info, newcomer lens)

### O1 — §the-siege line 65 (the hunger paragraph) — borderline, CLEAR
The mule/horse/dog/rat/shoe-leather/scurvy/malaria/dysentery + 70,000 vs half-sick + two mines paragraph is the densest in the draft. But it's a *list of concrete vivid images*, not abstract jargon, so a 15-year-old actually reads it eagerly rather than drowning. The one over-stuffed move is welding the **mine/crater** military detail onto the end of the *starvation* paragraph ("The Union engineers even tunneled a mine..."), which mixes two topics. **SHOULD-FIX (optional):** split the mine sentence into its own short beat so the starvation paragraph lands clean.

### O2 — §emancipation line 81 (Milliken's Bend roster) — SHOULD-FIX
"the 9th, 11th, and 13th Louisiana Infantry (African Descent) and the 1st Mississippi Infantry (African Descent)... stiffened by the white 23rd Iowa." Four-plus unit designations in one breath is the only spot where regiment-naming density spikes for a newcomer. The meaning survives (newly-freed Black regiments + one white regiment), but the precise roster is more than a cold reader can hold.
**Fix (optional):** keep "the African Brigade — four newly-recruited regiments of formerly enslaved men, days into uniform, stiffened by the white 23rd Iowa," and let the exact regiment numbers live in a caption/footnote rather than the prose line.

### O3 — everything else — CLEAR
The OUTCOME blocks and Meanwhile sidebars are consistently the *least* dense, most newcomer-friendly parts; they re-state each section's point in plain language and function as built-in safety nets for a lost reader. Strong structural asset — no changes.
