# Mansfield — NEWCOMER / CLARITY critic gate

> War pipeline, step 4 (clarity critic). Cold read as a **zero-knowledge but sharp
> 15-year-old**: no prior Civil War knowledge, but smart, impatient, and quick to
> notice when a term is dropped without a handle. Flags undefined terms,
> unexplained leaps, named actors without a side-tag or scene-set, the dual-name
> hazard, and overwhelming passages. CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST; MUST-FIX vs
> SHOULD-FIX. Never demands invented facts.

**VERDICT: PASS with fixes.** This is an unusually reader-friendly draft for a
newcomer — almost every core term (Trans-Mississippi, gunboat flotilla, division,
column, the dual name, the crescent) is inline-defined on first use, exactly as
the house rules require. The dual-name hazard is handled cleanly. Two MUST-FIX
gloss gaps remain (XIII/XIX Corps numbering used without ever explaining what a
"corps" is; the "ten percent" reconstruction plan named but never explained), plus
a handful of SHOULD-FIX scene-set and density notes.

## § the-river-and-the-cotton

- **"Trans-Mississippi Theater" — CLEAR.** Defined twice and well: "the
  Confederacy's whole command west of the Mississippi River" and "the war west of
  the great river, a sprawling, secondary front." A newcomer gets it.
- **"combined expedition — army and navy moving together" — CLEAR.** Glossed in
  the same breath. Good.
- **"gunboat fleet… a flotilla of armored river warships, floating artillery
  batteries with engines" — CLEAR.** The brief's flagged term ("gunboat
  flotilla") is handled beautifully — a 15-year-old who has never heard the word
  pictures it instantly.
- **"political general (a former governor and Speaker of the U.S. House who got
  his rank from his clout, not from West Point)" — CLEAR.** This also quietly
  teaches what "West Point" signals (earned rank), which pays off later when Banks
  pleads on the road. Good.
- **"Army of the Gulf" / "Department of the Gulf" — CLEAR ENOUGH.** Named as
  Banks's command; the reader doesn't need more.
- **"Lincoln's lenient 'ten percent' reconstruction plan" — NEEDS-GLOSS
  (MUST-FIX).** This is dropped with zero explanation. A newcomer has no idea what
  "reconstruction" is yet (the war isn't even over in the story), what "ten
  percent" refers to, or why standing up a "pro-Union state government" matters.
  It's one of the four aims, so it can't just be cut. **Fix:** one short
  parenthetical — e.g., "(Lincoln's plan to let a Southern state back into the
  Union once one in ten of its voters swore loyalty)". Without it this aim is
  noise to the target reader.
- **"French-occupied Mexico, where Emperor Maximilian had some 25,000 French
  troops and Washington was nervous" — CLEAR ENOUGH.** The "warning to
  French-occupied Mexico" framing gives a newcomer the gist (foreign army next
  door, US worried). Doesn't need the full backstory; the one-clause handle is
  adequate.
- **"100,000 bales of cotton" → grown "by enslaved people" — CLEAR and the
  strongest teaching in the draft.** The slave-economy point is built from the
  ground up with no assumed knowledge. A newcomer fully understands what the
  cotton *is* and why it's the cause. Excellent.
- **"Corps d'Afrique… regiments of Black soldiers… many of them formerly enslaved
  Louisianans" / "U.S. Colored Troops" — CLEAR.** Both defined inline. Good.
- **"prizes of war" — CLEAR ENOUGH.** Context ("Porter's gunboats seized cotton
  as 'prizes of war'… made a great deal of money selling it") makes the meaning
  recoverable.
- **DENSITY — SHOULD-FIX.** ¶25 ("And the greed was not subtle…") throws a
  congressional investigation, a historian's name (Ludwell Johnson), and a book
  title at the reader in quick succession. A sharp 15-year-old won't be *lost*,
  but it's the one spot in the section where the reading load spikes above the
  rest. (The storytelling gate flags this too, as a lecture.) Not a comprehension
  failure; a fatigue point.

## § the-narrow-road

- **The dual name — CLEAR (hazard handled well, MUST-PRESERVE).** "The fight that
  breaks the campaign goes by two names, and you should hold both. The Union called
  it the Battle of Mansfield… The Confederates called it the Battle of Sabine
  Crossroads… (a third name, Pleasant Grove…)." This is exactly how to defuse the
  dual-name hazard for a newcomer — it tells them *why* there are two names ("each
  side wrote its own history") so the alternation later never confuses them. Keep
  this intact; do not let any edit bury it.
- **"DeSoto Parish town" — CLEAR ENOUGH.** A non-Louisiana reader may not know
  "parish" = county, but "the DeSoto Parish town it was fought about three miles
  below" gives enough that it reads as a place name. **SHOULD-FIX (minor):** a
  three-word gloss "(parish = county in Louisiana)" would remove the only residual
  friction; optional.
- **"the son of a U.S. president, Zachary Taylor" — CLEAR.** Self-glossing.
- **"column — the army strung out in marching order" — CLEAR.** The brief's
  flagged term ("a column strung out on the march") is explicitly defined right
  where it's introduced, and then the cork-in-a-bottle / forest-tunnel imagery
  makes the *consequence* vivid. This is the single most important concept for the
  battle and a newcomer gets it completely. Model handling.
- **"cavalry division (a mounted division, here a force of roughly 3,300 to 3,500
  horsemen)" — CLEAR.** "division" is glossed-by-example (a force of ~3,300) on
  first use. Good enough that the bare word "division" later doesn't strand the
  reader.
- **"skirmishers" — NEEDS-GLOSS (SHOULD-FIX).** "Lee's cavalry van (North) shoved
  Confederate skirmishers back" — "skirmishers" is used without a handle, and
  again "van" (= the front/leading element). Both are recoverable from context
  (something at the front gets pushed), but a 15-year-old hits a small bump.
  **Fix:** a couple of words — "shoved back the Confederate skirmishers (the thin
  screen of troops out front)" — or just "shoved back the Confederate advance
  troops." Low cost.
- **"Honeycutt Hill… a low rise" — CLEAR.** Glossed in place.
- **"deploy its strength side by side" / "deploy" — CLEAR ENOUGH.** Surrounding
  imagery ("room to spread out," "on one track through the woods it can only go in
  a long thin file") carries the meaning for a reader who doesn't know the military
  verb.

## § the-rout

- **"crescent — bent like a horseshoe" — CLEAR.** Glossed instantly, and the
  geometry is reinforced ("along the western, northern, and eastern tree lines
  rimming the clearing… woods curling around three sides of them"). A newcomer can
  picture the trap. Good.
- **"held the left… held the right… covered the flanks" — CLEAR ENOUGH.** "left,"
  "right," and "flanks" are oriented by "east of the road" / "west of the road,"
  so the directions are anchored to something concrete. "flanks" isn't formally
  glossed but the later "wrapped around the Union flank… folded in from the end,
  where each man's flank and rear are suddenly open" defines it by use. Acceptable.
- **"Prince de Polignac… a genuine French nobleman, a prince" — CLEAR.**
  Self-glossing and delightful; the "not a typo" framing actively helps the
  reader trust what they're reading.
- **"battlefield promotion" — CLEAR ENOUGH.** "inherited a whole division by
  battlefield promotion in the middle of a firefight" makes it self-explaining.
- **The mechanism of the collapse — CLEAR and excellent.** "A battle line can
  absorb a blow to its front; it cannot absorb being folded in from the end, where
  each man's flank and rear are suddenly open." This explains *why* a flanked line
  breaks, in plain language, for a reader who has never thought about it. Strong
  teaching.
- **"cartridge boxes" / "Confederate crossfire" — CLEAR ENOUGH.** Context carries
  both (men throwing away weapons to run; fire "pouring in from both tree lines").
- **The casualty breakdown — CLEAR.** "the captured-and-missing figure dwarfs the
  killed, which is the fingerprint of a rout rather than a stand-up fight" — the
  draft doesn't just give numbers, it tells the newcomer *how to read* them. Good.

## § what-it-cost

- **"XIX Corps" / "XIII Corps" — NEEDS-GLOSS (MUST-FIX, cross-section).** This is
  the one real comprehension gap. "Corps" appears here ("roughly 5,000 men of the
  XIX Corps") and the Roman numerals XIII/XIX are used as if the reader knows the
  Union army's structure, but **"corps" is never once defined anywhere in the
  draft**, and a zero-knowledge 15-year-old does not know a corps is a big chunk of
  an army (several divisions). The draft carefully glosses "division" (~3,300–5,000
  men) but never tells the reader that a corps is the level *above* a division.
  Result: "the XIX Corps" and "the XIII Corps suffered most" read as opaque code.
  **Fix:** gloss "corps" on first use — e.g., "Emory's fresh division — roughly
  5,000 men of the XIX Corps (a corps being one of the big numbered chunks an army
  is divided into, each holding several divisions)". One sentence fixes every later
  use. (Note for the fact pack: the fact-checker flagged Franklin's exact corps
  title as [VERIFY]; the gloss need not name Franklin, only explain the unit.)
- **"Gen. Edmund Kirby Smith (South)" — CLEAR ENOUGH (scene-set thin,
  SHOULD-FIX).** He appears only as the source of the casualty figure ("the figure
  reported by Gen. Edmund Kirby Smith"). A newcomer doesn't know who he is or why
  his estimate is the authoritative one. It's a minor walk-on, so full
  introduction isn't needed, but a three-word tag — "the Confederacy's
  Trans-Mississippi theater commander" — would tell the reader why *his* number is
  the one that counts. Optional but cheap.
- **"Pleasant Grove" / "Pleasant Hill" — CLEAR (potential collision handled).**
  The draft is careful: "Pleasant Grove" is tied to "the rear-guard stand"
  (already introduced in §2's dual-name note and re-tagged here as Emory's stand),
  and "Pleasant Hill" is explicitly flagged as "its own battle and its own story"
  on April 9. The two similar names could have confused a newcomer; the draft
  distinguishes them clearly. Good discipline.
- **"Brig. Gen. Andrew J. Smith (North) — Sherman's loaned veterans" — CLEAR
  ENOUGH.** The "loaned" framing tells the reader these were borrowed troops; the
  newcomer doesn't need Sherman's full identity here (he's a famous name the reader
  may or may not know, used only as a source-of-troops handle).
- **Side-tags — CLEAR / COMPLIANT.** Spot-checked every named actor: each carries
  (North)/(South) on first mention per section and resets each section, including
  Porter (North, naval). Banks, Taylor, Lee, Mouton, Polignac, Walker, Green,
  Ransom, Emory, A.J. Smith, Kirby Smith all tagged. A newcomer always knows which
  side a person is on at the moment they appear. No untagged side-actors found.

## Cross-section summary

- **Overall reading level — CLEAR.** This draft is genuinely strong for a
  newcomer. The hardest concepts (the Trans-Mississippi theater, the gunboat
  flotilla, the strung-out column, the crescent flanking maneuver, the dual name)
  are all inline-defined on first use and reinforced with concrete imagery. The
  cause (slavery, via the cotton) is built from zero assumed knowledge and is the
  clearest-taught thing in the piece.
- **The two real gaps are both about army *structure*:** "corps" (never defined,
  used with Roman numerals) and the "ten percent" reconstruction plan (named, never
  explained). Both are MUST-FIX because each leaves a sharp 15-year-old genuinely in
  the dark, and both are one-sentence fixes.
- **No LOST passages.** Nothing in the draft is unrecoverable for the target
  reader; the worst spots are NEEDS-GLOSS bumps and one density spike (§1 ¶25).
- **No invented-fact demands.** Every fix above is a gloss, a scene-set tag, or a
  trim of existing material.
