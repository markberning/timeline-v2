## Finding 2 — Butler / "Woman Order" framing
**Rating: FAIR — with one SHOULD-FIX (see below)**

The draft gets the difficult balance mostly right. It presents the occupation as harsh ("martial law, forced loyalty oaths, confiscated weapons"), gives the actual text of the Woman Order accurately ("treated as a woman of the town plying her avocation"), notes the genuine "international uproar," and correctly identifies that the "Beast Butler" nickname came from *white New Orleans* — a key qualifier that flags whose narrative it is. That attribution ("white New Orleans gave Butler the nickname") is the right move and avoids uncritically adopting the Confederate framing.

**SHOULD-FIX (framing gap, not factual error):** The draft frames the Butler occupation entirely through white New Orleans's reaction — the burned cotton, the Mumford flag incident, the Woman Order outrage, the "Beast" nickname. The enslaved population of New Orleans — around 25,000 people — is completely absent from this section. This is a significant framing omission. The fall of the South's largest slave market to Union forces was a world-historical event for those 25,000 people. Butler also formed the first Black Union regiments in Louisiana from free Black New Orleanians and formerly enslaved men who entered Union lines; self-liberating people poured into the city after the occupation began. None of this appears. The draft's own opening promises the reader will see "a great enslaved-population city changing hands" — but the occupation section never cashes that check. The reader gets the Confederate women's fury and Mumford's execution, but not the other story happening in the same city at the same time.

This is a SHOULD-FIX rather than MUST-FIX because the section's *primary* focus is military (the run, the fall of the city, strategic consequences), and the draft does not affirmatively distort the occupation — it just omits. But the omission tilts the occupation subsection toward a white-New-Orleans-as-aggrieved-victim framing by default. One or two sentences giving the enslaved population's stake a concrete presence would correct the imbalance without expanding the section.

**Suggested addition** (after "white New Orleans gave Butler the nickname history kept: 'Beast' Butler"):  
> What white New Orleans mourned as occupation, the city's roughly 25,000 enslaved and free Black residents experienced as something else entirely. Self-liberating people streamed into Union lines almost from the day Farragut arrived. Butler soon formed the first Black Union regiments in Louisiana, drawing on free Black New Orleanians and men who had walked out of slavery the moment the fleet came upriver. The same occupation that outraged the women on the balconies was, for a substantial share of the city's people, the end of the machinery the draft's own opening paragraph named.

The specific sentence about the flag and the italic kicker ("The occupation was harsh, but the harshness was almost beside the point. The real blow had already landed.") is fine as written — "the real blow" refers to the strategic loss, not to minimizing the occupation's harshness, and that reading is clear in context.

## Finding 3 — Farragut / the run as adventure
**Rating: FAIR**

The night-battle sequence is vivid and fast-moving but it does not lose the stakes. The draft opens by naming what New Orleans was (the slave-market hub of the Confederate economy), so the reader carries that context through the battle. The human stakes for the sailors — "Farragut's own flagship... caught fire," "a few dozen Union sailors killed" — are present. The stakes for the city's people are implicit in the strategic framing ("the most valuable piece of real estate on the map," "hand around the Confederacy's throat"). The battle does not read as pure adventure; it reads as a high-risk military operation with real consequences clearly stated. No correction needed here.

## Finding 4 — Confederate romanticization
**Rating: FAIR**

No romanticization found. Confederate figures are presented with accuracy and appropriate framing:
- The Confederate defense is described as disorganized ("half-built ironclads, untrained crews, ships getting in each other's way — came apart"), citing the historian Winters's blunt judgment ("demoralized shambles").
- Lovell's evacuation is described correctly as "the only sane choice," which is factually accurate (he had no ability to resist a fleet in the streets) — not a compliment to Confederate leadership, which had left the city nearly undefended in the first place.
- The burning of New Orleans supplies is presented as "a tantrum," not heroic defiance.
- Mumford is identified as "a gambler" and his execution handled accurately without either making him a martyr or dwelling on the brutality for shock value.
- General Duncan's quote ("The mortar fire was accurate and terrible") humanizes the defenders without glorifying them.

No false-balance "both sides" construction appears. The Confederacy's strategic failures (stripping the city's defenses, the half-built ironclads, the strategic miscalculation) are stated plainly.
