# Second Bull Run — CLARITY / NEWCOMER critic gate (war pipeline step 4)

**Reader stance:** sharp 15-year-old, ZERO Civil War knowledge. Read cold. Flagging
every undefined term, unexplained leap, untagged actor, and any passage that would
lose or overwhelm a first-timer.

**Overall verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS.** The draft is genuinely strong at scene-setting —
it defines slavery, the Confederacy, Shenandoah Valley, "foot cavalry," cuts/fills,
Zouaves, the unfinished railroad, and the strategic stakes inline, and it re-tags
sides per section. A first-timer will follow the *story*. But a real cluster of
military vocabulary ships undefined, and one structural leap (how Longstreet got
"hidden" on the flank) will genuinely confuse a newcomer. No passage is fully LOST,
but several are NEEDS-GLOSS and 6 are MUST-FIX comprehension breakers.

---

## MUST-FIX (comprehension breakers — a first-timer cannot follow without these)

### M1. "corps" — used ~8 times, never defined
First appearance: "**Maj. Gen. Franz Sigel's (North) corps**" (the-railroad-cut),
then "a second Confederate corps," "the corps of Maj. Gen. Fitz John Porter," "his
corps commanders," etc. A 15-year-old does not know a corps is a large body of
troops (tens of thousands), bigger than a division, bigger than a brigade. This word
is load-bearing for the whole "Lee split his army in two wings" architecture. **Gloss
the army hierarchy ONCE, early** — ideally a single parenthetical the first time a
unit-size word appears: army > corps/wing > division > brigade > regiment. Right now
the reader meets "brigade," "division," "corps," "wing," and "regiment" with no sense
of how they nest or how big each is, which quietly breaks the scale of every troop
movement.

### M2. "division" / "brigade" / "regiment" — sizes never anchored
- "**Brig. Gen. John Gibbon's brigade (North)**" (~the-railroad-cut) — no size given.
- "**A.P. Hill's division (South)**" — no size.
- "the **5th New York Infantry — Duryée's Zouaves (North)**… with about 500 men" —
  this one DOES anchor regiment size (~500), which is good, but it comes late (the
  fourth section) and only for "regiment."
The reader needs at least rough numbers attached the first time each unit word
appears (brigade ≈ a few thousand, division ≈ ~ten thousand, corps ≈ tens of
thousands). Fold this into the M1 gloss. **This is the single highest-value fix in
the whole piece** — every "the line bent," "five divisions," "Jackson's wing" depends
on it.

### M3. The "hidden flank" leap — HOW did Pope not see Longstreet's whole corps?
This is the dramatic crux of the battle and the one place the logic genuinely breaks
for a newcomer. The draft repeatedly insists "**Pope did not realize Longstreet was
on the field at all**" and "a second Confederate corps was now coiled on his left,
waiting." A first-timer's honest reaction: *how do you not notice 30,000 men with
guns lining up next to you?* The draft never explains the mechanism — that Civil War
commanders had no aerial view, saw only what scouts and their own eyes on the ground
reported, that Longstreet deployed in woods/behind ridges, and that Pope's fixed
(wrong) belief that the enemy was fleeing made him discount what reports he did get.
Without one or two sentences on *why armies could be invisible to each other on the
same field,* the reader concludes Pope was simply an idiot, and the carefully built
tragedy collapses into farce. **Add a short gloss on Civil War "fog of war" — no maps
from above, vision blocked by terrain, intelligence only as good as your scouts.**

### M4. "piecemeal" attacks — the term AND why it matters
"the problem was that they went in **piecemeal** — one division, then another, then
another, never all at once." The draft does immediately gloss the word ("one
division, then another… never all at once"), which is good — BUT it does not explain
*why that loses.* A newcomer doesn't know that hitting a defended line one unit at a
time lets the defender shift troops to each spot in turn, while hitting all at once
overwhelms. The very next clause ("so that Jackson could shift his men to meet each
blow") actually DOES explain it — so on close read this is borderline CLEAR.
**Downgrade to SHOULD-FIX** *only if* the reader is trusted to connect the two
clauses; flagging here because the cause-effect is the entire point of the section
and is easy to miss. Tighten so "piecemeal = bad because" is unmissable.

### M5. "the line bent. In places it nearly broke." — what is "the line"?
"Line" is used throughout as military jargon for a formation of troops ("a line about
3,000 yards long," "Jackson's railroad line," "the line bent," "his unbroken line").
A 15-year-old may read "line" as the railroad, or a drawn line on a map, not "the row
of soldiers standing shoulder to shoulder." The first use — "roughly 20,000 men
packed into a **line** about 3,000 yards long" — half-anchors it, but "the line bent /
nearly broke / held" then asks the reader to picture a formation of men flexing under
attack. **Anchor "line" = the formation of soldiers** once, explicitly, the first
time it's used as a fighting formation. This recurs in every battle section, so one
fix pays off five times.

### M6. "Maj. Gen." / "Brig. Gen." / "Gen." — ranks never decoded
Every named officer carries an abbreviated rank: Maj. Gen., Brig. Gen., Gen. A
newcomer doesn't know "Maj. Gen." (Major General) outranks "Brig. Gen." (Brigadier
General), or that "Gen." (full General, here Lee) is the top. The ledger even notes
the draft deliberately tracks these ranks. Right now they read as noise. The ranks
also quietly encode the unit hierarchy (a brigadier leads a brigade, etc.), so
decoding them once reinforces M1/M2. **Spell out the rank ladder once** ("Brigadier
General… Major General above him… and full General, Lee's rank, at the top"), or at
minimum expand the first instance of each abbreviation.

## SHOULD-FIX (slows or mildly confuses a first-timer, not a hard breaker)

### S1. "flank" / "flank march" / "in the air" / "exposed flank/left"
"flank" appears constantly: "looping **flank** march around Pope's right," "strike
the exposed **flank**," "left his **flank in the air**," "exposed Union **left**."
The draft helps once — "around Pope's *right* — the far end of his line" — which is a
good inline gloss. But "flank" itself (= the side/end of an army, the vulnerable
spot) is never named as a concept, and "in the air" (= unprotected, not anchored to
any obstacle) is pure jargon a newcomer won't get. **Gloss "flank = the unprotected
side/end of an army, the place you don't want to be hit" once**, and replace or gloss
"in the air."

### S2. "salient" — NOT actually present (good)
Checked: the word "salient" does not appear in the draft. No action. (Listed in the
brief as a term to watch; the author avoided it.)

### S3. "enfilade," "in echelon," "in detail," "battery" — NOT present (good)
None of these jargon terms appear. "in detail" (as in "defeat in detail") is absent;
the related idea is conveyed in plain English via "piecemeal" instead — good choice.
"battery" is absent; artillery is described as "Union guns on Dogan Ridge," which is
correctly plain-language. No action needed; noting for the brief's checklist.

### S4. "theater" / "Eastern Theatre" / "western theater"
"Pope arrived from the **western theater**"; header says "Eastern Theatre." A newcomer
won't know a "theater" is a large geographic zone of the war (East vs. West of the
Appalachians). It's used to explain Pope's outsider status, so it carries weight.
**One-clause gloss**: "the western theater (the war's fighting out toward the
Mississippi, far from Virginia)."

### S5. "wing" vs. "corps" vs. "half the army" — overlapping words for the same thing
The draft calls Jackson's and Longstreet's commands variously "his wing of the army,"
"half of it," "a second Confederate corps," "Lee's other wing." These are roughly
synonyms here, but a newcomer juggling brand-new unit words may not realize "wing,"
"half," and "corps" point at the same body of troops. **Pick a consistent primary
term** (suggest "wing," since "Lee split his army in two" is the mental model) and
introduce "corps" as "(a corps — a wing-sized body of troops)" once.

### S6. "deployed" / "deploy his entire corps"
"Longstreet **deployed** his entire corps on Jackson's right." Minor — "deployed" =
moved into position / spread out for battle. A sharp reader infers it from context.
Light gloss or swap to "spread out / moved into position."

### S7. "court-martialed" / "disobedience" / "dismissed from the Army"
the-road-to-antietam: "Porter was **court-martialed**, found guilty of
**disobedience**, and **dismissed from the Army**." "Court-martialed" (= put on trial
by the military) may not be known to every 15-year-old. The surrounding sentence makes
the gist recoverable (blamed → tried → convicted → kicked out → later cleared), so
this is mild. **One-word gloss on "court-martialed (a military trial)."**

### S8. "rearguard" — used twice, undefined
"an organized **rearguard** kept it from becoming a stampede"; "the Union rearguard
stands on Chinn Ridge." A newcomer may not know a rearguard is the force left behind
to fight off pursuers and cover a retreat. Context ("kept it from becoming a
stampede") mostly rescues it. **Light gloss the first time.**

### S9. "Turnpike" / "Warrenton Turnpike"
"the **Warrenton Turnpike** — the main east-west road." Good — the draft glosses
"turnpike = main road" inline on first use. No fix; noting it as a model of what the
other terms need.

### S10. "the Iron Brigade" name-drop
"Gibbon's brigade — soon to be famous as the **Iron Brigade** — was gutted." A
newcomer has no idea why the Iron Brigade is famous or that this is forward-reference
flavor. Harmless (it reads as "they became legendary"), but it's a name with no
payoff in this article. Fine to keep; flag only as a place the reader briefly wonders
"am I supposed to know this?" Optional: "(a unit that would become one of the
North's most celebrated)."

### S11. Place-name density in the-hammer-falls
One paragraph fires Chinn Ridge, Henry House Hill, Stone Bridge, Bull Run,
Centreville, and the turnpike in quick succession. Each is individually fine and the
maps will help, but the cumulative load is high right at the climax. The Henry House
Hill callback ("the very hill where Stonewall Jackson had earned his nickname in
1861") is a *great* anchor and should stay. **Make sure the [FIG] battle map is
placed BEFORE or beside this paragraph** so the reader has the geography in front of
them — as drafted the map figure sits just above it, which is correct; just confirm
in layout.

### S12. "the killed-and-wounded core" / casualty-counting nuance
the-road-to-antietam opens with a fairly dense statistics passage (1,747 killed,
8,452 wounded, ~4,000 captured/missing; Confederate 7,300–9,500; "totals diverging on
how to count the missing"). The *reason* for the uncertainty (poor Confederate
record-keeping) is implied but not stated, and the numeric density is the heaviest in
the piece. A first-timer's eyes may glaze. **Lead with the human takeaway (~22,000
men) BEFORE the breakdown**, and one clause on *why* the Confederate number is fuzzy
("the Confederacy kept poorer casualty records"). Not a comprehension breaker — the
emotional point (it was staggering) lands — but it's the driest spot in the draft.

### S13. "the initiative"
"Lee now holds eastern Virginia and **the initiative**." Military-strategy sense of
"initiative" (= the power to choose where/when to attack, forcing the enemy to react)
won't be obvious to every newcomer. **One-clause gloss** or rephrase ("Lee now gets
to choose where the war goes next").

## CLEAR (newcomer-ready as written — noted as positives / models)

- **slavery / the Confederacy / "eleven Southern states that had broken away"** —
  defined plainly and early (same-ground-again ¶2). Model gloss.
- **Shenandoah Valley** — "(the long farming valley running down the western side of
  Virginia)." Inline-defined on first use. 
- **Seven Days Battles** — dated and explained ("a brutal week of fighting at the end
  of June… Lee drove McClellan back from the gates of Richmond"). Good.
- **Peninsula Campaign** — explained in plain English without the proper-noun jargon
  ("landed a huge army on the peninsula southeast of Richmond and crept to within
  sight of the capital's church spires"). Good.
- **"foot cavalry"** — glossed inline ("marching so hard and so fast that Jackson's
  foot soldiers had long since earned the nickname"). [Note: ledger flags this as a
  VERIFY/possibly-cut fact, but on CLARITY grounds it's well-handled.]
- **unfinished railroad grade / cuts and fills** — beautifully glossed ("cuts (where
  the grade dug down through high ground) and fills (where it built up across low
  ground) made instant earthworks"). Model passage. CLEAR.
- **Emancipation Proclamation** — defined twice, plainly ("the order that would
  declare enslaved people in the rebelling states free"). 
- **Zouaves** — glossed ("in their bright uniforms," "conspicuous in their colorful
  Zouave uniforms"). Enough for a newcomer.
- **Warrenton Turnpike / turnpike** — glossed inline. Model.
- **First Bull Run callback** — the "same ground" framing is set up clearly in ¶1;
  the reader is told this is the second battle on the same field. CLEAR and effective.
- **Antietam** — dated, located, and characterized ("a Maryland creek called
  Antietam — the bloodiest single day in American history"). CLEAR.
- **Side-tags** — every named actor carries (North)/(South) on first mention per
  section (verified against the ledger's side-tag audit). Lincoln, Lee, McClellan,
  Pope, Jackson, Sigel, Gibbon, A.P. Hill, Longstreet, Hood, McDowell, Reynolds,
  Porter all tagged. Scene-set for the marquee names (Pope's backstory, Lee's
  ascendancy). CLEAR. One nit carried from ledger flag #9: "under Longstreet" appears
  in the-long-march before Longstreet's first formal side-tag in the-railroad-cut —
  on CLARITY grounds, add "(South)" at that first literal "Longstreet" so the reader
  isn't briefly unsure which side he's on.

## Priority for the reconcile pass
1. **M1 + M2 + M6 together = one early army-hierarchy gloss** (army > corps/wing >
   division > brigade > regiment, with rough sizes and the rank ladder). This single
   insertion clears the largest cluster of breakers and reinforces S5.
2. **M3 fog-of-war gloss** — the one true logic gap; without it the central tragedy
   reads as Pope being stupid.
3. **M5 "line" = formation** anchor (one fix, pays off in every battle section).
4. **M4 piecemeal cause-effect** tighten.
5. SHOULD-FIX glosses (flank, theater, initiative, rearguard, court-martial) — cheap,
   one-clause each.
6. S12 casualty passage reorder — lead with the human number.

No passage rated fully LOST. The narrative spine, stakes, and slavery framing are all
newcomer-CLEAR; the gap is concentrated in undefined unit/rank vocabulary and the one
"how was Longstreet invisible" leap.
