# NEWCOMER-CLARITY CRITIC · The Siege of Vincennes draft

*Critic pass on `audits/war-pipeline/rev-vincennes-draft.md`, 2026-06-12. Reader model:
zero prior knowledge — has not read any other Revolution article, cannot place Illinois or
the Wabash on a mental map, does not know who Clark or Hamilton are before this page, does
not know what "habitants" or "bateaux" mean, cannot picture the scale of 170 men, and has
no frame for where the frontier is relative to the 13 colonies they may vaguely know.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is a clear, well-structured draft. The march spine is vivid, the hard surface
(executions) is handled at exactly the right level of precision, and the three-section
arc (setup / march / aftermath) gives a newcomer a clean through-line. The geography IS
introduced — Illinois Country, the posts, the Wabash, Detroit — but it arrives without
a frame the newcomer can hang it on: there is no early anchor to the 13 colonies or to
anything they know, so the first page-turn has them adrift in space. That is the one
BLOCKER. Below it, a cluster of undefined terms and one structurally confusing setup
sequence round out the SHOULD-FIX tier. Nothing here requires a rewrite; all fixes are
insertions of one clause or sentence.

---

## BLOCKER

**B1. No anchor to anything the reader knows — the Illinois Country floats in space.**

The draft defines "Illinois Country" as "the cluster of old French villages along the
Mississippi and the lower Wabash rivers, in the heart of what the eighteenth century
called the West." That is correct, but it gives the newcomer nothing to triangulate from.
They may have a vague sense of the 13 colonies on the Eastern seaboard, and they know
Detroit as a city. There is no sentence that orients the reader: how far west of the
colonies' coastline fighting (Boston, Philadelphia, New York) is this? Is Detroit north or
south of Vincennes? How far is this from anything?

The locator map will help, but prose must also carry it — many readers tap straight into a
section. One orienting sentence early in Section 1, before or just after the Illinois
Country definition, would anchor the whole article:

Draft text (S1 para 1): "The Illinois Country was the cluster of old French villages along
the Mississippi and the lower Wabash rivers, in the heart of what the eighteenth century
called the West..."

- FIX: Insert after "the West" (or as a following sentence): "These towns sat roughly 800
  miles west of Philadelphia and 400 miles west of the Appalachian ridge that most
  Americans still thought of as the edge of the world — far beyond any battle the war had
  yet fought." Then when Detroit enters as Hamilton's base, add "(Detroit lay another 300
  miles to the northeast, at the straits between Lake Erie and Lake Huron)" — this is already
  in the fact pack and directly answers the newcomer's mental question. Without this anchor,
  the opening section's strategic explanation ("Hamilton ran the frontier war from Detroit...
  dispatched Native war parties against the Kentucky and Pennsylvania-Virginia frontiers")
  has no geography to grip.

## SHOULD-FIX

**S1. "Fort Sackville vs. Vincennes the town" — the two-name problem is never explained.**

"Vincennes" is both the town (the French-speaking community) and the site of the fort. The
fort is named "Fort Sackville." The draft uses both names, but a newcomer reading Section 1
will encounter "Fort Sackville" at the December surrender of Capt. Helm and then watch the
article switch back to "Vincennes" interchangeably. The fact that the fort sits inside or
beside the town, and that the fort has a separate name, is never spelled out.

Draft text (S1): "...and Capt. Leonard Helm (American), with only a handful of men,
surrendered Fort Sackville on December 17 with the honors of war."

- FIX: At the first mention of Fort Sackville in Section 1 prose, gloss it: "surrendered
  Fort Sackville (the British-built stockade fort at Vincennes — the town and the fort share
  a location but carry different names throughout the campaign) on December 17 with the
  honors of war." One sentence. Then both names can coexist cleanly.

**S2. "170 men IS the army" — the scale is never made visceral for a newcomer.**

The draft states the force was "about 170 men" at departure. The dossier sides note says
"~170 on the overland march." The problem is that the newcomer has no frame for whether
170 is a large army, a small army, or a patrol. The article never says, in plain terms, that
this was the ENTIRETY of the American military presence in the Illinois Country — not an
advance party, not a detachment. The bluff's genius (making 170 look like several times
that) needs the baseline absurdity established: 170 men is a single company of regulars by
European standards, not a field army.

Draft text (S2 para 1): "On February 5, 1779, after Mass and a blessing from Father
Gibault, about 170 men left Kaskaskia..."

- FIX: Add a clause: "about 170 men — Clark's entire force in the Illinois Country, every
  man he had — left Kaskaskia in the afternoon." The dossier note already says "A force that
  should not have existed in February" — that framing belongs in the prose where the march
  begins, not only in the dossier tab.

**S3. "The fort changes hands twice" setup — the sequence requires a reread to track.**

The section title is "The fort changes hands twice." The first change of hands is July 1778
(to Americans, via Gibault). The second is December 1778 (back to British, via Hamilton).
The march in February 1779 will be the third change. The structure is clear in the section
title, but the prose never numbers or flags the transitions: "first time" / "second time"
is used once ("the second time the town had changed hands in six months, and it would not
be the last"), but the reader who is following casually may not have registered the first
change as "change #1" because the Gibault paragraph does not say "the town changed hands
for the first time" — it says Gibault "brought its habitants to the American oath."

- FIX (S1): At the close of the Gibault paragraph, add a short bridge: "Vincennes had
  changed hands for the first time — and it would change twice more before the war touched
  it again." Then when the December surrender paragraph ends with "the second time the town
  had changed hands in six months," it lands as an echo of an established count, not a new
  piece of information.

**S4. "Habitants" defined in Section 1, but the definition is embedded in parentheses that
could be missed on a fast phone read.**

Draft text (S1): "Their people were the habitants (the French Catholic farmers and traders
who had lived there for generations, under whatever flag flew)."

This is correct and follows the house style. The concern is that "habitants" is a
load-bearing concept — it explains the MEANWHILE about the four oaths, the militia refusing
to fight their kin, and why Gibault could deliver the town without a shot. If a phone reader
skims past the parenthetical gloss, the rest of Section 1 loses its logic. The gloss itself
is excellent; the issue is that it arrives inside a sentence that is itself the second
sentence of a paragraph. Consider pulling it forward to the start of the paragraph or making
the definition slightly more prominent.

- FIX (POLISH-level): Move the habitants gloss to the opening sentence of the paragraph, or
  restructure: "The people of these towns were called the habitants — French Catholic farmers
  and traders who had lived there for generations, under whatever flag flew." Then the
  sentence about "these were 'the posts'" follows. Minor; the current version is readable.

**S5. "Bateaux" not glossed anywhere — it is used in the fact ledger but the plural form
appears in the dossier and is the type of craft Hamilton used on his 600-mile route. The
draft does not use the word in prose, so this is a NON-ISSUE in the sections. Flag for the
reviser: if the integration uses "bateaux" in a caption or locator label, it needs a gloss
"(flat-bottomed river boats)" at first appearance.**

**S6. "Swivel guns" never glossed.**

The word does not appear in the prose sections — not a prose problem. If the locator labels
or dossier stats use it, gloss at first use. NOT a current blocker.

**S7. "Lakes vs. Wabash nations" — the distinction between the war party (Odawa / Lakes
warriors) and the Piankeshaw (Wabash neighbors) is made in Section 2, but the newcomer has
no geographic frame for "Lakes warriors far from home."**

Draft text (S2): "The men who died were Lakes warriors far from home, not from the Wabash
towns next door, and that was part of the calculation."

"Lakes" here means the Great Lakes region, but a newcomer does not know that. The
distinction matters — it is Clark's moral calculation, explicit in the text. Without
knowing that "Lakes" = far north, "Wabash towns next door" = the Piankeshaw who had aligned
with Clark, the sentence's logic ("staged to teach the watching nations a lesson at the
expense of men who were not their own") is opaque.

- FIX: Change "Lakes warriors far from home" to "Odawa warriors from the Great Lakes
  country, far to the north" or "warriors from the Great Lakes nations, far from home."
  The word "Lakes" alone does not do the geographic work for a newcomer.

**S8. "Honors of war" never defined.**

First use in Section 1 prose: "surrendered Fort Sackville on December 17 with the honors
of war." This is military-ceremonial vocabulary. The newcomer does not know it means Helm
was allowed to march out with his weapons and dignity rather than as a captive. The phrase
recurs later implicitly in the discussion of terms.

- FIX: "(surrendered with the honors of war — meaning his men were allowed to march out
  under arms, not disarmed and taken prisoner)" at the first appearance.

**S9. The year-jump from 1778 to 1779 is managed, but one date sentence near the end of
Section 1 requires attention.**

The transition from 1778 to 1779 is the hardest date-clarity moment in the article. The
draft handles it reasonably well but the phrase "that winter" (referring to Illinois County,
Virginia being organized) appears before the Vigo intelligence paragraph, and "that winter"
is ambiguous: the winter of 1778–79, or winter 1778? A newcomer who has lost track of the
year sees "that winter" and "January 29, 1779" arriving close together, and must do calendar
arithmetic.

Draft text (S1): "Virginia, astonishingly, organized the whole region as Illinois County,
Virginia, that winter."

- FIX: Change "that winter" to "that fall and winter" or to "by the end of 1778" to
  distinguish it cleanly from January 1779. The exact season of the organization is LOW
  confidence anyway; "by end of 1778" is safe.

**S10. "The rowing-galley Willing" — "row-galley" is glossed only as "armed row-galley"
without explaining what that means.**

Draft text (S3): "Two days after the surrender the armed row-galley Willing came up the
Wabash with about 40 more men, two days too late to fire a shot."

A newcomer cannot picture a "row-galley." It sounds like a Roman warship. It was an
armed, oar-and-sail river gunboat of the American inland fleet. One clause would fix it.

- FIX: "the armed row-galley Willing (a flat-bottomed river gunboat, the kind used on the
  Ohio and Mississippi) came up the Wabash..."

## NITS (POLISH)

**N1. "Pirogue" is glossed correctly ("a dugout canoe") — PASS. No fix needed.**

**N2. "3- and 6-pounder cannon" is glossed correctly ("named for the weight of ball they
threw") — PASS.**

**N3. "Shot the falls" — meaning the column ran the Ohio River rapids at the Falls of the
Ohio. Slightly idiomatic for a newcomer but clear in context. POLISH: "ran the falls
downriver" or "shot the falls (ran the Ohio rapids)" if the reviser wants belt-and-suspenders.**

**N4. "A solar eclipse his men read as an omen (an eclipse did occur that day)" — PASS.
The parenthetical verification actually helps the newcomer feel grounded rather than
confused.**

**N5. "Illinois County, Virginia" — stated but not explained. A newcomer may not know that
Virginia claimed this territory or what organizing it as a county meant politically. The
"astonishingly" adverb signals surprise but does not explain the mechanism. POLISH: "Virginia
formally claimed the whole region as Illinois County, Virginia — a legal assertion that it
owned this territory, however thinly it could govern it." The draft's MEANWHILE
about the four oaths does explain why this mattered; the two beats are close enough together
that a light polish here bridges them.**

**N6. The "Meanwhile" block about the four oaths is excellent and pays off exactly the
habitants introduction. PASS.**

**N7. "Clark renamed Fort Patrick Henry" — lands with a slight thud because the newcomer
does not know Patrick Henry is the Governor of Virginia who commissioned the campaign.
Clark (American) is established; Patrick Henry is not. One phrase fix: "Fort Patrick Henry,
after the Virginia governor who had authorized the whole campaign."**

**N8. Miles-first check: "~180 miles (290 km)" at first use — PASS. All subsequent uses
drop the metric — PASS per house rules.**

**N9. "Murtherer" in the Clark quote is the period spelling. It is flagged in the fact
ledger. No gloss needed; period spellings in block quotes read as authentic, not typos.**

**N10. Date clarity across the 1778→1779 jumps: the draft tracks dates consistently. The
ONLY soft spot is "that winter" (see S9 above). Otherwise all 1778 events are labeled 1778
and all February 1779 events are labeled February. PASS with S9 fix.**

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES CLEANLY

- **Geography of the Illinois Country relative to the 13 colonies: FAILS — see BLOCKER B1.**
  All other geography (Kaskaskia → Vincennes distance, Detroit as Hamilton's base, the
  Wabash as the spine) is introduced. Only the anchor to known space is missing.

- **Clark introduced with role + context: PASS.** "A 25-year-old Virginia surveyor turned
  Kentucky militia leader" at first use in S1 prose. Role-tag "(American)" at side-tag
  first use.

- **Hamilton introduced with role + context: PASS.** "the British lieutenant governor Henry
  Hamilton (British) ran the frontier war" — role and side both present.

- **The "fort changes hands twice" setup legible: CONDITIONAL PASS.** The count is correct
  but the first change-of-hands is not flagged as "change #1." See S3.

- **February flood explained: PASS.** "It was an unseasonably wet winter, and the prairies
  between them and the Wabash River were not prairies anymore but sheets of rain-flood."
  Clear causal explanation. The floods are described as rain-driven, not snowmelt or spring
  freshet — which is the pack's supported position. PASS.

- **Number scale (170 men IS the army): CONDITIONAL PASS.** The number is stated; the
  scale relative to what a reader thinks an "army" is, is not. See S2.

- **Confusing antecedents: PASS.** The draft is careful with "he/his" — Hamilton and Clark
  are named by surname or role when the referent might be ambiguous. The Section 2 parley
  sequence, where Hamilton, Clark, Helm, and Hay are all active, tracks cleanly.

- **Inline definitions on first use (Illinois Country, habitants, the posts, pirogue, field
  guns): PASS.** All are glossed at or near first use.

- **Side-tags (combatants only, once per section): PASS for the critic surfaces checked.
  The voice-restraint critic should verify whether "Vigo (American ally)" should be tagged
  per the combatants-only rule; noted in the fact ledger as a flag.**

- **The executions: PASS on clarity.** The sequence (ambush → tallied dead and captured →
  partisan + youth released → four taken to the street → tomahawked) is ordered correctly
  and the who/how/why is all present. The newcomer can follow it without rereading.

- **Hair-Buyer propaganda framing: PASS on clarity.** The deferred payoff (introduced S1,
  resolved S3) works; the S3 treatment is balanced and clear. The "both of those things are
  true at once" sentence is the right anchor.

- **The "won the Northwest" real-vs-claimed split: PASS.** Present in the dossier outcome,
  S3 prose, and the closing Downes quote. Never asserted as fact.
