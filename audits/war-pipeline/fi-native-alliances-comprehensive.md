## 2. ESSENTIAL omissions (the mustFix items)

### Q — Tanaghrisson and the start of the war (MISSING) — needs a fact-pack addition first

The draft's thesis is that "the Native nations were acting on their own strategy, not anyone else's," and its closing synthesis even names "Tanaghrisson, the Mingo headman the British called the Half King" in a list of self-directed leaders. But the draft never tells his story — and his story is the most literal proof the theme has. At Jumonville Glen on 28 May 1754, the Mingo (Ohio Iroquois) leader Tanaghrisson, guiding a young George Washington's Virginians, brought the war on: by the standard account he killed the wounded French officer Jumonville with his own hatchet, an act read as his attempt to force the French out of the Ohio for his own people's sake — not Britain's. This is the war's opening incident, and it is a Native leader steering two empires toward war to serve a Native aim.

The draft currently says "The collision in the Ohio is where the war started" (¶ on the Ohio Company) and lets a French-vs-British framing stand. That is the one place in the whole section where Native agency is invisible at exactly the moment the theme most needs it. A theme titled "The Contest for Native Alliances" that narrates the war's beginning without the Native actor who triggered it has a hole at its foundation.

- **Fact-pack status:** The fact pack mentions Tanaghrisson (§3, "the alienated Ohio nations") only as the Logstown 1752 figure who claimed authority over the Delaware. It does **NOT** contain Jumonville Glen, the 28 May 1754 date, or the killing of Jumonville. **This needs a fact-pack addition before it can be written** (born-from-fact-pack rule). The added facts to verify: date/place (28 May 1754, Jumonville Glen, Fayette Co. PA); Tanaghrisson a Seneca-born Mingo "Half King" empowered by the Six Nations over the Ohio nations; the hatchet killing of Jumonville (note the [CONTESTED] detail — accounts vary on the exact act, so hedge); his strategic aim (use the British to push the French out of the Ohio). Sources to anchor: Wikipedia *Battle of Jumonville Glen*, *Tanacharison*; NPS "Tanaghrisson, the Half King."

### R — The Ohio nations' pro-French frontier war, 1755–58 (THIN — coverage hole) — needs a fact-pack addition first

The draft sets up the Ohio Delaware and Shawnee as distrusting Britain over land (¶ Ohio Company), then jumps straight to 1758 — "By 1758 the Ohio Delaware and Shawnee... had reasons to reconsider" — skipping the four years in between. In those years the Ohio nations were not passively aggrieved: after Braddock's defeat they joined France in force and waged a devastating raiding war on the Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia frontier (1755–1758). That is precisely the agency the theme is about — the Ohio nations chose France, fought hard for their own land, and then chose to stop. Without it, Easton reads as nations "reconsidering" out of nowhere, and the single most important Native-allied war effort against the British colonies (TeachingHistory.org: the Ohio Delawares and Shawnees were "France's most important allies" in the first four years) goes untold. The "they always fought for their own land, not for France" line in the Easton section is asserting a turn the draft never showed.

This is underweighting, not total absence (the Ohio nations are present elsewhere), but it leaves the theme's central arc — distrust → active French alliance and frontier war → separate peace at Easton — missing its middle act. I am marking it a mustFix because the Easton beat's payoff depends on it.

- **Fact-pack status:** The fact pack covers the Ohio nations' alienation (§3) and Easton (§8) but has **no beat on the 1755–58 frontier raiding war**. **Needs a fact-pack addition first.** Facts to verify: post-Monongahela, the Ohio Delaware/Shawnee allied with France and raided the PA/MD/VA backcountry 1755–58; the scale of frontier devastation; that this is what Easton ended. Keep it tight — one short paragraph or a few sentences folded into the Easton run-up, not a campaign history. Sources: Wikipedia *French and Indian War*; TeachingHistory.org "Which Native American Tribes Allied Themselves with the French?"

## 4. Deliberately-omitted beats I judged SELECTIVE-OK (NOT findings)

- **Cherokee / Catawba and the Anglo-Cherokee War (1758–61).** This is a real and large Native-alliance story — the Cherokee began as British allies, the Catawba stayed loyal, and the alliance collapsed into its own war after British murders of Cherokee envoys. But it is a southern-theater war-within-the-war, largely after the interior had been decided, and the theme is built around the Ohio/Great Lakes/St. Lawrence interior. Omitting it is a legitimate storytelling-first scope choice. I would NOT block on it. At most, one sentence acknowledging the southern alliances existed would round the picture; the section is honest without it because it never claims to cover every alliance.

- **Acadian expulsion, the Mi'kmaq, the broader Wabanaki maritime war.** Out of scope for the interior thesis; correctly absent.

- **A full tribe roster of the pays d'en haut.** The draft names the load-bearing nations and says "and others." Correct restraint; an exhaustive list would be the encyclopedia failure mode the brief warns against.

## 5. Native perspective and weighting — the special-attention check

This is where the draft is strongest, and I want to be explicit that it passes this bar with room to spare. The Native nations are kept in the subject position throughout: France's alliances are "built in the villages, not signed at a fort gate"; the Iroquois "play their own game"; the Monongahela header is "How Native allies won France its early war"; Easton's nations "were not given peace... They chose it"; and the closing "Allies, not pawns" lands the framing directive cleanly. Gift-giving is correctly explained as obligation, not bribery. The Onontio/middle-ground frame is present and load-bearing. The Mohawk-fights-Mohawk irony, Hendrick's conditional loyalty, and the Fort William Henry betrayal-from-the-allies'-eyes are all rendered from the Native vantage. The human cost (smallpox carried home, land loss as the thing the whole war sat on) is given real weight, not tacked on.

The TWO essential gaps (Q and R) are, notably, **both places where Native agency drops out** — the war's start is told empire-vs-empire, and the Ohio nations' own four-year war is skipped. So the perspective failures and the comprehensiveness failures are the same failures. Fixing Q and R doesn't just add coverage; it closes the only two windows where the theme briefly stops practicing its own thesis.
