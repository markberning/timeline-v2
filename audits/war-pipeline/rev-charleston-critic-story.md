# STORYTELLING CRITIC · Siege of Charleston draft (rev-charleston)

*Critic pass 2026-06-12, against `rev-charleston-draft.md`. Bar = `rev-saratoga-final.md`.
Fact pack = `rev-charleston-factpack.md`. Storytelling lane only; factual accuracy belongs to
the fact critic. No edits made to the draft.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

The architecture is solid: three sections that are genuinely distinct acts (the strategy, the
engineering, the reckoning), and the page has a real throughline — the cased colors at noon on
May 12 rhyming with the cased colors at Yorktown eighteen months later. The locator logic, the
meantimes, the voice register (plain, no em-dashes, no drama-bold) are all correct. The Moultrie
meteor-quote is the sensory peak the page needs, and it is placed well.

What keeps it from a clean PASS: Lincoln is present but not a tragedy. The political-trap section
(the council, the threat, the staying) is the human core of this story, and right now it reads as
documented events in sequence rather than as a man caught between two intolerable options. The
1776 palmetto legend is set up (end of section 1) but never paid off in the moment Fort Moultrie
falls — the irony is told in advance, then the actual fall is handled in one clause in section 2
("barked at ships that did not stop to fight them"), and the connection is lost. The burning-houses
sensory peak arrives correctly but is compressed into two sentences before immediately pivoting to
the petition, leaving it without texture. And section 3's aftermath — the hulks, the proclamation
— is thorough but is organized as a history lecture rather than as the fuse the blurb promises.
The Lincoln-Yorktown close is strong; it earns the page.

## BLOCKER

### B1. Lincoln's trap is documented but not felt — the council scene needs interiority

The political trap is named as "the heart of what happened to Benjamin Lincoln" and the brief
calls for "the council trap as tragedy — a decent soldier politically cornered." The draft handles
the event layer correctly — Gadsden's opposition, Ferguson's threat, McIntosh and Moultrie for
evacuation, Duportail's professional verdict, Lincoln's staying. But the reader is never inside
the bind. The current rendering:

> "Pressed from both sides, with a government threatening to turn on him if he saved his army,
> Lincoln stayed."

That sentence is accurate and entirely inert. It tells us the outcome of the trap without letting
us feel the impossible geometry of it. A decent professional soldier, by whose trade the army is
the irreplaceable thing, is being told that saving the irreplaceable thing will cause the
government he serves to open the gates against him. The pack supplies the irony: the men who
threatened to open the gates were the same men who, five weeks later, petitioned him to surrender.
That irony belongs in section 2, not just in section 3 where it appears as a fairness-read
retrospective. Without it here, Lincoln in section 2 is an obstacle in a political process, not
a person in an impossible position.

The same passage also undersells Duportail: "took one look at the works and pronounced them
untenable and urged evacuation. None of it mattered." Washington's own chief engineer, brought
in specifically to assess the situation, said get the army out — and it did not matter. That
should land as the full stop on the professional case, not slide by in two clauses.

**Fix:** After the Ferguson-threat passage and before "Pressed from both sides," add one sentence
that names the trap's geometry from Lincoln's side — something that shows why neither option is
survivable. The pack's framing ("the men who threatened to open the gates were the same men who
petitioned him to surrender five weeks later") belongs in section 2 as dark irony, not only in
the section 3 fairness read. And give Duportail a full sentence: the professional judgment of
Washington's own engineer, refused, is the moment the professional case closes.

### B2. The 1776 palmetto legend is set up in section 1 but the ironic payoff vanishes in section 2

Section 1 ends by establishing the irony explicitly:

> "In 1780 the British were about to take the city by ignoring the harbor entirely, and the fort
> that had wrecked a fleet would change hands without firing a shot."

This is good setup. It tells us what to watch for. But when the moment arrives in section 2,
it reads:

> "The fort that had wrecked a British fleet in 1776 was simply sailed past in 1780; Moultrie's
> guns barked at ships that did not stop to fight them. The harbor was now British water..."

The connection to the legend established in section 1 is dropped. "Barked at ships that did not
stop to fight them" is technically accurate but it does not land the irony. There is no mention
of Moultrie watching it happen, no sense that the man whose name the fort carried was inside the
lines when his fort fell. The dossier commander bio for Moultrie establishes him as "the hero of
June 28, 1776" and as "one of the generals who wanted the army marched out while it still could
be." That conjunction — the 1776 hero, now watching the harbor his fort once controlled sail
past — is the character beat the page is owed. The image spec for the Sullivan's Island 1776
print even cues this: "Four years later the same harbor fell without a shot." That caption does
the work the prose is not doing.

**Fix:** Expand the Fort Moultrie moment in section 2 by one or two sentences that land the
specific irony: that Moultrie himself, the man for whom the fort was named, was watching from
inside the lines on April 8 as the harbor he had once owned sailed back to the British. No new
fact is needed; the commander bio has the material.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. The burning-houses sensory peak is compressed — the night deserves more texture

The brief calls for "the night of the burning houses as the sensory peak." The draft gives the
Moultrie meteor-quote the right prominence, and that is the correct peak for the bombardment.
But the hot-shot fires — the thing that finally broke the citizens' will — get only this:

> "In the siege's final days the British began firing hot shot, cannonballs heated red in
> furnaces to set wooden buildings alight, and fires started among the houses of the town.
> And that was what finally broke the city's will."

Two clauses. The Moultrie meteor-quote captures the abstract beauty of the bombardment; the
fires are the thing that made it real to the people who lived there, and they pass in a
subordinate clause. The Saratoga final's Fraser burial passage is the bar for how to slow down
at the sensory peak: Frederika's "About three o'clock in the afternoon, in place of the guests
who were to have dined with me, they brought in to me, upon a litter, poor General Fraser" earns
its length because it makes a dinner table into a deathbed. The Charleston burning needs the
same moment of concreteness — what it looked like from inside the city, not the abstract
mechanism (hot shot, wood catches fire).

The pack at §4 notes "fires inside the town," hot shot is "firmly attested in the last days" —
and the Moultrie account is the source. His Memoirs cover the bombardment in detail. The section
already quotes Moultrie for the meteor passage; a second short Moultrie thread about the fires
would serve the sensory peak without inventing anything.

**Fix:** After the meteor-quote and before the transition to "And that was what finally broke
the city's will," add two or three sentences grounding the fires: what the night looked like
from the lines, and that by morning the city was asking Lincoln for terms. The burning and the
petition need to feel continuous, not sequential. The current draft has them as two separate
facts; they are the same event.

### S2. Section 3's aftermath reads as a history lecture — the hulks and proclamation need sequence and cause

The blurb for section 3 promises: "prison hulks in the harbor, and a single proclamation that
abolished neutrality and lit the partisan war." The draft delivers both, but the organization is
expository: surrender mechanics → numbers gloss → magazine explosion → prisoners' fate (hulks)
→ proclamation → Clinton sails → Waxhaws handoff → Lincoln fairness read. The hulks and
the proclamation are separated by a long paragraph and arrive as parallel facts rather than as
a single fuse, which is what the blurb and the pack both frame them as.

The proclamation is the sharper of the two because it has an action inside it: Clinton
deliberately abolished neutrality, and thousands of paroled men "did the arithmetic and concluded
that if they had to fight for somebody, it would not be for the king." That sentence is in the
draft and it is good. But it arrives eight paragraphs into section 3, after the reader has
already processed the surrender mechanics, the casualty-number gloss, the magazine explosion,
and the hulks sequence. By the time the proclamation lands, the "fuse" framing feels like a
retrospective verdict rather than an ignition.

The Saratoga bar handles this structurally: the Convention Army is told plainly ("a genuinely
shabby episode on the winning side"), the Congress-broke-the-deal narrative is compact and
specific, and then the page moves to Arnold and France. Charleston needs the same discipline:
the hulks belong as one beat, the proclamation as the next, and together they should read as
two strokes of the same match — the city taken, and then the city's capture turned against itself
within weeks.

**Fix:** Tighten the transition from hulks to proclamation so the two read as cause-and-sequence
rather than adjacent paragraphs in a list. One transition sentence linking "this is what happened
to the men Clinton took" → "and this is what Clinton then did to the men he had let go home" would
knit the two beats and restore the "fuse" framing the blurb promises.

### S3. The McPherson braid is absent — the proclamation's consequence is summarized, not shown

The brief calls for "McPherson braid" — the simultaneous strands woven through the sections.
The Saratoga final uses four braided threads (Howe in Philadelphia, the militia surge, the
French watching, Clinton's raid) to make the reader feel the world the battle is happening inside.
Charleston has two MEANWHILEs and one PILL link for the partisan context. That is thin for a
battle whose direct consequence is the southern partisan war. The proclamation paragraph says:

> "Thousands of paroled men who had been perfectly content to sit out the war did the arithmetic
> and concluded that if they had to fight for somebody, it would not be for the king. The
> proclamation is standardly credited with helping ignite the partisan war, the war of the
> backcountry bands under leaders like Sumter and Marion and Pickens, that ate the British
> conquest from the inside over the next year."

That clause — "standardly credited with helping ignite" — is the historian's hedge keeping the
reader at arm's length. Sumter, Marion, and Pickens are named but they are a list. The Saratoga
final's equivalent beat (the France consequence) lands because Austin riding to Paris and Franklin's
doorstep exchange put a human face on the strategic consequence. Charleston's equivalent is the
proclamation itself putting a sword in the hands of every man it tried to disarm — a perfect
dramatic irony — but the draft handles it with summary attribution ("standardly credited").

**Fix:** The proclamation beat already has the right close ("Clinton had disarmed the rebellion
and then, with one order, re-armed it") — that sentence is earning its keep. The problem is the
"standardly credited" hedge in the middle. Replace it with the plain causal claim (the pack at
§6 uses "standardly credited with helping ignite" as well, but it also says "helped ignite" is
the safe phrasing): drop the hedge and just say the proclamation lit the partisan war. The
paragraph ending is the strength; don't undercut it with a mealy attribution in the middle.

## TRIM

### T1. The captured-number gloss in section 3 is too long — split or compress

The draft correctly handles the 5,466 vs. 2,650 counting dispute, per the pack's HANDLE-WITH-CARE
instruction. But the full treatment runs four sentences inside section 3's prose before the
magazine-explosion paragraph, which means the surrender climax (cased colors, the humiliation,
May 12 morning) is immediately followed by an explanatory footnote before anything else can land.
The dossier casualties block already carries this caveat in the footnote. The prose version in
section 3 does not need to repeat all four sentences; it needs one sentence ("That number needs
care, because it is easy to get wrong") plus the honest framing statement, with the full
arithmetic moved to the fact-ledger footnote or compressed to a single parenthetical.

### T2. The Duportail clause in section 2 is doing double duty, awkwardly

> "Even Louis Duportail (American), Washington's own chief engineer, who had slipped into the
> city in late April, took one look at the works and pronounced them untenable and urged
> evacuation."

This is a clause inside a long sentence that already contains Gadsden's opposition, the council
composition, and the Privy Council members. Duportail's professional verdict is load-bearing
for the story (see B1 above), but it is currently buried in an aside. The fix in B1 gives it its
own sentence; T2 is resolved by that fix.

## NIT

### N1. "None of it mattered" — the phrase does too little work for what it covers

> "Louis Duportail... took one look at the works and pronounced them untenable and urged
> evacuation. None of it mattered."

"None of it mattered" is the draft's summary of the professional case being refused — a whole
stack of military judgment dismissed. The phrase is too casual for what it is dismissing. The
Saratoga final's equivalent moment ("Gates's doctrine was to sit on chosen ground and let the
enemy bleed; the ground did most of the choosing for Burgoyne") works because it shows the
doctrine in action. "None of it mattered" shows nothing. The B1 fix addresses the structure
here; as a nit, even without a structural fix, this phrase should be replaced with something that
names what was refused.

### N2. "The precise humiliation Yorktown would answer" — in the blurb, not the prose

The section 3 blurb says: "An army marched out with its colors cased and forbidden to play a
British march, the precise humiliation Yorktown would answer." That framing is correct. But the
section 3 prose opens by describing the cased colors and "the formal denial of the courtesies
owed a respected foe" without the Yorktown connection, which does not arrive until the end of
the section (the Washington quote). The Saratoga final handles its throughline by putting the
key structural frame in the prose, not just in the blurb. Consider moving the "the precise
humiliation Yorktown would answer" phrase into the prose of the surrender moment itself, as a
planted anticipation, rather than front-loading it in the blurb and deferring it to the coda.
This is a nit because the coda lands regardless; it would just land harder with the plant.

### N3. "A naval disaster nested inside a military one nested inside a political one" — three nestings is one too many

> "It was the largest surrender of American troops in the whole war, a naval disaster nested
> inside a military one nested inside a political one."

The triple-nesting construction is clever but it collapses: "nested inside a military one nested
inside a political one" reads as a list that has been formatted as Russian dolls. The second
nesting ("nested inside a political one") is the claim that matters most and it arrives last,
as the third item. Either reverse the order (put the political trap first, as the thing that
contains everything else) or drop one level (the naval disaster is real, but it is a detail;
the military-inside-political is the story's structure and should stand on its own).

### N4. "The most precise piece of bookkeeping in the entire war" — good line, earned too early

> "Colors cased. The march denied. The most precise piece of bookkeeping in the entire war,
> and the man who gave up Charleston was standing there to collect it."

This is the correct closing image and the language is earned. One small nit: "piece of
bookkeeping" is slightly jarring against "colors cased / the march denied" which are both
short, declarative, concrete. Consider "The most precise ledger entry in the entire war" or
simply "The most deliberate exchange in the entire war" — something that stays in the same
register as the two short sentences before it. This is a matter of texture, not structure; the
close works as-is.

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES (for the record)

- **Voice ship-blockers: clean.** No em-dashes in original prose; no meta-narrator ("as we'll
  see," "remarkably"); no drama-bold; no "CATASTROPHE!" framing. The informal register is
  sustained throughout all three sections.
- **Apocrypha blacklist: untouched.** "Tarleton's quarter" / "Bloody Ban" firewalled to the
  Waxhaws handoff and properly set up but not told. Clean "5,466 soldiers" never appears; the
  framing is consistently "more than 5,000... about half of them regulars." The Adams "severest
  blow" line is dropped. The Clinton boast is dropped. The "Long live Congress" sword anecdote
  is omitted. The May 15 explosion toll is ranged. The Sgt. Jasper flag story is correctly
  omitted from the 1780 context.
- **The three-section architecture works.** Each section is a distinct act: the strategy (why
  the city was the prize and how Clinton got there), the engineering (the siege and the political
  trap), the reckoning (surrender, aftermath, coda). The blurbs for each section accurately
  describe what the section delivers.
- **The noose pacing in section 2 is effective.** The doors close in order: April 8 harbor,
  April 14 Monck's Corner, April 21-23 Cornwallis seals the east bank, May 6 Lenud's Ferry,
  May 7 Fort Moultrie, May 8-9 ultimatum and breakdown, May 9-10 night bombardment, May 11
  petition. Each closure is felt, not just listed.
- **The MEANWHILEs are doing real work.** The Savannah MEANWHILE in section 1 correctly places
  the bet's evidence. The Cooper River horses MEANWHILE in section 2 explains the cavalry war's
  whole logic in one beat. The Gadsden MEANWHILE in section 3 closes the political trap's human
  arc with the right detail (the man who threatened to hold the city spent ten months in a dungeon
  for refusing to capitulate a second time).
- **Disputed evidence handled correctly.** Turkish march attributed to Moultrie's account. The
  Ferguson threat rendered as reported speech (via McIntosh's journal), not verbatim quotation.
  The magazine explosion toll ranged. The Duportail arrival date hedged.
- **The Lincoln-Yorktown coda earns its close.** The Washington quote is byte-exact and
  correctly framed. "Colors cased. The march denied." as the structural echo is the right
  landing. The coda justifies the tragedy of the section without overreaching.
- **Gadsden's arc is the page's best character work.** The man who was the loudest voice for
  holding the city, then led the petition to surrender it, then spent ten months in a dungeon
  refusing a second surrender — that arc is earned and paid off correctly.
