> War content pipeline, step 4 (parallel critic). Cold read as a zero-knowledge

> named actor side-tagged and scene-set, no unexplained leaps, no wall-of-detail

> **VERDICT: PASS (1 MUST-FIX, 5 SHOULD-FIX).** The draft is unusually disciplined

> ironclad ram, "running" a fort, and lashing-in-pairs are all defined inline on

## The one real blocker

**MUST-FIX — "shell-whip" is never defined, and it's the term the emotional climax
rides on.** Both Medal of Honor stories — the payoff the whole article is built toward
— happen "on Hartford's shell-whip." The draft says the men were "the crew that hauled
ammunition up to the guns," which tells the reader what the *people* did but leaves the
*thing* ("returned to the shell-whip on the berth deck," "kept the ammunition moving")
as an undefined object. A newcomer pictures... a whip? A rope? **A shell-whip is the
tackle/hoist rig used to haul heavy shells up from the magazine to the gun deck.** One
clause fixes it: "the shell-whip — the rope-and-tackle hoist that lifted heavy shells
up from the magazine to the guns." Because this is the climax, the reader must not be
fuzzy here. This is the single comprehension defect that rises to MUST-FIX.

## Side-tags and scene-setting (named actors)

**Strong overall.** Every named person carries (North)/(South) on first mention per
section, per the rule, and most are scene-set with role: Farragut "commander of the
West Gulf Blockading Squadron," Granger "had landed troops to besiege the forts,"
Buchanan "the senior Confederate naval officer afloat," Johnston "Tennessee's flag
captain." Good.

**SHOULD-FIX — Percival Drayton arrives slightly under-set.** In §3 "His flag captain,
Captain Percival Drayton (North)" is the first the reader hears of him — "flag captain"
has not been defined anywhere, and the role (the officer who actually commands the
flagship so the admiral can command the fleet) is the reason the rigging anecdote makes
sense. A four-word gloss ("the officer who ran the flagship for him") would land it.

**SHOULD-FIX — "Landsman" rating unexplained.** Lawson and Brown are introduced by their
naval rating ("Landsman") with no gloss. A newcomer reads it as a surname or a vague
title. One clause — "a landsman being the Navy's entry rating for a man new to the sea"
— both clarifies and quietly *adds* to the cause story (these were the lowest-rated
men, doing the deadliest work). Worth it.

**CLEAR — John Collins.** Introduced as "the ship's pilot, John Collins" with the role
attached; the survivor-account framing is handled clearly. Good.

## Unexplained leaps / directional load

**SHOULD-FIX — the left/right channel geometry asks the reader to hold a map in their
head.** §2 and §3 lean hard on starboard/port and "the right hand as you steam in" /
"east of the buoys" / "west of the buoy line." Individually each is glossed (starboard
= right, port = left, and the draft helpfully repeats "the Fort Morgan side"). But §3's
crux — Craven steering "west of the buoy line — into the mined water" — requires the
reader to remember that the safe lane was the *narrow strip east of the buoys, hard up
under the fort*, established a paragraph earlier. This is the one place where a reader
who blinked could lose the *why* of the disaster (why was west fatal?). It IS explained
("The safe lane ran between that buoy line and the fort… stay east of the buoys") — so
this is comprehensible, not LOST — but it's the densest spatial reasoning in the piece
and would benefit from one more plain restatement at the fatal moment: "he crossed to
the wrong side — the mined side." A map (the pipeline calls for several) will carry most
of this load; flag for the map gate that the buoy-line / safe-lane / fort geometry is
the single most important thing the maps must make legible.

**SHOULD-FIX — the six-ship roster in §2 is a momentary fog.** "Hartford, then Richmond,
Lackawanna, Monongahela, Ossipee, Oneida — each with its small partner roped to its
flank." A newcomer cannot hold six ship names and will not need to; none but Hartford,
Brooklyn, and Metacomet recur. This isn't a *comprehension* failure (the sentence's
point — "a long line of paired ships" — survives skimming the names) but it's a wall of
proper nouns that briefly reads as homework. Naming a representative few would keep it
clear. (Storytelling gate flags the same passage.)

**CLEAR — the port-vs-city distinction.** Heavily (arguably over-) explained in §1 and
§5, so a newcomer cannot possibly think Mobile-the-city fell in 1864. From a clarity
standpoint this is a success; from a pacing standpoint the storytelling gate dings the
repetition. For THIS gate: CLEAR.

**CLEAR — why the mines didn't blow.** "long submerged, fouled, defective" answers the
obvious newcomer question ("wait, why didn't Hartford blow up too?") right where it
arises. Good anticipation of the reader's confusion.

**CLEAR — the casualty paradox.** The §3 MEANWHILE ("most of the dead, in one ship")
explicitly walks the reader through the counter-intuitive math (one mine = most of the
Union dead; the big Confederate number is *prisoners*, not battle dead). A newcomer
could easily have been confused by "150 killed" vs "1,500 captured"; the draft heads it
off cleanly. Strong.

## Overwhelming passages

No passage rises to LOST. The two crowded spots are the §2 ship roster (above) and the
§5 stretch where blockade-as-instrument-of-emancipation, the 18,000/20%/1-in-4
statistics, and two Medal of Honor stories arrive in quick succession — but that
section is *structured* (general → statistic → two named human anchors), so the density
reads as build, not blur. The numbers are each given a plain frame ("about a fifth of
its enlisted strength," "roughly one in four"), which keeps them legible. CLEAR.
