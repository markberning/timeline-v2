## 1. Identity / dates / location / theatre

| Field | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Siege of Port Hudson | [WIKI] |
| Dates | **May 22 – July 9, 1863** (48 days) — the investment/siege proper. AB Trust lists "May 21 – Jul 9, 1863"; the May 27 assault and June 14 assault fall inside it. | [WIKI], [ABT] |
| Location | **Port Hudson, Louisiana**, on the **east bank of the Mississippi River** — East Baton Rouge Parish / East Feliciana Parish. ~**15–25 miles upriver (north) of Baton Rouge**. | [WIKI], [NPS-TWHP], [ABT] |
| Theatre | **Trans-Mississippi / Lower Mississippi** — the final engagement in the Union campaign to open the entire Mississippi River. Companion operation to Grant's siege of **Vicksburg** (upstream). | [NPS-CWSAC], [WIKI] |
| Result | **Union victory** — Confederate garrison surrendered July 9, 1863. | [WIKI], [ABT], [NPS-CWSAC] |
| Distinction | The **longest true siege in American military history** (48 days of continuous investment) — up to that point and frequently cited as such overall. | [WIKI], [NPS-CWSAC] |

**Strategic frame:** By autumn 1862 the Confederacy held only one ~150-mile stretch of
the Mississippi — between **Vicksburg (upstream/north)** and **Port Hudson
(downstream/south)**. The **Red River empties into the Mississippi roughly halfway
between the two**, and was the main artery feeding Confederate supplies (cattle, food,
Trans-Mississippi resources) from west of the river to the east. Holding both forts kept
that supply line open and split the Union's control of the river. Take both and the
Confederacy is cut in two and the river is Union from source to the Gulf. [64P], [WIKI]

## 2. Geography — for map accuracy

- **The bluff + the bend.** Port Hudson sat on an **80-foot bluff** on the river's east
  bank, above a sharp **hairpin / ~150-degree bend** in the Mississippi. The bend forced
  steamboats to slow nearly to a stop directly under the guns — which is exactly what made
  the position command the river. [WIKI], [NPS-TWHP]
- **River batteries.** Confederate **heavy guns lined the bluffs** facing the river
  (Wikipedia/64P: ~**13 heavy guns** mounted along the bluffs). These are the batteries
  Farragut's fleet had to run. [64P], [WIKI]
- **The landward earthworks ring.** On the land side, Gardner's garrison built an
  **arc of earthworks, redoubts, and trenches** — sources give the line as **~3 to ~4.5
  miles long**, a semicircle anchored on the river at both ends, enclosing the town and
  bluff. Named strongpoints inside the ring: **Fort Desperate**, the **Priest Cap**, and
  the **Citadel**. [WIKI], [NPS-TWHP], [64P]
- **The killing ground.** The approaches were **deep, thickly forested ravines (up to
  ~60 feet deep), swamps, and cane brakes** — a natural maze that broke up every Union
  assault and turned the open ground before the works into a crossfire-swept kill zone.
  [WIKI], [NPS-TWHP]
- **Union siege lines.** Banks's army **invested (surrounded) the landward arc**, digging
  approach trenches (saps) and three mines toward Fort Desperate, the Priest Cap, and the
  Citadel under Chief Engineer Capt. John C. Palfrey. The Union line was the outer
  semicircle facing the Confederate inner semicircle. [WIKI]
- **Relative positions for the map (north up, east right):**
  - **Mississippi River** runs N→S along the west edge; Port Hudson's bluff on the **east
    bank** at the bend.
  - **Vicksburg** — far **upstream / north** (~150 river-miles).
  - **Red River mouth** — **between** Port Hudson and Vicksburg, ~halfway.
  - **Baton Rouge** — **downstream / south**, ~15–25 miles; the Union staging base.
  - **Bayou Sara** — just upstream of Port Hudson; one of Banks's two approach axes (the
    other from Baton Rouge to the south) — a pincer. [WIKI], [NPS-TWHP]

## 4. Casualties — both sides (scholarly ranges, contested)

> **NOTE the spread.** Sources diverge sharply. Use the ranges; do NOT present a single
> hard total as gospel. The fact-checker must flag which figure the author lands on.

**North (Union):**
- **Combat (battle) casualties:** Wikipedia infobox — **707 killed, 3,336 wounded, 319
  missing** (≈ **~4,360 combat**); often summarized as **~5,000 killed and wounded**. [WIKI], [ABT]
- **Disease/heat:** an **additional ~4,000–5,000** felled by disease, sunstroke, and the
  Louisiana summer (Wikipedia: "~5,000 died of disease"; NPS-CWSAC: "~4,000 fell prey to
  disease or sunstroke"). [WIKI], [NPS-CWSAC]
- **NPS-CWSAC total (highest figure):** estimates total casualties **~17,500 (US 10,000;
  CS 7,500)** — this larger figure **folds in the surrendered garrison and the campaign's
  disease toll**, which is why it dwarfs the battle counts. **[VERIFY]** how to reconcile
  the ~5,000 battle figure with the 10,000 US figure (disease + campaign-wide accounting).
  [NPS-CWSAC]
- **AB Trust total estimate:** **12,208** total (Union 5,000; Confederate 7,208). [ABT]

**South (Confederate):**
- **Combat casualties:** light by comparison — **~750 killed and wounded** (the works did
  the work). Specific assault tolls: **May 27 ≈ 235 CS**; **June 14 ≈ 47 CS**. [WIKI], [ABT]
- **Disease:** **~250 died of disease**. [WIKI]
- **Surrendered:** **~6,340 surrendered / ~5,935 paroled**; **405 officers were not
  paroled** and sent as prisoners to Memphis and New Orleans. [WIKI]

**The assaults' lopsided toll (the centerpiece of the "futility" story):**
- **May 27 assault:** Union ≈ **293 killed, 1,545 wounded, 157 missing** (≈ **~1,995 Union**)
  vs **~235 Confederate**. [WIKI]
- **June 14 assault:** **~1,792 Union casualties** vs **~47 Confederate**. [WIKI], [ABT]

## 5. Sequence of events

1. **Farragut runs the batteries — night of March 14, 1863** (two months *before* the land
   siege). Rear Adm. **Farragut (North)** tried to run **seven vessels** past the bluff
   batteries to cut the Confederate river/supply line above Port Hudson. Sloops *Hartford*,
   *Richmond*, *Monongahela*; side-wheel frigate *Mississippi*; gunboats *Albatross*,
   *Genesee*, *Kineo*, *Sachem* (smaller boats lashed to the sloops' port sides). In a
   ~3-hour night duel the fleet was savaged: **USS *Mississippi* ran aground and was burned**;
   *Richmond* and others drifted back downriver disabled. **Only the *Hartford* (with
   *Albatross* lashed alongside) got through** and continued upstream toward the Red River.
   Union losses that night: ~78 killed/missing, ~35 wounded; Confederate losses minimal.
   The run proved the bluff guns could not be simply steamed past — Port Hudson would have
   to be **taken from the land.** [FAR], [WIKI]

2. **Banks invests Port Hudson — May 22, 1863.** Maj. Gen. **Banks (North)** marched the
   **Army of the Gulf** up from **Baton Rouge** and in from **Bayou Sara** (a pincer) and
   **surrounded the landward earthworks** by May 22, sealing **Gardner's (South)** garrison
   inside. Banks, holding a ~4-to-1 edge, expected a quick assault to carry it. [WIKI], [NPS-TWHP]

3. **First frontal assault — May 27, 1863 (FAILS).** Banks ordered coordinated attacks
   (Weitzel, Grover, Augur, Sherman). They went in **piecemeal and uncoordinated**, were
   funneled into the forested ravines, and were cut down by crossfire before reaching the
   parapets. **~1,995 Union casualties; ~235 Confederate.** **This is the day the Louisiana
   Native Guard charged the bluffs (see §6).** [WIKI], [ABT]

4. **Second frontal assault — June 14, 1863 (FAILS again).** After a bombardment opened
   June 13 (~11:15 a.m.), Banks demanded surrender; **Gardner refused** (quote, §7). The
   pre-dawn (~3:30 a.m.) June 14 assault on the Priest Cap / Citadel was another bloody
   repulse: **~1,792 Union casualties to ~47 Confederate.** Brig. Gen. **Halbert Paine
   (North)** lost a leg. [WIKI], [ABT]

5. **The long siege — June 15 to July 9 (starvation + disease).** Banks gave up on frontal
   assaults and settled into a **textbook siege**: approach trenches (saps), three mines
   dug toward Fort Desperate, the Priest Cap, and the Citadel, constant bombardment, and
   the Louisiana summer doing the rest. Inside, the garrison **starved** — ate the beef,
   then the **mules**, then **dogs and rats**, and brewed beer from sugar and molasses;
   **scurvy, dysentery, and malaria** spread; men deserted. NPS framed it "Forty Days and
   Nights in the Wilderness of Death." [WIKI], [NPS-TWHP]

6. **Surrender — July 9, 1863 (FIVE DAYS AFTER VICKSBURG).** **Vicksburg fell to Grant on
   July 4, 1863.** Once **Gardner (South)** confirmed Vicksburg was gone, Port Hudson had
   **no strategic point left to defend** — it existed only to help hold the river that
   Vicksburg's fall had already lost. Recognizing his position as "hopeless," **Gardner
   surrendered on July 9, 1863.** Capt. Thornton A. Jenkins / Capt. (Union) accepted the
   surrender; **~5,935 paroled, 405 officers sent off as prisoners.** **With Port Hudson,
   the Union held the ENTIRE Mississippi River** — Confederacy split in two, the river open
   from its source to New Orleans/the Gulf. [WIKI], [ABT], [NPS-CWSAC]

## 6. THE CAUSE — SLAVERY (ship-blocking centerpiece)

> **NAME-THE-CAUSE rule (locked 2026-05-30):** the section MUST, in its own prose, name
> **slavery** as what the war — and each side — was ultimately fought over. Port Hudson is
> not merely *adjacent* to this theme; it is **one of the war's first proofs that the
> formerly enslaved would fight, and die, for their own freedom.** The cause is the story.

### The Louisiana Native Guard — one of the first major combat actions by Black troops

- On **May 27, 1863**, the **1st and 3rd Louisiana Native Guard** regiments — **United
  States Colored Troops** (USCT), raised in occupied New Orleans, **many of them formerly
  enslaved men** — were committed to the assault on the Confederate right under orders from
  Brig. Gen. **William Dwight (North)**. [WIKI], [ABT]
- This was **among the FIRST major combat actions by Black soldiers in the Civil War** —
  NPS-TWHP calls them "**the first black soldiers committed to combat in the Civil War**."
  (Milliken's Bend followed ~two weeks later, June 7, 1863; the famous 54th Massachusetts /
  Fort Wagner assault came in July 1863. Port Hudson came first.) **[VERIFY]** the exact
  "first" wording — frame as "one of the first major combat actions," which all sources
  support, rather than an unqualified absolute. [NPS-TWHP], [WIKI]
- They **charged across open ground and a pontoon-bridge approach (along Telegraph Road)
  toward the bluff** into "**heavy crossfire from rifles, field artillery, and heavy coast
  guns**," reformed and went forward repeatedly, and **took heavy losses** before being
  forced to retreat to avoid annihilation. [WIKI]
- **Why it mattered:** their courage under fire "**did much to dissipate the belief that
  black troops were unreliable under fire**." Port Hudson (with Milliken's Bend) is a
  turning point in **Northern opinion on Black soldiers** and a spur to USCT recruitment —
  **by the end of the war nearly 200,000 Black men had served in the Union forces.** [WIKI]

### Captain André Cailloux (North) — the martyr-hero

## 8. Human cost (make it felt — pipeline mandate)

- **The assaults were slaughter-for-nothing:** ~1,995 Union casualties May 27 and ~1,792
  June 14 — roughly **3,800 men down in two doomed charges** — against a few hundred
  defenders behind earthworks. The works were essentially untaken by assault; the river and
  starvation, not the bayonet, decided it. [WIKI], [ABT]
- **Disease and heat killed comparably to combat:** ~4,000–5,000 Union men felled by
  dysentery, malaria, sunstroke in the Louisiana summer. [WIKI], [NPS-CWSAC]
- **Inside the works:** a garrison eating mules, dogs, and rats; scurvy and dysentery;
  desertion — men holding a position whose entire purpose had already been erased by
  Vicksburg's fall before they knew it. [NPS-TWHP], [WIKI]
- **Cailloux's body on the field for 47 days** — the racial indignity that the Native Guard
  could not even recover their own dead — is the human image that ties the cause to the cost.
  [WIKI-CAIL], [ABT-CAIL]

## 9. Significance

1. **Completed Union control of the entire Mississippi River.** Vicksburg fell July 4;
   Port Hudson surrendered July 9, 1863. With both gone, **the Mississippi was Union from
   its source to the Gulf** — "the Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea" (Lincoln).
   [WIKI], [NPS-CWSAC] **[VERIFY]** if the Lincoln quote is used (it is about the river's
   opening generally, not Port Hudson specifically).
2. **Split the Confederacy in two.** The Trans-Mississippi (Texas, Arkansas, western
   Louisiana) was severed from the eastern Confederacy — its men, cattle, and supplies could
   no longer cross. [64P], [WIKI]
3. **Port Hudson's surrender was a CONSEQUENCE of Vicksburg, not an independent victory.**
   Gardner surrendered *because* Vicksburg was gone — the fort had nothing left to hold. The
   two sieges are one strategic event. [WIKI], [ABT], [NPS-CWSAC]
4. **Proved Black soldiers in combat.** Port Hudson (May 27) — with Milliken's Bend (June 7)
   — was an early, public demonstration that Black troops, many formerly enslaved, would
   stand and die under fire. It moved Northern opinion and accelerated USCT recruitment
   toward the ~200,000 who eventually served. [WIKI], [NPS-TWHP]
5. **The longest siege in American military history** (48 days) — a record cited for the
   engagement. [WIKI], [NPS-CWSAC]
