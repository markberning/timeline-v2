# FACT PACK — Bonhomme Richard vs Serapis (Battle of Flamborough Head), September 23, 1779

*American Revolution (the war at sea / "At Sea & the Frontier"). ONLY source for the battle page. The legend battle: a worn-out converted French merchantman under a Scottish-born American captain grapples a brand-new Royal Navy frigate within sight of crowds on the English coast, wins after three and a half hours of muzzle-to-muzzle slaughter, and sinks two days later — the victor losing his ship and gaining the most famous (and least documented) sentence in American naval history. ## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING
- **Battle of Flamborough Head, September 23, 1779** — North Sea, off Flamborough Head on the Yorkshire coast of England (~54.12N, 0.08W; the action drifted a few miles offshore). The Revolution's signature naval battle and its only major single-ship action fought in sight of the British home coast. [HIGH]
- **Result: American (Franco-American squadron) victory** — both British escort warships captured; but **the convoy escaped**, which was the escorts' entire mission. Both true; tell both. [HIGH]
- **The premise [HIGH]:** John Paul Jones, commanding a converted, elderly French East Indiaman renamed *Bonhomme Richard* in Franklin's honor, was raiding around the British Isles with a small French-financed squadron under American colors. Off Flamborough Head he intercepted a 40+-ship Baltic convoy carrying the timber, hemp, and tar of British sea power, escorted by the new 44-gun *Serapis* (Capt. Richard Pearson) and the hired 20-gun *Countess of Scarborough*. Pearson put his ships between the enemy and the convoy; the merchantmen fled inshore and escaped. The *Richard* and *Serapis* then fought lashed together for roughly 3.5 hours by moonlight, watched by crowds on the Yorkshire cliffs.
- **Signature elements [HIGH]:** (1) the early disaster — two of the *Richard*'s six old 18-pounders **burst at the first fire**, killing their crews and forcing Jones to abandon his heaviest battery; (2) **the lashing-together** — outgunned, Jones grappled and made the two ships one burning raft; (3) **William Hamilton's grenade** down a *Serapis* hatch, detonating a chain of powder cartridges along her lower gun deck; (4) the *Alliance* (Capt. Pierre Landais) sailing around the locked pair and **firing broadsides into her own flagship**; (5) Pearson striking his colors ~10:30 p.m. as his mainmast tottered; (6) the *Bonhomme Richard* sinking on the morning of September 25, the victor sailing away in the captured prize.
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — the famous line:** "I have not yet begun to fight!" is **not contemporaneous**. First print appearance: 1825, 46 years after the battle, in first lieutenant Richard Dale's recollection given to biographer John Henry Sherburne. Jones's own report says only that he answered "in the most determined negative." Full evidentiary chain and prescribed framing in Sec. 7. NEVER print the line as a bare fact.
- **Pearson is not the villain [HIGH]:** he saved his convoy (his actual mission), fought a ship that would not sink until it had taken his, was honorably acquitted by court-martial (March 10, 1780), and knighted. Treat him as the competent professional he was; the page's honesty beat.

## 2. BACKGROUND
### The war at sea, 1779 [HIGH]
- The Continental Navy never contested command of the sea and never tried; British sea power moved armies, supplied them, and blockaded American ports. The American naval war was commerce raiding, privateering, and propaganda — make the ocean expensive and embarrassing for Britain until a real navy (France, allied 1778; Spain, June 1779) joined the fight. [HIGH; this is the framing of the shipped war-at-sea theme]
- Summer 1779 context: a combined Franco-Spanish fleet (the "other Armada") was maneuvering in the Channel that August in an invasion scare; Britain's home coasts were jumpy before Jones ever appeared. His cruise landed on already-frayed nerves. [HIGH as context; keep to a clause]
### Jones to 1779 [HIGH unless tagged]
- Born plain **John Paul**, July 6, 1747, a gardener's son at Arbigland, Kirkcudbrightshire, on Scotland's Solway coast — the same firth he later raided. To sea ~age 13; merchant master; added "Jones" after killing a mutinous sailor at Tobago in 1773 (he claimed self-defense and fled to America rather than stand trial). [HIGH]
- Continental Navy 1775; as captain of the sloop *Providence* (1776) took or destroyed more than a dozen prizes in one cruise. Carried the war to home waters in the sloop-of-war *Ranger* (1778): the **Whitehaven raid** (night of April 22–23, 1778 — first hostile landing on English soil in roughly a century; materially trivial — one ship set briefly alight, batteries spiked — psychologically loud); the same-day attempt to kidnap the **Earl of Selkirk** on St Mary's Isle (earl not home; crew took the family silver; Jones later bought it back and returned it with a famously florid apology letter); and the capture of **HMS Drake** off Carrickfergus (April 24, 1778) in about an hour — an American warship beating a Royal Navy warship in a fair fight. [HIGH; all consistent with the shipped theme]
### The ship and the squadron [HIGH unless tagged]
- ***Bonhomme Richard***: built 1765 at Lorient as the East Indiaman **Duc de Duras** (French East India Company; two China voyages). Bought by Louis XVI in early 1779 — the financing arranged through French shipping magnate **Jacques-Donatien Le Ray de Chaumont** — and given to Jones on February 4, 1779. Jones renamed her **Bon Homme Richard / Bonhomme Richard** for Benjamin Franklin, whose *Poor Richard's Almanack* circulated in France as *Les Maximes du Bonhomme Richard*. ~40–42 guns: 28 twelve-pounders (the main battery), 6 old eighteen-pounders in a lower gun-deck battery near the waterline, 8 (or 6) nine-pounders. Old, slow, and tired; Jones's own report calls her "the good old Ship." [HIGH; gun-count varies slightly by source — use "about forty guns"]
- **Crew [MED on exact split, HIGH on the picture]:** ~320–380 by count date; a polyglot ship — American officers (1st Lt. **Richard Dale**), with American, English, Irish, Scottish, Scandinavian, Portuguese and other sailors, plus **~137 French marines/soldiers** (Walsh's Irish regiment in French service among them) and French volunteer officers (Col. Wuibert/Weibert, Col. de Chamillard). Do NOT print a precise nationality breakdown — sources conflict (one modern article claims ~300 Americans; standard accounts make Americans a minority). Safe: "a crew swept up from half the ports of Europe, stiffened by French marines, under American officers."
- **The squadron (sailed from the Groix roads off Lorient, August 14, 1779)** per Jones's own report: *Bonhomme Richard* (40), **Alliance** (36, the Continental frigate, Capt. Pierre Landais), **Pallas** (32, French, Capt. Denis-Nicolas Cottineau), **Cerf** (18, French cutter), **Vengeance** (12, French brig, Capt. Philippe Ricot), plus two French privateers (*Monsieur*, *Granville*) that soon left. American colors, French money. [HIGH]
- **HANDLE-WITH-CARE — the concordat:** before sailing, Chaumont had the captains sign a "concordat" that left each captain semi-independent — Jones was commodore in name with shaky authority over his own squadron. Standard in the literature (Morison) and it explains Landais all cruise long, but frame as background, one sentence. [MED-HIGH]
- **The cruise [HIGH, route from Jones's report]:** counter-clockwise around the British Isles — up the west coast of Ireland (boats and the *Cerf* lost company off the Skelligs/Blaskets), Cape Wrath and the Flannan Isles by August 31–September 1, down the Scottish east coast; September 14–17 an aborted descent on **Leith** (Edinburgh's port — a gale blew the squadron back out of the Firth of Forth as it stood within cannon shot); prizes taken and sunk along the way (16 on the cruise); off Whitby and then Flamborough Head by September 21–23.
### The convoy and the escort [HIGH]
- The Baltic convoy (sailed from the Skagerrak ~September 15): originally 50+ sail, just over 40 by September 23, carrying Baltic naval stores — timber, masts, plank, hemp, iron, tar — the strategic raw material of the Royal Navy. Losing it would have genuinely hurt; this is why Pearson's fight mattered to Britain. [HIGH]
- Escort: **HMS Serapis** — brand-new Roebuck-class two-decker frigate, launched March 1779, rated 44 (carrying ~44–50 guns: twenty 18-pounders on the lower deck, twenty 9-pounders, plus 6-pounders), Capt. **Richard Pearson**, commissioned into her that June; and the hired armed ship **Countess of Scarborough** (20 six-pounders, Capt. Thomas Piercy). On sighting Jones, Pearson stood out to sea between the strangers and the convoy and signaled the merchantmen to run inshore for Scarborough; they did, and (with at most one exception) all escaped. [HIGH; "all" vs "all but one" — see Uncertainties #4]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS
### Strengths (dossier stats)
- **Franco-American squadron present:** *Bonhomme Richard* (~40 guns, ~320 in crew), *Alliance* (36), *Pallas* (32), *Vengeance* (12). The *Cerf* and privateers had separated earlier in the cruise. [HIGH]
- **British:** *Serapis* (rated 44, ~280–305 crew), *Countess of Scarborough* (20). [HIGH]
- **Duration:** first broadsides ~7:00–7:15 p.m., colors struck "at half an hour past 10 o'clock" (Jones's report) — about three and a half hours, the core of it lashed together. [HIGH]
### American/squadron commander bios (60–90 w material, verified)
- **John Paul Jones (1747–1792) [HIGH]:** see Sec. 2. By 1779 already famous and infamous — to the British press a pirate (caricatured as "Paul Jones the Pirate"), to the French a usable hero, to Congress its best fighting captain. Prickly, vain, relentless; equally documented writing chivalric apology letters and fighting his ship while she sank under him. At Flamborough Head he was 32.
- **Richard Dale (1756–1826) [HIGH]:** Virginia-born first lieutenant of the *Richard*, 23 at the battle; had escaped from Mill Prison in England earlier in the war. Commanded the gun deck, led the boarding/possession of the *Serapis*, was wounded in the action's last minutes. Later one of the first six captains of the new United States Navy (1794) and commodore of the first Mediterranean squadron. **His 1825 recollection is the sole source of the famous line — the perfect closing-credit fact.**
- **Pierre Landais (c. 1731–1820) [HIGH on arc; tag specifics]:** Saint-Malo-born French ex-naval officer (he had sailed on Bougainville's circumnavigation, 1766–69); made an honorary citizen of Massachusetts and given the Continental frigate *Alliance* (June 1778) — France's answer to Lafayette, on paper. Insubordinate all cruise; at Flamborough Head he sailed around the locked ships and fired at least two broadsides that hulled his own flagship (Sec. 4). Aftermath in Sec. 6.
- **Denis-Nicolas Cottineau (1745–1808) [HIGH, minor]:** French captain of the *Pallas*; fought and took the *Countess of Scarborough* after about an hour — the battle's forgotten second action. Later called Landais a coward to his face and was badly wounded by him in the resulting duel.
- **Nathaniel Fanning (1755–1805) [MED-HIGH, optional]:** Connecticut midshipman commanding the *Richard*'s maintop, whose marksmen and grenade-throwers cleared the *Serapis*'s upper deck. His 1806 memoir is vivid, partisan, and a major source of the battle's embellishments — quote it only with attribution.
- **William Hamilton [HIGH that Jones's report credits the grenade chain to a man on the yard; the name via standard accounts]:** the sailor who crawled out along a yard with grenades and dropped one through a *Serapis* hatch onto loose powder cartridges (~9:30 p.m.) — the single most consequential enlisted act of the battle. Almost nothing else is known about him; say so, it's a feature.
### British commander bios
- **Capt. Richard Pearson (1731–1806) [HIGH]:** born at Langton Hall, Appleby, Westmorland; at sea from 1745, badly wounded as a lieutenant in the Seven Years' War, post-captain 1773. At Flamborough Head he read the situation instantly, put the *Serapis* between Jones and the convoy, and fought until resistance meant only more dead. Court-martialed (the automatic Royal Navy proceeding for a captain who lost his ship) and **honourably acquitted, March 10, 1780**; knighted; given the freedom of Hull, Scarborough, Lancaster and Appleby and plate by the Russia Company and the Royal Exchange Assurance (the convoy's insurers — the most honest reward of all). Later lieutenant-governor of Greenwich Hospital (1800), where he died in January 1806. The Royal Navy launched a new *Serapis* in 1782 — a rare honor for a beaten ship. [HIGH throughout]
- **Capt. Thomas Piercy [HIGH, minor]:** commanded the hired 20-gun *Countess of Scarborough*; fought the *Pallas* for about an hour before striking — outgunned, like his commodore, and likewise acquitted with Pearson.

## 4. THE BATTLE
### Sighting and approach (afternoon–7 p.m.) [HIGH]
- ~3–4 p.m., the convoy's topsails appeared rounding Flamborough Head. The merchantmen "Crowded Sail towards the Shore" (Jones's report) while *Serapis* and *Countess of Scarborough* stood out and cleared for action. Jones signaled a general chase, then the line of battle — "to Which the Alliance Showed no attention" (Jones's report). The *Richard* closed alone, the *Vengeance* hanging back, the *Pallas* heading for the *Countess of Scarborough*. [HIGH]
- ~7 p.m., within pistol shot, Pearson hailed. Jones, under false colors until the last moment, returned evasive answers, then hoisted American colors and the firing began — both broadsides opening near-simultaneously (each side later remembered the other firing first; don't adjudicate). [HIGH on the sequence; "who fired first" DISPUTED — hedge]
### The burst 18-pounders (first minutes) [HIGH — Jones's own report]
- At the first or second broadside, **two of the old 18-pounders on the lower gun deck burst**, killing almost all the men serving them and blowing up part of the deck above. Jones abandoned the entire heavy battery — verbatim: "they did no Service Whatever: two out of three of them burst at the first fire, and killed almost all the men Who Were stationed to manage them." From that moment the *Richard* threw a fraction of the *Serapis*'s broadside weight (her twenty 18-pounders vs. his remaining 12- and 9-pounders): a gunnery duel was suicide. [HIGH; note Jones's odd "two out of three" phrasing likely means the three guns of the engaged side]
### The lashing (~8:00–8:30 p.m.) [HIGH]
- Jones's only chance was to close and hold. After maneuvering, collisions, and a failed British boarding attempt, the ships fouled — the *Serapis*'s jib-boom in the *Richard*'s mizzen rigging, an anchor catching, Jones and his men lashing the two hulls together — bow to stern, gun muzzles touching the enemy's planking. Pearson dropped anchor hoping to tear free with the tide; the lashings held. The moon rose ~8:30 into a clear night; **the action was visible from the high Yorkshire coast from Scarborough down to Flamborough Head, and crowds gathered on the cliffs to watch** — thousands by standard accounts. [HIGH; "thousands" MED — say "crowds"]
- From then on the fight was vertical: the *Serapis*'s heavy lower battery tore the *Richard* apart at and below the waterline (eventually firing THROUGH her and out the far side), while the *Richard*'s tops — Fanning's maintop above all — swept the British upper deck clear with musketry and grenades. Two ships each winning the deck the other held. Both ships repeatedly on fire; the fighting paused at moments to fight flames. [HIGH]
### The grenade (~9:30 p.m.) [HIGH on event; MED on exact time]
- A sailor (William Hamilton in standard accounts) worked out along a yard hanging over the *Serapis* and dropped grenades at her open hatches. One went through and ignited a line of powder cartridges laid out behind the lower-deck guns; the explosion ran the length of the after gun deck, killing or terribly burning dozens and silencing several guns. Pearson himself wasn't sure how it happened (he speculated through shot-holes); Jones's report credits the man on the yard. [HIGH; flag the source split in one clause if the beat is told from above]
### The Alliance's broadsides (~9:00–10:15 p.m.) [HIGH — the battle's blackest fact]
- The *Alliance*, fresh and untouched, circled the locked ships and fired — into BOTH. Jones's report, verbatim: "Every tongue Cried that he Was firing into the Wrong Ship, but nothing availed; he passed round, firing into the B. H. R.'s head, stern, and broadside, and by one of his Vollies Killed several of my best men, and mortally wounded a good officer on the forecastle." The *Richard* took shots under water from her own consort; by Jones's account the *Alliance* killed exactly one man aboard the *Serapis*. Whether Landais was incompetent, panicked, or malicious was never settled at the time — see Sec. 6 for what he later said and what the courts found. Recognition signals (lanterns) were shown and ignored. [HIGH that she fired into the flagship repeatedly; intent DISPUTED — present the fact plainly, the intent as the open question it was]
### Crisis and the surrender beat (~10:00 p.m.) [HIGH framework]
- By 10 p.m. the *Richard* was holed, flooding, on fire toward the magazine, her pumps failing. Three warrant officers (gunner, carpenter, master-at-arms) concluded she was sinking; the gunner ran aft to strike the colors — and found the ensign staff already shot away, so he **shouted** for quarter. The master-at-arms released the ~100+ British prisoners from the hold (one escaped through a gunport to the *Serapis* and told Pearson the *Richard* was sinking); Dale and others put the prisoners to the pumps. Pearson hailed: did the *Richard* ask quarter? **Jones refused — see Sec. 7 for exactly what he did and did not say.** The battle resumed. [HIGH; prisoner count MED — "more than a hundred"]
### Pearson strikes (~10:30 p.m.) [HIGH]
- Jones had concentrated his three remaining quarterdeck 9-pounders on the *Serapis*'s mainmast with double-headed shot. With his mainmast shaking, his lower deck wrecked by the grenade explosion, his crew unable to stand the open deck, and the *Alliance* loose and untouchable while he was lashed to the *Richard*, **Pearson struck his colors in person** "at half an hour past 10 o'clock" (Jones's report). By tradition he had nailed the ensign to its staff before the action and had to tear it down himself [MED — widely told, frame as "by the accounts" if used]. Dale boarded and took possession; **the Serapis's mainmast went overboard moments after Pearson left her deck** (Jones: "the main-mast and mizen-top-mast of the Serapis fell overboard soon after the captain had come on board the B. H. R."). The *Pallas* had already taken the *Countess of Scarborough*. [HIGH]
### The sinking (September 24–25) [HIGH — Jones's report]
- The *Richard* could not be saved: rudder cut away, transoms shot through, old timbers "mangled beyond my power of description." Jones: "a person must have been an Eye-Witness to form a just idea of the tremendous scene of Carnage, Wreck, and ruin, that Every Where appeared." Wounded and crew were transferred through the 24th; pumping continued until the morning of **September 25**, when "it Was impossible to prevent the good old Ship from Sinking… a little after ten, I Saw With inexpressible grief the last glimpse of the B. H. R. No Lives were lost With the Ship." Jones shifted his flag to the captured *Serapis* and, with the squadron and ~500 prisoners, ran for neutral Holland, anchoring at **the Texel on October 3**. [HIGH; "about 11 a.m." appears in some modern accounts vs Jones's "a little after ten" — use "mid-morning"]
- **The wreck has never been found** despite repeated modern searches off Flamborough Head. [HIGH]

## 5. CASUALTIES (dossier ranges — genuinely contested; print ranges, not points)
- **Bonhomme Richard [MED-HIGH range]:** roughly **140–170 killed and wounded** of ~320 — about half the crew. Anchors: British press reports claimed ~70 dead; the American Battlefield Trust uses ~170 total casualties; Pearson's claim of ~300 American casualties is rejected as inflated (unless it counts prisoners). The standard modern formula — "**roughly half the men on each ship killed or wounded**" — is the safe headline (it is also what the shipped theme says).
- **Serapis [MED-HIGH range]:** **at least 117 killed and wounded** (Pearson's own incomplete return: 49 killed "and many more," 68 wounded), probably ~**130–150**. Same "about half" formula applies.
- No definitive muster-verified totals exist for either ship; every precise-looking number in print traces to a partisan report. Say "about half of each crew" and give Pearson's own return as the hardest single figure. [HIGH that the totals are unresolved]

## 6. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES
- **The mission ledger [HIGH — the page's honesty beat]:** Jones took both escorts and a Royal Navy frigate struck to the Continental flag in English home waters — and **Pearson accomplished his actual mission**: the Baltic convoy, the thing of strategic value, escaped essentially intact. Britain lost a frigate and a news cycle; it did not lose the naval stores, and it did not lose the sea. The Royal Navy's command of the ocean was untouched — the war's decisive naval battle (the Capes, 1781) would be fought by France. Write both columns of the ledger explicitly.
- **Pearson [HIGH]:** exchanged, home ~beginning of November 1779; **court-martial March 10, 1780, honourably acquitted**; **knighted (1780)**; freedom of four towns; plate from the Russia Company and the convoy's insurers. Given the *Alarm* (April 1780), later the *Arethusa*; retired to Greenwich Hospital 1790, lieutenant-governor 1800, died January 1806. The alleged Jones quip about his knighthood is apocrypha — Sec. 7.
- **The Texel standoff (Oct–Dec 1779) [HIGH]:** the squadron's arrival in neutral Holland set off a diplomatic crisis. British ambassador **Sir Joseph Yorke** demanded the ships and prisoners be handed over, calling Jones (Oct 8) a "rebel" and "pirate" sailing for no recognized state; Dutch authorities stalled; Amsterdam crowds treated Jones as a celebrity — a Dutch street song about him ("Hier komt Paul Jones aan," roughly "Here comes Paul Jones") circulated and survives [HIGH the song existed; MED any specific lyric — don't quote lyrics]. The legal dodge: the *Pallas* and *Vengeance* were declared French; the *Serapis* was transferred to France (she later became a French privateer and burned and blew up off Madagascar, July 31, 1781; that wreck was found in 1999). Jones took command of the *Alliance* (Landais having been suspended) and slipped out through the blockade on **December 27, 1779**, eventually reaching Lorient. The affair fed the Anglo-Dutch rupture that became the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (Dec 1780) — frame as "fed," never "caused." [HIGH; war-cause framing MED — keep one hedged clause]
- **The propaganda victory [HIGH]:** this is what the battle actually changed. Franklin in Paris had a victory in England's own waters to retail at Versailles; Paris lionized Jones through 1780 — **Houdon sculpted his bust**, **Louis XVI gave him a gold-hilted sword and admitted him to the Order of Military Merit** (whence "Chevalier" John Paul Jones), and Congress eventually voted him its **gold medal (October 16, 1787) — the only Continental Navy officer so honored**. For a cause whose navy had mostly burned (Penobscot, the same season), a frigate of the Royal Navy struck on its own coast was priceless theater. [HIGH]
- **Landais [HIGH on arc]:** Franklin's inquiry took testimony; squadron officers swore the *Alliance* had fired deliberately into the flagship, and Landais reportedly told one of the French colonels **his intention had been to help the *Serapis* sink the *Richard*, then take the *Serapis* himself** [MED — alleged in the Jones-side affidavits; attribute, don't assert]. Franklin suspended him. Challenged over his conduct, he fought a duel with Cottineau of the *Pallas* and ran him through (Cottineau survived) [HIGH duel happened; severity wording varies]. In June 1780 Landais seized the *Alliance* back at Lorient and sailed for America against orders; he behaved so erratically on the voyage that his own officers deposed him mid-ocean. A court-martial at Boston (begun late 1780, sentence early 1781) — **for the voyage, not for Flamborough Head** — found him guilty, revoked his commission, and declared him unfit to serve in the American navy. He died poor in New York in 1820 (Bellevue Hospital), still petitioning for his prize money; Congress had paid him $4,000 in 1806. NOTE: the shipped theme says he "was later court-martialed out of the navy over other conduct" — exactly right; keep that precision. [HIGH]
- **Jones coda (one-paragraph material) [HIGH]:** he never fought another major action under the American flag. Feted in Paris; briefly and unhappily a rear admiral in Catherine the Great's Russian navy (1788, against the Turks on the Black Sea; the appointment collapsed in court intrigue and an unresolved accusation of assaulting a young girl); died alone in Paris, **July 18, 1792**, aged 45. Buried in the St. Louis cemetery for foreign Protestants; the grave was lost. In **1905** U.S. Ambassador Horace Porter's six-year search found his lead coffin, the body preserved in alcohol and identified by autopsy (April 1905); ceremonial commemoration at Annapolis April 24, 1906 (Theodore Roosevelt speaking), and final entombment in **1913** in the marble crypt beneath the Naval Academy chapel — where a midshipman honor watch stands today. The navy that barely existed while he lived made him its founding saint after death. [HIGH; matches the shipped theme]

## 7. THE APOCRYPHA CENTERPIECE — "I have not yet begun to fight" (and the rest of the legend kit)
**This battle is the war's densest apocrypha site. The page should TELL the evidence story — it is better material than the legend.**
### 7a. The line — full evidentiary chain [HIGH on every link below]
1. **What Jones wrote, 10 days later (report to Franklin, Oct 3, 1779, the only same-season account in his hand):** "The English Commodore asked me if I demanded quarters, and I having answered him in the most determined negative, they renewed the battle with double fury." No quoted sentence. The DEFIANCE is documented; the WORDS are not. (Transcription variant flagged in Sec. 8 item 1.)
2. **Contemporary hearsay, within weeks:** *London Evening Post*, Sept 30, 1779 (from escaped British prisoners): Jones swore he would sooner obey the Devil's summons "than … strike to anyone" (garbled). *Edinburgh Advertiser*, Oct 12, 1779: "**I may sink, but I'll be damned if I strike**" — the saltiest and earliest-printed version, possibly closest in spirit. *London Observer* (letter from Amsterdam), Oct 12, 1779: "I have not as yet thought of it, but am determined to make you strike."
3. **Jones's own memoir (mid-1780s):** "I haven't as yet thought of surrendering, but I am determined to make you ask for quarter." Consistent with the Amsterdam version, NOT with the famous line.
4. **Fanning's memoir (1806):** "Ay, ay, we'll do that when we can fight no longer, but we shall see yours come down first; for you must know, Yankees do not haul down their colors till they are fairly beaten." (Fanning at his most embroidered.)
5. **The famous line, first appearance: 1825** — Richard Dale, then 68, recalling the battle 46 years on for John Henry Sherburne's biography: Pearson hailed "Has your ship struck?" and Jones answered "**I have not yet begun to fight!**" Dale stood with Jones at points in the action but his station was the gun deck; he is a real witness with a 46-year-old memory, supplying a biographer, 33 years after Jones died and could neither confirm nor deny.
- **PRESCRIBED FRAMING (the shipped theme already does this — match it):** the refusal happened and is documented in Jones's own hand; the sentence is a legend built around it, an old man's memory set down half a century later. Print the famous line ONLY inside that frame. The strongest authentic artifacts are Jones's "most determined negative" and the Edinburgh Advertiser's damned-if-I-strike. NEVER caption an image with the bare quote.
### 7b. The surrender-ceremony embellishments [DISPUTED — late accounts, not in either official report]
- The dialogue: Pearson surrendering his sword with "It is painful to deliver up my sword to a man who has fought with a halter around his neck" (i.e., a pirate due for hanging), Jones returning it with "you have fought like a hero, and I make no doubt but your sovereign will reward you in the most ample manner." **Neither Jones's nor Pearson's report contains any of this**; it comes from later memoir/biography tradition (Fanning's 1806 narrative and 19th-century lives are the carriers). The sword handover itself is standard period practice and is referenced in accounts of Pearson coming aboard [HIGH the formal surrender happened; the SCRIPT is legend]. PRESCRIPTION: either omit the dialogue or present it explicitly as the story later told; do not stage it as fact.
- The "fought with a halter around his neck" image IS independently real as a description of Jones's legal position (Britain regarded him as a pirate; the caricatures prove the reputation) — usable as authorial framing without the dialogue.
### 7c. "I'll make a lord of him" [DISPUTED — likely apocryphal]
- On hearing Pearson was knighted, Jones "is said to have" replied: "**Should I have the good fortune to fall in with him again, I'll make a lord of him!**" Universally quoted, never contemporaneously sourced; even hero-friendly accounts attach "is said to have." PRESCRIPTION: usable ONLY with "the story goes" / "is said to have" framing, ideally as the kicker to the honest Pearson paragraph — or skip it.
### 7d. Lesser legend-kit items (use with labels or not at all)
- **Pearson nailing his colors to the staff** before battle, then tearing them down himself: widely told, MED evidence; "by the accounts" framing if used. His striking in person is HIGH.

## 11. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)
- **Date:** September 23, 1779 (evening action, ~7:00–10:30 p.m., by moonlight) · **Where:** North Sea, off Flamborough Head, Yorkshire, England
- **Forces:** *Bonhomme Richard* (~40 guns, ~320 crew) + squadron (*Alliance* 36, *Pallas* 32, *Vengeance* 12) vs HMS *Serapis* (rated 44, new) + *Countess of Scarborough* (20), escorting a 40+-ship Baltic convoy
- **Duration:** about 3.5 hours, most of it lashed hull-to-hull
- **Casualties:** roughly half of each crew killed or wounded — *Richard* ~140–170, *Serapis* 117+ (Pearson's own incomplete return) — exact totals unresolved
- **Result:** both British escorts captured; the convoy escaped; the *Bonhomme Richard* sank Sept 25; Jones sailed the captured *Serapis* to the Texel
- **Winner line material:** Jones won the battle, lost his ship, and refused to surrender either way — and Pearson, who lost his ship doing exactly his duty, was knighted for saving the convoy and acquitted with honor. The Royal Navy still owned the sea the morning after; what changed was the story Europe told about the Americans.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES
1. **The famous line** — not contemporaneous (Dale 1825 via Sherburne); Jones's own hand says only "the most determined negative"; contemporary press version "I may sink, but I'll be damned if I strike." Tell at the Sec. 7a level, always.
2. **Casualties** — no verified totals; "about half of each crew" + Pearson's own 49+/68 return + the ~140–170 / 117–150 ranges. Never print a single confident number.
3. **"Double fury" vs "redoubled fury"** in the Jones-report quote — transcription editions differ; the shipped theme uses "redoubled." One manual Founders Online check owed before quoting verbatim on the new page (Sec. 8 item 1); reconcile the theme if needed.
4. **Convoy escape** — "all escaped" (Jones could not pursue) vs "all but one"; say "the convoy escaped essentially intact."
5. **Landais's intent** — deliberate aid to the enemy is ALLEGED (squadron affidavits + a reported private admission), never adjudicated; his court-martial conviction was for the 1780 voyage, not the battle. Keep the theme's precision.
6. **Who fired first** at the opening hail — both sides claimed the other; hedge or omit.
7. **The grenade** — Jones's report credits the man on the yard (Hamilton by standard accounts); Pearson speculated other causes; the ~9:30 p.m. time is approximate (one modern account puts it past midnight — the timeline in this pack follows Jones's "half past 10" strike time, which constrains it).
8. **Crowds on the cliffs** — visible from Scarborough to Flamborough Head, crowds well-attested; "thousands" is the standard but soft figure — prefer "crowds."
9. **Crew nationality split** on the *Richard* — sources conflict badly; use the polyglot description, no percentages.
10. **Serapis gun count** — rated 44, carried 44–50 by source; "a brand-new 44" is safe.
11. **Sinking hour** — Jones "a little after ten" (a.m., Sept 25) vs modern "~11 a.m."; "mid-morning."
12. **Pearson nailing his colors** — MED tradition; "by the accounts" framing only. His striking them in person is solid.
13. **"Terror of the English" nickname** and any Dutch song lyrics — popular-account tier; attribute loosely or omit.
14. **Sword-ceremony dialogue and the "make a lord of him" quip** — legend tier (Sec. 7b/7c); label or omit, never narrate as fact.
