# Franklin — CRAFT PANEL: STORYTELLING gate

> War content pipeline, critic step. Judges the draft `franklin-draft.md` on
> storytelling craft ONLY (hook, momentum, stakes, scene-setting, human core,
> arc, pacing). Accuracy is a separate gate. No invented facts requested; where
> a beat would land harder with a fact, it's flagged as a fact-pack ASK, never
> asserted.

**VERDICT: PASS WITH REVISIONS.** Strong, well-shaped draft with a genuine arc
and a payload that lands. Three MUST-IMPROVE pacing/lede issues and a cluster of
SHOULD-IMPROVEs. None are ship-blocking on craft once the MUST items are fixed.

---

## Beat-by-beat

### §1 `spring-hill` — "The night the army walked past"

**Hook: GOOD.** The opening line ("To understand why roughly twenty thousand men
charged two miles of open field into a wall of dug-in guns at dusk, you have to
start the night before, on a road in the dark") is the right hook — it promises
the slaughter, then withholds it and sends us backward. That's a real narrative
move, not a summary.

**Scene-setting / stakes: GOOD but BURIES ITS OWN LEDE.** Paragraph 2 ("Back up
first to why any of this was happening…") is necessary strategic context, and the
"long shot dressed up as a strategy" line earns its keep. But the section's most
dramatic beat — the army *physically walking past sleeping enemy campfires* — is
the title and the emotional engine, and it doesn't arrive until paragraph 5. The
two arithmetic paragraphs (the Schofield/Thomas math) are correct and clear but
they drain momentum at exactly the wrong place: right after a hook that promised
a road in the dark. **MUST-IMPROVE:** tighten the two strategic paragraphs into
one (the reader needs *the trap* and *the stakes of the trap*, not two separate
passes at the troop math), so the "walked past in the dark" beat lands sooner and
hits harder. The 27,000-vs-25,000 vs 20,000 numbers can be one clause, not a
paragraph.

**The Spring Hill missed chance — the human core of THIS section: GOOD, lands.**
"On November 29 he very nearly did. … On paper, Schofield was trapped. / And then
he wasn't." That hard turn is the best craft move in the section — the short
sentence after the build is exactly right. "Schofield's entire army and its wagon
train marched up the Columbia Pike and slipped past, in some accounts close enough
to the sleeping Confederate camps to see their fires. Nobody stopped them." This is
the missed chance and it does land. **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** the phrase "a cascade of
command failures that night — disputed orders, confusion in the dark, and Hood's
own premature confidence" is a slight info-list where the prose has otherwise been
scene-driven. It tells us there were failures rather than letting one concrete
image carry it. Not required (the fact pack is thin on Spring Hill specifics by
design), but if the fact-checker can confirm one vivid concrete detail, it would
turn a summary clause into a scene.

**Hood's motive handling: GOOD as craft.** The "wrathy as a rattlesnake" line is a
gift and it's used well. Presenting the debate ("one of the war's genuine
historical arguments") could read as a hedge that stalls the story, but here it
actually raises tension — it frames the next section's charge as a decision under
dispute. Keep it. It's tighter than most "historians disagree" asides.

**MEANWHILE — Sherman: GOOD.** "Sherman declined to care." is a great line and the
sidebar does real work — it kills the natural misconception (Hood chasing Sherman)
and reframes the whole campaign as a bid for attention that failed. "Franklin is
what that gamble cost the men who had to execute it" is a strong button.

### §2 `the-open-ground` — "Twenty thousand men, two miles, no cover"

**Hook: GOOD.** "To see why Franklin became a slaughter, you have to stand for a
moment where Hood's soldiers stood and look at the ground." Strong — it makes
terrain into suspense, which is hard.

**Scene-setting: GOOD, occasionally INFO-DUMPS.** The geography is genuinely well
rendered — the river-behind-the-line detail ("Schofield's army had its back to the
water"), the bridgehead, the deliberate gap at the Pike — all of this builds dread
correctly because the reader understands the box before the men walk into it. This
is the section doing its job. **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** the middle paragraph (works /
retrenchment line / Carter House / cotton gin / salient) introduces five named
features in quick succession and reads, briefly, like a map legend. The Carter
House and cotton gin will both recur and pay off, so they earn their introduction —
but the "retrenchment line about two hundred feet back" is a detail that the later
sections never use dramatically. Consider trimming the retrenchment aside or
folding it so the paragraph has fewer hard stops. (See clarity gate — these terms
also need gloss; here the craft note is purely about the *density* of new nouns in
one breath.)

**The "why this charge was suicidal" build: GOOD, this is the engine.** "A frontal
assault … is the costliest attack in the tactical book even in good conditions.
These were not good conditions." — clean, confident, house voice. The objections
(Forrest's flanking route, Cheatham's "did not like the look of the fight") give
the suicide a human texture: people in the room *knew*. Good.

**The artillery beat — the lede of the whole section: GOOD and well-placed.** "Then
came the detail that turned a long-shot attack into a near-certain massacre: there
was essentially no artillery preparation." Holding this as the *last* terrain
detail before the men step off is the right call — it's the worst fact, so it goes
last. The Gettysburg comparison ("roughly a hundred and fifty guns … for about two
hours") is exactly the contrast a newcomer needs, and "the honest comparison is
that Franklin was worse" earns the nickname instead of just dropping it.

**Closing image: GOOD.** "Around four in the afternoon, with the sun setting about
half an hour later, some twenty thousand Confederates stepped off and started
walking north into the open." The understatement ("started walking") after all the
dread is the right register. Strong button.

### §3 `the-cotton-gin` — "The breach at the Carter House"

**Hook: GOOD.** "For a few minutes, it looked like it might work — and the reason
why is one of the cruelest accidents of the day." Promises a reversal and a cruelty;
delivers both.

**The Wagner accident: GOOD, lands.** The mechanism (retreating Federals masking the
Union guns, defenders holding fire to avoid shooting their own) is explained as
*story* — cause and consequence — not as a tactical footnote. "rode that lull right
up to the wall" is good. The reader feels the freak luck.

**Opdycke seals the breach: GOOD.** "It didn't hold, and the reason it didn't is a
single colonel's stubbornness." The personalization (one colonel's refusal earlier
in the day becomes the thing that saves the line) is exactly how to make a tactical
turning point human. **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** the Stanley-wounded / MacArthur "Boy
Colonel" sentence is two facts bolted to the end of the Opdycke paragraph and reads
as a roll-call. MacArthur ("father of a future WWII general") is a fun aside but it
pulls the eye away from the breach at the exact moment the breach is the story. Move
it, trim it, or cut MacArthur to a half-clause — right now it's a momentum tax on
the climax of the section.

**The slaughter at the works — the section's true subject: GOOD, but the "six dead
generals" payoff is SPLIT awkwardly.** The five-hours grind ("shot to pieces, rank
after rank, with nowhere to go but forward into the guns or back across the open
ground") is well done, and ending the body on Cleburne falling on foot is a strong,
restrained image. BUT — the actual roster of six dead generals is parked in the
MEANWHILE sidebar. **MUST-IMPROVE:** the six dead generals are, per the brief, a
beat that must *land hard*, and right now the emotional weight (Cleburne on foot)
is in the body while the *scale* (six generals, fourteen casualties, the
Gettysburg/Antietam comparison) is in a sidebar that a reader can skip. The
sidebar is good writing, but burying the war's single deadliest day for general
officers in an optional box undersells the signature statistic of the whole battle.
Recommend: keep the sidebar's comparison, but let the *body* state plainly, in the
slaughter paragraph, that this is where the army lost six generals — name the
stakes in the spine, not only the sidebar. The body currently names only Cleburne.

**Schofield's withdrawal: GOOD.** "Holding the town had never been his goal" — the
"strange victory" reframe is handled cleanly and sets up §4 and Nashville. "It was
a hollow one." Good button.

### §4 `what-it-cost` — "The dead on the porch, and what they died for"

**Hook: GOOD.** "The numbers tell one story; the names tell the real one." Correct
thesis for the section and a clean promise.

**The casualty math: GOOD, slightly long.** The range-with-honesty approach reads as
trustworthy rather than hedgy, which is the right instinct given the contested
figures. **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** "the most-cited total is about 6,250, though Hood
himself reported a much lower figure and the count is genuinely contested, so a
range is the honest way to give it" — the meta-commentary about *how we're giving
the number* is one clause too many. The reader trusts you; you can land the
3x-disproportion harder if you spend fewer words explaining your sourcing in the
prose. Push the "genuinely contested / range is the honest way" bookkeeping toward
brevity; keep the punch ("lost roughly three times what his enemy did, while
failing to take the position").

**Carnton porch — human core: GOOD, lands hard.** "The human core of it sat on a
back porch the next morning." The four generals laid out together, "Carnton's wooden
floors are still stained from it," Cleburne "Irish-born … the Stonewall of the West
… die like men … went forward on foot after his horse was killed and never came
back." This is the draft at its best. It lands.

**Tod Carter — human core: GOOD, lands hard.** "A few hundred yards away, the Carter
House gave the battle its most unbearable image." The son returning home after three
years, riding forward when his duties didn't require it, shot a few hundred yards
from his own front yard, the family in the cellar, carried into the house, dying
inside the home he was trying to reach. This is devastating and it's earned. The one
craft risk: it arrives right after the Carnton porch, so two gut-punches sit
back-to-back. They're both strong enough to survive the adjacency, but watch that
the Tod Carter paragraph doesn't feel like it's *competing* with the porch. The
"a few hundred yards away" geographic stitch helps; keep it.

**The slavery turn — the cause: GOOD, and structurally brave.** "And now the thing
that has to be said plainly, because the porch full of dead generals can make
Franklin feel like nothing but a brave, doomed waste — and it was a great deal more
specific than that. These men died to preserve slavery." This is the single hardest
move in the draft to pull off — pivoting from elegy to indictment without feeling
like a lecture bolted on — and it *works*, because the draft has the Cleburne
proposal as a load-bearing fact, not an assertion. Making the battle's most famous
dead man the one who proposed freeing the enslaved, then having his own government
bury both the idea and the man, is the best possible delivery of the NAME-THE-CAUSE
mandate: it's narrative, not editorial. **GOOD.** "a Confederate general suggested
trading slavery for survival, and his own government chose slavery" is the line.

**The USCT-at-Nashville close: GOOD.** "People who had been held as property put on
Union blue and helped annihilate the army that had marched north to keep them
enslaved." Strong, and the right note to end the cause-argument on — the war's cause
returns as the war's instrument.

**The final two paragraphs (McGavock cemetery + strategic verdict): SHOULD-IMPROVE —
the section has THREE endings.** The draft already peaks twice (the USCT line; then
"the South had bet its army … on keeping human beings in chains, and it lost both").
Between them sits the McGavock-cemetery paragraph and a strategic-verdict recap
(Spring Hill / Franklin / Nashville failed catastrophically). All three are good
material, but stacking cemetery + strategic-verdict + the slavery button means the
ending decelerates and then re-accelerates. **SHOULD-IMPROVE:** pick the order
deliberately — the 1,500 reburied and the "largest privately owned military
cemetery" is a quieter, image-based landing that could either open the closing
movement (image) or be folded shorter; the strategic verdict is the *head*
conclusion; the slavery line is the *heart* conclusion and should go last (it does).
As written it's not broken, but it's the one place the otherwise-tight arc sags
into "and also, and finally, and the deepest verdict." Tighten so the section lands
on one clear final beat rather than three.

## Cross-cutting craft notes

1. **Pacing front-load (MUST-IMPROVE, §1).** The two strategic-arithmetic paragraphs
   delay the section's titular image. Compress to get to "the army walked past" faster.

2. **The six dead generals are under-weighted in the spine (MUST-IMPROVE, §3).** The
   signature statistic of the battle lives in an optional sidebar. Put the scale in
   the body; keep the comparison in the sidebar.

3. **The §3 climax carries a roll-call tax (SHOULD-IMPROVE).** Stanley/MacArthur facts
   interrupt the breach at its peak.

4. **Sourcing-in-prose meta-commentary (SHOULD-IMPROVE, §4).** Two spots explain *how*
   a number is being given rather than just giving it confidently; trims tighten the
   punch without losing the honesty.

5. **§4 has three endings (SHOULD-IMPROVE).** Sequence the close so it lands once.

6. **House voice: STRONG throughout.** "a long shot dressed up as a strategy,"
   "Sherman declined to care," "the costliest attack in the tactical book," "his own
   government chose slavery" — the informal-but-serious register is consistent and is
   the product. No sanding needed.

7. **The four required human beats all land:** Spring Hill missed chance (GOOD),
   the two-mile open-field charge (GOOD), the six dead generals (GOOD emotionally via
   Cleburne, but UNDER-WEIGHTED structurally — see MUST #2), Tod Carter dying yards
   from home (GOOD, devastating).
