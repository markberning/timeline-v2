# FACT PACK: "Ironclads & the Blockade"
### Section scope: the naval revolution and the Union blockade, 1861–1862 (Anaconda Plan → Hampton Roads ironclad duel → aftermath/global arms race)
### Prepared: 2026-05-30

## SCOPE / DEDUP NOTE

This section's spine is two intertwined stories: (1) the **Union blockade strategy** — Winfield Scott's "Anaconda Plan," the slow strangulation of the Confederate economy, and the blockade-runner cat-and-mouse; and (2) the **ironclad revolution**, centered on the **Battle of Hampton Roads (March 8–9, 1862)** — the CSS Virginia's rampage against the wooden fleet on March 8, the arrival of the USS Monitor that night, and the first-ever ironclad-vs-ironclad duel on March 9.

**DEDUP — do NOT write up as set-piece battles (they have their own sections):** Fort Sumter (covered in the secession/opening sections); Mobile Bay and Farragut's "Damn the torpedoes" (a separate Naval-theatre battle, August 1864); Forts Jackson & St. Philip / the capture of New Orleans (April 1862, a separate battle). These may be referenced in **passing** — e.g., New Orleans' fall is the blockade/Mississippi strategy paying off, and it's part of WHY the Virginia couldn't escape up-river — but the blockade strategy + Hampton Roads is THIS section's territory. The western **river ironclad war** (Eads' City-class gunboats) is included only as a brief cross-reference (Beat 7), to show the ironclad revolution wasn't only at Hampton Roads; the rivers' own battles belong to the Western theatre.

## BEAT 1: The Anaconda Plan — Winfield Scott's Strategy to Strangle the South

- **Winfield Scott's role and rank:** Scott was the U.S. Army's **general-in-chief** (its senior-most general) at the war's outbreak in 1861. A hero of the War of 1812 and the Mexican-American War, he was elderly and infirm by 1861 (he had been the losing Whig presidential candidate in 1852 — see the Lincoln's Rise fact pack). He devised the Union's first coherent grand strategy.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Anaconda Plan"; Britannica, "Anaconda plan"

- **The two-part plan:** Scott's strategy emphasized (1) a **naval blockade of the Southern ports**, and (2) **an advance down the Mississippi River to cut the South in two**. The goal was to isolate the Confederacy diplomatically and economically — preventing it from exporting cotton and tobacco to Europe and from importing weapons, foodstuffs, and other necessities — rather than to crush it in one big land battle.
  — Source: Britannica, "Anaconda plan"; Wikipedia, "Anaconda Plan"

- **When Scott outlined it:** Scott submitted the proposal to Major General George B. McClellan in a **letter dated May 3, 1861** (with a second, fuller letter dated May 21, 1861). This was just weeks after the bombardment of Fort Sumter (April 1861).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Anaconda Plan"

- **The Mississippi column:** Scott proposed "a strong column of perhaps **80,000 men**" to use the Mississippi River as a highway to thrust completely through the Confederacy.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Anaconda Plan"

- **Coastline scale:** The blockade was intended to guard **at least 3,000 miles (5,000 km)** of coastline in the seceded states (Scott's own framing). The Union blockade as actually implemented covered roughly **3,500 miles (5,600 km)** of Atlantic and Gulf coastline, including **12 major ports**, notably New Orleans and Mobile. (See Beat 2 for the blockade's full scale.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Anaconda Plan"; Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

- **Origin of the mocking name:** The plan "was widely derided by a vociferous faction of Union generals" who wanted a more aggressive, immediate prosecution of the war. They **likened it to the coils of an anaconda suffocating its victim**; "the snake image caught on, giving the proposal its popular name." The name began as press/critic mockery — the strategy was seen as too slow and passive, an "on to Richmond" public wanting a quick knockout instead.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Anaconda Plan"; Britannica, "Anaconda plan"

- **Vindicated in hindsight:** The blockade had much the effect Scott intended. When David G. Farragut captured New Orleans in April 1862 and Ulysses S. Grant took Vicksburg in July 1863, the Mississippi River belonged to the Union — splitting the Confederacy and creating logistical chaos. The slow squeeze, derided early, proved a foundation of Union victory. (Historians still debate how *decisive* the seaward blockade alone was — see CONTESTED.)
  — Source: Britannica, "Anaconda plan"; Wikipedia, "Anaconda Plan"

## BEAT 2: The Blockade Itself — Scale, the Navy's Explosive Growth, Effectiveness

- **Lincoln proclaims the blockade:** On **April 19, 1861** — about a week after Fort Sumter — Lincoln announced that the ports of all the seceded states, from South Carolina to Texas, would be blockaded; when Virginia and North Carolina seceded, their coastlines were added.
  — Source: Britannica, "Anaconda plan"; Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

- **The task:** Monitoring **3,500 miles (5,600 km)** of Atlantic and Gulf coastline, including **12 major ports** (New Orleans, Mobile, and others), plus countless inlets, river mouths, and shallow sounds.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

- **The Navy in 1861 (the starting point):** The U.S. Navy had only **42 ships in active service**, with another **48 laid up** and listed as available once crews could be assembled. A tiny force for an enormous coastline.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Ready for War? The Union Navy in 1861"; Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

- **The buildup under Gideon Welles (US Secretary of the Navy):** The Navy Department expanded the fleet at an astonishing rate.
  - By the **end of 1861**: ~160 blockading vessels (nearly 80 steamers and ~60 sailing ships added in 1861 alone).
  - By **November 1862**: 282 steamers and 102 sailing ships.
  - By **war's end (1865)**: ~**671 ships** — making it the largest navy in the world.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

- **The Blockade Strategy Board (1861):** A joint Union military-navy commission, the **Blockade Strategy Board**, was formed in 1861 to plan which Confederate ports and coastal points to seize as coaling/supply bases to make the blockade enforceable.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

- **Effectiveness — the runners DID get through, especially early:**
  - **Early war (1861–1862):** Only about **one in ten** ships trying to evade the blockade was intercepted.
  - **By 1864:** about **one in three** runners was being caught — the blockade tightened dramatically over time as the fleet grew.
  - Over the whole war, roughly **five out of six** attempts to run the blockade succeeded — but the *number* of attempts collapsed, and the volume that mattered.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

- **Runners captured/destroyed:** About **1,500 blockade runners** were captured or destroyed over the course of the war.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

- **The economic chokehold (this is the real story, not the runner box-score):** Cotton exports fell roughly **95%** — from about **10 million bales** in the three years before the war to just **~500,000 bales** during the blockade period. The blockade throttled Southern trade and starved the Confederate economy and war machine over time, even though individual fast runners kept slipping through.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

- **"King Cotton" diplomacy backfired:** The South believed cotton's importance to European mills meant "no power on earth dares make war upon it" — that Britain and France would be forced to break the blockade to get cotton. Instead the opposite happened: Europe rode out the cotton famine (and found other sources), and the blockade held. The cotton-as-weapon gamble failed.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Union blockade"

## BEAT 3: The Blockade Runners — Fast Steamers, the Nassau/Bermuda Run

- **What they were:** Purpose-built or repurposed **fast, shallow-draft steamers** — often 400–600 tons, with elongated low hulls (length six to nine times the beam), telescopic smokestacks to cut their silhouette, dull lead-gray paint for night camouflage, and smokeless **anthracite coal** to avoid betraying smoke. Speeds reported up to ~13–19 knots.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Blockade runners of the American Civil War"

- **The relay route (key geographic facts):** Supplies bought in England were shipped first to **neutral entrepôts** — **Nassau (Bahamas)** and **Bermuda** — in ordinary British vessels. There the cargo was transferred to the fast, light-draft runners for the final dash into Confederate ports.
  - **Nassau** was about **570 miles** from the Confederate coast.
  - **Bermuda** was about **674 miles** nearly due east of **Wilmington, North Carolina** — the most important runner port (protected by Fort Fisher).
  - Other principal destination ports: **Charleston** and **Savannah**.
  — Source: NC Historic Sites, "Running the Blockade"; Wikipedia, "Blockade runners of the American Civil War"

- **Cotton out, arms & luxuries in — and the corruption of the mission:** Runners exported **cotton** outbound (the South's only real export earner) and brought **arms, gunpowder, and military supplies** inbound. But because cannon, weapons, and powder were bulky and heavy, private runner owners increasingly preferred to import **high-margin luxury goods** (lighter, more profitable per ton), which "began to compromise the purpose" of the runners' original military-supply mission.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Blockade runners of the American Civil War"

- **Scale of the trade:** By war's end, **397 ships sailed from the Confederacy to Nassau**, and **588 went from Nassau to the Confederacy**. One famous runner, the **SS Syren**, made a record **33 successful runs**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Blockade runners of the American Civil War"

## BEAT 4: The Ironclad Revolution — Mallory's Gamble and the Birth of the CSS Virginia

- **Stephen Mallory (CS Secretary of the Navy):** Knew the Confederacy could never out-build the Union Navy ship-for-ship, so he bet on **ironclads** as a force-multiplier — technology to leapfrog the wooden fleet. On **May 10, 1861**, Mallory told the Confederate Congress / its Naval Affairs Committee: **"I regard the possession of an iron-armored ship as a matter of the first necessity."** He argued such a vessel could "traverse the entire coast of the United States, prevent all blockades, and encounter, with a fair prospect of success, their entire navy." (Some sources date the statement May 8 or April 26, 1861 — see CONTESTED for the exact-date flag.)
  — Source: USNI Naval History Magazine, "Proving the Power of Iron Over Wood" (Apr 2012); Essential Civil War Curriculum, "Battle of the Ironclads"

- **The scuttled Merrimack:** When Union forces abandoned **Gosport Navy Yard** (Norfolk/Portsmouth, Virginia) on **April 20, 1861**, they burned the steam frigate **USS Merrimack** to the waterline and scuttled (sank) her to deny her to the enemy. The scuttling was imperfect. Confederates secured the yard on April 21, 1861.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "USS Merrimack (1855)"; Wikipedia, "CSS Virginia"; Battlefield Trust, "The Gosport Navy Yard in the Civil War"

- **Raised and rebuilt as an ironclad:** The Confederates **raised the hull by May 30, 1861**, and Mallory ordered it converted into a **casemate ironclad**. The result was the **CSS Virginia** — built on the salvaged Merrimack hull and machinery at Gosport. (Note: Northerners kept calling her the "Merrimack," which is why the battle is often called "the Monitor and the Merrimack.")
  — Source: Wikipedia, "CSS Virginia"

- **What she was:** A **casemate ironclad** — a sloped armored box ("casemate") built atop the hull, mounting her guns through gunports, with an **iron ram fitted to the bow**. She was **commissioned February 17, 1862**.
  - Length ~275 ft; beam ~51 ft; **draft ~21–22 ft** (this deep draft will matter — it's why she couldn't escape up the James later).
  - Displacement ~4,000 long tons; casemate armor ~4 inches (two 2-inch plates) backed by ~2 feet of iron/pine.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "CSS Virginia"; Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"

## BEAT 5: March 8, 1862 — The Virginia's Rampage (the worst day in U.S. Navy history to that point)

- **The setup:** On the morning of **March 8, 1862**, the CSS Virginia steamed out into **Hampton Roads** — the roadstead where the James, Elizabeth, and Nansemond rivers meet Chesapeake Bay near Norfolk — to attack the wooden Union blockading squadron anchored there. She was commanded by **Flag Officer Franklin Buchanan (CS)**.
  — Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, "The Battle of Hampton Roads"; Wikipedia, "CSS Virginia"; American Battlefield Trust, "Hampton Roads"

- **USS Cumberland — rammed and sunk:** The Virginia **rammed** the sailing sloop **USS Cumberland** in the forward starboard bow; the Cumberland sank. Solid shot simply bounced off the Virginia's iron sides. Casualties: **121 of the Cumberland's crew went down with her**; counting the wounded, the Cumberland's casualty total approached **150** (out of ~376 aboard).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"; American Battlefield Trust, "Hampton Roads"

- **USS Congress — pounded and burned:** The Virginia then turned on the frigate **USS Congress**. After roughly an hour of one-sided fire, the Congress surrendered; she was later set ablaze by hot shot and destroyed. Casualties: about **110 killed or missing/presumed drowned**, plus **26 wounded** (of whom 10 died within days) — out of ~434 aboard (~27% of the crew killed).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"

- **Buchanan wounded:** Flag Officer Buchanan was **wounded on March 8** — struck in the **left thigh by a rifle shot** (reportedly while exposed on deck firing at the burning Congress / the shore). Command of the Virginia passed to his executive officer, **Lieutenant Catesby ap Roger Jones (CS)**, for the next day's fight.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"; Wikipedia, "CSS Virginia"

- **The day's toll (verify framing):** Over the two-day battle the Federal navy suffered **261 killed and 108 wounded** — most of it on March 8 — more than in any other sea battle in American history to that time. The Virginia herself lost only **2 killed** and a handful wounded on March 8. **March 8, 1862 remained the bloodiest day in U.S. naval history until December 7, 1941 (Pearl Harbor).**
  — Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, "The Battle of Hampton Roads"; American Battlefield Trust, "Hampton Roads"

- **Why it was a revolution, not just a defeat:** Solid shot bounced off the Virginia; she destroyed two powerful wooden warships and shrugged off everything thrown at her. In a single afternoon she demonstrated that the world's wooden navies were obsolete. The wooden steam frigate **USS Minnesota** ran aground trying to escape and was expected to be the Virginia's next victim the following morning.
  — Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, "The Battle of Hampton Roads"; Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"

## BEAT 6: The USS Monitor — Ericsson's "Cheesebox on a Raft" — and the Duel of March 9, 1862

- **John Ericsson's radical design (US):** The **USS Monitor** was designed by **John Ericsson**, a Swedish-born engineer. Her revolutionary feature was a **revolving armored gun turret** mounted on a **near-flush deck** — almost the entire hull rode at or just above the waterline. Her bizarre look earned the nickname **"a cheesebox on a raft."** The design reportedly contained **40 patentable inventions**.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "USS Monitor: A Cheesebox on a Raft"; Wikipedia, "USS Monitor"

- **Armament:** **Two 11-inch Dahlgren smoothbore guns** in the rotating turret. The turret was ~20 ft in diameter, ~9 ft high, with ~8 inches of iron.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"; Wikipedia, "USS Monitor"; American Battlefield Trust, "USS Monitor: A Cheesebox on a Raft"

- **Built astonishingly fast in Brooklyn:** Contract issued **October 4, 1861**, stipulating completion in **100 days**. She was built at the **Continental Iron Works, Greenpoint, Brooklyn, New York**, and **launched January 30, 1862** (often cited as built in ~98 days). Commissioned late February 1862.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "USS Monitor: A Cheesebox on a Raft"

- **The dramatic arrival:** The Monitor was towed down from New York and **arrived in Hampton Roads on the night of March 8, 1862** — the very evening after the Virginia's rampage — placing herself between the Virginia and the grounded Minnesota. Her commander was **Lieutenant John L. Worden (US)**. (Worden is often styled "Captain" in popular accounts; his rank was lieutenant.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "USS Monitor"; American Battlefield Trust, "USS Monitor: A Cheesebox on a Raft"

- **March 9, 1862 — the first ironclad battle in history:** On the morning of March 9, the Virginia (now under **Lt. Catesby ap Roger Jones**) came out to finish the Minnesota and met the Monitor instead. The two ironclads fought at close range for **about three to four hours** (~three hours of actual close combat in most accounts). Their shot largely bounced off each other; **neither could inflict significant damage** on the other. The Virginia even rammed the Monitor; both ran aground at points. The result was a **tactical draw.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"; Naval History and Heritage Command, "The Battle of Hampton Roads"

- **Worden blinded:** Late in the fight a shell from the Virginia struck the Monitor's **pilothouse** and exploded, driving paint and iron fragments through the viewing slits into **Worden's eyes, temporarily blinding him.** He turned command over to his executive officer, **Lieutenant Samuel Dana Greene (US)**, and the Monitor briefly sheered off; both ships eventually disengaged.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"; Wikipedia, "USS Monitor"; American Battlefield Trust, "USS Monitor: A Cheesebox on a Raft"

- **What it meant strategically:** Tactically a draw, but **strategically a Union success** — the Monitor checked the Virginia, the Virginia withdrew, the Minnesota was saved, and the **Union blockade fleet at Hampton Roads was preserved.** The Confederacy never broke the blockade there. And the duel instantly **rendered the world's wooden battle fleets obsolete** — every naval power took notice.
  — Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, "The Battle of Hampton Roads"; Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"

- **Lincoln's reaction to the Monitor design (verify as anecdote):** Lincoln, shown the radical turret concept, reportedly remarked, **"It strikes me there's something in it."** Treat as widely-repeated anecdote, not a verified-from-papers quote — see KEY QUOTES.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "USS Monitor: A Cheesebox on a Raft"

## BEAT 7: Aftermath — Both Ships Lost in 1862; the River Ironclads; the Global Arms Race

- **The Virginia's end (scuttled, May 11, 1862):** When Union forces advanced and the Confederates abandoned Norfolk in May 1862, the deep-draft Virginia (drawing ~22 ft) **could not escape up the shallow James River** toward Richmond. Rather than let her be captured, **Captain Josiah Tattnall III** ordered her destroyed; she was **scuttled and blown up on May 11, 1862, near Craney Island.** Her brief, world-changing career lasted only weeks.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "CSS Virginia"; Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"

- **The Monitor's end (foundered, December 31, 1862):** The Monitor's near-flush deck made her dangerously un-seaworthy in open water. Under tow off **Cape Hatteras, North Carolina**, she **foundered in a storm and sank on December 31, 1862**; **16 of her crew went down with her.** (Her wreck was located in 1973; the recovered turret is conserved at The Mariners' Museum, Newport News.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "USS Monitor"; Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"

- **The global naval arms race:** Hampton Roads was watched worldwide. It confirmed that armored, turreted, steam-powered warships had supplanted wooden sail/steam ships of the line — accelerating the ironclad arms race already begun in Europe (Britain's HMS *Warrior*, France's *Gloire*). "Monitor" became a ship-type: the U.S. and other navies built whole classes of low-freeboard turreted **monitors**. The age of wooden navies ended in two days.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Battle of Hampton Roads"; Naval History and Heritage Command, "The Battle of Hampton Roads"

- **CROSS-REFERENCE (brief) — the river ironclads:** The ironclad revolution was not only at Hampton Roads. On the western rivers, St. Louis industrialist **James B. Eads** built the **City-class ironclad gunboats** (designed by naval constructor **Samuel M. Pook**; nicknamed **"Pook's Turtles"** for their humped, awkward look). Eads won the contract at **$89,600 per vessel** to build **seven** boats; by late January 1862 all seven were delivered to the Army's Western Gunboat Flotilla. The seven: **Cairo, Carondelet, Cincinnati, Louisville, Mound City, Pittsburgh, and St. Louis** (later renamed Baron DeKalb). These shallow-draft ironclads were decisive on the Mississippi and its tributaries (Forts Henry and Donelson, Island No. 10, Memphis, Vicksburg). The **USS Cairo** has the grim distinction of being the **first ship ever sunk by a remotely (electrically) detonated naval mine ("torpedo")**, on **December 12, 1862**, on the Yazoo River — no lives lost. (The river battles themselves belong to the Western theatre, not this section.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "City-class ironclad"; Wikipedia, "USS Cairo"; NPS, "U.S.S. Cairo"

## CONTESTED / ESTIMATES

1. **Federal casualties on March 8 — exact ship-by-ship counts vary slightly by source.** Widely-cited figures: Cumberland ~121 killed (≈150 with wounded), out of ~376; Congress ~110 killed/missing + 26 wounded (10 of whom died), out of ~434. NHHC summarizes the day as "more than 240" killed on the two ships. Two-day Federal total commonly given as **261 killed, 108 wounded**. **Author guidance:** use "roughly 120 dead on the Cumberland, around 120 on the Congress, well over 240 killed in all" and cite the ~261/108 two-day total; don't present any single number as exact-to-the-man.

2. **Duration of the March 9 ironclad duel:** sources give "about three hours" (Wikipedia: ~3 hours of close action) up to "about four hours" (popular accounts counting maneuvering). **Author guidance:** "roughly three to four hours."

3. **Mallory's iron-ship statement — exact date:** most sources give **May 10, 1861** (to the Confederate Congress / Naval Affairs Committee); some give May 8, 1861, or an earlier April 26, 1861 letter to the chairman of the House Naval Affairs Committee with similar language. **Author guidance:** "in the spring of 1861, weeks into the war" is safe; if dating it, use May 10, 1861, and note the quote is well-attested. The quoted wording itself is consistently reported.

4. **Monitor build time — "98 days" vs "100 days":** the contract specified completion in 100 days from October 4, 1861; she launched January 30, 1862. "Built in about 100 days" / "less than 100 days" are both supportable. **Author guidance:** "built in roughly a hundred days."

5. **Navy size figures:** "42 ships in commission in 1861" is the standard figure (with ~48 more laid up); "~671 ships by war's end." End-of-1861 blockading vessels ≈160. These are round/standard figures, reliable enough to state plainly.

6. **Blockade effectiveness is genuinely DEBATED by historians.** The runner success rate ("five of six got through," "only 1 in 10 caught early") is real and is sometimes used to argue the blockade "failed." The counter-argument (and the mainstream view) is that the blockade's *deterrent* effect collapsed the *volume* of trade (cotton exports down ~95%), strangling the Southern economy regardless of individual runners' success. **Author guidance:** present BOTH — the runners' high success rate AND the 95% collapse in cotton exports — and frame the blockade as a slow economic chokehold, not a leak-proof wall. This tension IS the story; don't flatten it either way.

7. **"~3,000 miles" (Scott's figure) vs "~3,500 miles" (blockade as implemented):** both appear; the difference is Scott's 1861 estimate vs the full coastline once VA/NC seceded. Use ~3,500 miles for the blockade's actual scope.

8. **Worden's rank:** he was a **Lieutenant**, though popular and even some contemporary accounts call him "Captain" (the courtesy title for a ship's commanding officer). Pin him as **Lt. John L. Worden** to be precise; if "captain" is used, mean it as the role, not the rank.
