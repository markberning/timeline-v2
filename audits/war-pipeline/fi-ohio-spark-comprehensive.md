## Fact-Pack Blind Spots

These are gaps in the fact pack itself — items that every downstream agent (author, fact-checker, storytelling critic) would inherit if not flagged:

**BS1. The Logstown Treaty of 1752 (already flagged in the pack as a 404 gap).**
This is the most significant blind spot. Tanaghrisson attended Logstown in 1752 and helped broker a treaty where Ohio nations agreed to British settlement at the Forks in exchange for British protection. That treaty is why he felt entitled to demand British fort-building and why French occupation of the Forks was not just a European geopolitical move but a direct violation of an Indigenous diplomatic agreement he had helped make. The fact pack correctly flags it but no fallback information was compiled. Every agent downstream would write Tanaghrisson as a broker without understanding the specific promise that was broken.

**BS2. What the Shawnee and Delaware (Lenape) ACTUALLY DID in 1754 — not just their leanings.**
The pack correctly notes they "leaned toward France" but doesn't document whether they participated militarily at Fort Necessity or were already raiding British settlements by late 1754. The Delaware's alignment decision had immediate military consequences (Braddock Road raids, 1755). Knowing whether they were already active combatants or still formally neutral in 1754 changes how the closing "Ohio nations as independent actors" section reads.

**BS3. The European diplomatic chain that turned a colonial skirmish into the Seven Years' War.**
The pack documents the North American military story thoroughly but provides no facts about how France used the Fort Necessity casus belli to activate European alliances — specifically the Diplomatic Revolution of 1756 (France-Austria alliance, Britain-Prussia alliance). The "world on fire" claim needs at least a skeleton of this mechanism to be defensible. Currently the author can state the claim but can't explain the mechanism.

**BS4. King George II's reaction to Washington's "bullets charming" letter (flagged as unverified in the pack).**
The pack correctly marks this as unverified. However, it's a famous enough anecdote that readers may expect it. The pack's advice — treat as unverified — is right, but a downstream agent might still write it without the hedge. The pack should recommend omitting entirely or noting the king reportedly dismissed it.

**BS5. Hendrick (Tiyanoga) — the Mohawk chief who declared the Covenant Chain broken.**
The pack names him in the cast but doesn't give the narrative: Hendrick's specific 1753 speech to New York officials ("the Covenant Chain is broken"), his role at the Albany Congress, and his death at Lake George in 1755. He is a first-class historical figure whose arc connects the Albany Congress failure directly to the early British military disasters. A downstream agent writing about Albany Congress without knowing Hendrick's story will underwrite the Native diplomatic dimension.

**BS6. The exact French diplomatic rationale post-Fort Necessity.**
The pack documents that France used "l'assassinat" as a casus belli but doesn't source or describe the French government's formal communications. A downstream agent would have to write the France-weaponizes-the-document section on inference.

## MUST-ADD (Blocking — essential gaps)

**MA1. Tanaghrisson's critique of Washington at Fort Necessity — "that little thing upon the meadow" — and his stated reason for withdrawal ("Washington would not take advice").**
This is the most memorable thing Tanaghrisson said about Washington and it closes his arc in the draft. Currently the draft reports he "withdrew his warriors" without his voice. He was a major character throughout; he deserves a line of direct speech at the exit. The phrase also lands the theme: Native agency was not just about the killing in the glen — it was about a strategic actor who saw clearly that Washington was going to lose and chose not to die for it. **Currently THIN, should be COVERED.**

**MA2. Whether the Albany Congress actually repaired the Covenant Chain (it didn't, fully), and what that meant for the war ahead.**
The draft presents the Congress as having "two jobs" but reports only on the Plan of Union failure. The Iroquois diplomacy result is unresolved in the narrative. One sentence: "The Iroquois left Albany only partly satisfied — land grievances were acknowledged but not remedied — and the Six Nations' formal neutrality would persist into 1755, leaving the British without their most powerful North American ally at the worst moment." This closes the Albany section's second job.

**MA3. The mechanism connecting Fort Necessity's "assassinat" document to formal war declaration.**
The draft says France had "exactly the justification it wanted" but a reader is left asking: and then what? One sentence connecting the document to France's formal escalation (declared war 1756, Diplomatic Revolution in Europe) closes the "how did this light the world" loop and makes the "global war" claim in the opening and closing sections feel earned rather than asserted.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER (Non-blocking enrichments)

**SC1. Tanaghrisson's treatment by the French during the 1753 mission — the contempt scene.**
The French commanders during Washington's 1753 trip told Tanaghrisson directly that he had no authority over the Ohio Country. The draft mentions "he was living near the Forks as something close to a refugee" and that the French "treated him with contempt" but the concrete humiliation (a named French officer dismissing his claim to speak for Ohio peoples, during the 1753 trip) would make his motive visceral rather than analytical.

**SC2. Virginia's non-attendance at the Albany Congress.**
The colony most directly at war with France — Virginia — sent no delegates. This irony tightens the "unready" argument considerably.

**SC3. The Logstown Treaty of 1752.**
Even a single sentence noting that in 1752 Tanaghrisson helped broker a treaty at Logstown granting British rights to build at the Forks — and that the French were directly violating that agreement — gives his rage more grounded logic.

**SC4. The fate of the two hostages (Van Braam and Stobo).**
Van Braam held for years; Stobo passing intelligence to Braddock, condemned to death, later pardoned. A sentence on each closes the human cost.

**SC5. The Shawnee and Delaware in 1754 — what they actually did (vs. just "leaned toward France").**
Even "both nations formally remained neutral through 1754 but would align with France in 1755" would sharpen the "independent actors" argument in the closing section.

**SC6. The name "Hendrick" for the Mohawk chief at Albany.**
Currently "Mohawk leaders" is generic. Hendrick is a significant named figure.

## Summary Verdict

**MUST-ADD items: 3**

The single most important omission is **MA1 — Tanaghrisson's "that little thing upon the meadow" exit line and his stated reason for withdrawing.** He is the draft's most original analytical subject; the draft introduces him brilliantly, explains his motives fully, and then lets him exit silently. His own words at the end complete the arc. Everything else in this draft is well-told — this is the one place where Native agency is given the analysis but not the voice.
