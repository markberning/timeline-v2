# FRAMING CRITIC — Siege of Charleston draft (rev-charleston)

*Critic pass 2026-06-12 against `rev-charleston-draft.md` (all 331 lines, incl. FACT LEDGER
and compliance lists) and `rev-charleston-factpack.md` (sole source). Scope: fairness,
emphasis, interpretive honesty. Cross-checked all handle-with-care surfaces against the
pack's DISPUTED register (§7 / Known Uncertainties) and the APOCRYPHA BLACKLIST (§8).*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

No blocker. The hardest framing duties are largely met: Lincoln is treated as the trap's
victim, not its architect; Clinton's siegecraft is rendered as professional competence, not
arrogance; the Yorktown echo is set up cleanly; the honored-refused humiliation lands with
weight, not melodrama; Gadsden's council trap is documented politics, not villainy. But two
should-fixes matter: enslaved people appear in a single demographic headcount and nowhere
else, despite the pack documenting their presence in the city and Lincoln's documented attempt
to arm them for the defense; and the June 3 proclamation's role in the partisan war is stated
as near-certainty where the pack's own framing calls for "standardly credited with helping
ignite" — a cause, not THE cause. One blocker: the prison-hulk death toll (one in three
over the following year) is given two sentences in a body that can carry them, but the
paragraph pivots quickly to named officers and the tone of the hulks section works against
the weight the pack explicitly assigns to it. Total: 1 BLOCKER, 2 SHOULD-FIX, 2 POLISH.

## BLOCKER

**1. The prison-hulk passage undersells the death toll and pivots to officers before the
scale registers.**

Section 3, the hulks paragraph:

> "over the following year, roughly 800 of about 2,500 Continental prisoners died in those
> hulks, of disease and heat and starvation, about one in three."

The one-in-three figure is correct and the pack endorses it. But the paragraph immediately
follows this with: "Hundreds more escaped the hulks only by enlisting in British regiments...
The officers sat in crowded parole at Haddrell's Point, where Brigadier General James Hogun
(American) died that January; Woodford, the Virginian who had marched in just in time to be
captured, died on a British prison ship in New York harbor in November."

The structural problem: the ratio (one in three) is carried as a bare number — faster than
the explosion paragraph, which spent five full sentences on a smaller death toll. The pack's
own guidance for the explosion gives it the "closing note" weight ("the right closing note for
it") and the comparison is earned with Ewald, Peebles, and the physical sequence. The hulks
killed roughly four times as many people as the explosion, over a year of systematic neglect,
but the draft gives them one clause and then pivots upward to named officers — a structural
movement that reads as escape from the uncomfortable material. The pack says this section
should carry the toll "with weight but no atrocity-porn." One sentence of weight-giving prose
between the ratio and the officer pivot would meet that standard.

FIX: After "about one in three," add one sentence that keeps the reader with the privates
before moving to officers. Something like: "These were the regulars who had held Charleston's
lines for six weeks; a third of them did not leave the harbor." Then continue with "Hundreds
more escaped..." The tone is documentary, not atrocity-porn. The beat earns its place next to
the explosion paragraph.

## SHOULD-FIX

**2. Enslaved Carolinians appear in one demographic sentence and then vanish from the story.**

Section 1 correctly includes this line in the background:

> "Take Charleston and the whole door to the South stood open."

And the dossier correctly introduces the city's population: "roughly half of them enslaved."

Lincoln's attempt to arm enslaved men is documented in the political-trap paragraph in Section 2:

> "(Lincoln had also pleaded with the South Carolina authorities to arm enslaved men for the
> defense, as John Laurens had proposed the year before; the state refused outright.)"

This is correct and present. But the pack's background explicitly frames Charleston as a city
where roughly half the population was enslaved, and the city-fathers' council that trapped
Lincoln included the men who owned much of that population and whose calculation about
"abandoning the city" was inseparable from what abandoning it meant for the system that made
it rich. The political trap is not just "we won't let you leave because we love Charleston"
— it is at least partly "we will not let the army leave because British occupation of our city
without us here is a different kind of catastrophe for our specific property." The draft
treats the parenthetical on arming enslaved men as a social-conscience note rather than a
narrative thread connected to the same political trap it is sitting inside.

Additionally: once the city falls, enslaved Carolinians disappear entirely. British
occupation changed the calculation for enslaved people in Charleston (some fled to British
lines, as they did across the southern theater; the pack does not address this but does not
forbid a sentence). At minimum, the city's enslaved majority should not vanish from the story
the moment the walls come down when that majority was structurally present in every political
decision described.

FIX: Two moves. First, move the arming-enslaved-men note from a parenthetical tail at the
end of the political-trap paragraph to a full sentence in the same paragraph that names it as
connected to the council dynamics: the same government that refused to let the army leave also
refused to arm enslaved men to help defend the city, and the pack draws both from Lincoln's
documented pleading. Second, add one sentence in the aftermath section (before or after the
Gadsden Meanwhile) noting what British occupation meant for the city's enslaved population
— even one clause framed with appropriate hedge: "For the roughly six thousand enslaved
Carolinians in the city, the British occupation opened a different calculation: some would
reach British lines over the following months." The pack does not provide this directly, so
frame it with the hedge the pack's confidence level warrants, or omit it if the reviser
cannot source it — but the current erasure is a framing problem, not just an omission.

**3. The June 3 proclamation is framed as cause rather than a contributing cause.**

Section 3:

> "The proclamation is standardly credited with helping ignite the partisan war, the war of
> the backcountry bands under leaders like Sumter and Marion and Pickens, that ate the British
> conquest from the inside over the next year."

This sentence is word-for-word correct and preserves the hedged "standardly credited." But
the surrounding sentences frame the proclamation's mechanism as something close to
determinative:

> "Clinton had disarmed the rebellion and then, with one piece of paper, handed the war back
> to the people he had just beaten."

And the dossier's outcome text:

> "the victory that won Britain a city handed the rebellion back its soldiers, and lost it a
> countryside."

The pack's own framing (§6) says: "the proclamation is standardly credited with helping
ignite the partisan war." The draft preserves that hedge in the middle of Section 3 but the
sentences before and after it do not preserve it: "with one piece of paper, handed the war
back" and the dossier's "lost it a countryside" set up the proclamation as the turning point
of the whole southern war, not one of several causes. The actual ignition of the partisan war
involved the defeat at Camden (August), the British post-Waxhaws brutality reputation, the
forfeiture of Loyalist credibility in the backcountry, and a dozen local grievances the
pack acknowledges in §6's reference to "Sumter, Marion, Pickens." The proclamation was real
and its contribution was real, but the draft elevates it above the pack's confidence level
at the sentence level while preserving the hedge at the clause level.

FIX: Revise the "one piece of paper" sentence to restore the pack's hedge internally, not
just in the adjacent clause. E.g.: "Clinton had disarmed the rebellion; one piece of paper
helped arm it again." Drop "with one piece of paper, handed the war back" — that framing
assigns a singular causal arrow the pack does not authorize. The dossier outcome note can
keep "lost it a countryside" as an outcome judgment (which the pack endorses) without
implying the proclamation alone caused it.

## POLISH

**4. Clinton's departure framing implies the proclamation was born of hubris, not
Clinton's genuine strategic judgment.**

Section 3:

> "Clinton himself sailed for New York in early June with about a third of the army, worried
> about a French fleet on the northern coast, and left Cornwallis behind with some 8,300 men
> and the whole conquered South to hold."

This is accurate. But it immediately follows the proclamation paragraph, and the juxtaposition
— "here is the proclamation that restarted the war; now here is Clinton leaving believing
he'd won" — creates an implicit ironic framing that reads as: he issued a disastrous order
and then sailed away, apparently oblivious. The pack frames Clinton as a careful professional
who "sailed home believing the South was won" (§3) — genuinely, not foolishly. The French
fleet concern was real. The pack asks for Clinton as competent, not perfidious. The current
paragraph ordering creates a structure that lets the sequence do ironic work the pack does
not endorse.

FIX: One sentence or a comma clause that names Clinton's departure as a genuine strategic
call, not a retreat-from-consequences. E.g.: "Clinton sailed north not because he thought the
South was fragile — he genuinely believed it was secured — but because a French fleet in New
York waters was the larger danger." The pack's framing is: a careful soldier making a
reasonable call, who happened to be wrong about what the proclamation would ignite.

**5. The Yorktown echo closing is right in tone but risks feeling pre-ordained.**

The section ends:

> "Colors cased. The march denied. The most precise piece of bookkeeping in the entire war,
> and the man who gave up Charleston was standing there to collect it."

This is excellent and earns its place. It does not gloat and it does not editorialize beyond
what the pack explicitly authorizes. But "the most precise piece of bookkeeping in the entire
war" is a judgment the draft should probably attribute or hedge slightly — not because it is
wrong, but because the pack does not offer this exact phrasing as a historical verdict, and
the sentence functions as a narrator's climactic summary. It works emotionally; the framing
risk is small (it does not assert inevitability, it characterizes the act). This is a polish
note, not a fix.

FIX (optional): If the reviser wants to reduce the narrator-verdict footprint here, change
"the most precise" to "the most deliberate" or "a precise" — the superlative is the only
thing that puts the narrator's thumb on the scale. The quoted Washington order before it is
sufficient; the closer does not need to editorialize it.

## CHECKED AND PASSING (for the reviser's confidence)

- **Lincoln not scapegoated or whitewashed:** "His defeat was at least as much political as
  military" is the exact pack framing (§6). The political-trap mechanics — Gadsden, Ferguson's
  threat, the same men who kept him in then begged him to surrender — are all carried. Congress
  and Washington's treatment of him (exchanged in six months, never charged, restored to
  Washington's side) is documented. The Yorktown receiving is named as the answer, not a
  reward. Lincoln is treated fairly, as the pack instructs.

- **City-fathers' council trap as documented politics, not villainy:** Gadsden's arc is the
  "political trap personified" — introduced fairly (the rattlesnake-flag radical who loved
  the city) and closed fairly (ten months in a dungeon, his own terms, his own reckoning).
  Ferguson's threat is framed as reported speech via McIntosh's journal, not verbatim
  quotation, exactly as the pack instructs. The citizens are neither heroes nor villains:
  they are people trapped by what they valued.

- **Clinton's siegecraft as competence:** "taking the city by arithmetic" and "he had not
  put a foot wrong" are correct characterizations that match the pack's framing of Clinton
  as careful and methodical. The "master of it" line for siege engineering in Section 2 is
  supported. The 1776 humiliation is named as the motivation for his care, not as a taunt.
  No perfidious-Albion framing appears anywhere.

- **Honors refused as humiliation, not melodrama:** The surrender section names the mechanism
  (colors cased, no British march, Turkish march by Moultrie's account) and calls it "a
  calculated humiliation, the formal denial of the courtesies owed a respected foe." That is
  the pack's register: deliberate, cold, professional. No overwrought language. The Yorktown
  echo is set up with the Washington quotation in his own hand — the pack's highest-confidence
  source for that close.

- **"Tarleton's quarter" firewall held:** Tarleton appears at Monck's Corner and Lenud's Ferry
  as a fast, ruthless, effective cavalry officer. The Waxhaws is handoff-only: "Tarleton ran
  them down. What happened there is a story this page does not tell." No massacre content,
  no "Bloody Ban" or "Butcher," no Waxhaws atrocity description. Blacklist items 1 and 2 are
  clean.

- **Captured-count framing honest everywhere (dossier included):** The dossier, the stats
  block, the section-3 prose, and the fact ledger all use "more than 5,000... about half of
  them regulars" with the Clinton-return / Borick effective-Continental breakdown in the
  footnote. The clean "5,466 soldiers" figure never appears. The dossier winner note uses the
  correct framing. Blacklist item 5 is clean.

- **No worst-defeat + redemption-was-coming arc:** The draft does not set up Charleston as
  the nadir on a trajectory to victory. The outcome note says "for a summer it was" — total —
  and then traces the realistic mechanism (the proclamation, the partisan war) without
  implying that redemption was structurally guaranteed. The southern-turn pill is a handoff,
  not an "and then they bounced back" promise. Camden is named as a second blow ("the lowest
  point of the American cause anywhere") before the recovery arc is gestured. The inevitability
  trap is avoided at the macro level.

- **May 15 explosion weight and no atrocity-porn:** The explosion gets five full sentences with
  the cause sequence (ignored warnings, the misfire, the chain reaction), two named eyewitnesses,
  and the closing comparison (more than the whole siege). The tone is documentary. No corpse
  inventory, no graphic staging beyond what Ewald records. The pack's "atrocity-porn" threshold
  is not crossed; the weight is earned.

- **Blacklist clean:** Items 3 (eloquent contemporary verdict / "severest blow") dropped;
  item 4 (bombardment annihilation) not present; item 6 (Sgt. Jasper) handled correctly —
  the 1776 flashback names the palmetto log fort and Moultrie's flag without the Jasper
  flag-rescue story; item 7 (Bataan comparison) absent; item 8 ("Long live Congress") absent.
