## Passage-Level Assessment

### CLEAR passages
- Opening two paragraphs: excellent framing, no jargon, sets both faces of the war cleanly
- Three-kind taxonomy section: very clear, the distinctions actually stick
- Fort Pillow scene-setting: geography, garrison composition, chain of command all given
- Casualty arithmetic explanation: brilliant — the "asymmetry is the massacre" framing is both clear and devastating
- Achilles V. Clark quote + framing: perfect, the "a Confederate writing to his own family" setup does real work
- Mosby section up to the Front Royal episode: clear contrast with bushwhackers
- Order No. 11 moral balance paragraph: clear and honest

### NEEDS-GLOSS passages
- "Bleeding Kansas" intro (border war section): the phrase is named and dated but the *why it matters* is gesturally waved at ("inherited, fully loaded, the hatreds"). A newcomer gets the gist but the connection to slavery as the driver is implicit, not stated. See #2.
- The MEANWHILE HANDOFF: contains habeas corpus, Copperheads, and "election of 1864 held while the war still raged" — all useful teaser but habeas corpus especially is opaque to a newcomer. See #4 and #5.

### LOST passages
- None. No passage lost this reader.

### Passages that overwhelm (density risk)
- The Lieber Code paragraph is dense but the payoff is clear enough that it holds. No action required.
- The "pattern, not a single day" final section adds Poison Spring, Saltville, and the Crater in quick succession. A newcomer might lose track of which event is which. Not a breaker, but the section is aware of the risk and keeps each to one sentence — acceptable.

## SHOULD-FIX (Real Gaps, Not Comprehension Breakers)

**#1 — Klan: zero setup before the "Grand Wizard" section**
The Klan section drops "fraternity" and "curdled into something else" but never says in plain language what the Klan *was* — a white-supremacist terror organization that used violence to prevent Black people from exercising their legal rights. A newcomer gets "Grand Wizard = supreme national leader" but not what that organization's actual purpose and method were before the section explains the violence. One sentence up front: "The Ku Klux Klan was a white-supremacist secret society that used beatings, arson, and murder to terrorize Black Southerners and white Republicans, keeping them away from the polls and out of public life." The section then earns everything it says. Without it, the newcomer must wait two paragraphs to understand what Forrest led.

**#2 — "Bleeding Kansas" handwave**
"The years of pre-war violence, roughly 1854 to 1859, over whether Kansas would enter the Union as a slave state or a free one" — told elsewhere, referenced here. Fine. But the sentence "By the time the war proper began, the Kansas–Missouri line already had armed free-state 'jayhawkers' on one side and pro-slavery 'border ruffians' on the other" introduces "border ruffians" without defining it. A newcomer could confuse it with "bushwhacker." One-word fix: add "(pro-slavery Missouri raiders)" after "border ruffians" on first use.

**#3 — "furlough" undefined**
"24 unarmed Union soldiers — men on furlough" — a newcomer may not know furlough means authorized leave from duty, i.e., these men were off duty and heading home. Add "(authorized leave — heading home between postings)" parenthetically.

**#4 — "habeas corpus" in the MEANWHILE HANDOFF**
"Lincoln was suspending habeas corpus" — a newcomer has no idea what this means. It's in a handoff teaser so one brief gloss suffices: "habeas corpus (the legal right not to be jailed without a formal charge or trial)."

**#5 — "Copperheads" in the MEANWHILE HANDOFF**
Named but not identified beyond "Northern opposition." Since this handoff is the reader's first encounter, add a one-phrase gloss: "the 'Copperheads' — Northern Democrats who opposed the war and called Lincoln a tyrant." The next section will flesh it out, but the handoff should not leave the word hanging.

**#6 — "mustered into service"**
"Eighteen of twenty-three army recruits who had not yet even been mustered into service" — a newcomer does not know "mustered in" means formally sworn in and enrolled as soldiers. Add "(officially sworn in and enrolled as soldiers)" parenthetically.

**#7 — "Appomattox"**
Used twice in the legacy section as shorthand for the war's end — "past the official end" and "before the guns even fall silent" are clear, but "after Appomattox" and "at Appomattox" in the Mosby afterlife para assume the newcomer knows this is the surrender ceremony. The word appears in the Mosby section ("before Appomattox," "after Appomattox") without any gloss. Add "(where Lee surrendered in April 1865, effectively ending the war)" on first use in the section.

**#8 — "Reconstruction"**
The conclusion uses "Reconstruction" as if the reader knows what it was. This is the reader's first encounter with the word in this app context (the Reconstruction section presumably covers it, but a newcomer reading this chapter doesn't know that yet). Add a one-phrase gloss: "Reconstruction (the postwar period, 1865–1877, when the federal government tried to rebuild the South and protect Black civil rights)."

**#9 — "Red Legs" undefined beyond the name**
Named as "a related Union irregular band on the Kansas side." A newcomer is left wondering if they're a distinct organization or a sub-unit of the jayhawkers. One clause: "the 'Red Legs' (a Kansas Union irregular band named for the red-leather leggings they wore, known for particularly savage anti-Confederate raids)." If that level of detail is not verified, at minimum clarify they were a distinct Kansas pro-Union raiding band, not synonymous with jayhawkers.

**#10 — Shenandoah Valley**
Dropped in the Mosby section without geographic context: "Union forces under the command of Philip Sheridan and George Custer executed six of Mosby's captured men…at Front Royal, Virginia." A newcomer does not know where Front Royal is or what Sheridan was doing there. One clause: "during Sheridan's Union campaign to destroy the Shenandoah Valley (the rich agricultural corridor running through western Virginia that fed the Confederate army)."

**#11 — "Trans-Mississippi"**
"The Black battle cry became 'Remember Poison Springs!' in the Trans-Mississippi" — a newcomer does not know this means the Confederate and Union military theater west of the Mississippi River. Add "(the military theater west of the Mississippi River)" parenthetically.

**#12 — "pickets"**
"raids on Union outposts, supply lines, and pickets" — a newcomer may not know pickets are small guards posted at the outer edges of a camp to watch for enemy approach. Add "(small guard posts at a camp's outer edge)" or replace with "guard posts."

**#13 — Wade and Gooch identified by party/allegiance, not explicitly**
"hard-line Republicans" is stated in the same sentence, which is sufficient context. Actually CLEAR on re-read — no fix needed, striking this.

**#14 — Cole Younger, Frank and Jesse James: no side tag or scene-set on first mention**
In the Quantrill paragraph: "Cole Younger, William T. 'Bloody Bill' Anderson, and a pair of farm boys named Frank and Jesse James" — Anderson gets "(Confederate guerrilla)" later when he's reintroduced as a section subject, but Younger and the James brothers get nothing. A newcomer doesn't know who they are or why the names matter. The prose does say they "rode with Quantrill," which establishes Confederate-guerrilla side implicitly, but the names are dropped with the gravity of household names (because they are — to someone who knows American history). For a true newcomer: add "(Confederate guerrillas, and the men who will become America's most famous outlaws)" after "Frank and Jesse James" on this first mention.

**#15 — Sheridan and Custer: no side tag**
"Union forces under the command of Philip Sheridan and George Custer" — "Union forces" tags them by association but the draft should add "(Union)" or "(both Union generals)" since these names will appear again and a newcomer should have the side tag locked. Small fix, high clarity value.

**#16 — Henry C. Magruder: zero context**
"some sources add a third name — the Kentucky guerrilla Henry C. Magruder, also executed." No side given (Confederate, presumably), no date, no brief note on his crimes. Since he's introduced only to hedge the "only two" claim, a brief "(a Confederate Kentucky guerrilla, also executed for war crimes in 1865)" would serve.

**#17 — John Hunt Morgan: no side tag**
"men like Nathan Bedford Forrest and John Hunt Morgan" in the raiding-warfare taxonomy. Forrest is given the full treatment but Morgan just appears with no tag. Add "(Confederate)" on first use.

**#18 — Robert E. Lee: no side tag**
"Robert E. Lee among the loudest" opponents of the Partisan Ranger Act. A newcomer encountering Lee for the first time in this app will know the name from popular culture, but the house rule is side-tag every named person on first mention. Add "(Confederate)" or "(the Confederacy's top general)" parenthetically.
