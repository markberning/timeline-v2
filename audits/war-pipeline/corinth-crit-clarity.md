# Corinth draft — NEWCOMER/CLARITY critic gate (war pipeline, step 4)

> Read COLD as a sharp 15-year-old with ZERO Civil War knowledge. Flag every
> undefined term/jargon, unexplained leap, named actor lacking side-tag/scene-set,
> and overwhelming passage. Verdict per item: CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST.
> Findings only — no edits to the draft or app files, no commit.

## Verdict at a glance

The draft is **strong on the big "why"** — it defines its own object (railroad)
vs reason (slavery) up front, glosses "contraband" humanely, and explains
double-envelopment, redan, parapet-ditch in plain terms. The clarity gaps are
almost all in the **military-org and fortification vocabulary** dropped without a
first-use gloss, and a few **named generals introduced with no scene-set** beyond a
side-tag. Nothing here is structurally LOST to a motivated newcomer, but several
NEEDS-GLOSS items will make a 15-year-old stop and re-read.

## MUST-FIX (a cold newcomer cannot follow without these)

### 1. "old outer works" / "the old works" — NEEDS-GLOSS (line 27 heading, 30, 34, 44)
The very section title is "Driving the Federals back through the old works."
"Works" as a noun meaning fortifications/entrenchments is pure military jargon a
newcomer does not own. The draft does eventually unpack it ("the entrenchments
Beauregard's men had thrown up," line 30), but the **title and the first body use
land before that gloss**, and "works" recurs unglossed (line 34 "old outer works,"
line 44 "outer works"). FIX: gloss "works" = field fortifications/dug-in defense
lines at first contact, ideally before or inside the section heading payoff.

### 2. "earthwork" / "earthwork forts" — NEEDS-GLOSS (line 44, 55, 61)
Central to the whole second day and the casualty lesson ("throwing infantry against
fortified earthworks," line 61), but never defined. A newcomer pictures... dirt?
FIX: one clause on first use (line 44, "five earthwork forts") — earthworks =
defensive walls built of piled-up dirt, cheap and fast and very hard to take.
This also retroactively makes "works" (item 1) click.

### 3. "battery / batteries" — NEEDS-GLOSS (line 44, 55, 59, 63, and the section/heading names)
Used heavily and in TWO senses, neither defined:
  - a *unit* of cannon ("the close-in 'College Hill Line' of batteries," line 44;
    "the crossfire of the batteries," line 59), and
  - a *named fortification* ("Battery Robinett," "Battery Powell" — these are the
    section title and the climactic set-piece).
A newcomer reading "Battery Robinett" with no gloss does not know if that's a place,
a person, or a thing. FIX: at first use, gloss battery = an emplacement of cannon
(and note the forts are named "Battery X" because that's what they were). This is
load-bearing — it's literally the title of section 3.

### 4. "redan" — NEEDS-GLOSS, but currently borderline OK (line 55)
The draft DOES define it inline ("a *redan* — an arrowhead-shaped earthwork, its
point aimed out at the enemy"). That gloss is good. KEEP. Only flagged here because
it depends on "earthwork" (item 2) already being defined; if item 2 is fixed,
redan is CLEAR. As written, redan is glossed but rests on an unglossed word.

### 5. Generals introduced with side-tag but NO scene-set — NEEDS-GLOSS (line 32)
The brief asks me to flag named actors lacking scene-set. Line 32 fires four
brigadier generals in one sentence — McKean, Davies, Hamilton, Stanley — each
tagged (North) and given a position (left/center/right/reserve) but with zero
sense of who they are or scale. For a newcomer this is a wall of names. It's
*tolerable* because the draft immediately frames them positionally (left/center/
right), which is the only thing the reader needs to track the battle. Borderline
MUST/SHOULD. FIX (light): a single framing clause that these are Rosecrans's
subordinate commanders each holding one stretch of the line — so the names read as
"the three doors Van Dorn is trying to kick in," not as people to remember.

## SHOULD-FIX (a sharp reader infers it, but it costs a beat)

### 6. "divisions of brigades of regiments" / "the standard ladder of a Civil War army" — NEEDS-GLOSS (line 31, also "corps" not present but division/brigade/regiment are)
Line 31 names the hierarchy ("divisions of brigades of regiments") and even calls
it "the standard ladder," which is a nice signal — but it never says how big each
rung is. A newcomer has no feel for whether a brigade is 50 men or 50,000. FIX: one
parenthetical scale anchor at first use — e.g. a regiment is ~hundreds of men,
brigades group regiments, divisions group brigades — so the troop numbers elsewhere
(22,000; 23,000) have a frame. NOTE: the brief lists "corps" and "salient" as terms
to watch — neither appears in this draft, so no action; "parapet" and "battery" and
"redan" DO appear (see items 3, 4, 8).

### 7. "parapet" — NEEDS-GLOSS, mild (line 55, 63, 65 context)
"clawed up the parapet" / "the top of the parapet" / "planted the colors" — a
newcomer can infer from context (you climb up it, you fight on top of it) that it's
the wall/top edge of the earthwork, and the draft helps with "on top of the
earthwork itself." Borderline CLEAR via context. FIX (optional): one word at first
use — parapet = the protective top wall of the fortification — since it's the exact
spot the climax happens on.

### 8. "20-pounder Parrott rifles" — NEEDS-GLOSS, partially handled (line 55)
The draft glosses it ("heavy field cannon") which is the key thing — GOOD, KEEP.
But "rifles" meaning cannon (not handheld rifles) may briefly confuse a newcomer
who just read about muskets. The parenthetical "heavy field cannon" mostly saves
it. Borderline CLEAR. Optional: "rifled cannon" to disambiguate from infantry
rifles. Low priority.

### 9. "rail junction" / "depot" / "garrison" — mostly CLEAR (line 15, 19, 30, 76)
  - "rail junction" / "railroad junction" (line 21, 78): the opening paragraph
    thoroughly explains two railroads crossing at one point, so junction is CLEAR
    by the time it's used as a shorthand.
  - "depot" (line 15, 30, 88): used as "the town depot" / "the depot" = the rail
    station. A newcomer likely infers it. Borderline CLEAR; optional one-word touch.
  - "garrison" (line 19, 21, 30, 76): "the Corinth garrison," "his garrison" =
    the troops occupying/defending the town. Context carries it (Rosecrans is
    "holding Corinth itself with around 23,000"). CLEAR by context, but a first-use
    gloss would be cheap insurance. SHOULD-FIX (low).

### 10. "double envelopment" — CLEAR (line 32)
Glossed inline ("hitting both ends of the Union line at once to fold it inward").
Good plain gloss. KEEP, no action.

### 11. "contraband / contrabands" — CLEAR, and well done (line 82)
The brief specifically flagged this as needing a humane gloss given the camp's
centrality. The draft delivers: "a clumsy wartime term for enslaved people who
crossed into Union lines and would not be sent back," plus "would not be sent back"
carries the humanity, and the surrounding prose ("freeing themselves by walking
into Union-held Corinth," "had been somebody's legal property a year earlier")
makes the dignity explicit. This is CLEAR and is the model the other glosses should
match. No action — hold the line on this one.

### 12. "high-water mark" — CLEAR via apposition (line 53)
"the high-water mark of the whole Confederate effort, a captured Union fort and its
guns in Confederate hands" — the apposition defines the metaphor in place. CLEAR.

### 13. "court of inquiry" / "superseded" / "kicked upstairs" (line 69) — CLEAR via context
"cleared Van Dorn of charges," "superseded all the same — command in Mississippi
passed to Pemberton," and "kicked upstairs to command the army" all self-define by
their immediate context. A sharp 15-year-old gets these. No action.

## Unexplained leaps / scene-set gaps

### 14. The Iuka MEANWHILE assumes the reader tracks two separate Confederate armies — SHOULD-FIX (line 23)
Sidebar says Price's "Army of the West" joined Van Dorn so "the two Confederate
armies that had been chasing around northern Mississippi became one." But the main
text (line 21) only introduced ONE Confederate force (Van Dorn's ~22,000, "Army of
West Tennessee"). A newcomer hits "the two Confederate armies that had been chasing
around" and asks: *which two? when were there two?* The windup/punch metaphor is
great, but the "two armies → one" needs the reader to already know Price was a
separate command. FIX (light): one clause making explicit that Price commanded a
SECOND, separate Southern army before the merge.

### 15. "Grant's forces in the region were scattered" — Grant appears before being set (line 21)
Grant is named ("Grant's forces in the region") and tagged later, but at line 21
he's dropped in as a known quantity — the reader hasn't been told Grant is the
overall Union commander in this theater (vs. Rosecrans who holds Corinth, vs.
Halleck who took it in the spring). The Halleck → Grant handoff is never stated.
A newcomer can be mildly confused about the Union chain of command (who outranks
whom: Halleck, Grant, Rosecrans). SHOULD-FIX: one clause establishing Grant as the
regional Union commander over Rosecrans. (Note: Grant IS side-tagged at line 59/78,
but his FIRST mention at line 21 is untagged — see item 17.)

### 16. "the same campaign" (line 23) — minor, CLEAR-ish
The Iuka sidebar calls it "the opening move of the same campaign." A newcomer hasn't
been told this is a multi-battle *campaign* with a name (the draft later calls Iuka
and Corinth "the last real Confederate offensives," line 78). Mildly underset but
the windup/punch framing rescues it. Low priority.

## Overwhelming-passage check

- **Line 21 (Van Dorn's logic / the dispersal figures):** five separate force
  figures (12k / 7k / 6k / 23k / 22k) plus four place names plus three generals in
  one paragraph. This is the densest passage in the draft. A newcomer can wade
  through it because the *point* (Grant's forces are scattered, Van Dorn wants to
  hit before they gather) is stated plainly first — but it's right at the upper
  limit. SHOULD-FIX: consider trimming to the two figures that matter (Rosecrans's
  ~23k in town vs Van Dorn's ~22k) and folding the rest into "the rest of Grant's
  forces were spread across the region." Not LOST, just heavy.
- **Line 32 (four generals + positions + double-envelopment + two Confederate
  division commanders):** dense, but positionally framed (left/center/right), so a
  newcomer tracks the geometry even if the names blur. See item 5. Acceptable with
  the light scene-set fix.
- Everything else reads at a comfortable pace. The MEANWHILE sidebars are well-sized
  and actually RELIEVE density by parking side-threads (Iuka, the Hatchie retreat,
  the railroad's meaning) out of the main spine.
