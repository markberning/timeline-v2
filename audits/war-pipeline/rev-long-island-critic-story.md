# STORYTELLING CRITIC · The Battle of Long Island (rev-long-island)

Draft: `audits/war-pipeline/rev-long-island-draft.md`
Bar: `audits/war-pipeline/fi-carillon-draft.md` (shipped, gated)
Lane: story only. Accuracy is the fact critic's lane; nothing below changes a fact.

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is a strong draft. The arc the brief asks for is all here: the harbor fills,
the back door is planted at the exact right moment (the last line of prose in
section 1), the trap springs with real rhythm, and section 3 earns its miracle
without ever using the word — "The British woke on the 30th to empty trenches"
is as good a beat as anything in Carillon. The British are allowed to be
brilliant. The voice ship-blockers are clean: I found no em-dashes in our prose,
no drama-bold, and no structural meta-narration.

What keeps it from a clean PASS is two things, both fixable without touching a
fact: (1) the draft never once puts the reader INSIDE the event the way
Carillon's "Imagine being one of them" abatis passage does — the marsh swim and
the embarkation, the two natural homes the brief names, are both narrated from
above; and (2) the section-2 climax paragraph (the Maryland stand) is a single
enormous block that stacks three sourcing caveats on top of the emotional peak,
so the peak never gets to land before the footnote voice arrives.

## BLOCKER

### B1 · No "with them" passage anywhere in the draft (the Carillon bar)

Carillon's signature move:

> "Imagine being one of them. You hit a belt of felled trees too thick to run
> through... The volleys come from a wall of logs you cannot see over, close
> enough that you can hear the order to fire before each one lands."

Long Island has no equivalent. Its two candidate moments are both dispatched in
summary:

The marsh swim, one clause —

> "Stirling ordered the bulk of his command to escape the only way left, across
> the Gowanus marsh and its mill creek, about 80 yards of water; most made it,
> and some men drowned trying."

The embarkation, beautiful but aerial —

> "sailors and fishermen, in the dark, rowing an entire army with its gear,
> horses, and cannon across a mile of tidal river, trip after trip after trip."

**Fix (pick ONE; two would dilute both): the marsh swim is the better home** —
it sits beside the Maryland stand and deepens the same peak, and section 3
already has the fog and the Tallmadge quote carrying its emotion. Concrete
insertion after "about 80 yards of water":

> "Imagine being one of them. You have been fighting since before dawn, the
> firing is now behind you as well as in front, and the only way out is water:
> you go in, mud pulling at your knees, the creek at the middle over your head,
> your cartridges ruined, men to either side of you wading and swimming the
> same 80 yards while the volleys keep coming. Most made it. Some men drowned
> trying."

(If the reviser prefers the embarkation instead, the move is the same: you were
told you are being relieved, but the column files downhill toward the water
instead of the rear, no one is allowed to speak, and somewhere ahead in the dark
you can hear oars. But do the marsh.)

### B2 · The climax paragraph buries the Maryland stand under its own footnotes

The single paragraph beginning "By late morning Stirling, still trading
volleys..." carries: the encirclement, the marsh escape, the charges, the
Maryland-400 arithmetic correction, the lost casualty list, the Washington
"brave fellows" line PLUS its reliability caveat, Stirling's surrender, AND the
Field 1869 verdict. The emotional peak of the whole battle is in there, and it
is interrupted twice at the worst possible moments:

> "Posterity calls them the Maryland 400, and the famous figure of 256 killed
> needs honest handling: the rear guard was about 270 men, not 400, and the 256
> comes from a report of killed and missing combined (the original casualty
> list is lost; later research showed many of the 'killed' had actually been
> captured)."

— this audit-voice block lands BETWEEN the charges and their cost, exactly
where the reader should be feeling it. And:

> "The line is contemporary but secondhand; no one can put it in Washington's
> mouth as certainty."

— a second hedge sentence stapled to the draft's single best human moment.
Compare how lightly Carillon badges its quotes ("One who was there wrote
that..."). Every fact here is right to keep (the pack mandates the honest
handling); the problem is purely WHERE it sits.

**Fix — restructure into three paragraphs, no facts changed:**
1. Encirclement + marsh escape (+ the B1 immersion passage).
2. The charges → the Washington line → Stirling's surrender → the Field
   verdict. Compress the brave-fellows badge into the attribution itself, e.g.:
   "An anonymous American account, written days later and secondhand, which is
   as close as the record gets, has Washington watching from the Cobble Hill
   fort, wringing his hands: 'Good God, what brave fellows I must this day
   lose!'" Let the quote END a sentence, not be followed by a disclaimer.
3. The reckoning, merged with the existing burial paragraph: "Posterity calls
   them the Maryland 400. The arithmetic is messier..." → two-thirds lost →
   Gist and a dozen back → "Where the Maryland dead lie, nobody knows..." This
   is where the honest-handling material reads as elegy instead of erratum —
   the burial paragraph already proves the draft can do exactly this register.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1 · Section 2 opens on the draft's driest paragraph

The reset opener ("By August 26, 1776, some 20,000 British and Hessian...
troops were ashore...") is a near-verbatim recap of section 1's geography with
no story pulse. Resets are house standard, but Carillon's sections reset
INSIDE a forward-moving sentence ("While the British army gathered itself
after Howe's death, the French commander spent every hour he had building a
trap"). Fix: lead with the situation as a problem, then reset within it —
e.g. "On the night of August 26 the American army was watching the wrong
doors." Then the ridge, the passes, the five horsemen. Cut the recap by about
a third; keep the five-officers line as the paragraph's last beat (it already
is — good).

### S2 · The Howard's tavern badge stalls the night column and reads knotty

> "...so the dialogue is tradition rather than record, but the core of it,
> local men marched along at the head of a British army, is solid."

The appositive ("the core of it, X, is solid") is hard to parse without dashes,
and the whole badge sits mid-stream in an already very long paragraph, braking
the column's momentum. Fix: split the night-march paragraph at the tavern, and
recast the badge so it points forward instead of pausing:
"...so the dialogue is tradition rather than record. The bones of it are solid:
a tavern keeper and his teenage son walking at the head of a British army."
Then the five captured officers, then dawn. The paragraph's closing line
("Through the whole night, an army of 10,000 men had marched across the
American front, and no one on the American side ever detected it") is the right
ending — protect it.

### S3 · The dawn realization is skipped on the American side

> "...light infantry and grenadiers rolled in from Bedford, directly behind
> Sullivan's position. The center broke."

The brief's arc asks for "the dawn realization," and the draft cuts straight
from the British arriving to the collapse. One sentence inside the forward line
buys the whole beat — e.g. "For the men on the ridge, the first sign was
musketry from the wrong direction: behind them, between them and home." Then
"The center broke" lands twice as hard.

### S4 · Section 1's armada build is numbers-forward where it should be masts-forward

The buildup paragraph is a ledger: "more than 9,000 troops and some 130
ships... about 150 more ships... roughly 32,000 troops and 10,000 sailors on a
fleet of around 400 ships." McCurtin's "a wood of pine trees trimmed" and the
pull-quote rescue it at the end, but the dread should accumulate physically
through the middle too. Fix: keep the mid-August tally as the single summary
sentence and convert one count into watching — e.g. "Through July and August it
never stopped: every few days, more sails standing in past Sandy Hook, more
masts off Staten Island, until the count stood at roughly 32,000 troops..."

### S5 · Light meta-narration: "the battlefield's whole geometry"

> "Between those beaches and the American works at Brooklyn ran the
> battlefield's whole geometry: the Heights of Guan..."

"The battlefield's whole geometry" is the narrator announcing the diagram
rather than showing the ground (the "the shape is this" pattern). Fix:
"Between those beaches and the American works at Brooklyn stood the Heights of
Guan, a long wooded ridge that an army could cross only at the passes." The
rest of the paragraph already does the work.

## TRIM

### T1 · Section 3's Loyalist/occupation paragraph is a catalog

The paragraph beginning "For Long Island itself, the battle was not an ending
but the start of an occupation..." carries the Dutch towns, the Queens vote
(with a dated parenthetical), the pass intelligence callback, DeLancey and his
three battalions, requisitions/billeting/woodcutting, the Suffolk flight, the
whaleboat war, and then Woodhull bolted on with "One American general was
still to fall:". Cut the Queens-vote parenthetical ("(Queens had voted
overwhelmingly against sending deputies to the rebel provincial congress in
1775)") and compress the occupation list to one sentence. The pass-intelligence
callback ("it was Loyalist intelligence that handed Clinton the unguarded
Jamaica Pass, and Loyalist farmers who guided the night column") is the
paragraph's best beat — promote it to the front. Woodhull earns his place
(he's the occupation's brutality made personal) but give him his own short
paragraph instead of a trailing clause.

### T2 · Section 1's MEANWHILE repeats its own prose

The prose already lands the coincidence ("on the very days that Congress in
Philadelphia voted for and then declared independence"), and the MEANWHILE
re-explains it before getting to its real payoff. Trim the MEANWHILE's first
sentence to a clause and lead with the line that's doing new work: "The United
States declared itself a country in the same week the force sent to strangle
it began arriving in the harbor." That sentence is the block; let it breathe.

## NIT

### N1 · "(American)" on George Washington

"George Washington (American), commanding the rebel army..." — the side-tag on
Washington reads as silly as "(Union)" on Lincoln would; the Civil War policy
exempts the un-mistakable top tier for exactly this reason. If the Revolution
exemption list exists, Washington belongs on it (and arguably Howe). For the
integrator, not the author.

### N2 · Tense wobble in the verdict line

"The army had survived, and that is the entire meaning of the Battle of Long
Island." — the sentence is good (a verdict, not meta-narration), but "had
survived... that is" mixes the battle's past with the essayist's present.
"that was the entire meaning" keeps the reader in 1776.

### N3 · Hedge stacking

"roughly 32,000 troops and 10,000 sailors on a fleet of around 400 ships,
several dozen of them warships" — "roughly... around... several dozen" in one
sentence. The ranges are mandated; the variety isn't. One "roughly" can govern
the sentence.

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES (for the record)

- **Pull-quotes at peaks:** all three are placed right. "All London afloat" at
  the armada's crest; the no-quarter letter at the rout's darkest moment (and
  the pack forbids "Good God" as a QUOTE block, so this is the correct choice);
  Tallmadge exactly at the fog. No relocation needed.
- **MEANWHILE placement:** "Why Howe stopped" immediately after "Howe said no"
  is the best block in the draft — it frames the halt debate as interpretation
  ("that reading is interpretation, not record") without deflating it ("but the
  halt itself changed the war"). The Wallabout Bay closer ("Many of the men the
  boats could not save never came home at all") is the right last note.
- **PILLs:** both sensible — the campaign-context pill as a breather before
  "Then the ships came," the civil-war pill on the Loyalist beat. Correctly,
  there is no pill inside the trap in section 2.
- **Both sides good at war:** yes. "He proposed the kind of move armies attempt
  only in books," the undetected 10,000-man march, Grant's boast giving the
  diversion a face, Clinton's local knowledge. The British win is earned on the
  page, not asserted.
- **Tradition badges that already work as texture (don't touch):** the Field
  line ("a historian's verdict, not a participant's, but it has stuck"),
  Sullivan's cornfield ("by tradition"), Tallmadge ("the polish is old age's,
  but he was there"), Washington's boat ("'among the last' is as much as the
  record will bear"), and the burial paragraph's opener ("Where the Maryland
  dead lie, nobody knows"). These are the register B2 should be revised toward.
- **Miracle-without-calling-it-one:** section 3 never says miracle; "a peculiar
  providential occurrence" is left in Tallmadge's mouth where it belongs. "The
  lie was the point" and "The troops themselves were told nothing true" are
  house voice at its best.
