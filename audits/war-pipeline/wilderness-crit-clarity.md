# Wilderness — NEWCOMER / CLARITY critic gate

> War content pipeline, step 3 critic (clarity). Read COLD as a sharp 15-year-old with
> ZERO prior knowledge of the Civil War, armies, or tactics. Flags every undefined
> term, unexplained leap, un-side-tagged actor, and any passage that would lose or
> overwhelm a first-timer. CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST per item, then MUST-FIX
> (real comprehension breakers) vs SHOULD-FIX (friction, not breakage).

**VERDICT: CLEAR, with ISSUES (a handful of real comprehension breakers in the May 6
section).** The draft is unusually disciplined about gloss — corps, plank road,
canister, flank, breastworks, pontoon bridges, cavalry, USCT are all defined inline on
first use, and side-tags are applied conscientiously. The trouble is concentrated:
the longstreet-attacks section dumps ~13 named people on a first-timer in a few
paragraphs, and a few specific terms/actors slip through ungloss­ed. Fix those and a
zero-knowledge reader can follow the whole thing.

---

## What the draft already does RIGHT (so the fixes don't regress it)
- **CLEAR — "corps"** is glossed exactly where a newcomer first needs it ("a 'corps'
  being one of the big building-block chunks an army is divided into, ten or twenty
  thousand men under one general"). 
- **CLEAR — "plank road," "canister," "flank," "breastworks," "pontoon bridges,"
  "cavalry (mounted scouts)," "the Rapidan (the river marking the northern edge of
  this country)"** are all defined inline on first use. This is model gloss discipline.
- **CLEAR — "general-in-chief … the top soldier in the country"** for Grant, and
  "the man he called his 'Old War Horse'" for Longstreet, give a newcomer the rank/
  role without jargon.
- **CLEAR — side-tags** are applied on first mention per section for nearly every
  named actor (North)/(South). Good.

---

## MUST-FIX (genuine comprehension breakers)

1. **LOST — "rolling up a line" / "rolled up his line" is used twice and never
   glossed; the one place flank IS defined doesn't cover it.** Section
   longstreet-attacks: "the flank attack rolling up his line, in his own phrase, like
   a wet blanket," and the meaning section: "an order meant to guard that flank had
   miscarried." The draft glosses *flank* ("the exposed end of a battle line … fire
   down the length of the enemy's line instead of butting into the front") — good —
   but "roll up a line" is a separate idiom a 15-year-old won't picture. A reader who
   doesn't already know the phrase will read "rolling up … like a wet blanket" as
   word salad. **Fix:** either drop "rolling up" and reuse the already-glossed
   mechanic ("collapsing his line from the exposed end"), or add three words of
   gloss the first time ("rolled up his line — collapsed it sideways, one unit folding
   into the next").

2. **NEEDS-GLOSS → LOST in context — the name overload in longstreet-attacks.** In
   roughly four paragraphs a cold reader meets: Poague, Gregg, the Texas Brigade,
   Field, Kershaw, Wadsworth, Martin L. Smith, Sorrel, Mahone, Gibbon (implied by
   "an order meant to guard that flank had miscarried"), the 12th Virginia, Micah
   Jenkins, Mott, Carroll, Gordon, Early, Ricketts, Shaler, Seymour, Sedgwick. Most
   get a side-tag, which is correct, but a side-tag is not the same as a reader
   knowing *why this person matters*. Several are pure plot-furniture (Poague's guns,
   Carroll's counterattack) and others are load-bearing (Longstreet, Sorrel/Mahone on
   the railroad, Jenkins killed with Longstreet). A first-timer can't tell which is
   which and will start skimming. **Fix (this is a comprehension fix, not just
   pacing):** for the minor actors, lead with the ROLE and let the name be secondary
   or droppable — e.g. "a brigade counterattacked and drove the breach back out"
   (Carroll's name optional), "twelve cannon under one colonel" (Poague's name
   optional). Reserve full name-plus-tag emphasis for the people whose fate the reader
   must track: Longstreet, Jenkins, Hancock, Gordon. This is the single biggest
   overwhelm risk in the draft.

3. **NEEDS-GLOSS — "Confederate" / "Confederacy" / "the Confederate capital" appear
   before the reader is told what they ARE.** First uses: section the-thicket's
   MEANWHILE ("the drive on the Confederate capital at Richmond") and the side-tag
   system itself. The draft never plainly says, early, that the United States had
   split and the southern states that broke away called themselves the Confederacy
   (the South), fighting against the United States (the North). The side-tags
   (North)/(South) are used from the first paragraph as if the reader already maps
   North=Union=United States and South=Confederacy. A zero-knowledge reader can infer
   it, but inference is exactly what this app's "reader has zero prior knowledge" rule
   forbids. **Fix:** one early sentence (in the-thicket) establishing that the country
   had split in two — northern states (the Union / "North") fighting the southern
   states that had seceded and formed the Confederacy ("South") — and that this is what
   the (North)/(South) tags mean. The meaning section eventually says "Eleven states
   had broken away from the United States," but that's the LAST section; the reader
   needed it in the FIRST. (Note: if this is a series and an earlier war article
   already establishes Union/Confederacy, this drops to SHOULD-FIX — flag for the
   integrator to confirm whether a prior section carries the definition.)

4. **NEEDS-GLOSS — Grant "set the strategy" while Meade "actually commanded the Army
   of the Potomac on the ground" is the right info but lands confusingly because
   "Army of the Potomac" is never glossed.** The phrase appears in the-two-roads
   ("the Army of the Potomac, who actually commanded … while Grant rode along") and is
   used repeatedly after (the-turn, the meaning) as if the reader knows it's the main
   Union army in the East. **Fix:** gloss on first use — "the Army of the Potomac
   (the main Union army in Virginia)." Right now a newcomer may not connect "the Army
   of the Potomac" with "Grant's large army" from section one and could think they're
   two different forces.

## SHOULD-FIX (friction, not breakage)

5. **NEEDS-GLOSS — "Overland Campaign" is named before it's explained, then explained
   later.** the-thicket MEANWHILE: "His whole campaign — later called the Overland
   Campaign, the drive on the Confederate capital at Richmond." That's actually a fine
   inline gloss — but then the meaning section re-defines it more fully ("the
   continuous six-week grind … Wilderness, then Spotsylvania …"). The first gloss is
   enough; just confirm the reader isn't told the name three times before it sticks.
   Minor.

6. **NEEDS-GLOSS — "earthworks" / "hasty earthworks" (May 5, Ewell's men) appears
   before "breastworks" is glossed (May 6).** A newcomer meets "dug into hasty
   earthworks" with no gloss, then a section later gets "log breastworks (chest-high
   barricades of piled timber)." Earthworks is the same family (dirt/log field
   fortifications) and should get its quick gloss on ITS first use, not implicitly via
   a later term. **Fix:** three words at the May 5 use ("hasty earthworks — dirt
   barricades thrown up fast").

7. **NEEDS-GLOSS — "division," "brigade," "regiment" are used freely but only "corps"
   is glossed.** The reader is told a corps is "ten or twenty thousand men," then meets
   divisions, brigades, and regiments (the 63rd Pennsylvania, the Texas Brigade, the
   12th Virginia) without being told these are the smaller nesting pieces inside a
   corps. A sharp reader infers a hierarchy, but one half-sentence the first time
   ("a corps splits into divisions, divisions into brigades, brigades into regiments —
   biggest to smallest") would let them place every unit that follows. SHOULD-FIX
   because the story survives without it, but it removes real low-grade confusion.

8. **NEEDS-GLOSS — "the Confederate right wing" / "Lee's entire right wing" / "Lee's
   right flank" use directional army-geometry the reader hasn't been taught.** May 6:
   "Lee's entire right wing teetered." A newcomer may not know that an army's "right"
   and "left" are fixed to the army's own facing, not the reader's map. Since "flank"
   IS glossed as "the side rather than the front," this is close to covered — but
   "right wing" specifically could still read as ambiguous. Low priority; one clause
   tying "right" to "the southern end of Lee's line, along the Plank Road" would
   resolve it.

9. **SHOULD-FIX — "the 23rd USCT" appears as an acronym in the meaning section.**
   "United States Colored Troops" IS spelled out and glossed ("formerly enslaved and
   free Black men in Union uniform") — good — but then "the 23rd USCT" abbreviates it.
   The abbreviation is fine *because* it's defined first; just confirm the spelled-out
   form precedes every use, which it does. No change needed; noting it so the
   integrator doesn't "fix" it backward.

10. **CLEAR but worth a glance — "the Rapidan" vs. "Germanna Ford."** The draft
    glosses the Rapidan as the river and later says Grant ordered "the pontoon bridges
    … at Germanna Ford taken up." A newcomer may not connect Germanna Ford to the
    Rapidan (a "ford" is an unexplained term — a river crossing). **Fix (small):**
    "Germanna Ford (the Rapidan crossing the army had entered through)" or gloss
    "ford" once. Borderline MUST/SHOULD because the whole emotional beat — taking up
    the bridges = not retreating — depends on the reader knowing those bridges crossed
    the river *back the way they came*. The draft does say "Rather than recross the
    river to safety," which mostly rescues it. Leaving at SHOULD-FIX.

---

## Bottom line for the integrator
A zero-knowledge reader CAN follow this draft today — the gloss discipline is genuinely
good — but four things will trip a true first-timer: (1) "rolling up a line" is jargon,
(2) the May-6 name flood, (3) "Confederacy/Union" assumed before it's defined, and (4)
"Army of the Potomac" unglossed. Fix those four (MUST-FIX 1–4) and the rest are
polish. None of the fixes requires a new fact — all are gloss, ordering, or
name-demotion.
