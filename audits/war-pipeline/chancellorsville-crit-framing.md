## FIRST CHECK — Is SLAVERY named as the cause in the section's OWN prose?

**YES.** The word "slavery"/"enslaved"/"enslavement" appears repeatedly, and it is
carried in the section's OWN narrative voice, not only quarantined in a sidebar. This
is the single most romanticization-prone battle in the war ("Lee's masterpiece," the
Lee–Jackson partnership, Jackson's martyr-death), and the draft meets the bar without
flinching. Concretely:

1. **§the-plan "Meanwhile"** — Emancipation Proclamation in effect Jan 1, 1863, four
   months prior; "fighting to end the thing the Confederacy had been built to protect."
2. **§lee-divides "Meanwhile"** — names the Confederacy as a society "built on the
   enslavement of Black people"; the army "ran… on enslaved labor: cooks, teamsters,
   blacksmiths, hospital hands, and officers' body servants, men held in bondage."
   Critically, this sidebar is in plain declarative voice and explicitly pivots off the
   generalship: "Lee's generalship at Chancellorsville was extraordinary. The cause it
   served was the ownership of human beings."
3. **§hooker-quits "Meanwhile" + closing prose** — 6,000–10,000 enslaved people
   shadowing Lee's army north; free Black Pennsylvanians seized into slavery; the
   victory framed as the launching pad "into the teeth of the new emancipation policy."
4. **Main prose, not just sidebars** — §hooker-quits closing paragraph (line 141), the
   section's own concluding voice, names "the new emancipation policy" and the free
   states as the stake. The throughline is load-bearing in the narrative, not bolted on.

The factpack hook is fully honored: EP-in-effect-4-months-prior is in §the-plan; the
6,000–10,000 enslaved-laborers-shadowing-Lee figure is in §hooker-quits. **PASS — no
automatic MUST-FIX on the slavery-named test.**

## Findings

### F1 — "Lee's masterpiece" framing — **FAIR**
The draft does call it "Lee's masterpiece," "his perfect battle," and "the perfect
battle" (lines 131, 141). This is the exact phrasing most prone to romanticization. The
draft handles it correctly:
- It attributes the label to historians, not to the narrative voice asserting reverence
  ("It is, fairly, called Lee's masterpiece… by the historians who have studied it,"
  line 131; ledger line 159 confirms the attribution discipline).
- It immediately undercuts the word: "the word *masterpiece* hides the bill" (line 133),
  then prices it in blood — a fifth of Lee's army lost, Jackson dead, "a victory Lee's
  army could not really afford to keep winning" (line 135), "The perfect battle was the
  beginning of the end" (line 141).
This is admiration of the *generalship* explicitly severed from approval of the *cause* —
exactly the line the brief asks to hold. FAIR.

### F2 — Lee–Jackson partnership / "cracker box" scene — **FAIR**
The Lee–Jackson planning-by-firelight scene (line 55) is told vividly, which is the
romanticization trap. But §lee-divides immediately attaches the cost-of-the-cause sidebar
(line 62) naming what "this brilliant maneuver was in the service of." The admiration is
for the audacity of the maneuver; the moral weight is supplied in the same section. Not
a clean partnership-hagiography. FAIR.

### F3 — Jackson's death / "martyr" risk — **FAIR**
Jackson's wounding and death is the classic Lost Cause martyr set-piece. The draft does
NOT sanctify him. It calls his death "one of the cruelest accidents in American military
history" (human-reality language, not veneration), names that his own aggression killed
him (line 98), renders the "right arm" quote indirectly and flags it (line 102), and
flags the "cross over the river" last words as McGuire-uncertain (line 137). There is no
"South's greatest loss / the war turned on his death" mysticism in the narrative voice —
the closest is "a wound the Confederacy never healed" (line 139), which is a military
claim about command capacity, not a lament for the Confederate cause. Jackson is treated
as a lethal, effective enemy commander, not a tragic hero. FAIR.

### F4 — False both-sides balance / unfair sympathy — **FAIR**
No "brother against brother," no equal-nobility framing. Where sympathy IS extended, it
is to the right target: the XI Corps German-American immigrants ("Flying Dutchmen,"
lines 77, 89), scapegoated for a command failure — a Union-side fairness correction, not
Confederate sympathy. The human cost of the Wilderness fires is given to "wounded men of
both sides" (line 135), which is accurate battlefield reality, not false moral
equivalence between the war aims. FAIR.

### F5 — Euphemism / soft-pedaling — **FAIR**
No euphemism for slavery: it is "enslavement," "men held in bondage," "the ownership of
human beings," "seized free Black Pennsylvanians and sent them south into slavery." No
"servants" without "officers' body servants… men held in bondage" framing. No "states'
rights," no "peculiar institution," no "the Cause." Clean.

### F6 — Presentism / unfair sympathy to Confederates — **FAIR**
The draft does not retroactively excuse Lee or his soldiers, nor does it lapse into
anachronistic moralizing that flattens the actors. "whatever any soldier in the ranks
thought of it" (line 35) acknowledges the period without absolving the cause. No
presentism flag.

### F7 — Human reality: sanitized vs gratuitous — **FAIR**
The Wilderness brush-fires burning the wounded (line 135) is named — the human horror is
not sanitized. It is also not gratuitous: one sentence, flagged for sourcing, not dwelt
on for shock. The XI Corps rout's human scale (2,500 lost in minutes) is given without
either bloodlessness or wallowing. Balance is right.

---

## Verdict

**Overall: FAIR.** This is a model of how to tell the most romanticization-prone battle
of the war: it admires the generalship openly, prices it in blood and in the slave-system
it served, attributes the "masterpiece" reverence to historians rather than asserting it,
and refuses the martyr/partnership mythology in its own voice. The slavery throughline is
present in three sections AND in the closing narrative voice, not quarantined.

### MUST-FIX
**None.** (No framing/distortion defect rises to ship-blocking. The slavery-named test
passes.)

  command-capacity claim, but in isolation it brushes the edge of elegiac Lost Cause

  end" / "couldn't afford to keep winning" framing, so it is fine as-is; if the
  storytelling critic wants belt-and-suspenders, it could be tightened to make explicit
  it means Lee's command depth, not a lament. Discretionary, not a framing fault.
