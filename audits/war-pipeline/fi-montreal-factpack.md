## A. STRATEGIC SETTING

1. The Montreal campaign (also called the fall of Montreal) was a British three-pronged offensive against Montreal, July 2 to September 8, 1760, part of the French and Indian War / the global Seven Years' War. [S1]

2. By 1760 Quebec had already fallen (the British took it in September 1759 after the Battle of the Plains of Abraham). The French had attempted to retake Quebec in spring 1760, winning the Battle of Sainte-Foy (April 28, 1760) but failing to recapture the city after a British relief fleet arrived. Montreal was the last major French stronghold remaining in Canada. [S1][S4]

3. Overall British command in North America was held by Major General Jeffery Amherst, who directed the convergence. [S1]

4. Three British armies, totaling roughly 18,000 men, converged on Montreal from three directions. [S1][S3]

## B. THE THREE COLUMNS

5. **Amherst's column** descended the St. Lawrence River from Lake Ontario. Strength: "just over 10,000 men of which just under 4,000 were regulars" (~11,000 per some summaries). Departed Oswego on August 10, 1760. Included roughly 700 Native warriors (Stockbridge Indians and others) recruited via Sir William Johnson. [S1]
   - DISPUTED: Amherst's strength given as ~10,000 (Wikipedia detail) to ~11,000 (summary). Use "roughly 10,000 to 11,000."

6. **Murray's column** came up the St. Lawrence westward from Quebec. Commander: Brigadier General James Murray. Strength: ~3,800 to 4,000 men. Departed Quebec July 13, 1760; some marched overland, the rest on armed vessels and bateaux. [S1][S3]
   - DISPUTED: 3,800 [S3] vs 4,000 [S1]. Use "about 3,800 to 4,000."

7. **Haviland's column** advanced up the Lake Champlain / Richelieu River corridor. Commander: Brigadier General William Haviland. Strength: ~3,400 to 3,500 men. Set out from Crown Point on August 11, 1760; men crammed aboard "80 whaleboats, 330 bateaux and four radeaux." Included four companies of Rogers' Rangers. [S1][S5]
   - DISPUTED: 3,400 [S5] vs 3,500 [S1]. Use "about 3,400 to 3,500."

## C. AMHERST DOWN THE ST. LAWRENCE — FORT LÉVIS AND THE RAPIDS

8. Fort Lévis was a small French fort on the upper St. Lawrence (built 1759, on an island; named for the duc de Lévis). It barred Amherst's descent. [S6]

9. Commander: Captain Pierre Pouchot of the Régiment de Béarn. Garrison: about 340 regulars and Canadian militiamen, plus some Native warriors; the fort had wooden stockades and a handful of cannon (~five), supported by two small French vessels. [S1][S6]
   - DISPUTED garrison detail: "340 regulars and Canadian militiamen as well as natives" [S1]; another account says "200 soldiers ... plus corvettes l'Outaouaise and l'Iroquoise crewed by 200 sailors and voyageurs" [S6]. Core fact: a tiny garrison, on the order of a few hundred.

10. Pouchot's tiny garrison held up Amherst's huge army for about a week (the bombardment/assault period; the fort surrendered August 24/25, 1760, when Pouchot ran out of ammunition). The defense sank/crippled British warships before falling. [S1][S6]

11. British siege losses at Fort Lévis were light: about 21 redcoats and provincials killed. The British captured the fort's artillery (about 35 guns) and stores. [S6]
   - Source quote (battle summary): "Twenty-one redcoats and provincials had been killed in the siege of Fort Lévis; four times that number drowned before Amherst's boats had shot the last of the white water that lay between the fort and Montréal." [search snippet of military history summary]

12. **The rapids.** Amherst's flotilla then had to run the dangerous rapids of the St. Lawrence above Montreal (Lachine rapids / Les Cèdres, Buisson, Cascades), around September 4, 1760. Many boats were wrecked and men drowned. [S1]
   - DISPUTED death toll: Wikipedia detail says "46 boats ... and seventeen whale-boats were totally wrecked, eighteen were damaged, one row galley was grounded and overall 84 men were drowned." Pouchot's account puts the drowned at 336. [S1][S6] Use "about 84 (French sources say far more, up to 336)."

13. A French force under the Chevalier de La Corne was positioned to defend the rapids but did NOT attack; neither Canadian militia nor Native warriors appeared to harass the British passage — a direct consequence of the Seven Nations going neutral (see Section G). [S1][search snippet, National Battlefields Commission]

14. Amherst's army landed near Lachine / Île Perrot, roughly 22 miles (about 35 km) from Montreal, on September 6, 1760; the French retired within the city walls. [S1]

## D. HAVILAND'S ADVANCE AND THE COLLAPSE OF FRENCH DEFENSES

15. The French posts barring Haviland on the Richelieu were Île aux Noix, Fort Saint-Jean, and Fort Chambly. Île aux Noix was defended by Colonel Louis-Antoine de Bougainville with about 1,500 French regulars and militia. [S5]

16. British forces (Darby's guns, August 25, 1760) destroyed the French naval squadron on the river, breaking French control. Bougainville withdrew; the French burned Forts Saint-Jean and Chambly rather than defend them. Haviland was slowed less by fighting than by habitants and deserters pouring into his camp to surrender and take oaths of allegiance. [S5][search snippet]

## E. THE CONVERGENCE

17. The three armies arrived at Montreal within about 24 hours of one another. Amherst's column landed near Lachine September 6; Murray's army landed (at Pointe-aux-Trembles) the morning of September 7; Haviland completed the encirclement September 7. [S1][S5]
   - Source quote: "Three British armies that had started from different sides of the French colony had converged on Montreal within 24 hours of one another." [S5]

18. Montreal's defenses were weak; the city's walls were not built to withstand siege artillery. The French had no relief possible. [S1][S3]

## F. THE TRAPPED FRENCH AND THE SURRENDER

19. French military leader: François-Gaston de Lévis (the Chevalier / later duc de Lévis), the field commander. French civil authority: Pierre de Rigaud, Marquis de Vaudreuil-Cavagnal, Governor-General of New France. [S1][S2][S4]

20. French regular strength in Montreal at the end: fewer than about 2,200 regulars plus ~200 men of the Compagnies Franches de la Marine (French colonial regulars). [S1]

21. **The deserting army.** As the three armies closed in, the Canadian militia deserted en masse to protect their homes and families; then regular French troops followed, "even the élite grenadiers," leaving "the French army rapidly melting away." The Native allies of France had also gone over to neutrality/the British. Further resistance was judged impossible. [S3][S4]
   - Source quote (DCB): the militia deserted, then regulars, "even the élite grenadiers," leaving "the French army rapidly melting away." [S4]

22. Vaudreuil called a council of war. Per The Canadian Encyclopedia: "since all the militia and many of the regulars had abandoned the army, and the Indian allies of France had gone over to the British, further resistance was impossible." [S3]

23. Lévis wanted to keep fighting. He had earlier insisted "the army would defend the colony tenaciously" and that it would be "more honourable to perish, arms in hand, than to submit." When Amherst refused the honours of war, Lévis said it would be "unthinkable to submit to such humiliating terms before the enemy had been obliged to launch an assault." [S4]

24. Lévis requested permission to withdraw the French regiments to Île Sainte-Hélène (an island off Montreal) for a last stand to save the army's honor. Vaudreuil refused and ordered him to accept the terms and lay down arms. [S4]

25. **The capitulation.** The Articles of Capitulation of Montreal were signed September 8, 1760, in the British camp before Montreal, by Vaudreuil and Amherst, on behalf of the French and British crowns. 55 articles. With it, "Canada and all its dependencies passed to the British crown." This effectively completed the British conquest of New France. [S1][S2][S3]

26. British grenadiers and light infantry (led by Colonel Frederick Haldimand) entered the city on September 8. [S1]

## G. THE DENIED HONOURS OF WAR AND THE BURNED COLORS

27. Amherst refused the French garrison the customary "honours of war" (the ceremonial right of a defeated garrison to march out under arms, with flags flying, before laying them down). He demanded the whole garrison and all French troops in Canada lay down arms and not serve for the remainder of the war (even in Europe). [S1][S4]
   - Source quote (Amherst's demand, per Wikipedia): "The whole garrison of Montréal and all other French troops in Canada must lay down their arms, and shall not serve during the present war." [S1]

28. Amherst justified the refusal by citing French conduct in inciting Native allies to commit atrocities earlier in the war (e.g., the 1757 massacre after the surrender of Fort William Henry). [S1]
   - Source quote (Amherst's stated reason, per Wikipedia): "for the infamous part the troops of France have acted in exciting the savages to perpetrate the most horrid and unheard of barbarities." [S1]

29. **Lévis burned the colors.** Denied the honours of war and forbidden a last stand, Lévis ordered his battalions to burn their regimental colors rather than surrender them to the British. [S1][S4][S12]
   - DISPUTED location: tradition says the flags were burned on Île Sainte-Hélène; some research argues they were burned wherever found around Montreal. State plainly that he burned them; do not over-specify the island as certain. [S12]
   - Some accounts add that Lévis "broke his sword"; this detail is in French-language secondary sources [S12] and is less universally reported — treat as a reported detail, hedge or omit.

30. The seven French regular battalions present (La Reine, La Sarre, Royal Roussillon, Languedoc, Guyenne, Berry, Béarn), about 2,200 fit men, laid down their arms. About 1,000 more were sick or missing. [S1]

## H. THE NATIVE STORY

31. The "Seven Nations of Canada" (also "Seven Fires" — a confederacy of Catholic, mostly French-allied Native communities of the St. Lawrence valley, including the Kahnawake Mohawk near Montreal) had been key French allies. [S7]

32. Seeing French defeat coming, the Seven Nations negotiated with the British. Sir William Johnson (British superintendent of Indian affairs) secured their neutrality. On August 30, 1760 (in talks at/around Oswegatchie–Swegatchy), about 30 representatives agreed to abandon the French and stay neutral for the rest of the war. This removed more than 800 Native warriors from the French side and let Amherst descend the St. Lawrence and run the rapids unmolested. [S7][S8]

33. These terms were confirmed at the Treaty of Kahnawake conference, September 15–16, 1760, days AFTER the capitulation. Ratified by Johnson for the British Crown and by representatives of the Kahnawake Mohawk, witnessed by the Iroquois Confederacy. The Seven Nations secured free passage / trade access between Canada and New York (the Montreal–Albany fur trade). [S7][S8]

34. **Article 40** of the Articles of Capitulation addressed the Native allies of France. Although the articles were drafted without First Nations participation, Article 40 promised that the "Savages or Indian allies" would be maintained in the lands they inhabited if they chose to stay, would not be molested for having borne arms and served the French king, and would keep liberty of religion and their missionaries. [S2]
   - Source paraphrase (Wikipedia): the Indian allies "were to be maintained in the Lands they inhabit, if they chose to remain there; they were not to be molested on any pretence whatsoever for having carried arms and served his most Christian Majesty; they were to have, as well as the French, liberty of religion, and were to keep their missionaries." [S2]

35. **What conquest meant.** With France defeated, Native nations lost the leverage they had long held by playing the two empires against each other; they now faced a single expanding power with no counterweight. [S9][S10]

36. Amherst, after the war, cut back the gift-giving / diplomatic presents that the French had used to maintain alliances. Native peoples regarded gifts as a necessary part of diplomacy and a recognition of their lands/sovereignty; Amherst viewed gifts as bribery made unnecessary by victory and was under pressure to cut costs. Many saw the change as treating them as conquered people rather than allies. [S9][S10]
   - Source quote (Mount Vernon/Wikipedia framing): Indians regarded gifts as "a necessary part of diplomacy." [S9][S10]

37. **Pontiac's War** began in May 1763, when Native nations of the Great Lakes / Ohio Country (associated with the Ottawa leader Pontiac and others), alarmed by Amherst's policies, attacked British forts and settlements. They could not drive the British out, but the uprising forced Britain to modify its policies — in the peace, the British quietly agreed to resume annual gifts of trade goods and ammunition. [S9][S10]

## I. THE 1763 CODA

38. The fall of Montreal ended French rule on the ground in Canada in September 1760, but the FORMAL cession came later: the Treaty of Paris, signed February 10, 1763, by Great Britain, France, and Spain, ended the Seven Years' War. France formally ceded New France / Canada (and its mainland North American territory east of the Mississippi) to Britain (keeping a few fishing islands; Louisiana west of the Mississippi went to Spain in 1762). [S11]

## J. CASUALTIES / SCALE SUMMARY

39. The Montreal campaign itself was near-bloodless: it was a convergence and capitulation, not a pitched battle. The notable death toll on the British side was the rapids drownings (~84, French sources up to 336) and ~21 killed at the Fort Lévis siege. The French garrison surrendered intact (~2,200 fit, ~1,000 sick/missing). [S1][S6]

40. With the capitulation, the French regular regiments, the colonial administration, and Canada itself passed to British control; New France as a French possession ended. [S1][S2][S3]
