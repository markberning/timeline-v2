## BEAT 1: The Copperheads / Peace Democrats

- **Who they were:** A faction of Northern Democrats during the Civil War (1861–1865) who nominally favored the Union but strongly opposed the war and demanded an immediate **negotiated peace** with the Confederacy. Also called **Peace Democrats**. They opposed the draft, opposed emancipation, and blamed abolitionists for the war. They distinguished themselves from **War Democrats**, who supported prosecuting the war (McClellan would be a War Democrat — see Beat 9).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Copperhead (politics)"; Britannica, "Copperhead (American political faction)"

- **What they wanted — the slogan:** "**To maintain the Constitution as it is, and to restore the Union as it was.**" Coined by Vallandigham in an address **May 8, 1862**, endorsed by fifteen Democratic congressmen. The phrase captured the core demand: end the war, restore the *prewar* Union (i.e., with slavery intact), and stop what they saw as Lincoln's unconstitutional war measures.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"

- **Geographic strength — the lower Midwest:** Strongest base just **north of the Ohio River** — Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois (the "Old Northwest") — and in some urban ethnic wards of large cities. Many families in this region had **Southern roots** and economic ties down the Ohio/Mississippi river system, which shaped their sympathies.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Copperhead (politics)"; Britannica, "Copperhead"

- **The name — DOUBLE origin (cover both):**
  1. **The snake (hostile origin):** Republicans coined "Copperhead" as a slur, after the venomous **eastern copperhead snake** (*Agkistrodon contortrix*) that strikes without warning/rattle — i.e., a hidden, treacherous enemy at home. First applied to this group by the *New York Tribune*, reportedly **July 20, 1861**.
  2. **The copper Liberty-head cent (defiant re-appropriation):** Peace Democrats **embraced** the label and reinterpreted the "copper head" as the head of **Liberty** — they cut the Liberty-head image from large copper cents ("Liberty Head" pennies) and wore them as lapel badges, casting themselves as defenders of liberty and the Constitution against the administration.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Copperhead (politics)"; New World Encyclopedia, "Copperheads"
  — NOTE on the July 20, 1861 first-use date: widely repeated; treat the *exact* date as a secondary-source claim. The snake-vs-Liberty-cent double meaning is well established and is the strong, sourceable fact to lead with.

## BEAT 2: Clement L. Vallandigham — the Copperhead in the Flesh

- **Biography:** Born **July 29, 1820**, New Lisbon, Ohio. Democrat. U.S. Representative from **Ohio's 3rd district**, May 25, 1858 – March 3, 1863. The acknowledged **leader of the Copperhead faction**. Died June 17, 1871 (Lebanon, Ohio), age 50 — see ASIDE below.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"

- **His anti-war position:** A lame-duck congressman by early 1863 (he had lost his House seat after redistricting). His central charge: that the war had been hijacked from a war for the Union into a war to free slaves at the cost of white Northerners' liberties, under an administration he branded a despotism — "**King Lincoln**."
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"; American History Central, "Clement Vallandigham"

- **The January 14, 1863 House speech:** Delivered two weeks after the Emancipation Proclamation took effect (Jan 1, 1863). He declared that "war for the Union was abandoned; war for the negro openly begun" (see VERIFIED QUOTES). He demanded an armistice and negotiation.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"; "Deo Vindice"/sesquicentenary transcript of Jan 14, 1863 speech

- **The Mount Vernon speech (May 1, 1863):** At a Democratic rally in **Mount Vernon, Ohio**, Vallandigham denounced the war and the administration and openly defied a recent military order (General Order No. 38, below), charging that the war's purpose was to free Black Americans while enslaving white ones under "King Lincoln." Union officers in plain clothes attended and took notes.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"; Encyclopedia.com, "Vallandigham Incident"

## BEAT 3: General Order No. 38, the Arrest, and the Military Trial

- **General Order No. 38:** Issued by Union Maj. Gen. **Ambrose E. Burnside**, commander of the **Department of the Ohio**, on **April 13, 1863**. It declared that **"declaring sympathies for the enemy" would not be tolerated** in the department, and that those who committed "treason, expressed or implied" could be tried by **military commission** — warning that "the habit of declaring sympathy for the enemy will not be allowed." It explicitly reached civilians and political speech.
  — Source: Encyclopedia.com, "General Order No. 38"; Bill of Rights Institute, "Clement Vallandigham and Constitutionalism"

- **The arrest (May 5, 1863):** Vallandigham's Mount Vernon speech (May 1) was taken as a direct, deliberate violation of Order No. 38. At about **2:40 a.m. on May 5, 1863**, roughly **150 Union soldiers** from Burnside's command surrounded and broke into Vallandigham's home in **Dayton, Ohio**, and arrested him. He was taken to Cincinnati.
  — Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Vallandigham Incident"; Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"

- **The trial by military commission (May 6–7, 1863):** He was tried not in a civilian court but before a **military commission** in Cincinnati. Charge: publicly expressing — in violation of General Order No. 38 — sympathy for those in arms against the U.S. government and declaring disloyal sentiments "with the object and purpose of weakening the power of the Government in its efforts to suppress an unlawful rebellion." He challenged the commission's jurisdiction over him as a civilian, refused to plead, and was convicted. Sentence: confinement for the duration of the war (Fort Warren, Boston Harbor, was named).
  — Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Vallandigham Incident"; UMN/Samaha transcript, "Military Commission, Clement Vallandigham 1863"; Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"

- **Lincoln's intervention — commutation to BANISHMENT:** Lincoln, recognizing that imprisoning Vallandigham would make him a **martyr**, on **May 19, 1863** ordered the sentence commuted: instead of prison, Vallandigham was to be sent through Union lines **into the Confederacy**. He was delivered under guard to Confederate lines near Murfreesboro, Tennessee (Gen. Rosecrans's front) in late May 1863. (Lincoln privately called him a "wily agitator" — see the Corning letter, Beat 4.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"; Encyclopedia.com, "Vallandigham Incident"

- **The unwanted exile and the return via Canada:** The Confederacy did not want him either — he was an embarrassment, an anti-war Northerner, not a secessionist. Briefly detained at **Wilmington, North Carolina**, he then ran the blockade on a blockade-runner to **Bermuda**, and from there sailed to **Canada**, settling in **Windsor, Ontario** (just across the river from Detroit). On **June 14, 1864** he slipped back across the border into Ohio "under heavy disguise"; Lincoln chose not to re-arrest him, judging that ignoring him was wiser than martyring him again.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"

- **Running for Ohio governor IN EXILE (1863):** While in the Confederacy/Canada, the Ohio Democratic convention nominated Vallandigham for **governor of Ohio** *in absentia* on **June 11, 1863**, by a vote of **411–11**. He campaigned from a hotel in **Windsor, Ontario**, unable to set foot in the state he sought to govern.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"

- **The October 1863 defeat:** In the **October 13, 1863** Ohio gubernatorial election he was **decisively defeated** by War Democrat (running on the Union ticket) **John Brough**: Brough **288,374**, Vallandigham **187,492** — a margin of roughly **100,000 votes** (the soldier vote in the field went overwhelmingly against Vallandigham). The result was read nationally as a referendum: Ohio had rejected the Copperhead peace platform, a major morale boost for the Lincoln administration in the fall of 1863.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"

- **ASIDE (optional color — verify before use, but it's true and famous):** Vallandigham died in 1871 by **accidentally shooting himself** while, as a defense lawyer, demonstrating to a jury how the victim in a murder case could have shot himself — proving his client's innocence (the client was acquitted) but killing himself with a pistol he believed was unloaded.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham"

## BEAT 4: The Corning Letter — Lincoln Defends the Arrest in Public (June 12, 1863)

- **What it was:** After the Vallandigham arrest, a group of New York Democrats led by railroad magnate **Erastus Corning** sent Lincoln resolutions protesting the arrest as unconstitutional. Lincoln answered in a long public letter dated **June 12, 1863** — addressed to "Hon. Erastus Corning & others." It became one of his most important public defenses of wartime executive power and was widely reprinted. (A companion letter making similar arguments, the **"Conkling letter,"** went to James C. Conkling on Aug 26, 1863 — distinct document; the Corning letter is the one that contains the "wily agitator" passage.)
  — Source: Abraham Lincoln Online, "Letter to Erastus Corning"; House Divided (Dickinson), "Letter to Erastus Corning and Others (June 12, 1863)"

- **Lincoln's core argument — arrested for *acts against the army*, not for *opinions*:** Lincoln insisted Vallandigham was not arrested for criticizing the administration but for materially damaging the war effort: "Mr. Vallandigham avows his hostility to the war on the part of the Union; and his arrest was made because he was laboring, with some effect, to prevent the raising of troops, to encourage desertions from the army, and to leave the rebellion without an adequate military force to suppress it." (See VERIFIED QUOTES for the full "wily agitator" passage.)
  — Source: Abraham Lincoln Online, "Letter to Erastus Corning"

- **The civil-liberties tension to present FAIRLY:** This is the crux. Lincoln's case: in a rebellion, speech that recruits desertion is a weapon, and the Constitution itself contemplates suspending normal protections "when in cases of rebellion or invasion the public safety may require it." The critics' case (then and since): arresting a sitting/recent congressman by soldiers in the night, trying a civilian before a military commission for a political speech, and banishing him for dissent set a chilling precedent that a wartime executive could silence opposition. **Present both — this is a genuine, unresolved tension, not a settled verdict.**
  — Source: Abraham Lincoln Online, "Letter to Erastus Corning"; Bill of Rights Institute, "Clement Vallandigham and Constitutionalism"

## BEAT 5: Civil Liberties — Habeas Corpus, Merryman, and the Scale of Arrests

### What habeas corpus IS (plain-language, for inline definition)
- **Habeas corpus** (Latin, "you shall have the body") is the legal right that lets a person who has been jailed demand that the government **bring them before a judge and show a lawful reason** for holding them. It is the basic protection against being locked up indefinitely without charge or trial — often called the "Great Writ." Suspending it means the government can **hold people without charges and without a judge's review**.
  — Source: general legal background; cf. U.S. Constitution, Art. I, Sec. 9, the "Suspension Clause"

### Lincoln's suspension
- **First suspension (April 27, 1861):** Within weeks of Fort Sumter, with Washington nearly cut off and Maryland (a slave state surrounding the capital on three sides) seething, Lincoln authorized the suspension of habeas corpus along the **military line between Philadelphia and Washington** to let the army arrest and hold suspected saboteurs and secessionists without going through civil courts.
  — Source: National Constitution Center, "Lincoln and Taney's great writ showdown"
- **Constitutional sticking point:** The Suspension Clause sits in **Article I** (the *Congressional* article), so critics argued only **Congress**, not the **president**, could suspend the writ. Lincoln acted unilaterally; **Congress did not formally authorize suspension until the Habeas Corpus Act of March 3, 1863** — nearly two years later. (A nationwide presidential suspension followed on Sept. 24, 1862, tied to the draft and disloyalty.)
  — Source: National Constitution Center; Teaching American History, "Message to Congress in Special Session"

### Ex parte Merryman (1861) and Lincoln's defiance
- **The case:** **John Merryman**, a Maryland secessionist militia officer, was arrested by the army and held at Fort McHenry. Chief Justice **Roger B. Taney** — the author of *Dred Scott* — riding circuit, issued an opinion **(May 1861; dated May 28, 1861)**, *Ex parte Merryman*, holding that the **president had no power to suspend habeas corpus**; that power belonged to Congress. He ordered Merryman released.
  — Source: National Constitution Center, "Lincoln and Taney's great writ showdown"
- **Lincoln ignored the order.** Merryman stayed in military custody (later released on bail by civil authorities). Lincoln did not directly answer Taney; instead he laid out his reasoning to **Congress in his July 4, 1861 special-session message**, posing the famous question (see VERIFIED QUOTES): if obeying one law (habeas corpus) means the whole government falls, must that one law be obeyed while all the rest go unexecuted?
  — Source: National Constitution Center; Teaching American History, "Message to Congress in Special Session"; ScienceViews, "All the Laws but One"

### The scale of military arrests of civilians (CONTESTED NUMBER — use a range)
- The traditional figure long cited is roughly **13,000–14,000** civilians arrested by military authority during the war. The specific number **13,535** is repeated in older literature.
- **Mark E. Neely Jr.**, in his Pulitzer-winning *The Fate of Liberty: Abraham Lincoln and Civil Liberties* (1991), argued the **13,535 figure is "unverifiable and erroneous,"** found documentation of **at least ~14,000** arrests, and concluded the **true number is higher and ultimately unknowable** because many arrest records were lost. He also showed many arrests were of blockade-runners, Confederate deserters, smugglers, and citizens of border/occupied areas — **not** primarily Northern political dissenters.
- **Author guidance:** Say "**more than 13,000 — and by the leading modern study, likely more**," and cite Neely. Do NOT present 13,535 as a precise count, and do NOT imply all (or most) were political prisoners; the precision is false and the composition is misunderstood. This is the most-abused statistic in the topic.
  — Source: Mark E. Neely Jr., *The Fate of Liberty* (Oxford, 1991); TCU/Woodworth summary of Neely; Oxford UP catalog page

### Newspaper suppression — the Chicago Times (June 1863) and Lincoln's reversal
- **June 1, 1863:** Burnside issued **General Orders No. 84** (Dept. of the Ohio) **suppressing the Chicago Times** (for "repeated expression of disloyal and incendiary sentiments") and barring circulation of the *New York World* in his department. Federal troops occupied the *Times* offices and stopped the presses.
  — Source: ohiocivilwarcentral.com, "General Orders, No. 84"; Loyola eCommons thesis, "Suppression of the Chicago Times"
- **The backlash:** That evening some **20,000 Chicagoans protested** in the streets; the Illinois legislature formally protested on June 3. Across party lines, even some Republicans were alarmed at the precedent of an army shutting down a major daily.
  — Source: Loyola eCommons, "Suppression of the Chicago Times"
- **Lincoln REVERSED it:** On **June 4, 1863**, Lincoln wrote Secretary of War **Edwin M. Stanton** directing that Burnside's order **be revoked**; the *Times* reopened. This is the crucial fairness beat — Lincoln repeatedly pulled his commanders *back* from the most aggressive suppressions (Times, and he overruled Burnside's earlier instinct to imprison rather than banish Vallandigham). The civil-liberties record is genuinely mixed, not monolithically authoritarian.
  — Source: "Abraham Lincoln and the Civil War" (Lincoln-to-Stanton, June 4, 1863); House Divided, "the Chicago Times reopens"

## BEAT 6: The Secret Societies — Real Conspiracy vs. Campaign Bogeyman

- **The lineage:** A chain of overlapping secret fraternal/paramilitary orders, frequently renamed:
  **Knights of the Golden Circle (KGC)** → reorganized **late 1863** as the **Order of American Knights** → became, in **1864**, the **Order of the Sons of Liberty**, with **Clement Vallandigham** named "**supreme commander**." The KGC's origins (founder **George W. L. Bickley**, 1850s) lay in a pro-slavery scheme to create a "golden circle" of slave territory around the Caribbean; by the war it was a label for Copperhead secret organizing in the Midwest.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Knights of the Golden Circle"; Britannica, "Knights of the Golden Circle"; TSHA, "Knights of the Golden Circle"

- **The "Northwest Conspiracy":** An alleged 1864 plot — encouraged by some **Confederate agents operating out of Canada** with Confederate money — to spark an uprising in the **Old Northwest** (Indiana, Illinois, Ohio), free Confederate POWs from Northern camps (e.g., Camp Douglas in Chicago), arm them, and pull the lower Midwest out of the war or into a separate peace.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Knights of the Golden Circle"; Britannica

- **Real vs. exaggerated — the honest assessment (present this nuance):** Membership in these orders ran into the (claimed) tens of thousands, but historians emphasize a **large gap between perceived and actual threat**. In most areas only a **minority** was radical enough to actually discourage enlistment, resist the draft, or shield deserters; the grand armed insurrection never materialized. Crucially, **Republicans had every incentive to inflate the menace**: the **1864 Indianapolis treason trials furnished campaign material** painting all Democrats as treasonous Sons of Liberty. Net: there were real plotters and real Confederate money, but the "vast secret army about to rise" was substantially a **campaign-season exaggeration**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Knights of the Golden Circle"; Citizendium/Essential Civil War Curriculum, "Copperheads"

- **The 1864 treason trials & Ex parte Milligan (note 1866):** In 1864, **Lambdin P. Milligan** and others, alleged leaders of the Order of American Knights/Sons of Liberty in Indiana, were arrested for a plot to free Confederate prisoners, seize an arsenal, and raise insurrection. A **military commission** convicted Milligan and sentenced him to **death** (1864). After the war, the U.S. Supreme Court — in ***Ex parte Milligan* (1866)** — **overturned** the conviction, holding (opinion by Justice **David Davis**) that **civilians cannot be tried by military commissions where the civil courts are open and functioning** ("where the courts are open and their process unobstructed"). Indiana's civil courts had been open in 1864, so the military trial was unconstitutional. **Note for the author: Milligan is the legal reckoning that comes AFTER the war and after Lincoln's death — it is the verdict history rendered on the wartime military-commission practice, and belongs as the closing civil-liberties beat, flagged as 1866.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Ex parte Milligan"; Oyez/Justia, *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. 2 (1866); Indiana Historical Bureau, "Lambdin P. Milligan"

## BEAT 7: The Election of 1864 — Lincoln Expects to Lose (Summer 1864)

- **The grim summer:** By August 1864 the war looked stalled and bloody: Grant bogged down in the siege of **Petersburg** after the appalling casualties of the Overland Campaign; **Sherman stalled before Atlanta**; the public weary and blaming Lincoln. Lincoln privately believed he would be **defeated** for re-election.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1864 United States presidential election"; Library of Congress blog, "Abraham Lincoln's Blind Memorandum"

- **The "Blind Memorandum" (August 23, 1864):** Lincoln wrote a short memo (see VERIFIED QUOTES) stating it seemed "exceedingly probable" his administration would **not be re-elected**, and pledging to **cooperate with the president-elect to save the Union** between the election and the inauguration. He **folded and pasted it shut** and had his **cabinet sign the blank outside, sight unseen**, at the Aug. 23 cabinet meeting — Seward, Fessenden, Stanton, Welles, Bates, Usher, and Montgomery Blair signing without knowing the contents. He revealed it to them only **after** his victory, at the **November 11, 1864** cabinet meeting.
  — Source: Library of Congress, "Abraham Lincoln's Blind Memorandum"; House Divided (Dickinson), "Blind Memorandum (August 23, 1864)"; Friends of the Lincoln Collection / Allen Guelzo

- **The Radical challenge — Frémont:** Radical Republicans, unhappy that Lincoln was too soft on the South and slow on Black rights, ran **John C. Frémont** (the 1856 Republican nominee) as a third-party **"Radical Democracy"** candidate (running mate **John Cochrane**), nominated in **late May 1864**. Frémont **withdrew in September 1864**, saying dividing the Union vote was too dangerous — clearing Lincoln's left flank just as the war news turned.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1864 United States presidential election"

- **The National Union ticket — and dropping Hamlin:** To broaden his appeal beyond Republicans to War Democrats and border-state Unionists, Lincoln ran under the **"National Union Party"** banner. At the **National Union Convention, Baltimore, June 7–8, 1864**, the party **dropped sitting Vice President Hannibal Hamlin** (a Maine Republican) and replaced him with **Andrew Johnson** of Tennessee — a **War Democrat** and the only Southern senator who had stayed loyal to the Union. The Lincoln–Johnson "balanced" ticket was meant to symbolize a cross-party, cross-section Union coalition. (Historical irony for the author: Johnson would become one of the most disastrous presidents after Lincoln's assassination — note lightly if at all; it's outside this section's scope.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1864 United States presidential election"

## BEAT 8: The Democratic Convention and McClellan (August 1864)

- **The convention:** **August 29–31, 1864**, in **Chicago, Illinois**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1864 United States presidential election"; American Presidency Project, "1864 Democratic Party Platform"

- **The "war is a failure" peace plank:** The platform contained a **peace plank written by Clement Vallandigham** (yes — the banished Copperhead, now back and a force at the convention). It declared the war a **failure** and demanded an **immediate cessation of hostilities** and a negotiated settlement (see VERIFIED QUOTES for the verbatim plank: "...after four years of failure to restore the Union by the experiment of war..."). This is the decisive fact of the convention: the platform was, in effect, a Copperhead document.
  — Source: American Presidency Project, "1864 Democratic Party Platform"; Wikipedia, "1864 Democratic National Convention"; Teaching American History, "Democratic Party Platform of 1864"

- **The nominee — a WAR Democrat on a PEACE platform:** The convention nominated **Gen. George B. McClellan** — the popular former commander of the Army of the Potomac, whom Lincoln had relieved — for president, with **George H. Pendleton** of Ohio (a peace man) as running mate. The glaring contradiction: McClellan was a **War Democrat** who wanted to keep fighting to restore the Union, running on a platform that called the war a failure. **McClellan repudiated the peace plank** in his acceptance letter, insisting the Union must be preserved and that he could not tell the soldiers their sacrifice had been in vain — leaving the Democratic ticket split against itself (peace platform, war candidate).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1864 United States presidential election"; Wikipedia, "1864 Democratic National Convention"

## BEAT 9: The Turning Point — Atlanta — and the Result

- **The fall of Atlanta (September 2, 1864):** Sherman captured **Atlanta** on **September 2, 1864** — days after the Democrats nominated a "war is a failure" platform. The timing was devastating for the Democrats: the war was visibly **not** a failure. Atlanta's fall (followed by Sheridan's Shenandoah victories that fall) transformed Northern morale and Lincoln's prospects almost overnight. The Democratic peace plank was overtaken by events within a week of its adoption.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1864 United States presidential election"; American Battlefield Trust, "The Election of 1864 and the Soldiers' Vote"

- **The soldier vote — the army kept its commander:** 1864 was the **first U.S. election with widespread absentee/field voting**; about **19–20 Northern states** passed laws letting soldiers vote in camp or by proxy (only Wisconsin had allowed it in 1862). The soldiers — the very army that, by simply voting Lincoln out, could have ended his power — **voted overwhelmingly to keep him**: of **40,247** counted army votes, **Lincoln received 30,503 (≈75.8%)** to McClellan's **9,201 (≈22.9%)**. (Some states tallied soldiers separately, others mixed them in; the ~78% figure is the field-vote share.) The men doing the dying re-elected the man prosecuting the war.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1864 United States presidential election"; American Battlefield Trust, "The Election of 1864 and the Soldiers' Vote"

- **The result (November 8, 1864):**

  | | Lincoln (National Union) | McClellan (Democrat) |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Electoral votes** | **212** | **21** |
  | **Popular votes** | **2,218,388** | **1,812,807** |
  | **Popular %** | **≈55.0%** | **≈45.0%** |

  - **McClellan carried only three states:** **Kentucky, Delaware, and New Jersey** (21 electoral votes).
  - **Turnout** ≈ **73.8%**.
  - Lincoln was the **first president re-elected since Andrew Jackson (1832)**, and the **first Northern president ever re-elected**. It was the **first wartime presidential election since 1812**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "1864 United States presidential election"; American Battlefield Trust; NPS, "Election of 1864"

## BEAT 10: The Meaning — Democracy Survived Itself

- **The framing fact:** In **November 1864**, in the middle of the bloodiest war in its history, a republic held a **free, contested, regularly-scheduled presidential election** — with a real opposition party, a peace platform, anti-administration newspapers, and the incumbent fully expecting to lose. The election went forward **on schedule**; Lincoln **accepted that he might be voted out and pledged to hand over power**; the army voted and went home; and when Lincoln won, the loser conceded. **This is almost unheard of in world history** — wars routinely become the pretext to cancel elections and concentrate power.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "The Election of 1864 and the Soldiers' Vote"; History.com, "How the Union Pulled Off a Presidential Election During the Civil War"

- **Lincoln's own framing (Nov. 10, 1864 serenade response — verify exact wording in *Collected Works* before quoting):** Lincoln said the election had demonstrated that a people's government could "sustain a national election in the midst of a great civil war" — that the question of whether such a government was "not too strong for the liberties of its people, or too weak to maintain its own existence" had been tested and answered. **Author guidance: the *substance* of this is solid; confirm the exact wording from *Collected Works* vol. 8 (Response to a Serenade, Nov. 10, 1864) before putting it in quotation marks.** `[VERIFY exact wording]`
  — Source: widely cited; *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln*, vol. 8 (to be confirmed)

- **The honest verdict on the tension:** The North fought a war for democratic self-government and, in doing so, suspended habeas corpus, arrested thousands without trial, tried civilians before military commissions, banished a congressman, and shut down newspapers. And yet — it also held the election, reversed its own worst suppressions (the Times), and submitted the military-commission practice to the Supreme Court's judgment (*Milligan*, 1866), which curbed it. **The section should land on this paradox, not resolve it cheaply in either direction:** the war tested whether a democracy could survive dissent *and* a wartime election without becoming a dictatorship — and, narrowly and imperfectly, it passed.

### Vallandigham — "war for the negro" (House speech, Jan 14, 1863)
> "War for the Union was abandoned; war for the negro openly begun, and with stronger battalions than before."
— Source: Vallandigham, U.S. House speech, January 14, 1863 (transcript at sesquicentenary.wordpress.com / "Deo Vindice"). NOTE: this is the strongly-sourced verbatim line; surrounding sentences vary by transcript — quote only this line verbatim.

### Vallandigham — the Copperhead slogan (coined May 8, 1862)
> "To maintain the Constitution as it is, and to restore the Union as it was."
— Source: Wikipedia, "Clement Vallandigham" (slogan endorsed by fifteen Democratic congressmen).

### Lincoln — Corning letter, the "wily agitator" passage (June 12, 1863)
> "Must I shoot a simple-minded soldier boy who deserts, while I must not touch a hair of a wiley agitator who induces him to desert? This is none the less injurious when effected by getting a father, or brother, or friend, into a public meeting, and there working upon his feeling, till he is persuaded to write the soldier boy, that he is fighting in a bad cause, for a wicked administration of a contemptable government, too weak to arrest and punish him if he shall desert."

And, concluding the passage:
> "I think that in such a case, to silence the agitator, and save the boy, is not only constitutional, but, withal, a great mercy."

And (the "arrested for acts not opinions" line):
> "Mr. Vallandigham avows his hostility to the war on the part of the Union; and his arrest was made because he was laboring, with some effect, to prevent the raising of troops, to encourage desertions from the army, and to leave the rebellion without an adequate military force to suppress it."
— Source: Lincoln to Erastus Corning and others, June 12, 1863 (abrahamlincolnonline.org, from *Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln*; House Divided, Dickinson College). Original spelling "wiley," "contemptable" preserved.

### Lincoln — Merryman defiance (Message to Congress, July 4, 1861)
> "Are all the laws but one to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?"
— Source: Lincoln, Message to Congress in Special Session, July 4, 1861 (Teaching American History; National Constitution Center). [The "one law" = habeas corpus.]

### The "Blind Memorandum" (August 23, 1864) — FULL TEXT
> "This morning, as for some days past, it seems exceedingly probable that this Administration will not be re-elected. Then it will be my duty to so co-operate with the President elect, as to save the Union between the election and the inauguration; as he will have secured his election on such ground that he can not possibly save it afterwards."
> — A. Lincoln (Washington, Aug. 23, 1864)
— Source: Library of Congress, "Abraham Lincoln's Blind Memorandum"; House Divided (Dickinson). Cabinet signed the sealed outside sight-unseen; revealed Nov. 11, 1864.

### The 1864 Democratic peace plank ("war is a failure") — VERBATIM
> "Resolved, That this convention does explicitly declare, as the sense of the American people, that after four years of failure to restore the Union by the experiment of war, during which, under the pretense of a military necessity of war-power higher than the Constitution, the Constitution itself has been disregarded in every part, and public liberty and private right alike trodden down, and the material prosperity of the country essentially impaired, justice, humanity, liberty, and the public welfare demand that immediate efforts be made for a cessation of hostilities, with a view of an ultimate convention of the States, or other peaceable means, to the end that, at the earliest practicable moment, peace may be restored on the basis of the Federal Union of the States."
— Source: 1864 Democratic Party Platform (American Presidency Project, UCSB; Teaching American History). The decisive clause: "after four years of failure to restore the Union by the experiment of war."

### McClellan repudiates the plank (acceptance letter, Sept. 1864) — PARAPHRASE, verify before quoting
McClellan, in his September 1864 acceptance letter, refused to accept the peace plank, saying in substance that the Union must be preserved and that he could not look the soldiers in the face and tell them their sacrifices had been in vain. **Author guidance:** the substance is well-attested; pull the exact wording from McClellan's acceptance letter before quoting verbatim. `[VERIFY exact wording]`
— Source: Wikipedia, "1864 United States presidential election" (substance); McClellan acceptance letter, Sept. 8, 1864 (to confirm).

## CONTESTED / FLAGGED

1. **Civilian arrests "13,535" (HIGH PRIORITY — DO NOT state as precise).** The classic figure is unreliable; Neely (*Fate of Liberty*, 1991) calls 13,535 "unverifiable and erroneous," documents ≥14,000, and says the true total is higher and unknowable. Also: most arrestees were NOT Northern political dissenters (many were blockade-runners, smugglers, deserters, border-area civilians). Use "**more than 13,000, and by the leading study likely more**," and do not imply they were all political prisoners.

2. **Copperhead name — exact first-use date.** "First used July 20, 1861, by the *New York Tribune*" is widely repeated but is a secondary-source claim; treat the *date* as approximate. The snake-vs-Liberty-cent double meaning is the solid, sourceable fact.

3. **Soldier-vote percentage (~78% vs 75.8%).** Sources give "over 70%," "~75.8%" (30,503 of 40,247 counted *field* votes), and "~78%." These count differently (some states tallied soldiers separately; others folded them into the home total, so a full national soldier-share is not cleanly recoverable). **Use "roughly three-quarters / about 78% of the field/army vote went to Lincoln," and cite the 30,503-of-40,247 figure as the documented army-vote count.**

4. **Popular-vote percentages.** Lincoln ≈55.0% / McClellan ≈45.0% is standard (2,218,388 vs 1,812,807). Minor rounding differences across sources; "about 55%/45%" is safe. Electoral 212–21 is undisputed.

5. **Lincoln's Nov. 10, 1864 "sustain a national election in the midst of a great civil war" remarks.** Substance solid; confirm exact wording from *Collected Works* vol. 8 (Response to a Serenade, Nov. 10, 1864) before quoting verbatim. `[VERIFY]`

6. **McClellan's repudiation of the peace plank.** Substance well-attested; pull exact wording from his Sept. 8, 1864 acceptance letter before quoting. `[VERIFY]`

7. **General Order No. 38 — exact wording.** Paraphrases circulate ("declaring sympathies for the enemy will not be tolerated"; "treason, expressed or implied"). The substance (forbade expressions of sympathy for the enemy; authorized military-commission trials in the Department of the Ohio; issued by Burnside, April 13, 1863) is solid; pull the precise text from the order itself if quoting verbatim.

8. **Vallandigham's Mount Vernon (May 1, 1863) speech.** No fully reliable verbatim transcript survives (it was an outdoor stump speech; the army's note-takers and his own account differ). Use his documented *January 14, 1863* House quote for verbatim material; describe the Mount Vernon speech in paraphrase ("King Lincoln," defied Order No. 38), not in quotation marks.
