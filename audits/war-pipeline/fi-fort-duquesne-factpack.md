## A. The prize: the Forks of the Ohio

1. The Forks of the Ohio is the point where the Allegheny River and the Monongahela River meet to form the Ohio River; the site is present-day Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. [WP-Forbes][WP-FtD]
2. The French built Fort Duquesne at the Forks in 1754; it served as the French staging base for raids on British frontier settlements. [WP-FtD][ABT]
3. The Forks was the strategic objective the war had been fought over since 1754. The earlier British attempts on it: George Washington's 1754 campaign (Jumonville Glen skirmish, then his surrender at Fort Necessity in July 1754) and Major General Edward Braddock's 1755 expedition, which was destroyed at the Battle of the Monongahela on July 9, 1755 on the road to Fort Duquesne. [WP-Forbes][MV] (Braddock detail cross-referenced from the existing Monongahela fact pack/draft in this repo.)
4. The Forbes Expedition of 1758 was the third and final British attempt to take Fort Duquesne / control the Forks of the Ohio. [WP-Forbes]

## B. John Forbes and the protected advance

5. Commander: Brigadier-General John Forbes (British), a Scot, promoted brigadier-general 28 December 1757. [DCB] He was a veteran of the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–48) and the Jacobite campaigns of 1745–46. [Encyclopedia/Wikipedia bio]
6. Second-in-command: Lieutenant Colonel Henry Bouquet, a Swiss professional officer in British service. [WP-Forbes]
7. French commander at Fort Duquesne: Captain François-Marie Le Marchand de Lignery (French). [WP-FtD][ABT]
8. Forbes was gravely ill throughout the campaign with the "bloody flux" (dysentery), plus a painful skin condition. By September 1758 he could travel only in a litter (hammock) slung between two horses. [DCB][MV]
9. Strategy contrast with Braddock: instead of following Braddock's 1755 route, Forbes cut a NEW road westward across Pennsylvania (the Forbes Road), and ADVANCED METHODICALLY, building defensible stockades / supply forts at intervals (DCB: roughly 40 miles / ~64 km apart) to protect his line of communication back to the rear, rather than racing forward. This was a deliberate "protected advance." [DCB][MV]
10. The Forbes Road ran west from Raystown (present-day Bedford, Pennsylvania). Forward depots / forts on the line included Fort Bedford (at Raystown) and Fort Ligonier. [DCB][WP-Forbes]
11. The road, per DCB, ran about 193 miles (≈311 km) and the campaign took about five months from the start of the road to the fall of the fort. [DCB] (NOTE: popular accounts often round the new road to "about 100 miles / ~160 km" of wilderness cut [MV]; treat the road's total length as roughly 100–193 mi / 160–311 km depending on what segment is counted. DISPUTED/scope-dependent — present as "roughly a hundred miles of new road" or hedge.)

13. ## C. Grant's defeat at Fort Duquesne (Grant's Hill), September 14, 1758

15. Date: September 14, 1758. [WP-FtD][DCB][ABT]
16. Major James Grant (British), 77th Regiment (Highlanders), led an advance reconnaissance-in-force toward Fort Duquesne. He was authorized/sent forward on about September 11, 1758. [WP-FtD]
17. Grant's force size: about 800–850 men. [WP-FtD][ABT][DCB] (WP gives 850; ABT/DCB give ~800. RANGE: ~800–850.)
18. Grant exceeded his reconnaissance mission and attempted to provoke/strike the fort. He badly underestimated the garrison: he believed the fort held about 200 men; the actual French/Native force was roughly 500 French + 300 Native allies under Lignery (≈800), or "about 600" per some accounts. [WP-FtD][ABT] (RANGE: French side ~600–800 engaged.)
19. Grant divided his force (a decoy company with drums/pipes, an ambush party, a separate Virginia contingent under Major Andrew Lewis near the baggage). The French/Native force sortied, the Native warriors fought from cover / from the high ground while the Highlanders were caught in close order, and the British were routed. [WP-FtD]
20. British/American casualties: about 340–342 total. [WP-FtD][ABT] One detailed WP tabulation: 104 killed, ~220 wounded, 18 captured, of which 232 casualties were from the 77th Regiment; 5 of 8 Virginia officers killed. [WP-FtD] (Use "about 340" / "roughly a third of his force.")
21. French/Native casualties: very light — about 8 killed and 8 wounded (WP-FtD); ABT phrases it as a handful (ABT's "324 French and 16 Indian casualties" appears to be a typo/transposition — DISCARD that ABT casualty number; use the ~8 killed / ~8 wounded figure). [WP-FtD]
22. Major Grant survived but was taken prisoner. Survivors retreated to Fort Ligonier. [WP-FtD][ABT]
23. The location is remembered as Grant's Hill, in downtown Pittsburgh. [WP-FtD/Clio]
24. Despite the win, Lignery understood ~600 men could not hold Fort Duquesne against Forbes's main force of more than ten times that number. [WP-FtD]

### Fort Ligonier
25. The French later struck at Forbes's forward outpost Fort Ligonier (Battle of Fort Ligonier) to disrupt the advance or seize supplies, and were repulsed. [WP-Forbes]

## D. The French supply line cut: Fort Frontenac, August 1758

26. Fort Frontenac (at Cataraqui, present-day Kingston, Ontario), at the point where Lake Ontario flows into the St. Lawrence, controlled the French line of communication to their western posts including Fort Duquesne and Fort Niagara. [WP-Frontenac]
27. Lieutenant Colonel John Bradstreet led roughly 3,100 (mostly provincial) troops in a raid; the fort surrendered August 27, 1758 and was looted and burned. [WP-Frontenac] (Some accounts date the fall August 26–27. RANGE: Aug 26–27, 1758.)
28. Captured/destroyed: an enormous quantity of provisions, naval stores, munitions, trade goods, and 60+ cannon, much of it intended to supply the western posts. [WP-Frontenac]
29. The loss cut the supply line to Fort Duquesne; the French commander-in-chief Montcalm regarded the fall of Frontenac as serious. Fort Duquesne fell before the year was out, in part because its supply was severed. [WP-Frontenac]

## E. The Treaty of Easton, October 1758 — the decisive (diplomatic) blow

30. Concluded October 26, 1758 at Easton, Pennsylvania. Negotiations ran over a week. [WP-Easton]
31. Parties: the British colonial governors of Pennsylvania and New Jersey and representatives of 13 Native American nations, including Iroquois, Lenape (Delaware), and Shawnee. Over 500 Native Americans attended the outdoor ceremony. [WP-Easton]
32. Key Native figure: Teedyuscung, called "King of the Delawares," advised by Charles Thomson (treaty secretary). Conrad Weiser served as British interpreter/arbitrator. Pennsylvania Attorney General Benjamin Chew documented proceedings. [WP-Easton]
33. Terms: the Native nations agreed not to fight alongside the French; Pennsylvania returned previously ceded lands to the Iroquois; the British recognized tribes' hunting rights in the Ohio valley; and the governors committed to refrain from establishing colonial settlements WEST of the Allegheny Mountains after the war. The Lenape ceded remaining New Jersey land claims for 1,000 Spanish dollars; PA Governor William Denny agreed to negotiate directly with the Lenape without Iroquois mediation. [WP-Easton]
34. INDEPENDENT-ACTORS FRAMING (well documented): the Ohio nations were pursuing their OWN interest — keeping both empires (and especially British settlers) off their land. The promise to limit settlement west of the mountains is WHY they pulled away from the French. [WP-Easton, and synthesis with Post material]
35. EFFECT (the central fact): the treaty neutralized / drew away the Ohio Native nations (Lenape/Delaware, Shawnee, Mingo) who had been allied with or fighting alongside the French at Fort Duquesne. This loss of Native support is widely described as the single most important factor in the weak, near-bloodless French collapse at the Forks. [WP-Easton][DCB][WP-Forbes] DCB: the Easton conference "secured the neutrality of the Delawares, Shawnees, and Mingos."

### Carrying the word: Christian Frederick Post
36. Christian Frederick Post, a Moravian missionary, made a 1758 mission (and a second, Oct 25, 1758 – Jan 10, 1759) on behalf of the Pennsylvania government to the Delaware, Shawnee, and Mingo on the Ohio, to detach them from the French alliance and carry news of the Easton peace. [WP-Post]
37. He traveled with Pisquetomen, an influential Delaware (Lenape) headman, through great danger, carrying the Easton terms to the nations on the Allegheny and Ohio. [WP-Post]

## F. The fort blown up; British occupation; Fort Pitt and Pittsburgh

38. Cut off — Native allies gone (Easton), the Frontenac depot destroyed, winter closing, supplies failing — Lignery's small garrison blew up / burned Fort Duquesne and abandoned it on November 24, 1758, retreating under cover of darkness. [WP-FtD][WP-Forbes][DCB][ABT][Heinz]
39. Forbes's army occupied the ruins at the Forks on November 25, 1758, taking the place, in the period phrase, "without stroke of sword." [Heinz][WP-Forbes]
40. Forbes ordered November 26, 1758 a "Day of publick Thanksgiving to Almighty God for our Success," with celebratory musket and artillery fire on the 27th. [Heinz]
41. Forbes renamed the site after William Pitt, the British Secretary of State (the war minister driving Britain's North American effort): Pittsburgh / Pittsbourgh / Fort Pitt. [DCB][Heinz][WP-Forbes]
42. Forbes left about 200 troops under Colonel Hugh Mercer to build a temporary fort and hold the Point through the winter; this was the foundation of Fort Pitt. [Heinz]
43. Forbes left Pittsburgh December 3, 1758, and died in Philadelphia March 11, 1759, aged 51. [DCB][Heinz]

## G. The broken promises — the thread forward

44. The British did not keep the Easton promise to limit settlement west of the Alleghenies. Despite Easton, and despite later efforts by Bouquet at Fort Pitt to remove squatters, the colonial population west of the mountains grew rapidly. [WP-Easton]
45. The Royal Proclamation of 1763 (October 7, 1763) extended the Allegheny settlement line of Easton, but few settlers respected it. [Proc]
46. The Lenape felt betrayed by the broken Easton promises and unchecked settlement; this grievance fed Pontiac's War (1763–1765). [WP-Easton, Journal of the American Revolution synthesis] (Causal language: "contributed to" / "fed" — do not over-claim a single cause.)
