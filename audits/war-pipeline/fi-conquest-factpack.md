## 1. PITT'S GRAND STRATEGY: THE THREE-PRONGED 1759 PLAN

**William Pitt the Elder** (British Secretary of State, no side-tag needed — he's the London architect, not a field commander) devised a coordinated three-front invasion of New France for 1759:

1. **Eastern / St. Lawrence prong** — Major General James Wolfe to sail from Louisbourg up the St. Lawrence and capture Quebec, the capital of New France.
2. **Western / Lake Ontario prong** — Brigadier General John Prideaux (British) to capture Fort Niagara on Lake Ontario, severing France's western supply and fur-trade network.
3. **Northern / Lake Champlain prong** — General Jeffery Amherst to drive north through Fort Ticonderoga (formerly Fort Carillon) and Fort St. Frédéric toward Montreal.

**Strategic objective:** The three armies would converge on the heart of New France — Montreal and Quebec. If all three succeeded, France would be severed from its western fur-trade lifeline, cut off from the Ohio Country, and left isolated along the St. Lawrence.

**Source:** Encyclopedia.com, "1759: The Fall of Canada"; Search results via encyclopedia.com + motherbedford.com

**Pitt recruited 20,000 troops from American colonies; governors delivered ~17,000.** Source: encyclopedia.com summary of Pitt's 1759 campaign.

## 2. THE ANNUS MIRABILIS — 1759, BRITAIN'S YEAR OF VICTORIES

1759 became known in Britain as the "Annus Mirabilis" (Year of Miracles / Year of Victories). North American victories were part of a global sweep that included:
- Fort Niagara (July 1759) — western New France severed
- Fort Ticonderoga and Fort St. Frédéric (July 1759) — abandoned by the French without major battle
- Fall of Quebec (September 18, 1759)
- Battle of Quiberon Bay (November 1759) — Admiral Edward Hawke destroyed France's Atlantic fleet, killing ~2,500 French sailors vs. ~300 British; eliminated any hope of French naval reinforcement or invasion of Britain

**Source:** encyclopedia.com, "1759: The Fall of Canada"; Amherst Wikipedia article

## 3. BATTLE OF FORT NIAGARA AND BATTLE OF LA BELLE FAMILLE (July 1759)

### The Siege of Fort Niagara

**Dates:** July 6–26, 1759 (American Battlefield Trust gives July 2–26)  
**Location:** Present-day Youngstown, New York (modern New York State)

**British/Allied commanders:**
- Brigadier General John Prideaux (British) — killed in action during the siege
- Sir William Johnson (British, Superintendent of Indian Affairs) — took command after Prideaux's death
- Sayenqueraghta (Iroquois / Seneca leader) — allied with British

**French commander:**
- Captain Pierre Pouchot (French) — defended the fort; taken prisoner

**How Prideaux died:** "Decapitated after accidentally stepping in front of a mortar" — killed by friendly fire (shell fragment from one of his own guns). Source: American Battlefield Trust, Fort Niagara article. ⚠️ The Wikipedia article says "killed when a shell fragment from one of his own guns hit him" — the ABT's "decapitated" is the more specific account; use cautiously; both agree it was friendly fire.

**Command succession:** Johnson held a royal colonel's commission as commander of Iroquois auxiliaries. This legitimized his authority over regular army officer Lieutenant Colonel Frederick Haldimand (British), who technically outranked him in the regular army but lacked the royal commission. Source: Wikipedia, Battle of Fort Niagara.

**French strength:** Pouchot had ~500 defenders when the siege began. He had earlier sent ~2,500 men southward on orders, leaving the fort undermanned. Source: encyclopedia.com

**British/Allied strength:** ~2,200 regulars (44th and 46th Regiments, 60th companies), ~2,500 New York militia, ~700 Rhode Island militia + ~600 Iroquois. American Battlefield Trust gives total British forces as 6,520; French as 6,000 (apparently counting the relief force). Source: ABT / Wikipedia.

### The Iroquois Neutrality at La Belle Famille — a Pivotal Moment

Pouchot's Seneca (Iroquois) allies who had been inside the fort **withdrew** from the French position rather than fight against other Iroquois allied with the British. Before the relief battle, the British-allied Iroquois told their French-allied counterparts that they intended to remain neutral in the upcoming clash — and urged them to do the same. Many French-allied Native warriors deserted the expedition as a result. This was a decisive blow to French capability.

**Source:** Wikipedia, Battle of La Belle-Famille; ABT, Fort Niagara 1759 article

### Battle of La Belle Famille (July 24, 1759)

**Exact date:** July 24, 1759  
**Location:** Two miles south of Fort Niagara, along the Niagara River portage trail

**French commanders of the relief force:**
- François-Marie Le Marchand de Lignery (French) — mortally wounded
- Charles Philippe Aubry (French)
- Michel Maray de La Chauvignerie (French)

**British/Allied commanders:**
- Eyre Massey (British, 46th Foot) — commanded the regulars; wounded
- Sir William Johnson (British) — commanded Iroquois forces

**Forces:**
- French: ~800 regulars and militia + ~500 Native Americans (many deserted before battle)
- British/Allied: ~350 regulars + ~100 New York militia + ~450 Iroquois

**Ambush:** Johnson positioned forces at a chokepoint where the French relief column had to pass. British held fire until the enemy was at close range.

**Casualties:**
- French: At least 334 killed, 96+ captured (including Lignery); French sources suggest possibly 500 killed
- British/Allied: ~12 dead, 40 wounded

**Source:** Wikipedia, Battle of La Belle-Famille; oldfortniagara.org (Pennsylvania Gazette account)

### Fort Niagara's Surrender

Following La Belle Famille, Pouchot surrendered Fort Niagara on **July 26, 1759**. He initially disbelieved reports of his relief force's destruction but verified it with prisoners. He surrendered with "Honours of War." Garrison of ~607 surrendered.

**Strategic significance:** The capture "initiated a chain reaction that saw the French abandon several key western forts," including Fort Rouillé (burned by its garrison) and Fort St. Frédéric. Britain gained control of the Great Lakes and the Ohio Valley. The western pillar of New France had collapsed.

**Source:** ABT; Wikipedia, Battle of Fort Niagara; oldfortniagara.org

## 4. AMHERST'S LAKE CHAMPLAIN ADVANCE (Summer 1759)

General Jeffery Amherst personally led the Lake Champlain prong. In July 1759 he advanced against **Fort Carillon** (Ticonderoga) and **Fort St. Frédéric** (Crown Point). The French abandoned and demolished both forts before Amherst arrived — he captured them with minimal casualties (5 killed, 31 wounded at Ticonderoga) compared to the British catastrophe there in 1758 under Abercromby (2,000 casualties). Amherst halted at Crown Point to build a new fort and did not reach Montreal in 1759; his advance was slower than Pitt had hoped.

**Source:** encyclopedia.com, "1759: The Fall of Canada"; Amherst Wikipedia article

## 5. THE QUEBEC CAMPAIGN AND SIEGE (June–September 1759)

### Wolfe's Force

**James Wolfe** sailed from Louisbourg with approximately **8,500 troops** (reduced from a planned 12,000, partly due to a measles outbreak). His fleet under Admiral **Charles Saunders** (British, Royal Navy) was the indispensable instrument — without the navy's ability to operate in the St. Lawrence, the campaign was impossible.

British forces established a base camp on **Île d'Orléans** on June 26–28, 1759. The fleet entered the river and Quebec came under threat.

**Source:** encyclopedia.com; Wikipedia, Siege of Quebec 1759

### French Defensive Position

Montcalm commanded approximately **12,000–13,390 troops** along the St. Lawrence (⚠️ sources vary: the Wikipedia article on Plains of Abraham gives 13,390 available; encyclopedia.com gives "roughly 12,000 defenders"). The position was extremely strong — "the strongest country in the world," Wolfe reportedly wrote.

Governor General **Pierre de Rigaud de Vaudreuil de Cavagnial** (French) was the civilian commander of New France. He and Montcalm had deep strategic disagreements throughout the war: Vaudreuil favored colonial guerrilla/defensive methods using Native allies; Montcalm preferred European-style formal warfare and was dismissive of Indigenous tactics.

**Source:** Wikipedia, Louis-Joseph de Montcalm; encyclopedia.com

### The Bombardment of Quebec City

British artillery established a battery at **Point Lévis** in early July 1759. The bombardment:
- Lasted approximately two months, beginning June/July 12
- Total ordnance fired: **more than 10,000 bombs and 40,000 cannon balls** (three times the amount used at Louisbourg in 1758)
- Buildings destroyed: **503 houses and one church** in Quebec City proper
- Lower Town: over 50 of the best houses destroyed; vaults with merchandise also damaged
- Specific landmarks destroyed: Quebec's cathedral (July 22–23); Church of Notre-Dame-des-Victoires (August 8–9)
- British used **carcasses** — incendiary devices made of meal-powder, saltpetre, sulphur, broken glass, shavings of horn, pitch, turpentine, tallow and linseed oil; they included loaded pistol barrels to prevent defenders from extinguishing them

**Civilian impact:** Parish priest **Jean-Félix Recher** recorded: *"the gunfire and the bombardment... terrorized the whole town… the women, with their children… were continually in tears, wailing and praying."* Women formed groups reciting rosaries seeking divine protection.

**Source:** militaryheritage.com, "The British Fire Bombing of Quebec, 1759"

### Scorched-Earth in the Countryside

While the city burned, Wolfe dispatched troops to destroy French settlements along the St. Lawrence. An estimated **1,400 stone houses and manors** were destroyed throughout the countryside during August 1759. Many colonists were killed.

Wolfe issued written orders stating his intention to *"set the town on fire with shells, to destroy the harvest, houses and cattle"* if Quebec did not surrender. This has widely been regarded as counterproductive, driving neutral inhabitants toward active resistance.

In early September, a large raiding party under **Major George Scott** (British), including all six American ranger companies in the army, was sent to lay waste the south shore from Kamouraska to Pointe-Lévy.

**Source:** Wikipedia, James Wolfe article; search summary citing multiple sources including ResearchGate article "Wolfe at the Gates: Guerilla Warfare and Hyperviolence in the Siege of Quebec, 1759"

### The Night Landing at the Anse-au-Foulon (September 12–13, 1759)

After months of failed attempts to force Montcalm into open battle, Wolfe selected **L'Anse-au-Foulon** (Wolfe's Cove) as his landing site on September 12. The cove was located approximately **three kilometers (about 1.9 miles) upstream from Cap Diamant**, below a cliff face **53 meters (174 feet) high**.

Under cover of darkness on the night of September 12–13, British forces scaled the cliff. The French sentries were deceived. ⚠️ Which officer answered the French sentries in French is disputed — sources name either Captain Fraser or Captain Donald McDonald. The camp atop the cliff was commanded by **Louis Du Pont Duchambon de Vergor** (French), who failed to sound the alarm adequately.

By dawn, approximately **4,400–5,000 British troops** stood on the Plains of Abraham, west of the city walls.

**Source:** Wikipedia, Siege of Quebec 1759; Wikipedia, Battle of Plains of Abraham

## 6. BATTLE OF THE PLAINS OF ABRAHAM (September 13, 1759)

**Date:** September 13, 1759  
**Location:** The Plains of Abraham, west of Quebec City walls

### British Commanders
- **James Wolfe** — mortally wounded (no side-tag per author note)
- Robert Monckton (British) — second in command; wounded
- George Townshend (British) — took overall command after Wolfe fell
- James Murray (British) — brigade commander
- William Howe (British) — led light infantry at Foulon ascent
- Charles Saunders (British, Royal Navy) — fleet commander

### French Commanders
- **Louis-Joseph de Montcalm** — mortally wounded (no side-tag per author note)
- Louis Antoine de Bougainville (French) — commanded a separate force upstream at Cap Rouge; arrived too late
- Louis Du Pont Duchambon de Vergor (French) — commanded the Anse-au-Foulon guard post; failed to prevent the landing

### Forces Engaged
- **British:** 4,426 regulars with 2 bronze 6-pounder cannon. Note: Wolfe's total expedition force was ~8,500; only a portion was deployed on the plains.
- **French:** approximately 3,500 deployed (mix of regulars, militia, and Indigenous allies) from a total available of ~13,390. ⚠️ Sources vary significantly on French strength deployed.

### The Battle

Around **10 a.m.**, Montcalm ordered a general advance on the British position rather than waiting for reinforcements. This decision is *"largely considered to be Montcalm's greatest mistake"* (Wikipedia, Montcalm article). Had he waited, Bougainville's separate force could have arrived to hit the British rear, potentially trapping Wolfe's men with nowhere to retreat but back down the cliff face.

**Wolfe's tactical innovation:** He deployed his men in a **two-rank line** rather than the traditional three ranks, increasing the width and firepower of his formation.

British forces held their fire until the French approached within approximately **30 yards (27 m)**, then delivered devastating volleys. The French force broke and retreated.

**Bougainville's failure:** By the time Bougainville's force reached the Plains of Abraham, the battle was over. He had been upstream at Cap Rouge and missed the British night movement downriver.

### Casualties
- **British:** 58 killed, 600 wounded (Source: Wikipedia, Plains of Abraham)
- **French:** ⚠️ Highly contested — Wikipedia gives 116–350+ killed, 600 wounded, 350 captured; encyclopedia.com gives "approximately 1,400 killed or wounded" for the combined battle and immediate aftermath. The higher figure likely includes the subsequent days' fighting.

### Deaths of Both Commanders

**Wolfe's death:**
Wolfe was struck multiple times — in the wrist, stomach, and chest. He was carried to the rear. His recorded final exchange (per Francis Parkman, *Montcalm and Wolfe*, 1884, drawing on contemporary accounts):

When told "The enemy, sir… they give way everywhere," Wolfe reportedly ordered: *"Then... tell Colonel [Burton], to cut off their retreat from the bridge."*

His last reported words: *"Now, God be praised, I will die in peace!"*

⚠️ Variant versions exist: "Now, God be praised, I die contented" / "I die contented & on the very spot I wished to die." — These are secondary-source reconstructions, not verbatim transcription.

**Wolfe and Gray's Elegy:**
The night before the battle (evening of September 12, 1759), Wolfe reportedly recited Thomas Gray's *Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard* to his officers in a boat on the St. Lawrence, ending: *"Gentlemen, I would rather have written those lines than take Quebec."*

**Source of anecdote:** John Robison, a young midshipman present that night who later became Professor of Natural Philosophy at the University of Edinburgh. The account was transmitted through him and appears in Francis Parkman's *Montcalm and Wolfe* (1884). Wolfe's personal annotated copy of Gray's *Elegy* is held at the University of Toronto Library. Most historians regard the anecdote as plausible and multiply attested through Robison, though it is not a contemporary written record. **[VERIFY: treat as well-sourced oral tradition, not a confirmed verbatim quote. Mark as traditionally reported.]**

**Montcalm's death:**
Montcalm was shot while riding toward Quebec's walls with the retreating forces. A woman on the street recognized his wound and cried out; he reportedly said: *"It's nothing, it's nothing… don't be troubled for me."* He was carried to the home of the Marquis de Vaudreuil.

His surgeon informed him he would not survive the night. When told he had only a few hours to live, Montcalm reportedly replied: *"So much the better. I am happy that I shall not live to see the surrender of Quebec."*

⚠️ Also reported as: *"All the better. I will not see the English in Quebec."* These are different paraphrases of the same reported sentiment. The primary historical source is Francis Parkman's *Montcalm and Wolfe* (1884); Parkman used primary documents but the exact wording varies by source. The phrase "I shall die content" does not appear prominently in scholarly sources. **[Mark as: traditionally reported, Parkman source, exact wording uncertain.]**

Montcalm died at approximately **5:00 a.m., September 14, 1759**. He was initially buried in a shell hole beneath the Ursuline church choir in Quebec City. His remains were relocated in 2001 to a mausoleum at the Hôpital-Général de Québec cemetery.

**Source:** Parkman, *Montcalm and Wolfe* (1884) via canadahistory.com primary source transcription; Wikipedia, Louis-Joseph de Montcalm; search results aggregating multiple sources.

## 7. THE FALL OF QUEBEC (September 18, 1759)

Quebec City formally surrendered on **September 18, 1759**. The Articles of Capitulation for Quebec were signed by **Jean-Baptiste Nicolas Roch de Ramezay** (French), the town's acting governor — Vaudreuil had retreated with the remaining French army.

Brigadier General **George Townshend** (British) accepted the surrender and then immediately returned to Britain, handing the Quebec garrison command to Brigadier General **James Murray** (British).

**Source:** Wikipedia, Battle of Plains of Abraham; Wikipedia, Siege of Quebec

---

## 8. MURRAY'S WINTER IN QUEBEC (1759–1760)

Murray commanded approximately **7,000–8,000 British troops** through the brutal Quebec winter. Disease and cold reduced his force severely; by spring 1760 he had roughly **3,800–4,000 effective men**. The winter of 1759–60 was extremely harsh; scurvy, cold, and illness killed hundreds of soldiers.

**Source:** Wikipedia, Battle of Sainte-Foy (force figures)

## 9. BATTLE OF SAINTE-FOY (April 28, 1760)

### Context

**François Gaston de Lévis** (French, Chevalier de Lévis) had assumed command of all French forces in North America after Montcalm's death. He spent the winter of 1759–60 reorganizing and retraining the demoralized French army at Montreal.

In spring 1760, Lévis marched downstream toward Quebec at the first breaking of the ice, attempting to retake the city before the Royal Navy could arrive.

### Battle Facts

**Date:** April 28, 1760  
**Location:** Sainte-Foy (west of Quebec City walls), near present-day Quebec City, Quebec

**Commanders:**
- French: François Gaston de Lévis (French)
- British: James Murray (British)

**Forces:**
- French: ~5,000 deployed from a total army of ~6,910, with only **3 cannons**
- British: ~3,800 regulars with **20 cannons and 2 howitzers**

**The battle:** Murray made the controversial decision to sortie from Quebec's fortifications and attack Lévis before his deployment was complete. Initial British success masked their disadvantage. The battle became a two-hour close-range fight; French counterattacks collapsed the British left flank. The British *"spiked and abandoned their guns in the retreat."* Murray's force fell back into Quebec.

**Casualties:**
- French: 833 total (193 killed, 640 wounded)
- British: 1,182 total (292 killed, 837 wounded, 53 captured)

The battle is described as "one of the bloodiest battles ever fought on Canadian soil."

**Outcome:** French tactical victory — a rare French field victory in the entire war. But Lévis lacked the artillery and siege equipment to assault Quebec's fortifications. The battle decided nothing by itself.

**Source:** Wikipedia, Battle of Sainte-Foy

### The Sea Decides It

Lévis settled in for a siege, hoping for French ships to arrive first. Both sides awaited the spring thaw and the first vessels.

The ships that appeared in the St. Lawrence were **British**: HMS *Lowestoffe*, HMS *Diana*, and HMS *Vanguard* arrived in May 1760. They **destroyed Lévis's supply ships** on the river.

Why no French ships came: France's Atlantic naval capacity had been broken at **Quiberon Bay** (November 1759) and the **Battle of the Bay of Chaleur** (July 1760). France could not send a relief fleet.

Lévis lifted the siege and retreated to Montreal, where he would make his final stand.

**Source:** Wikipedia, Battle of Sainte-Foy; encyclopedia.com, Quiberon Bay context

## 10. THE FALL OF MONTREAL — THREE ARMIES CONVERGE (Summer–September 1760)

### Amherst's Three-Pronged Convergence

Amherst directed approximately **18,000 British troops** converging on Montreal from three directions beginning in July 1760:

**Force 1 — Amherst** (~10,000 men, including ~4,000 regulars: 42nd, 44th, 46th, 55th regiments + provincials + 700 Iroquois)
- Route: From Lake Ontario (Fort Oswego), down the St. Lawrence River
- Challenge: Navigated the dangerous Lachine rapids; lost **84 men drowned and 46 boats wrecked** in the rapids

**Force 2 — Murray** (~4,000 men: 15th, 28th, 35th, 43rd, 47th, 48th, 58th, 60th, 78th regiments + rangers)
- Route: West from Quebec along the St. Lawrence
- Actions en route: Disarmed inhabitants of riverside parishes; thousands of Canadian militia deserted the French cause and went home

**Force 3 — Brigadier General William Haviland** (British) (~3,400–3,500 men: 17th, 22nd, 27th, 40th, 60th regiments + Rogers' Rangers)
- Route: North from Crown Point via Lake Champlain and the Richelieu River
- Haviland sailed north on August 11, crammed aboard 80 whaleboats, 330 bateaux, and 4 radeaux
- Key action: Besieged and took **Île-aux-Noix** (where Bougainville commanded the French garrison); Bougainville evacuated on the night of August 27–28 after a British raid seized his schooner and support vessel
- Then took Fort Saint-Jean and Fort Chambly

All three armies surrounded Montreal by **September 6–7, 1760**.

**Source:** Wikipedia, Montreal campaign article; historynet.com on Île-aux-Noix

### Vaudreuil's Capitulation

Governor General Vaudreuil presented **55 articles of capitulation** on September 7. Amherst rejected French demands for honors of war, insisting troops *"lay down their arms."*

**September 8, 1760:** Vaudreuil signed the unconditional surrender of New France.

### Amherst's Denial of Honors of War

**Jeffery Amherst** refused to grant Lévis and his army the customary military honors of war (marching out with colors flying, drums beating). His stated justification in reply to Lévis's envoy:

> *"I am fully resolved, for the infamous part the troops of France have acted in exciting the savages to perpetrate the most horrid and unheard of barbarities in the whole progress of the war, and for other open treacheries and flagrant breaches of faith, to manifest to all the world by this capitulation my detestation of such practices."*

**Source:** Montreal Rampage, "1760: The Capitulation of Montreal"; search results quoting Amherst's answer to La Pause's envoy. This quote is multiply sourced and regarded as accurate.

### Lévis Burns the Colors

Denied the right to march out with their colors, Lévis ordered his battalions to **burn their regimental standards** rather than surrender them as British trophies. The flags of the regiments of New France were burned.

⚠️ One source (erenow.org, drawing on *Crucible of War* by Fred Anderson) adds a nuance: French officers subsequently claimed under oath that their colors had been *"destroyed years earlier"* — a face-saving agreed-upon fiction that Amherst accepted without pressing. This detail is in a secondary synthesis source; verify against Anderson's *Crucible of War* if possible.

**Source:** Montreal Rampage; erenow.org, *Crucible of War* (Anderson)

### Articles of Capitulation — Key Terms (55 Articles)

- **French regular troops:** Laid down arms; could not serve during the present war; transported to France
- **French Canadian civilians (Article 37, 39):** Property ownership protected; could not be forcibly transported to British colonies; retained *Coutume de Paris* property law
- **Catholic Church (Articles 27–28):** Free exercise of Roman Catholic faith guaranteed; clergy could continue functions
- **Seigneurs and clergy:** Rights and privileges retained
- **Slavery:** Capitulation explicitly protected *"the continuation of the ownership of Black and Indian slaves by French Canadians"* ⚠️ — a term Amherst granted
- **First Nations — Article 40:** *"The savages or Indian allies of his most Christian Majesty, shall be maintained in the lands they inhabit"* — promised sovereignty, land rights, religious freedom, and no punishment for having fought for the French
- **Acadians:** Promised identical treatment to Canadians, but the British refused repatriation of Acadian prisoners held in New England — a notable exception
- **Most articles were granted** except those relating to the Acadians

**Source:** Wikipedia, Articles of Capitulation of Montreal; english.republiquelibre.org full text of the Articles

## 11. NATIVE NATIONS — SHIFTING CALCULATIONS AND THE COLLAPSE OF THE FRENCH COUNTERWEIGHT

### During 1759–1760: Pragmatic Neutrality Replacing Alliance

As French power collapsed, Native nations throughout the Great Lakes and Ohio Country made pragmatic calculations:

- The **Iroquois (Haudenosaunee)** Seneca at Fort Niagara withdrew rather than fight other Iroquois — kin ties overrode alliance obligations. Before La Belle Famille, both sets of Native allies negotiated neutrality with each other. Many French-allied warriors deserted before the battle.
- In the 1760 Montreal campaign, approximately **800 French-allied warriors were disarmed by William Johnson** (British). Many had already deserted the French cause; some joined British forces as scouts and prisoner handlers.
- **Johnson** (British, Superintendent of Indian Affairs) negotiated neutrality treaties in exchange for future friendship guarantees.
- **Article 40** of the Montreal capitulation promised Native allies land sovereignty and no punishment — but these promises would prove empty.

**Source:** Wikipedia, Battle of La Belle-Famille; Wikipedia, Montreal campaign; Articles of Capitulation, Article 40

### The Removal of the French Counterweight

The French had maintained relationships with Native nations through trade, gift-giving, and treating them as military partners. With French power gone:

- General Jeffery Amherst's post-war policies eliminated gift-giving — a diplomatic tradition central to Franco-Indian relations — and restricted gunpowder and ammunition sales
- Amherst believed Native nations would have to accept British rule without the French to turn to; he stationed only ~500 troops at western forts
- Shawnee diplomat **Nimwha** articulated the Native perspective: *"You think yourselves Masters of this Country, because you have taken it from the French, who, you know, had no Right to it, as it is the Property of us Indians."*
- **Neolin** (Delaware/Lenape), the "Delaware Prophet," catalyzed spiritual resistance: *"If you suffer the English among you, you are dead men. Sickness, smallpox, and their poison [alcohol] will destroy you entirely."*
- By 1761, **Senecas** were sending war messages to Great Lakes and Ohio Country tribes urging united resistance
- **Pontiac** (Ottawa leader) launched coordinated attacks in May 1763 — Pontiac's War — after Native nations learned of the formal French cession of Canada to Britain in the Treaty of Paris

**Source:** Wikipedia, Pontiac's War; search results from battlefields.org and study.com summaries

## 12. HUMAN COST — THE WAR'S PRICE ON CANADA

### Military Casualties (Overall French and Indian War, North America)
- British regulars: ~3,000 killed in action or died of wounds; ~10,400 died of disease
- British provincial troops and Native allies: ~11,000+ died of all causes
- **Total: ~25,000 dead** (British side alone)
- French casualty figures are less precisely documented in available sources

**Source:** Wikipedia, French and Indian War infobox

### Quebec City Destruction
- 503 houses and one church destroyed in the city proper
- 10,000+ bombs and 40,000 cannon balls fired during the bombardment
- Lower Town nearly leveled
- Cathedral and Notre-Dame-des-Victoires church destroyed
- Countryside: ~1,400 stone houses and manors destroyed in the surrounding parishes

### The City the British Inherited
When Quebec surrendered in September 1759, the British inherited a devastated, largely ruined city. The population had endured three months of constant bombardment. The subsequent winter under Murray was one of scarcity; the civilian population suffered alongside the garrison.

## Revision additions (verified) — round 2

### A. French fireship / fire-raft attacks on the British fleet at Quebec, 1759

**Two separate attacks confirmed:**

**Attack 1 — June 28, 1759 (fireships):** France launched seven fireships and two fire-rafts against Admiral Sir Charles Saunders' fleet anchored off Île d'Orléans. Saunders had received advance warning; boats from the fleet were sent out and grappled the burning vessels, towing them clear. No British ships were damaged. Confirmed FAILED.

**Attack 2 — July 28, 1759 (fire-rafts):** A much larger raft attack — approximately 100 fire-rafts — was sent down the St. Lawrence about a month later. Fared no better; the fleet's boats again towed the rafts safely aside. Confirmed FAILED.

**Strategic consequence:** Both failures left Saunders' fleet undamaged and free to support the siege and, on the night of September 12–13, to ferry Wolfe's troops to the Anse-au-Foulon landing below the Heights of Abraham.

**Safe draft language:** "On the night of June 28, the French launched seven fireships and two fire-rafts at the British fleet anchored off Île d'Orléans. Saunders had been warned; his sailors rowed out, grappled the burning hulls, and towed them clear. A month later, on July 28, France tried again with roughly a hundred fire-rafts — same result."
