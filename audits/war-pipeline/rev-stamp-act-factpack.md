# Fact Pack — "The Stamp Act" (Revolution / Causes arc, Chapter 2)

**Status:** Source-of-truth for the author. NOT reader-facing. Zero-hallucination bar.
**Scope:** The Stamp Act crisis, 1765–1766. Covers passage, the colonial explosion,
the British response and repeal, and what it meant.

**Confidence tags:** HIGH = corroborated across reference sources / primary record.
MED = generally accepted but with a soft edge (estimate, single strong source,
common-but-loose phrasing). DISPUTED = historians genuinely disagree, or the only
source is late/single — handle with care, flag in prose.

**Hand-off note from Chapter 1 ("The Bill Comes Due"):** Already established and NOT
to be re-explained — the post-1763 war debt, the Proclamation Line of 1763, the Sugar
Act of 1764 (a trade duty), vice-admiralty courts (no jury), the external-vs-internal
tax distinction, "virtual representation" vs "actual representation," James Otis Jr.'s
1764 pamphlet, and "no taxation without representation." Chapter 1 ends teeing up the
Stamp Act as the first tax aimed at the INTERNAL side of that line. Start there.

## SECTION 1 — WHAT THE STAMP ACT WAS

- The Stamp Act received royal assent / was passed by Parliament on **March 22, 1765**,
  and was set to take effect **November 1, 1765**. (HIGH)
- It passed the House of Commons by a wide margin (49 opposed; the "for" count is
  reported variously as **205 or 245** across standard references — Wikipedia gives 205,
  NPS/others give 245) and passed the House of Lords without a recorded division
  (effectively unopposed there). (DISPUTED on the exact "for" tally — do NOT assert one
  figure as settled; say "a wide margin" or give the 205/245 range; "49 opposed" is firm.)
- It was sponsored under Prime Minister **George Grenville** (First Lord of the Treasury,
  the head of the ministry that pushed it). (HIGH)
- Purpose: raise revenue from the colonies to help pay down Seven Years' War / French and
  Indian War debt and to help fund the British army stationed in North America. (HIGH)
- It was the **first DIRECT / INTERNAL tax** Parliament laid on the colonies — a tax on
  activity inside the colonies, as opposed to the Sugar Act of 1764, which was a duty on
  trade (external). This is the chapter's hinge: it landed squarely on the internal side
  of the line Chapter 1 set up. (HIGH)
- Mechanism: it required that a wide range of paper goods be printed on, or carry, an
  embossed **tax stamp** (stamped paper purchased from royal distributors). (HIGH)
- Taxed items (a deliberately broad list): legal documents, contracts, court documents,
  ship's papers / clearances, licenses, **newspapers**, **pamphlets**, **almanacs**,
  **playing cards**, and **dice**. (HIGH)
- Why it bit so hard: it fell precisely on the loud, literate, organized, articulate
  classes — **lawyers** (legal paper), **printers and newspapermen** (newspapers,
  pamphlets, almanacs), **merchants** (ship's papers, contracts) — and on everyday
  social life (playing cards, dice in taverns). It touched daily life nearly everywhere.
  (HIGH — this is the standard analysis; frame as analysis, not a dated event.)
- Payment had to be made in **hard money / specie** (scarce coin), not colonial paper
  currency — a real burden given chronic specie shortage in the colonies. (HIGH)
- Violations were to be tried in **vice-admiralty courts** — i.e., **without a jury**
  (judge only). This is continuous with the Sugar Act enforcement Chapter 1 established;
  the no-jury point is a live grievance. (HIGH)
- It was **modeled on a stamp duty that already existed in Britain itself**. Stamp duties
  on vellum, parchment, and paper had been levied in England since **1694** (an act of
  the reign of William III and Mary II, originally to help fund war against France).
  Grenville's view: Britons at home had paid a stamp tax for ~70 years, so it was fair to
  extend it. Useful for presenting the British side as reasoned. (HIGH)

## SECTION 2 — THE COLONIAL EXPLOSION (1765)

### Patrick Henry and the Virginia Resolves
- **Patrick Henry** introduced a set of resolves in the Virginia House of Burgesses on
  **May 29, 1765** (some sources say "late May 1765"). (HIGH on Henry + late May; the
  exact day is most often given as May 29.)
- He proposed **seven** resolutions. The House actually **adopted only the first four**;
  the **fifth was passed and then struck from the record the following day** (it was
  rescinded once the more radical Henry had reportedly left); the sixth and seventh were
  **never formally adopted** (too extreme). (HIGH — this distinction matters and is often
  flattened.)
- Crucial propagation fact: **newspapers across the colonies printed ALL SEVEN** resolves
  as though all had passed, including the most inflammatory. The colonies' shared sense of
  Virginia's defiance was built on resolves Virginia had not actually enacted. (HIGH — a
  great "the myth traveled faster than the fact" beat.)
- The "**Caesar had his Brutus**" episode: in debate Henry is said to have warned, in
  substance, that "Caesar had his Brutus, Charles the First his Cromwell, and George the
  Third —" at which point he was interrupted with cries of "**Treason!**" (the Speaker,
  **John Robinson**, among them), and Henry finished, "**— may profit by their example.
  If this be treason, make the most of it.**" (DISPUTED — see Handle-With-Care; the exact
  words, the alleged apology, and even which ancient names he used vary by account.)

### The Stamp Act Congress
- The **Stamp Act Congress** met in **New York City** from **October 7 to October 25,
  1765**. (HIGH)
- **Nine colonies** sent delegates (of the 13); **27 delegates** total. (HIGH — nine
  colonies is firm; "27 delegates" is the standard count.)
- It produced a **Declaration of Rights and Grievances** (agreed ~October 19, 1765),
  petitioning the King and both houses of Parliament. (HIGH)
- The Declaration's substance: colonists are entitled to the rights of Englishmen,
  including **no taxation without consent** (through their own representatives) and the
  **right to trial by jury**; only colonial assemblies may tax the colonies. It **also
  professed loyalty and allegiance to the Crown** — it was cautious and constitutional,
  not a break. (HIGH — the loyalty/caution point is essential to the anti-inevitability
  framing.)
- Significance: the **first time representatives of multiple colonies gathered and acted
  collectively** to oppose a British measure — an early rehearsal of inter-colony
  coordination. (HIGH)

### The Sons of Liberty
- The **Sons of Liberty** were local resistance organizations that formed in 1765 (Boston,
  New York, and elsewhere), coordinating protest and crowd action. (HIGH)
- The **name** is generally traced to a February 1765 speech in the House of Commons by
  Irish-born MP **Isaac Barré**, who called the colonists "**sons of liberty**" while
  opposing the tax. Date usually given as **February 6, 1765**. (MED-HIGH — the Barré
  origin is the standard account; treat the exact day as MED.)

### Crowd / mob action (Boston)
- **August 14, 1765:** a Boston crowd hung an **effigy of Andrew Oliver** — the
  Massachusetts **stamp distributor** — from the Liberty Tree, then paraded and destroyed
  it, pulled down a building said to be his stamp office, and went to his house. **Oliver
  resigned the next day** (Aug 15). (HIGH)
- **August 26, 1765:** a Boston crowd **ransacked the mansion of Lt. Gov. Thomas
  Hutchinson** (who was also chief justice), gutting the interior — woodwork, doors,
  furniture, his library and papers — and his garden. Damage was estimated at roughly
  **£2,200** at the time; some accounts add ~£900 in cash taken. (HIGH on the event and
  the ~£2,200 estimate; treat the cash figure as MED.)
- Hutchinson **privately thought the Stamp Act was bad policy** but, as a Crown officer,
  held that the law must be obeyed until repealed. The mob attacked a man who actually
  opposed the tax. (HIGH — central to the "Britons vs Britons" / divided-colonists frame.)

### Coercion of distributors → de facto nullification
- Across the colonies, **stamp distributors were intimidated into resigning** (effigies,
  threats, crowd pressure) before November 1, so there was almost **no one left to sell
  the stamps**. (HIGH)
- Result: the Act was **effectively nullified** — **almost no stamps were ever sold or
  used** in the mainland colonies; courts and presses largely carried on without stamped
  paper or briefly suspended. (HIGH — phrase as "effectively nullified / barely enforced,"
  not "literally zero stamps anywhere.")

### Merchant nonimportation (boycott)
- **Nonimportation agreements** — merchants pledging to stop importing British goods until
  repeal — began in late 1765. **New York merchants** agreed on **October 31, 1765**;
  **Boston** and **Philadelphia** merchants followed (late 1765). New York is often cited
  as ~200 merchants. (HIGH on NYC Oct 31, 1765 and the NY→Boston→Philadelphia spread;
  the ~200 figure is MED.)
- These were among the first large-scale colonial economic boycotts and became a reusable
  tool. Agreements were dropped after repeal news reached the colonies (NYC heard
  **April 26, 1766**). (HIGH)

### The death's-head / tombstone newspaper protest
- **The Pennsylvania Journal and Weekly Advertiser**, printed by **William Bradford**,
  ran a famous protest front page dated **October 31, 1765** (the eve of the Act taking
  effect): the paper laid out like a **tombstone / gravestone**, with a **skull-and-
  crossbones** mock stamp ("An emblem of the effects of the STAMP"), the line
  "**EXPIRING: In Hopes of a Resurrection to LIFE again,**" and a coffin notice mourning
  "the last Remains of the Pennsylvania Journal." (HIGH)
- Note: Bradford **did not actually stop printing** — the paper continued (e.g., the
  November 7 edition). The "death" was theatrical protest, not a real shutdown. (HIGH —
  good honesty beat; don't let it read as a literal closure.)

## SECTION 3 — THE BRITISH RESPONSE & REPEAL

- The colonial **boycott hurt British merchants and manufacturers**, who **petitioned
  Parliament** for repeal on economic grounds. (HIGH)
- The **Grenville ministry fell** and was replaced by the **Rockingham ministry**
  (Charles Watson-Wentworth, **2nd Marquess of Rockingham**) in **July 1765** — note this
  was over court/political reasons, before the worst colonial unrest, not because of it;
  but the new ministry was more conciliatory toward the colonies. (HIGH on July 1765 and
  Rockingham; the "why Grenville fell" nuance is HIGH — it was a falling-out with the King,
  not the colonies.)
- **Benjamin Franklin** (then in London as a colonial agent) was **examined before the
  House of Commons** (sitting as Committee of the Whole) on **February 13, 1766**. He
  answered scores of questions (commonly cited as **174**) and argued the colonies
  accepted external trade duties but rejected internal taxes like the stamp tax; his
  testimony was influential and widely circulated. (HIGH on the Feb 13, 1766 examination
  and its influence; the "174 questions" count is MED-HIGH.)
- The **Stamp Act was repealed on March 18, 1766**. (HIGH)
- **CRITICAL — same day:** Parliament paired repeal with the **Declaratory Act (1766)**,
  asserting Parliament's full authority to make laws binding the colonies "**in all cases
  whatsoever.**" Repeal of the *tax*, NOT the *principle*. (HIGH — the "in all cases
  whatsoever" wording is exact and load-bearing.)
- Repeal was justified to Parliament on grounds of **expediency** (it was hurting British
  trade and was unenforceable), explicitly NOT as conceding the colonists' constitutional
  argument — the Declaratory Act exists precisely to make that point. (HIGH)

## SECTION 4 — WHAT IT MEANT / THE AFTERMATH

- The colonies **celebrated repeal as loyal Britons** — as a victory won *inside* the
  empire by petition and protest, not a step toward leaving it. (HIGH)
- New York commissioned, in gratitude, **two statues** from London sculptor **Joseph
  Wilton**: a statue of **William Pitt the Elder** (the British statesman credited with
  pushing repeal) and a **gilded lead equestrian statue of King George III** (King in
  Roman dress, ~4,000 lb). Both were **commissioned 1766**; both **statues went up in
  1770** — **George III on Bowling Green (erected Aug 21, 1770)**, **Pitt on Wall Street**.
  (HIGH — VERIFY/PRECISION: the statues honor repeal but were ERECTED in 1770, several
  years later; don't imply they went up in 1766. The George III statue was famously pulled
  down July 9, 1776 — that's a later chapter, do not narrate.)
- The colonists largely believed they **had won and wanted to stay in the empire**. In
  1765–66 independence was essentially nobody's goal. (HIGH)
- But the **Declaratory Act meant nothing was actually settled**: Parliament had conceded
  the tax while explicitly reaffirming the very power the colonists denied it had. The
  **constitutional gap remained open**. (HIGH)
- The crisis built durable colonial tools: **inter-colony organization** (the Stamp Act
  Congress precedent, the **Sons of Liberty** network, committees of correspondence to
  come) and the **habit of coordinated economic boycott** — the playbook they would reuse
  at the next flashpoint. (HIGH)
- Set-up for next chapter (do NOT narrate): the Townshend duties of 1767 would test the
  unresolved principle almost immediately. Leave it as a tee-up only.

## DISPUTED / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

- **No inevitability / no patriotic-myth frame.** In 1765 the colonists were NOT a united
  people marching toward independence. They protested **as aggrieved Britons demanding the
  rights of Britons** — petitions, professions of loyalty, "rights of Englishmen." The
  Stamp Act Congress's Declaration explicitly affirmed allegiance to the Crown. Document
  that independence was not the goal. (HIGH that this is the accurate frame.)
- **The colonists were divided.** Many were ambivalent about, or alarmed by, the **mob
  violence**. The **ransacking of Hutchinson's house disturbed even many opponents of the
  tax**, and Hutchinson himself privately opposed the Stamp Act. Elites were uneasy about
  unleashing crowds; the Sons of Liberty / crowds spanned a real range of motives (genuine
  principle, local grievance, opportunism). This supports the war's "first civil war"
  thesis — Britons vs Britons in 1765, before American vs American later. (HIGH.)
- **Repeal was driven substantially by British economic self-interest**, not by Parliament
  conceding the constitutional argument. Merchant boycott pressure + unenforceability did
  the work; the **Declaratory Act is the proof** Parliament gave up nothing in principle.
  Do not let repeal read as a clean colonial moral victory. (HIGH.)
- **"Virtual representation" (carried over from Ch.1) was a sincere, serious British
  constitutional doctrine**, not a cynical sham. Present the British side as reasoned (and
  note Grenville's genuine "Britons at home already pay this" logic). (HIGH that it should
  be framed as sincere.)
- **Patrick Henry's "Caesar / treason" speech is DISPUTED.** The fullest version (Caesar–
  Brutus / Charles I–Cromwell / George III "may profit by their example" / "If this be
  treason, make the most of it") rests heavily on a **single account found decades later**
  (a French traveler's journal of May 1765, surfaced in the 20th century), which differs
  from later popular versions. Accounts vary on the **exact ancient names** (Tarquin /
  Julius / Caesar), on whether Henry **apologized / half-apologized** to the House, and on
  the precise wording. Safely sayable: Henry gave a defiant, near-treasonous speech that
  invoked tyrant-killers as a warning to George III and was met with cries of "treason."
  Flag the rest as tradition, not transcript. (DISPUTED.)
- **Don't overstate the Stamp Act Congress.** It was **cautious and professed loyalty**;
  it petitioned, it did not threaten. (HIGH.)
- **"No stamps were used" is near-true but not absolute** — write "effectively nullified /
  barely enforced," not "zero stamps existed anywhere." (MED — guard the phrasing.)
- **The Virginia Resolves "as passed" ≠ "as printed."** Only four (arguably five briefly)
  of seven passed; newspapers spread all seven. Don't credit Virginia with enacting the
  radical resolves. (HIGH.)
- **The statues went up in 1770, not 1766.** They honor the 1766 repeal but were erected
  years later. Precision matters. (HIGH.)
- **Grenville's ministry fell (July 1765) over a royal falling-out**, not over colonial
  protest. Don't imply the colonists toppled Grenville. (HIGH.)
