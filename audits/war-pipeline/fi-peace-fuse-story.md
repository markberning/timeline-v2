# Storytelling Critic — "The Peace and Its Fuse"
> Gate: STORYTELLING · Vertical: War (French & Indian) · Draft: fi-peace-fuse-draft.md

---

## Overall Grade: **GOOD** (with two MUST-IMPROVE sections pulling it toward NEEDS WORK)

The draft carries the right macro-shape — every section builds toward the "victory as fuse" thesis — and the prose is clean, fact-accurate, and house-voice in most places. The intro section and "The Fuse" closer are genuinely strong. But two sections are doing consequences-list work where they should be doing story work: "The Treaty of Paris" is a recitation, and the Pontiac's War section lands as a chronicle instead of the moral climax the chapter promises it will be.

---

## Section Grades

### Intro (paragraphs 1–2) — **STRONG**
Sets the paradox cleanly, earns the reader's attention. The phrase "almost everything that went wrong for Britain over the next twenty years flowed directly from the size of that victory" is exactly the hook this chapter needs. No violations.

### The Conquered and the Conquerors — **GOOD**
Murray's "conquered yet dignified" quote is the right human anchor. The four-year military rule detail is genuinely surprising and earns its space. The section reads a bit like a timeline (the dates cluster), but the facts are interesting enough to carry it.

**FLAG — em-dash:** "military régime lasted roughly four years, the longest stretch of British military rule over a civilian population in Canadian history to that point" — no em-dashes here, fine. Clean.

### The War the Treaty Was Still Finishing — **GOOD**
The Havana/Manila double-capture lands well — "two of its greatest colonial cities in a single year" is a genuinely good line. The card-table metaphor implicit in "Britain held cards nobody had imagined three years earlier" works.

Minor: The Pocock/Keppel/Grimaldi name-dump feels like a roster. The reader doesn't need all three negotiators named before the treaty terms even appear. Cut to Bedford for the British side and let the others drop to passing reference or omit.

### The Treaty of Paris — **NEEDS WORK**
This is the chapter's weakest section. It lists terms and quotes a line from the treaty itself, which is exactly the "consequences list" failure mode the gate is watching for. The treaty quote ("confines between the dominions...shall be fixed irrevocably") adds nothing a reader can feel. It is a legal instrument, not a story moment.

**MUST-IMPROVE:** There is no *human* reaction to the treaty anywhere in this section. No colonist's relief that the French are finally gone. No French Canadian's dread at what the signature means for them. The Fontainebleau secret-transfer detail is genuinely interesting (France secretly handed Spain the west while no one was watching) but it arrives as a footnote instead of as the quiet-knife-in-the-back moment it is.

The cross-ref pill at the end is correct placement.

**House-voice violation:** "The negotiators were John Russell, 4th Duke of Bedford (British), César Gabriel de Choiseul (French), and Jerónimo Grimaldi, 1st Marquis of Grimaldi (Spanish)." This sentence is a Wikipedia citation list. The negotiators are not characters in this chapter — drop the roster or integrate one name with a human detail.

### Canada or Guadeloupe — **STRONG**
This is the best section of the draft. The Choiseul "calculation" is handled with admirable care (you flag it as historian-attributed, not his exact words), the pamphlet count is a vivid specificity, and the Franklin quote earns its place. The payoff — "the calculation, whoever made it, proved exactly right" — is a clean, low-key gut-punch. No violations.

### The Bill Comes Due — **STRONG**
The "Britain had won the world and mortgaged itself to do it" line is the chapter's best single sentence. The Grenville-taxed-Americans-because-cider-tax logic is a great story beat — absurd and true. The Stamp Act Congress closing paragraph does the job of showing the pan-colonial moment forming. The cross-ref pill is well-placed.

**Minor:** The parenthetical on the debt figure ("The figure varies across sources...so it is best given as approximate") is honest but slightly academic in tone. The house voice would deliver this as "roughly £130 million — the exact figure depends on whose accounting you trust, but the shape of it is plain" and move on.

### The Line on the Map — **STRONG**
The Washington detail is the section's payoff and it earns it: the man who fired the war's first shots now had a personal grievance, and he quietly ignored the line he'd helped create. That is excellent story-level irony. "The Proclamation Line allied frontier settlers, ordinary veterans, and rich speculators in a single resentment" is a crisp structural sentence.

**FLAG:** "It had been sketched out before the war's end as a way to manage westward expansion in an orderly fashion. But it was a crisis in the west, which we will come to, that turned the idea into law in a hurry." — "which we will come to" is a meta-narrator aside. Cut it; just say "And it was Pontiac's War that turned the idea into law in a hurry" and the reader arrives there naturally.

### The Master of Life — **GOOD**
Neolin reads well. The gift-giving-as-rent framing ("the tribute and reciprocity that signified an ongoing relationship, the rent paid for permission to occupy Native land") is one of the draft's better explanatory moves. The structure — Amherst's contempt → Neolin's vision → the connection between them — is correct and well-paced.

**Minor flag:** "a coherent vision of renewal and of taking back a homeland, a spiritual program with a political edge" — that last phrase ("a spiritual program with a political edge") leans slightly academic. Sharpen it: "not a call to riot — a call to reclaim a world."

### Pontiac's War — **NEEDS WORK**

This is the section where the draft does the most and earns the least. The fall of the forts is genuinely dramatic material — eight forts overrun in two months, only Niagara, Detroit, and Fort Pitt left standing — and the draft narrates it as a list with pronunciations. The reader is told "this was not a riot. It was a coordinated military offensive that nearly swept the British out of the interior in two months" but is never made to *feel* that stunning reversal.

**MUST-IMPROVE:** The section needs at least one human moment among the fort falls — a garrison's last dispatch, a commander's surprise, a Native warrior's account if one exists — to make the "stunning rush" land as a story rather than a fact. The word "stunning" is doing all the emotional work; the prose is not.

The structure right now is: council → ruse at Detroit → siege → forts fall (list) → end of section. That is chronological but flat. Consider reordering so the list of fallen forts arrives *as a shock* — cut to Detroit still holding while everything around it collapses, so the reader grasps the scale of what's happening before they learn Detroit survived.

**FLAG — meta-narrator:** "Older accounts cast him as the mastermind of a single coordinated conspiracy; historians now see him more accurately as a powerful regional leader whose early successes inspired others to rise, rather than a supreme commander directing a continental plan." This is a fine historiographic note but it arrives in the middle of a battle story and pulls the reader out. Move it to a parenthetical or cut to one sentence: "He was a powerful regional leader whose early successes inspired others to act, not a mastermind directing a continent-wide conspiracy."

### The Blankets at Fort Pitt — **STRONG**
This is the section that most closely hits the moral weight the chapter needs. The sequencing — Trent's journal first, then Amherst's letter, then the historian's honest "intent is documented, effect is not" — is rigorous and unsettling in the right way. The two direct quotes carry the weight without the narrator editorializing. "Extirpate this execrable race" needs no embellishment.

The opening ("Fort Pitt was besieged from late June 1763, with nearly 550 people crowded inside, more than 200 of them women and children") is the section's strongest opening image and it works.

### Bushy Run and the Stalemate — **GOOD**
The stalemate framing is correct and necessary. "Hardly a surrender at all" is the right note to close Pontiac's formal peace on. The Pontiac assassination detail (killed by a Peoria warrior in a quarrel unrelated to the British) is the kind of specificity the house voice is built for.

**Minor:** The casualty paragraph ("Roughly 400 British soldiers... around 450 colonial civilians... at least 200 Native warriors, with the toll on Native communities certainly higher and largely unrecorded") is correct but would land harder if the final clause had a sentence of its own: "The Native toll is almost certainly higher, and largely unrecorded — which is itself part of the story." As written it reads as a footnote; as a beat it becomes the moral point of Bushy Run.

### The Fuse — **STRONG**
This closing section does exactly what the chapter promised. The feedback loop — debt → taxes → resentment → army → more taxes → more resentment — is clearly stated and drives to the right conclusion. "What replaced it was a single empire that regarded them with contempt, a flood of settlers the empire could not or would not hold back, and a frontier of violence on every side" is the chapter's moral close for the Native nations and it earns the weight it's given.

The final sentence ("The victory had lit it") is clean and lands.

**Minor:** "The Proclamation Line, the one barrier between them and the settlers, would not survive the next war." This is strong. Consider adding one clause that names the cost: "...and the nation built from that war would finish the dispossession the French and Indian War began." Names the chain explicitly.

## MUST-IMPROVE

**1. "The Treaty of Paris" section: give the treaty a human reaction.**
The treaty terms land as a legal recitation. Cut the treaty-text quote (it adds no story value), drop the three-negotiator name roster, and replace with one human beat — a French Canadian learning the terms, or a Choiseul observation on what France just gave away. The Fontainebleau secret transfer deserves to be the section's knife-in-the-back moment, not a footnote: "Three months before the treaty was signed, France had already given the west away — to Spain, in secret, while the ink on Paris was still wet."

**2. "Pontiac's War" section: make the fort cascade land as a story, not a list.**
The eight-fort collapse is the section's dramatic core and it reads like a gazette. Add one human ground-level moment from the fall of the forts. Restructure so the list arrives as a shock after the Detroit siege begins — the reader should feel the interior crumbling around the one fort that held. Move the historiographic note on Pontiac-as-mastermind to a subordinate clause or cut it; it interrupts the battle story.

## NICE-TO-HAVE

- **"The Bill Comes Due" debt-figure parenthetical:** soften the accounting disclaimer into the house voice — "roughly £130 million, give or take whose ledger you trust."
- **"The Master of Life" closing phrase:** "a spiritual program with a political edge" is slightly academic; sharpen.
- **"Bushy Run and the Stalemate" casualty paragraph:** give the Native casualty note its own sentence — "The Native toll is almost certainly higher, and largely unrecorded" — so it reads as the moral point it is, not a caveat.
- **"The Line on the Map" meta-aside:** cut "which we will come to" and substitute "Pontiac's War" by name.
- **"Pontiac's War" Pontiac-mastermind note:** compress to a subordinate clause so it doesn't interrupt the action.
