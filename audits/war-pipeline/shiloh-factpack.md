## 2. CASUALTIES

### Official / Widely Cited Figures

| Side | Killed | Wounded | Missing/Captured | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Union** | 1,754 | 8,408 | 2,885 | **13,047** |
| **Confederate** | 1,728 | 8,012 | 959 | **10,699** |
| **Combined** | 3,482 | 16,420 | 3,844 | **~23,746** |

*Sources: Wikipedia infobox; American Battlefield Trust (cites 10,669 CS, slight rounding variant); Britannica (~10,000 per side, rounded).*

### Scholarly Range / Higher Estimates
- Some historians estimate actual totals may reach **~14,500 Union** and **~12,000 Confederate**, accounting for soldiers who died of wounds in subsequent weeks. [Wikipedia]
- ABT: "Of 110,000 soldiers engaged, 23,800 became casualties (3,500 killed)." [ABT article]

### The Shock in Context
- The ~23,746 combined casualties exceeded the combined casualties of all major prior battles: First Bull Run, Wilson's Creek, Fort Donelson, and Pea Ridge totaled roughly ~12,000. [ABT; Wikipedia]
- **CONTESTED CLAIM — "more than all previous American wars combined":** Frequently repeated (including in the Ken Burns Civil War series) but *disputed by historians*. The ~3,482 battle deaths at Shiloh are roughly one-third of the ~8,624 Americans killed in battle during the Revolutionary War alone (per Howard Peckham's study). The claim holds better for *total casualties* (killed + wounded + missing) if comparing only to *prior Civil War engagements*, not all American wars back to 1775. **Flag this if using it in prose — specify "all previous battles of this war" or note the claim is contested.** [CivilWarTalk forum thread citing Peckham; Wikipedia]
- Samuel B. Todd, brother of Mary Todd Lincoln, was killed at Shiloh. [Wikipedia]

## 3. WHAT CAME BEFORE

### The Western Theater Context (February–March 1862)
- **Feb. 6, 1862** — Grant captured **Fort Henry** on the Tennessee River. [ABT Fort Donelson fact page]
- **Feb. 16, 1862** — Grant captured **Fort Donelson** on the Cumberland River, taking ~12,000–15,000 Confederate prisoners and earning the nickname "Unconditional Surrender Grant." After these defeats, Confederate Gen. Johnston withdrew from Kentucky, ceding much of western and central Tennessee to Union forces. [ABT; Wikipedia]
- The strategic objective: the **Memphis and Charleston Railroad** and the rail junction at **Corinth, Mississippi** — where the M&C crossed the Mobile and Ohio Railroad. Seizing Corinth would sever the Confederacy's key east-west rail line. [Britannica; ABT]

### Grant Encamps at Pittsburg Landing
- Gen. **Henry W. Halleck** (US), commanding the Department of the Missouri, ordered Grant to move his army south to Pittsburg Landing on the Tennessee River and **wait for Buell's Army of the Ohio** before advancing on Corinth. [ABT; historyofwar.org]
- Grant's Army of the Tennessee (six divisions) encamped around **Shiloh Church** and the surrounding woods and fields between early March and April 5, 1862. The army spent this time drilling recruits. [ABT; ABT article "Shiloh"]

### No Entrenchments — and Why
- Grant chose **not to entrench** his army. His stated reason: he did not want to dampen the army's "offensive spirit" and did not expect to fight a major defensive battle there. His intention was to advance, not defend. [NPS "Was Grant Surprised" article; ABT]
- Sherman, commanding day-to-day operations at the camps, stated explicitly: *"I always acted on the supposition that we were an invading army. We did not fortify our army against an attack, because we had no orders to do so, and because such a course would have made our men timid."* [ABT "Shiloh" article; Sherman's own statement in official records]
- Grant wrote on **April 3** that his army would "advance on Corinth by Saturday the 5th" and expressed confidence: "I do not feel that there is the slightest doubt about the result." [NPS "Was Grant Surprised" article, citing Grant correspondence]

### Confederate Plan: Strike First
- Johnston assembled roughly **44,000–45,000 men** at Corinth as the Army of Mississippi, planning a surprise offensive strike to destroy Grant's army before Buell could reinforce him. [Wikipedia; ABT]
- Johnston set out from Corinth on **April 3**, intending a one-day march. Rain, complicated orders, rugged terrain, and inexperienced troops turned it into a **three-day trek**, not reaching jump-off positions until the night of April 5. [History.com; ABT]
- **Beauregard wanted to call off the attack** on the evening of April 5, convinced the delays had alerted the Union army and that the element of surprise was lost. Johnston overruled him with a reported statement: *"I would fight them if they were a million."* [ABT "Shiloh" article; History.com]
- Despite Beauregard's fears, Grant and Sherman had dismissed the skirmishing and reports of Confederate movements. The Union army was not alerted. [NPS "Was Grant Surprised"]

## 4. DAY 1 — APRIL 6, 1862

### The Dawn Surprise
- **~5:00 am** — A Union patrol under Col. Everett Peabody encountered Confederate skirmishers in **Fraley Field** (a farm clearing south of the Union camps). This was the opening contact. [Wikipedia chronology]
- **5:30 am** — Johnston ordered the general Confederate attack to begin. [Wikipedia]
- The Confederate army attacked in three parallel corps-sized lines from the southwest, driving toward Pittsburg Landing to the northeast. The axis of advance was generally from **Shiloh Church** toward the river. [Wikipedia; ABT]
- The Union camps were overrun in succession. Many soldiers were still cooking breakfast. [ABT article]
- **~7:00 am** — Sherman confirmed a major engagement; he was slightly wounded. [Wikipedia]
- **~9:00 am** — Grant arrived at Pittsburg Landing by steamboat from his headquarters at Savannah, Tennessee (9 miles downstream). He immediately ordered Lew Wallace's reserve division to march from Crump's Landing. [Wikipedia]

### Sherman's Stand
- Sherman commanded the **5th Division** on the Union right, nearest Shiloh Church, bearing the initial Confederate onslaught. He repeatedly rallied his men, was wounded twice (hand and shoulder), and had three horses shot under him. [Wikipedia; ABT]
- Sherman managed to establish successive defensive positions, slowing the Confederate advance on the right and buying time. [ABT]

### The Hornet's Nest / Sunken Road
- The center of the Union line held along an **old farm lane** — a slight depression in some places, later called the "Sunken Road" — running through the edge of a field (Duncan Field) fronted by dense brush. This position became known as the **Hornet's Nest** from the sound of bullets in the thickets. [Wikipedia; ABT "Shattering Myths"]
- **Brig. Gen. W.H.L. Wallace** (US, 2nd Division) and **Brig. Gen. Prentiss** (US, 6th Division) held this position for approximately **six hours**, repelling repeated Confederate assaults. [ABT; Wikipedia]
- Confederate forces made multiple infantry charges — estimates range from **8 to 14** separate assaults — all of which were repulsed. [Wikipedia] The exact number is contested.
- **~4:00 pm** — Confederate Brig. Gen. **Daniel Ruggles** assembled a massed battery of **approximately 62 cannons** against the Hornet's Nest — described as "the largest assembly of artillery in the war up to that time." [ABT "Shattering Myths" article; Wikipedia says 50+]
- The bombardment + encirclement broke the Union position.
- **~4:15 pm** — W.H.L. Wallace was **mortally wounded** (shot in the head/eye area) while attempting to lead his division out of encirclement; most of his men escaped but he was left behind and died April 10. [Wikipedia]
- **~5:30 pm** — The remaining Union troops in the Hornet's Nest pocket — approximately **2,200 men including Prentiss** — surrendered. This was the largest Union surrender of the day. [Wikipedia; ABT]

> **CONTESTED — Sunken Road depth:** Post-war veterans increasingly described the road as deeply sunken, like a trench. But contemporary battle reports contain no mention of it being depressed. Post-battle photographs show a simple path. The word "sunken" did not appear in descriptions until an **1881 publication**. Modern historians (including research cited by ABT) conclude the road was a slight farm track, not a genuine entrenchment — veterans embellished the story over decades. [ABT "Shattering Myths" article; History.com]

> **CONTESTED — Hornet's Nest significance:** Traditional accounts credit Prentiss's stand as saving Grant's army by buying crucial hours. Revisionist historians argue the position was less decisive than claimed, that W.H.L. Wallace's brigades did more of the actual holding, and that Prentiss received outsized credit partly because he was the only surviving officer to publicize his account. [ABT "Shattering Myths" article]

> **CONTESTED — Bloody Pond:** A "bloodstained pond" between the Peach Orchard and Wicker Field became a staple of Shiloh lore. Modern researchers have found **no contemporary evidence** that such a pond existed at that location in 1862. The sole source is a local civilian's account recorded decades later. [History.com; ABT "Shattering Myths"]

### The Peach Orchard
- An orchard south of the Hornet's Nest position, held by **Brig. Gen. Stephen Hurlbut's** (US) brigades, providing open ground with clear artillery fields of fire. Scene of intense fighting on Apr. 6. [ABT]
- Johnston was killed in or near the Peach Orchard area. [Wikipedia; ABT]

### Johnston's Death
- **~2:30 pm, April 6** — Johnston was shot behind the **right knee** while personally leading an attack near the Peach Orchard sector. The bullet nicked his **popliteal artery** (the major artery behind the knee). His boot filled with blood. [Wikipedia; NPS; ABT]
- Johnston initially did not realize the severity of the wound, possibly due to nerve damage from an **1837 dueling wound** that had desensitized the area. [ABT; Wikipedia]
- Critically, Johnston had **sent his personal physician** earlier in the day to attend to wounded Union prisoners, leaving himself without medical care. A tourniquet applied in time would likely have saved him. [NPS biography; ABT biography]
- Governor Isham G. Harris of Tennessee, riding with Johnston, noticed him slumping in the saddle. Johnston's reported last words: *"Yes… and I fear seriously."* [Multiple sources citing official records; ABT]
- Johnston **bled to death** approximately 100 yards south of the Bell Farm. He was the **highest-ranking officer of either side killed in combat during the entire Civil War**. [NPS; Wikipedia; ABT]
- Confederate President Jefferson Davis later wrote: *"When Sidney Johnston fell, it was the turning point of our fate; for we had no other hand to take up his work in the West."* [ABT; Wikipedia]

> **CONTESTED — Friendly fire:** Because Johnston was riding in advance of his own troops and the bullet struck from behind his right knee, some historians argue the fatal shot was fired by Confederate soldiers in the rear of the charge rather than Union troops ahead. This is not confirmed. The NPS article on Johnston's death states he was "wounded in the leg, likely by friendly fire" but acknowledges it is not definitively established. [NPS; ABT; Wikipedia]

### Beauregard Takes Command and Halts
- Beauregard assumed command of the Confederate army after Johnston's death and directed operations from the rear. [Wikipedia]
- **~6:00 pm** — Beauregard called off all Confederate attacks near dusk, believing the battle could be finished the next morning. He sent a telegram to Richmond claiming *"a complete victory."* [Wikipedia; ABT]
- Why Beauregard halted: His army was disorganized after a full day of fighting; approximately 8,000 Confederate casualties had already been suffered; plundering of the overrun Union camps had disrupted unit cohesion; ammunition was running low; and — critically — Beauregard had received a **false report** that Buell's Army of the Ohio was detained in Alabama and could not reinforce Grant. [Wikipedia; ABT article "Shattering Myths"]
- Modern historians debate this decision. Cunningham argues critics ignore the real battlefield conditions; Daniel notes it had taken six hours just to capture the Hornet's Nest, and Grant's Last Line was a stronger position. [Wikipedia summary of historians' views]

### Grant's Last Line
- As the Confederate tide advanced toward Pittsburg Landing, Grant's chief of staff, Col. **Joseph D. Webster**, assembled approximately **50 artillery pieces** on the ridge above Dill and Tilghman Branches, less than a quarter-mile from the landing. [USNI Naval History Magazine; ABT; Wikipedia]
- The **USS Tyler** and **USS Lexington** — two timber-clad gunboats on the Tennessee River — provided critical fire support. The *Tyler* opened fire at approximately 4:00 pm; the *Lexington* began firing at ~4:30 pm. Both fired into Confederate infantry advancing on the last line throughout the evening. [Wikipedia; ironbrigader.com; emergingcivilwar.com]
- This combined artillery position — roughly 50 land pieces plus the gunboats — halted the final Confederate advance and held the line until dark. [ABT; Wikipedia]

### Arrivals on the Night of April 6
- **~7:15 pm** — **Lew Wallace's** (US) division (~5,800 men) finally arrived at Pittsburg Landing after a delayed and disputed march from Crump's Landing (~5 miles away). Wallace arrived after the fighting had ended for the day. [Wikipedia chronology]
- **~9:00–midnight** — Divisions of **Buell's Army of the Ohio** (Nelson's, Crittenden's, McCook's) began arriving by steamboat and crossing to the Pittsburg Landing side. [Wikipedia]
- A violent thunderstorm broke out overnight, soaking both armies. [Wikipedia]

## 5. DAY 2 — APRIL 7, 1862

### The Union Counterattack
- **~5:00–6:00 am** — Buell's fresh troops led the counterattack, with **Nelson's three brigades** advancing first, striking the Confederate left. [Wikipedia chronology; ABT]
- Grant's battered Army of the Tennessee also advanced simultaneously. [Wikipedia]
- The Confederates were pushed back over much of the same ground they had seized on April 6 — including the Hornet's Nest area, which Buell's men recaptured by around noon. [Wikipedia]
- **~Noon** — Sherman and McClernand's advance on the west side was slowed by Cheatham's division near Shiloh Church. [Wikipedia]
- **~1:00 pm** — Beauregard began preparing a withdrawal. He recognized he was outnumbered with no reinforcements coming. [Britannica; ABT]
- **~2:00 pm** — Breckinridge's corps formed a rear guard near Shiloh Church. [Wikipedia]
- **~3:30 pm** — The last Confederate artillery departed toward Corinth. [Wikipedia]
- The Confederate army retreated ~20 miles south to Corinth, Mississippi, in deteriorating weather (rain turning to hail by evening). [Wikipedia; ABT]

### Grant's Decision Not to Pursue
- Grant ordered only a **limited pursuit** on April 8, led by Sherman and Wood. Near **Fallen Timbers**, Confederate cavalry under Nathan Bedford Forrest turned on the pursuing Union infantry.
- **April 8, Fallen Timbers:** Forrest's ~350 horsemen (a mix of his own men, Morgan's Kentucky cavalry, Texas Rangers, and 1st Mississippi cavalry) charged into the pursuing Union force. The Union suffered approximately 15 killed, 25 wounded, 53 captured. Forrest himself was wounded — he rode into the Union line, was shot, and escaped by pulling a Union soldier onto his horse as a shield. [Wikipedia]
- Grant did not mount a sustained pursuit of the Confederate army. He was subsequently criticized for this. [ABT; Britannica]
- A major reason: Halleck arrived to take personal field command after the battle, limiting Grant's authority. Halleck's subsequent slow "siege" approach to Corinth (not captured until May 30, 1862) contrasted sharply with the swift pre-Shiloh pace. [Wikipedia; ABT]

## 7. WHAT IT MEANT

### The Death of the Short-War Illusion
- Grant wrote in his *Personal Memoirs*: *"Up to the battle of Shiloh I, as well as thousands of other citizens, believed that the rebellion against the Government would collapse suddenly and soon, if a decisive victory could be gained over any of its armies."* After Shiloh: *"I gave up all idea of saving the Union except by complete conquest."* [Grant, *Personal Memoirs*; verified via historymaking.org transcript; ABT]
- The same shock reverberated in the South: Confederate casualties were nearly as bad, and Johnston — considered irreplaceable — was dead. [ABT; Wikipedia]

### Grant Nearly Relieved
- After news of the casualties and the near-disaster on Day 1 spread, newspapers revived old rumors of Grant's drinking and called for his dismissal. The public outcry was substantial. [History.com; ABT]
- Halleck effectively sidelined Grant by taking personal field command — Grant served as a largely ceremonial "second in command" for weeks before being restored. [Wikipedia]
- Lincoln defended Grant. See Section 8 for the quote and its attribution issues.

### Strategic Aftermath
- The Confederate army retreated to Corinth and was too weakened to mount further offensive operations in the western theater for months. [ABT; Wikipedia]
- Halleck eventually took Corinth by May 30, 1862, in a slow siege that many felt squandered the victory's momentum. [Wikipedia]
- The road to Vicksburg — and Grant's eventual dominance of the Western Theater — ran through Shiloh. The battle established Grant as a commander who would not retreat and would fight to complete conquest. [ABT; Wikipedia]
- Jefferson Davis: *"When Sidney Johnston fell, it was the turning point of our fate; for we had no other hand to take up his work in the West."* [ABT; Wikipedia]

## 9. GEOGRAPHY FOR A MAP

*Orientation: North up. Confederates attacked from the southwest; Union army was backed against the river to the northeast.*

### The Terrain Frame
- **Tennessee River** — eastern boundary of the battlefield; Pittsburg Landing sits on the west bank. The Landing was the Union supply base and escape route. [Wikipedia; NPS]
- **Owl Creek** — northern boundary, draining west. Flanked the Union right / Confederate left. [Wikipedia]
- **Lick Creek** — southern boundary, draining east into the Tennessee River. Flanked the Confederate right / Union left. [Wikipedia]
- These two creeks **channeled the battle** into a roughly 5-mile-wide killing ground between them.

### Key Landmarks (Southwest to Northeast — the Confederate axis of advance)
| Location | Significance | Approx. Position |
|---|---|---|
| **Shiloh Church** | Small Methodist church; gave the battle its name; center of Sherman's original camp; Confederate axis of advance originated from this direction | SW quadrant |
| **Corinth Road** | Main Confederate approach route; ran NE from Corinth through Shiloh Church toward Pittsburg Landing | SW–NE spine |
| **Fraley Field** | First contact point, ~5:00 am Apr. 6; south of the Union camps | SW, south of Church |
| **Peach Orchard** | Fighting zone SW of the Hornet's Nest; Johnston killed near here; open orchard terrain, good artillery ground | Center-south |
| **Bloody Pond** | Traditional lore site between Peach Orchard and Wicker Field — **existence disputed** by modern historians | Center-south |
| **Sunken Road / Hornet's Nest** | Old farm lane along Duncan Field; the Union center's defensive line held for ~6 hours Apr. 6; **not truly "sunken"** per modern historians | Center |
| **Duncan Field** | Open farm field in front of the Sunken Road; repeated Confederate charges across it | Center |
| **Grant's Last Line** | Ridge above Dill and Tilghman Branches; ~50 artillery pieces assembled by Webster; ¼ mile from the Landing | NE, near the river |
| **Pittsburg Landing** | Steamboat landing on the Tennessee River; Union supply base, reinforcement point; gunboats anchored here | E edge, on the river |
| **Crump's Landing** | ~5 miles north on the river; Lew Wallace's starting position | North, off main battlefield |

### Gunboat Positions
- **USS Tyler** and **USS Lexington** anchored in the Tennessee River at Pittsburg Landing; fired westward into Confederate lines on the evening of Apr. 6 and through the night. [USNI; ironbrigader.com]

## 10. CONTESTED / UNCERTAIN FACTS

### Summary Table

| Claim | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Casualty totals (23,746 combined) | ACCEPTED WITH RANGE | Official figures; historians' higher estimates (~14,500 US / ~12,000 CS) possible when including died-of-wounds |
| "More casualties than all previous American wars combined" | DISPUTED | True for *prior Civil War battles* only; does not hold when counting all American wars since 1775 (Revolutionary War alone had more battle deaths). Frequently misquoted. |
| The Sunken Road was truly sunken | DISPUTED | Term not used until 1881; contemporary records and photos show a slight farm track, not a genuine depression; veterans embellished over time |
| Bloody Pond | PROBABLY MYTHOLOGICAL | No contemporary documentation; sole source = a civilian's decades-later recollection |
| Number of Confederate charges at Hornet's Nest (8–14) | UNCERTAIN | Range spans multiple sources; the exact number of discrete assaults cannot be determined |
| Grant was surprised by the Confederate attack | CONSENSUS: YES | Multiple historians (Chernow, Sword, McPherson, NPS) conclude Grant was operationally surprised despite scattered skirmishing April 3–5. His own correspondence shows no expectation of a major attack. |
| Johnston killed by friendly fire | PLAUSIBLE, UNCONFIRMED | Bullet entered behind the right knee (toward his own lines); NPS says "likely friendly fire"; not definitively established. Could have been fired at an angle from Union troops ahead. |
| Johnston's physician being absent saved Johnston's life | PROBABLE | A tourniquet applied promptly would very likely have saved him; the popliteal artery wound was survivable with immediate care |
| Lew Wallace took the "wrong road" | CONTESTED/VINDICATED | Wallace followed the Shunpike Road (he thought correctly, toward Sherman's original camp); by the time he arrived, the lines had shifted and he was behind Confederate lines. Grant expected him on the River Road. The original written orders have never been found. Modern historians largely vindicate Wallace tactically while acknowledging his tardiness cost Union forces roughly 7 hours of Wallace's 5,800 men. |
| Beauregard's halt saved Grant | DEBATED | Traditional view: Beauregard "let Grant off the hook." Revisionist view: Grant's Last Line with 50 guns + gunboats was a very strong position; Confederate army was disorganized, low on ammo, and misled about Buell. Historians are split. |
| Hornet's Nest saved Grant's army | DEBATED | Traditional: Prentiss's stand bought 6 crucial hours. Revisionist (ABT "Shattering Myths"): W.H.L. Wallace did more of the work; the hold may not have been as decisive as claimed given how quickly the Confederate army reorganized after the position fell. |
| Lincoln's "I can't spare this man; he fights" | LIKELY APOCRYPHAL | First recorded by McClure in 1892 (30 years after the fact); no contemporary source; disputed by historians |
| Sherman's "We've had the devil's own day" / Grant's "Lick 'em tomorrow" | RELIABLE | In Sherman's *Memoirs*; corroborated by other witnesses; treated as authentic by mainstream historians |
| Grant's *Memoirs* quote on "complete conquest" | CONFIRMED | Primary source, *Personal Memoirs* (1885) |
