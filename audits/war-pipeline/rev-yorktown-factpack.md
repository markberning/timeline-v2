# FACT PACK — Siege of Yorktown, September 28 – October 19, 1781

*American Revolution (decisive finale). ONLY source for the battle page. The war ends here — or nearly: a French admiral's choice in the Caribbean sets an eight-week deadline, a naval battle off the Virginia Capes seals the bay before Cornwallis can be rescued, and the largest British field army in the South surrenders in a ceremony that deliberately echoes — and answers — Charleston, 1780. The battle is unwinnable without the French fleet; that is the central fact. Confidence tags: HIGH / MED / DISPUTED; HANDLE-WITH-CARE flags inline.*

## 1. IDENTITY & FRAMING

- **Siege of Yorktown, September 28 – October 19, 1781** — Yorktown, Virginia, on the York River, a small tobacco port on a peninsula (York Peninsula) in the Virginia Tidewater, across the river from Gloucester Point. The siege lasted twenty-two days; the formal surrender was October 19. [HIGH]
- **Result: decisive Franco-American victory** — the largest British capitulation of the American Revolution and the effective end of major combat operations on the North American mainland. More than 7,000 British and German soldiers surrendered. [HIGH]
- **The strategic premise [HIGH]:** After Cornwallis's exhausting Carolinas campaign (Camden, Cowpens, Guilford Courthouse) left his army depleted and his opponent Greene undefeated in the south, Cornwallis moved into Virginia in the spring of 1781, raiding deep into the interior. His superior, General Henry Clinton in New York, was fighting with him by letter about strategy. Under a series of contradictory orders, Clinton finally directed Cornwallis to establish a deep-water naval station on the Chesapeake. Engineers ruled out Old Point Comfort as unsuitable; Cornwallis selected Yorktown on the York River — a deep-water anchorage on a defensible peninsula — in August 1781. The position made perfect sense as long as the Royal Navy held the sea. [HIGH on substance; Clinton–Cornwallis command relationship dispute confirmed via multiple sources — see Landmines §8]
- **The Naval Hinge [HIGH]:** On September 5, 1781, off the Virginia Capes at the mouth of the Chesapeake, the French fleet under Admiral de Grasse (24 ships of the line) fought the British fleet under Admiral Thomas Graves (19 ships of the line). Two hours of broadsides left neither fleet destroyed, but the British took heavier damage. Graves kept the fleets at sea for days, during which de Barras's smaller squadron (8 ships) slipped from Newport into the Bay with the heavy siege artillery. Graves, with five ships badly damaged and one later scuttled, turned back to New York. The bay was shut. Cornwallis's sea escape and any British relief were now structurally blocked. **The spine's framing holds: "Remove any one strand and the year closes on nothing in particular."** [HIGH as strategic analysis; the "simultaneity" point — all pieces had to arrive at once — is confirmed across scholarly sources as the standard historiographical framing; the word "simultaneity" itself appears to be attributed to historians working in this tradition generally, not to a single McPherson quote that can be pinned — see §8 Landmines]
- **Lafayette pins Cornwallis (before Washington arrives) [HIGH]:** While Washington and Rochambeau were still in the north, the Marquis de Lafayette commanded the only Continental force in Virginia — roughly 4,500 men (Continentals, plus units under Anthony Wayne and Baron von Steuben). Though outnumbered by Cornwallis's 7,000–9,000, Lafayette successfully shadowed and contained Cornwallis, prevented him from escaping south through the Carolinas, and tracked him as he settled at Yorktown. Washington and Rochambeau's combined army did not arrive until September 28. Lafayette's months-long containment operation is the direct reason Cornwallis was still at Yorktown when the trap closed. [HIGH]
- **Signature elements [HIGH]:** (1) **The naval battle that won it before the land battle** — the Chesapeake capes action is the actual decisive moment; the siege is the execution; (2) **the two siege parallels** — classical European siege technique applied by French engineers; (3) **the storming of Redoubts 9 and 10** (Oct 14) — Hamilton's Americans on 10, French on 9, unloaded muskets, fixed bayonets; (4) **the surrender ceremony built as the answer to Charleston** — honors of war refused, colors cased, Lincoln receives O'Hara's sword; (5) **the "World Turned Upside Down" tradition** — probably apocryphal, first published 41–47 years after the event (see §8).

## 2. BACKGROUND — THE ROAD TO YORKTOWN

- **The southern theater exhausted both sides [HIGH]:** After Camden (Aug 16, 1780) and Cowpens (Jan 17, 1781), Cornwallis won at Guilford Courthouse (Mar 15, 1781) — technically a British victory but he lost nearly a quarter of his force and had to retreat to Wilmington, NC to refit. He wrote that if he could not destroy Greene's army "we must give up every idea of continuing the war in America." He then marched north into Virginia rather than go back south, a decision Clinton opposed. [HIGH on the arc; Cornwallis quote MED — widely reported, confirm before byte-quoting]
- **Virginia ravaged [HIGH]:** Cornwallis entered Virginia in May 1781 with ~7,200 men (the core of the southern army plus ~2,500 under Benedict Arnold and Phillips who had been raiding Virginia since January). He sent Tarleton's Legion on a famous raid toward Charlottesville (June 3–4) — the legislature fled, Jefferson escaped from Monticello minutes ahead of the dragoons. Anthony Wayne's detachment was mauled at Green Spring (July 6). Then contradictory orders from Clinton drove Cornwallis to the coast. [HIGH on sequence; details as above confirmed in the spine]
- **The Franco-American strategic decision [HIGH]:** On May 21–22, 1781, Washington and Rochambeau met at Wethersfield, Connecticut. Washington wanted to attack New York — his first choice throughout the war. The conference memorandum says so plainly. But Rochambeau, that same week, wrote separately to the French admiral de Grasse in the West Indies making the case for the Chesapeake. The campaign that ended the war started as a plan Washington did not choose and on a letter he never saw. [HIGH — this framing is in the spine and confirmed cross-source]
- **De Grasse's decision and deadline [HIGH]:** On August 5, at Cap-Français (Cap-Haïtien, Saint-Domingue / present-day Haiti), de Grasse committed not part but ALL of his West Indies fleet — 28 ships of the line — to the Chesapeake, with ~3,000 French troops under the Marquis de Saint-Simon borrowed from the islands. He also arranged a cash loan in Havana to fund the campaign. He set his own return deadline: mid-October. The hinge of the war was a French admiral's choice made in the Caribbean. [HIGH — spine framing; details confirmed via multiple sources this session; the Havana loan is confirmed in secondary literature though the exact amount varies — use "a cash loan arranged in Havana" not a specific figure without verification]
- **De Barras from Newport [HIGH]:** Simultaneously, the smaller French squadron at Newport, Rhode Island, under Admiral de Barras (8 ships of the line) carried the **heavy siege artillery** required for European-style siege operations. De Barras hugged the Atlantic coast and slipped into the Chesapeake while de Grasse held Graves at sea. Without his siege guns there could be no conventional siege. The allied land force would not have had the artillery to reduce Yorktown's earthworks. [HIGH — confirmed via multiple sources this session]
- **The march south [HIGH]:** Washington and Rochambeau's combined force left the Hudson in late August, marching south. To deceive Clinton into thinking they were attacking New York, Washington maintained the pretense as long as possible; the march south became apparent in mid-August. Rochambeau's ~5,500 French troops plus ~3,500 American Continentals and militia covered roughly 450 miles in approximately four weeks — an extraordinary feat of logistics and march discipline. [HIGH on substance; mileage/timing as approximate]

## 3. FORCES & COMMANDERS

### Allied strengths (use ranges) [HIGH as aggregate; sub-figures MED — sources vary]
- **Allied total: ~19,000–19,800** on land
  - American (Continental + Virginia militia): ~8,800–9,000 (spine: "roughly eighty-eight hundred Americans, Continentals and Virginia militia")
  - French (Rochambeau's army + de Saint-Simon's troops): ~7,800–10,800 (the higher figure adds de Saint-Simon's 3,000 landed from the fleet; the spine uses "around seventy-eight hundred French regulars" for the siege army on the Yorktown side, likely counting Rochambeau's force only)
  - Note: French naval forces (de Grasse's fleet ~19,000 sailors and marines) are additional; the French contribution in aggregate was larger than the American
- **British total: ~8,000–9,000** (Cornwallis's original army depleted by the Carolinas campaign; approximately half were British regulars, the rest German auxiliaries — Hessians and Anspach-Bayreuth troops)
  - British at Yorktown: ~6,000–7,000 effective (disease rampant — malaria endemic to the Virginia Tidewater in summer; Cornwallis later reported half his army unfit)
  - British at Gloucester Point (across the York River): ~700 under Tarleton
  - Prisoners on surrender Oct 19: **7,157 soldiers + 840 sailors + ~80 camp followers** (standard figures; range 7,416–7,685 in other counts — use "more than 7,000" per the spine)

### Allied commanders (one-line bios)

- **George Washington (1732–1799) — American, overall commander** [HIGH]: Commander-in-chief of the Continental Army since 1775; had never won a pitched battle against a major British force (Long Island, Brandywine, Germantown all lost; Trenton, Princeton, and Monmouth were smaller-scale affairs). Yorktown was his only siege victory of the war. Washington ceremonially fired the first American cannon on October 9 — the detail comes from Continental Army surgeon James Thacher's contemporary journal (HIGH attestation). After the surrender he warned Virginia's governor not to stop fighting: see §7 Quotes.
- **Jean-Baptiste Donatien de Vimeur, comte de Rochambeau (1725–1807) — French, army commander** [HIGH]: French career officer since the 1740s (War of Austrian Succession, Seven Years' War). Commanded the ~5,500-man French expeditionary force that arrived at Newport, Rhode Island, in July 1780 and waited an entire year for naval superiority before being used. Older and more experienced than Washington (56 to Washington's 49 at Yorktown); exercised command with exceptional diplomatic tact, deferring publicly to Washington while privately steering toward the Chesapeake option. At the surrender ceremony he deflected O'Hara's approach and pointed to Washington.
- **François Joseph Paul, comte de Grasse (1722–1788) — French, fleet commander** [HIGH]: Born in Bar-sur-Loup, France; French Navy from age 17. The man whose individual decision made Yorktown possible — committed his full West Indies fleet rather than a detachment, arranged the Havana loan, set his own October deadline. Won the Battle of the Chesapeake (Sept 5, 1781) against Graves. After Yorktown he was defeated and captured by Admiral Rodney at the Battle of the Saintes (April 12, 1782), spent two years a prisoner-guest in England. His fleet's 24-ship superiority over Graves's 19 was the arithmetic of the siege. [HIGH — de Grasse died Jan 11, 1788, Paris; born Sept 13, 1722]
- **Marquis de Lafayette (1757–1834) — Franco-American, Virginia force commander** [HIGH]: French volunteer who had been fighting as a major general in the Continental Army since 1777. In the spring of 1781, Washington sent him to Virginia with a small Continental force specifically to contain Cornwallis and deny him escape south. Cornwallis, with numerical superiority, could have destroyed Lafayette's command but did not push aggressively — he was being pulled by Clinton's orders back toward the coast. Lafayette dogged him through Virginia for months. After Washington arrived and took command, Lafayette commanded the American light infantry in the siege, including nominally supervising the Oct 14 assault sector (see §4). The spine calls him explicitly: "the chain of evils" that ended at Yorktown included his successful containment operation.
- **Henry Knox (1750–1806) — American, artillery commander** [HIGH]: Boston bookseller turned artillery general; mastermind of the "Noble Train of Artillery" that dragged the Ticonderoga cannon to Boston in 1775–76. Chief of Artillery for the Continental Army throughout the war. At Yorktown commanded the 14-gun American battery (3 × 24-pounders, 3 × 18-pounders, 2 × 8-inch howitzers, 6 mortars) and coordinated siting of the 155-gun combined Allied bombardment. The artillery barrage ran virtually continuous from Oct 9 through Oct 17. Knox and Lincoln stood beside Washington at the opening gun. [HIGH on his role; guns count confirmed via multiple sources]
- **Alexander Hamilton (1757–1804) — American, led Redoubt 10 assault** [HIGH]: Washington's former aide-de-camp and secretary for most of the war; had resigned in February 1781 after a quarrel, and had been pressing Washington for a field command. Got it at Yorktown: commanded the light infantry battalion (Lafayette's corps) that stormed Redoubt No. 10 on the night of October 14, in a charge with fixed bayonets and deliberately unloaded muskets (noise discipline for secrecy). The assault took the redoubt in about ten minutes; Hamilton was first over the parapet by some accounts. Nine Americans killed, 25 wounded. [HIGH — confirmed NPS and ABT sources; Hamilton was born January 11, 1755 or 1757, in Nevis — precise birth year genuinely contested but irrelevant here; died July 12, 1804]
- **Benjamin Lincoln (1733–1810) — American, Washington's second in command** [HIGH]: The officer who surrendered Charleston to Clinton on May 12, 1780 — the largest American capitulation of the war, with ~5,500 men; Lincoln was denied the honors of war by the British (colors furled, no march-out tune). Exchanged in November 1780. Washington chose him as second in command for Yorktown — and deliberately positioned him to receive O'Hara's sword at the surrender ceremony, the direct symmetrical reversal of Charleston. The gesture was the opposite of accidental. After the war he became the first Secretary of War under the Articles of Confederation. [HIGH]

### British commanders (one-line bios)

- **Charles Cornwallis, 1st Marquess Cornwallis (1738–1805) — British, overall commander at Yorktown** [HIGH]: Career soldier, had served in America since 1776; won at Camden (Aug 1780), prevailed at Guilford Courthouse at heavy cost (Mar 1781). His relationship with Clinton had deteriorated into mutual blame by 1781; their post-war pamphlet war is one of the loudest in the era's memoir literature. At Yorktown he sent Tarleton to Gloucester Point, planned the sortie (Oct 16) and the Gloucester escape (Oct 16–17), and — citing illness — did not attend the surrender ceremony. Whether the illness was real or diplomatic disgust is unresolvable; historians typically present both (see §8 Landmines).
- **Brigadier General Charles O'Hara (c. 1740–1802) — British, represented Cornwallis at the surrender** [HIGH]: Irish-born professional soldier; had served throughout the southern campaign and was wounded at Guilford Courthouse. Cornwallis sent him to lead the surrender column on October 19. He first offered the sword to Rochambeau (the French being the senior allied power by some readings of protocol, or because he hoped to avoid the humiliation of surrendering to a colonial general); Rochambeau shook his head and pointed to Washington; Washington, matching a deputy with a deputy, referred him to Lincoln. O'Hara later served as governor of Gibraltar (captured by Napoleon at Toulon in 1793). [HIGH on the sword sequence — confirmed in multiple first-hand and near-contemporary sources; see §7 Quotes]
- **Banastre Tarleton (1754–1833) — British, cavalry commander at Gloucester Point** [HIGH]: Commanded the British Legion cavalry at Gloucester Point (across the York River), roughly 700 men. On October 3–4 his foraging party collided with the Duc de Lauzun's French hussars and Virginia militia under Brigadier General de Choisy at the Battle of the Hook (Gloucester Point action) — the most significant cavalry engagement of the Revolution; Tarleton was unhorsed and wounded, saved by his men, and forced to retreat. For the remainder of the siege his force was pinned at Gloucester by de Choisy. It was Cornwallis's intended escape route. In-repo portrait: `rev-charleston-tarleton.jpg` (Reynolds, 1782) — REUSE.

## 4. THE SIEGE OPERATIONS

### Sept 28 — Allied arrival and investment
- Washington and Rochambeau marched out of Williamsburg on September 28 and closed around Yorktown: the French on the left (west), Americans on the right (east), surrounding the peninsula. Cornwallis held outer and inner defensive lines of earthwork redoubts and batteries. [HIGH]
- On the night of September 29–30, Cornwallis abandoned his outer defensive line and pulled his forces into the inner works — a decision he made on the strength of a letter from Clinton promising a relief fleet by October 5. The allies walked into the abandoned outer works without a fight. The relief fleet never arrived on schedule. Clinton sailed on October 19 — the day Cornwallis surrendered. [HIGH]

### The first parallel (October 6)
- On the night of October 6, under cover of a diversionary cannonade, the allied engineers opened the **first parallel** — a trench line ~2,000 yards long, running roughly east-west about 600–800 yards from the British inner works. Washington ceremonially struck the first pickax blow (tradition — widely reported but assign confidence MED; the first cannon detail from Thacher is HIGH). The ground had been softened by recent rain and the line was substantially complete by morning. [HIGH on the construction date and method; the pickax story MED]
- By October 9, French and American batteries were operational along the first parallel. Washington personally fired the first American cannon, per Continental Army surgeon James Thacher's contemporary journal — HIGH attestation. That first shot reportedly traveled through the house where British officers were dining (widely told, but the specific "Symonds's dinner" version is anecdotal — MED; do not quote without hedge). 155 combined Allied guns firing all told; the barrage was devastating. British ships in the York River were hit; HMS Charon caught fire and burned. [HIGH on batteries, date, scale; specific anecdotes MED]

### The second parallel (October 11) — and why the redoubts mattered
- On October 11, engineers began the **second parallel**, another trench line ~400 yards closer to the British works, under 76-gun Allied cover fire. [HIGH]
- The second parallel could not extend to the right (American sector) because two British outer redoubts blocked the way: **Redoubt No. 9** (southeast, held by ~120 men, mostly Hessians) and **Redoubt No. 10** (easternmost, held by ~70 British and Hessian troops). Until those redoubts fell, the second parallel was incomplete and the inner British works could not be brought under direct artillery fire. [HIGH]

### The storming of Redoubts 9 and 10, October 14, 1781 [HIGH]
- At approximately 7:00 p.m. on October 14, six signal guns fired and two assault columns moved out simultaneously.
- **Redoubt No. 10 (American column):** Lt. Col. Alexander Hamilton commanded ~400 American light infantry. The men fixed bayonets and left their muskets unloaded (secrecy discipline — a single shot would have given the game away). Hamilton — who had maneuvered to get this command over the original assignee, Lt. Col. Gimat, by appealing to Washington over Lafayette's head — led the assault. The Americans swarmed the ditch and parapet; in about ten minutes of hand-to-hand fighting the redoubt fell. Lt. Col. John Laurens led a detachment around to cut off the rear. American casualties: approximately 9 killed, 25 wounded. [HIGH — confirmed via NPS, ABT, and Wikipedia]
- **Redoubt No. 9 (French column):** ~400 French regulars under the Baron de Viomenil (also rendered Deux-Ponts for the column's senior officer, the Comte de Deux-Ponts). The French assault was more contested — the redoubt was larger, the garrison stronger (~120 men vs ~70). French casualties: approximately 15 killed, 77 wounded, heavier than the American losses. [HIGH on the larger French losses; individual officer attributions MED — de Viomenil commanded the French light infantry corps; Deux-Ponts commanded the actual storming party; both are correct depending on level of attribution — attribute to both or to "the French light infantry under the Baron de Viomenil"]
- With both redoubts taken, the second parallel was extended, and within two days the allies could bring direct fire onto the British inner works from less than 300 yards. [HIGH]

### The British sortie (October 16, pre-dawn)
- In a last attempt to disrupt the siege, Lt. Col. Robert Abercrombie led ~350 British light infantry and Guards out of the lines at approximately 4:00 a.m. on October 16, hit the second allied parallel, and **spiked several Allied cannon** — driving metal spikes into the touchholes to render them temporarily unfireable. By Washington's own diary, approximately four French and two American guns were spiked. Abercrombie is reported to have shouted "Push on my brave boys, and skin the bastards!" [MED on the exact quote — widely reported, but sources generally cite secondary accounts, not a contemporary document]. French troops drove the sortie back. The spiked guns were restored to service within hours. [HIGH on the event, date, and rapid repair; exact gun count varies by source — Washington's diary entry for Oct 16 gives "four French pieces … and two of ours," which is the authoritative figure]

### The escape attempt (October 16–17)
- On the night of October 16–17, with the inner works crumbling under artillery and the parallel now at point-blank range, Cornwallis attempted to evacuate his healthiest troops across the York River to Gloucester Point, intending to break out through de Choisy's investing force and march for New York. The first wave of boats carried some troops across. Then **a severe squall struck the river** — the boats were dispersed and driven downriver, the crossing abandoned. By morning it was too late. The troops already at Gloucester were ferried back. [HIGH — confirmed via NPS timeline, Wikipedia, and multiple secondary sources; the storm is the documented cause; "a storm foiled the crossing" is the high-confidence sentence]
- Note: even a successful Gloucester breakout would have been enormously difficult — the French and American cavalry were waiting, de Choisy's force was substantial, and the route north was long. Some historians treat the escape as a fantasy born of desperation. The page may leave that evaluation to the reader.

### October 17 — the white flag
- On October 17, 1781, a British officer appeared on the Yorktown ramparts beating a parley (one account says he waved a white handkerchief; another says he drummed the parley — both circulate). Washington ordered a cease-fire. Cornwallis had signaled his intention to negotiate capitulation. [HIGH on the date and event; the specific gesture — drumbeat vs. handkerchief — MED, keep generic]
- Negotiations took place at the **Moore House** on Temple Farm, 1½ miles below Yorktown, on October 18. British negotiators: Lt. Col. Thomas Dundas and Major Alexander Ross. Allied negotiators: Lt. Col. John Laurens (American) and the Marquis de Noailles (French). [HIGH — confirmed via NPS and Yale Avalon documents]

## 5. THE SURRENDER (OCTOBER 19, 1781)

### The articles and the denied honors [HIGH]
- The **Articles of Capitulation** were finalized and signed on October 19. Signatories included Washington, Rochambeau, and de Barras (for the French Navy); Cornwallis and Royal Navy Captain Thomas Symonds for the British. [HIGH]
- Washington **refused the British garrison the honors of war** — the ceremonial courtesy allowing a surrendering army to march out with colors flying, bayonets fixed, and a tune from the enemy's own repertoire playing (the last a signal of respect for a worthy opponent). This was the direct repayment for Charleston: when Lincoln surrendered that city in May 1780, Clinton had denied American troops the same honors. Washington refused the same terms to the British without exception. The British and German troops marched with **colors cased** (furled and sheathed), muskets shouldered, and their bands playing a British or German march rather than an American tune. [HIGH — the symmetry with Charleston is explicitly documented in the secondary literature and confirmed as Washington's deliberate choice]

### The sword ceremony [HIGH]
- The British column was led by **General Charles O'Hara** (Cornwallis's second in command), not Cornwallis, who cited illness. O'Hara **first offered his sword to Rochambeau** — whether as a diplomatic maneuver (hoping to surrender to the representative of a European royal court rather than a colonial general) or a genuine misreading of protocol is debated; Rochambeau's response ended it: he **shook his head and pointed to Washington**. Washington, following the same protocol of meeting a deputy with a deputy, then **directed O'Hara to Major General Benjamin Lincoln**, who received the sword. [HIGH — the sequence is confirmed in multiple near-contemporary and primary sources, and the Lincoln/Charleston symmetry was noted at the time and since]
- The symbolism was not subtle: the man who had surrendered Charleston and been denied honors received the British sword at Yorktown. This is the page's signature human moment. Name it plainly.

### "The World Turned Upside Down" — tradition, not documented fact [HANDLE-WITH-CARE; see §8]
- By tradition, the British bands played the tune "The World Turned Upside Down" as the troops marched to the surrender field. **This is not documented in any contemporary eyewitness account.** The story first appeared in Alexander Garden's *Anecdotes of the American Revolution* (published 1822 at the earliest, 1828 for the version with the relevant anecdote) — 41–47 years after the event. Garden's source was a letter from Major William Jackson who was in Europe, not at Yorktown, at the time. No eighteenth-century British tune or march called "The World Turned Upside Down" has been identified in over a hundred years of music scholarship. Contemporary accounts say only that "a British or German march" was played. Douglas Southall Freeman dismissed the report as "secondhand, thin, and doubtful." **Write this as tradition; flag its evidence problem explicitly.** The spine already does so. [HIGH on the evidence problem — confirmed via Mount Vernon Digital Encyclopedia and AmericanRevolution.org analysis this session]
- The tune *may* have been "When the King Enjoys his Own Again" (a well-known 18th-century loyalist air) — some scholars have suggested this as a deliberate political statement about the surrender terms — but this too is unverified. Write neither as fact.

### The prisoner count and trophies [HIGH]
- Surrendered October 19: **more than 7,000 soldiers** (standard count 7,157 soldiers + ~840 sailors; total British/German personnel ~8,000); **240+ guns** (sources say 240 to 244); the army's equipment and stores. [Match the spine's "more than seven thousand soldiers … and more than two hundred guns"; use "more than 7,000" and "more than 200 guns" for the body text, fuller range in the dossier stats]
- Represented **roughly one-third of all British forces in North America** at the time.

- **Prisoners (Oct 19): 7,000–7,685** soldiers, ~840 naval; use "more than 7,000."

## 7. AFTERMATH / CONSEQUENCES

- **The war did not end at Yorktown [HIGH]:** This is the spine's explicit framing and is historically accurate. Britain still held New York, Charleston, and Savannah with approximately 30,000 troops; the Royal Navy was intact; the world war (France, Spain, Holland vs. Britain) continued and actually turned Britain's way. What ended was Britain's political will to reconquer the colonies by force. Within four months the House of Commons voted against continuing the offensive war and Lord North's government fell. The Treaty of Paris was not signed until September 3, 1783. The battle page should close on this: Yorktown ended a campaign, not a war — and ended it so decisively that the political outcome became inevitable.
- **Washington's letter to Nelson [HIGH on substance; MED on exact text — verify against Founders Online before byte-quoting]:** Eight days after Yorktown, Washington wrote Virginia Governor Thomas Nelson warning him not to relax — the danger was that victory would make everyone complacent and drag the war out. The spine uses this (§ "The long tail"). The battle page should plant this only if the words are verified; the spine framing handles it.
- **De Grasse and the Saintes (April 12, 1782) [HIGH]:** The fleet that won Yorktown was largely destroyed at the Battle of the Saintes in the Caribbean six months later. Admiral Rodney shattered the French fleet; de Grasse's flagship Ville de Paris struck her colors with the admiral aboard. De Grasse spent two years a prisoner in England. His court-martial on return to France exonerated all his captains while effectively ending his career. This is a one-clause aftermath note — not a section, just the end of de Grasse's story.
- **Clinton–Cornwallis post-war blame war [MED — widely documented but the battle page should note it without taking sides]:** Clinton published his *Narrative of the Campaign of 1781 in North America* (1783) blaming Cornwallis; Cornwallis replied; both generals spent the remainder of their careers in this dispute. The orders are genuinely confusing; historians have never fully settled who was more at fault.
- **The peace negotiation [HIGH on timing]:** Paris peace talks began in 1782; preliminary articles signed November 30, 1782; final Treaty of Paris signed September 3, 1783. Britain recognized American independence, set the Mississippi as the western boundary, and made generous terms — partly because negotiating with Americans separately gave Britain leverage against France and Spain. The battle page should note the 1783 Treaty date as the legal end of the war.

## 9. APOCRYPHA BLACKLIST / HANDLE-WITH-CARE

1. **"The World Turned Upside Down" as documented fact** — first published 41–47 years after the event in Alexander Garden's *Anecdotes* via a man who was not there. No contemporary account names any tune; no 18th-century British march of this title has been identified. Write as tradition explicitly: "By later tradition, though no contemporary account confirms it, the British band played 'The World Turned Upside Down.'" The spine already handles this correctly; the battle page must match. [APOCRYPHAL — evidence problem confirmed HIGH this session]

2. **Cornwallis's "illness" as cowardice or shame** — the illness is documented (malaria was endemic; Cornwallis himself reported half his army sick); his personal shame or diplomatic calculation may also have operated. Both are plausible; neither is proven. Write as the spine does: "Cornwallis did not come out himself, pleading illness." Do not diagnose the motivation. [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — the illness is real; the motivation is interpreted]

3. **"Surrender of Lord Cornwallis" (Trumbull's painting) as documentary** — Trumbull's Capitol Rotunda painting (1820) depicts a scene Trumbull himself acknowledged he could not directly document (Cornwallis was absent; the depicted arrangement is idealized). It is a history painting, not a photograph. Caption it as such. The Yale version (c. 1828, 1,920 × 1,304 px) is the small study; the Capitol version (12 × 18 feet, 3,000 × 1,978 px in the Wikimedia scan) is the monumental work. Both are PD. [FRAMING — not apocryphal as a painting; apocryphal only if treated as a documentary record]

4. **The "McPherson simultaneity argument" as a named quote** — the argument that Yorktown was unwinnable without the simultaneous convergence of all its elements is standard in the historiography and is attributed to historians broadly; attempting to pin a specific McPherson "Battle Cry of Freedom" passage as a named quote risked error this session (the relevant framing for the CW-era McPherson doesn't apply to the ARW). Use the argument, attributed to "historians of the campaign," not to a specific named scholar's phrase. [NOTE — the spine uses this argument without a byline, which is the correct approach]

5. **The Clinton–Cornwallis dispute** — both men's post-war accounts are self-serving. The orders were genuinely confused and contradictory. Present the debate without settling it: "Clinton later insisted; Cornwallis replied; the orders were contradictory enough to sustain both." [HANDLE-WITH-CARE — disputed]

6. **The "first shot kills a British officer dining" anecdote** — one anecdote holds that Washington's first cannon shot struck the house where British officers were dining at the table, killing or wounding them. Circulates widely in popular histories; no strong primary source chain has been identified. Use at most as "a story going round the camp" [MED — see also Thacher's journal for what he does and doesn't say]

7. **"The world will little note nor long remember…"** — this is Lincoln's Gettysburg Address about a Civil War battle, not a Yorktown source. It appears near Yorktown discussion in some popular accounts because of Lincoln + surrender. Irrelevant here; do not cite.

8. **The war "ended at Yorktown"** — a persistent popular framing the page should actively correct. The legal end was the Treaty of Paris, signed September 3, 1783. The political end was the Commons vote against offensive war in March 1782 and North's government falling. Yorktown ended a campaign; the war took two more years to formally close. [FACTUAL BLACKLIST — frame accordingly]

## 12. DOSSIER STATS (ready-to-use)

- **Dates:** September 28 – October 19, 1781 · **Where:** Yorktown, Virginia, on the York River (York Peninsula), with Gloucester Point across the river
- **Forces:** Allied ~19,000–19,800 (Americans ~8,800 + French ~7,800–10,800, plus de Grasse's fleet); British ~8,000–9,000 (on land; ~half sick by the surrender)
- **Duration:** 22 days of siege operations
- **Key sub-events:** Battle of the Chesapeake (Sept 5), first parallel (Oct 6), second parallel (Oct 11), Redoubts 9 & 10 stormed (Oct 14), British sortie (Oct 16), Gloucester escape foiled by storm (Oct 16–17), parley (Oct 17), Moore House negotiations (Oct 18), surrender ceremony (Oct 19)
- **Casualties:** Allied ~389 killed and wounded; British several hundred killed and wounded during the siege (precise figures unreliable); **7,000–7,685 British/German prisoners** plus ~840 sailors, 240+ guns captured
- **Winner line material:** Decisive Franco-American victory — the last major land engagement of the American Revolution on the mainland. The French fleet sealed the bay; Washington and Rochambeau ran classical siege operations; Hamilton's light infantry stormed the redoubt that unlocked the second parallel; and Benjamin Lincoln received the British sword as the deliberate, pointed answer to Charleston. The war was not over for two more years, and France's admiral would be captured in the Caribbean six months later, but what died at Yorktown was Britain's will to keep fighting for thirteen colonies it had already lost.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES

1. **Allied strength:** figures vary across sources (19,000–19,800); the spine uses "roughly eighty-eight hundred Americans … around seventy-eight hundred French regulars" for the siege forces only. The higher totals include de Saint-Simon's troops from the fleet. Use ranges and match the spine's specific language for the land forces.
2. **British strength:** "eight thousand or so" per the spine; individual sources range 7,500–9,000. Use ranges; note half were sick.
3. **Prisoner count:** the standard figure is 7,157 soldiers + ~840 sailors; aggregate totals in sources range 7,416–7,685. Safe sentence: "more than 7,000 soldiers, plus more than 800 naval personnel."
4. **Guns captured:** 240 to 244; use "more than 200 guns" for body text.
5. **De Grasse's fleet at the Chesapeake:** "24 ships of the line" at the September 5 battle (confirmed this session); the total later in the bay after de Barras arrived was 36 ships. The battle was 24 vs. 19; the bay lock post-de Barras was 36 vs. 0.
6. **Redoubt assault casualties:** American 9/25 (HIGH), French ~15/77 (HIGH on the larger losses, exact figures MED).
7. **British sortie guns spiked:** Washington's diary gives "4 French, 2 American" = 6; other sources say as many as 11; use "several" or cite Washington's diary for the 6 figure.
8. **Cornwallis's illness:** documented as real (malaria endemic, his own troops half-sick); whether shame or calculation also played a role is unresolvable and disputed — do not diagnose.
9. **"World Turned Upside Down":** APOCRYPHAL — no contemporary source; first published 41–47 years later. Flag as tradition.
10. **Hamilton's age/birth year at Yorktown:** Hamilton was born either 1755 or 1757 (genuinely disputed; the 1755 date makes him 26, 1757 makes him 24). Do not state a specific age.
11. **The "first pickax" ceremony:** Washington ceremonially striking the first blow opening the first parallel is widely reported but with the same evidence profile as the "first cannon" — secondary and popular accounts. The first cannon (from Thacher, a contemporary journal) is HIGH; the pickax story is MED.
12. **McPherson attribution:** The "simultaneity" argument is standard historiography, not a named McPherson quote from a specific passage. Use as the page's conceptual frame, attributed to historians of the campaign, not as a named quotation.
13. **O'Hara's sword-first-to-Rochambeau reasoning:** Why he approached Rochambeau first is interpreted variously (protocol confusion; deliberate snub; hope of avoiding surrender to a colonial general). St. George Tucker's letter records the event; the reasoning is not recorded by any contemporary source. Present the event, not the motive.
