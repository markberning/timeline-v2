## MUST-ADD — Blocking Gaps

### MUST-ADD 1 (BLOCKING): Anderson's personal identity — the Unionist slaveholder from Kentucky

**Status in draft: MISSING**

The draft introduces Anderson as "U.S. Army major" and a professional soldier. It never tells the reader who Anderson actually was: a Kentucky-born former slaveholder who personally sympathized with the South and yet stayed loyal to the Union. This is not a biographical footnote — it is the reason the teacher-student irony lands so hard and why Anderson's moral position is genuinely interesting. The fact that the man holding the fort against the Confederacy had himself owned enslaved people and understood the South's grievances is one of the most revealing details about what "loyalty to the Union" cost in April 1861. The draft's teacher-student section aches, as it says — but it aches more if you know what Anderson was giving up personally by not going South.

The fact pack has this: "A Unionist but personally sympathetic to the South (his wife was Southern; he had owned enslaved people)" (§6, Anderson row). It is in the pack but never used.

**Fact needed**: In the pack. No additional sourcing required.

---

### MUST-ADD 2 (BLOCKING): Anderson spiked Moultrie's guns when he left

**Status in draft: MISSING (flagged as [VERIFY] in the fact ledger)**

The draft says Anderson "abandoned" Fort Moultrie and ferried his men out. It omits that before leaving he spiked the guns and burned the carriages — the act that told South Carolina the move was deliberate and permanent, not a temporary repositioning. Without this detail, Pickens's fury ("breach of faith") is plausible but somewhat abstract. With it, the reader understands: Anderson destroyed the weapons so no one else could use them, and walked out. That is not ambiguous.

The draft's own fact ledger flags this: "Anderson spiked Moultrie's guns — [VERIFY] the fact pack does NOT explicitly state he spiked the guns." Wikipedia's Battle of Fort Sumter article confirms it: "Anderson spiked Moultrie's guns and burned its gun carriages before departing." This is well-attested.

**Fact needed**: Not in the current fact pack (the ledger flag is correct — pack says only "indefensible Fort Moultrie, easily assailable from the rear"). The coordinator should add this to the pack before writing it in.

---

### MUST-ADD 3 (BLOCKING): The relief fleet's failure — and why it mattered

**Status in draft: THIN (one clause, no cause)**

The draft dispatches the relief fleet in a single parenthetical: "relief fleet that had arrived too late to help." This buries what is actually a significant beat. The fleet did arrive — Fox's ships were sitting off the harbor bar while the bombardment was happening and Anderson was surrendering. The reason they could not act was the diversion of the USS Powhatan (the fleet's most powerful vessel) to Fort Pickens, Florida — a confusion caused by a secret, cross-cutting order from Secretary of State Seward that the Navy Department did not know about. Fox, waiting off the bar, could not enter against Confederate guns without the Powhatan.

This matters narratively because it closes the trap Lincoln set: the relief fleet came, it could be seen, and it still could not help. The Confederacy fired on the fort before any supply could arrive, and the fleet that was supposed to change the equation was hobbled by a bureaucratic blunder inside Lincoln's own cabinet. It also gives the reader a concrete reason why "too late" was literally true — not just timing, but the missing warship.

The fact pack covers this (§5, "The relief fleet's failure") but it is essentially unwritten in the draft.

**Fact needed**: In the pack. No additional sourcing required.

---

### MUST-ADD 4 (BLOCKING): Buchanan's role — the four months of inaction that made the crisis

**Status in draft: MISSING**

The draft opens with Anderson's move on December 26 and then jumps to "all spring, a ring of guns rose." It skips the Buchanan administration entirely. The reader never learns that for the four months between Anderson's move and Lincoln's inauguration, President Buchanan sat on his hands — protesting secession in principle while doing essentially nothing to act on it, partly because he believed he lacked the constitutional authority to coerce a state, partly because he was terrified of pushing the remaining slave states out. It was Buchanan's paralysis that allowed the Confederate fortification of the harbor to proceed unimpeded. The whole reason Lincoln inherited a "lit fuse" (as the draft nicely puts it) is that Buchanan handed it to him already burning.

This is not a deep dive into Buchanan history. One tight paragraph — or even a single pointed sentence — about the lame-duck president who watched the harbor get ringed with guns and did nothing is enough. The draft currently implies the ring just... appeared, while Washington did nothing, and the reader has no idea why.

The fact pack does not explicitly cover this — it is a fact-pack blind spot (see below).

**Fact needed**: Not in the pack. This is a gap in the fact pack itself. The coordinator should source: Buchanan's stated position that secession was illegal but that he lacked authority to prevent it; his refusal to reinforce Sumter proactively; why this left Lincoln inheriting an already-fortified standoff. Wikipedia and Britannica both cover this; no exotic sourcing needed.

## SHOULD-CONSIDER — Non-Blocking Enrichment

### SC-1: Anderson's father at Fort Moultrie / Revolutionary War connection

Anderson's father, Richard Anderson, was an officer in the Revolutionary War and served at the original Fort Moultrie. The son who spiked Moultrie's guns and moved to Sumter had a family connection to the fort he abandoned. This is a nice layer of historical echo — not essential, but genuinely atmospheric for a narrative about inherited loyalties and war passing between generations. The fact pack does not cover this detail; would need sourcing (Wikipedia's Robert Anderson article confirms the family's military prominence and the father's Revolutionary War service).

---

### SC-2: The Buchanan "not immediately" promise to Pickens

The draft says Pickens considered Anderson's move "a breach of faith." It never explains what the faith was based on. Buchanan had used ambiguous language to Pickens — reportedly promising not to "immediately" reinforce or change the situation at Sumter — language careful enough to be plausibly deniable. Pickens understood it as a promise; Buchanan understood it as a hedge. One sentence on this would make Pickens's fury more than personal pique and give the reader the actual story of how the standoff was supposed to be frozen in place. This is more granular than MUST-ADD 4 above; if Buchanan's general paralysis is covered (MUST-ADD 4), this can be omitted.

---

### SC-3: Anderson promoted to brigadier general after the battle; 100,000-person rally in Manhattan

On April 19, over 100,000 people turned out in Manhattan to receive Anderson and his garrison. He was promoted to brigadier general. This is a strong coda: the man who lost the first battle became an instant national hero. The draft covers the flag becoming "a rallying symbol" but skips the scale of what happened when Anderson arrived in New York. It is a vivid concrete scene — if any section of the draft is thin at the ending, this could reinforce it. Non-blocking.

---

### SC-4: Border state governors' responses to the 75,000-volunteer call

The fact pack covers this (§7, "Border state defusals"): Missouri's and Kentucky's governors both publicly refused Lincoln's call in striking language. The draft covers Upper South secession (the 4 states that left) but skips the slave states that stayed — Kentucky and Missouri, which seesawed on the edge and ultimately did not secede, but whose governors called Lincoln's request "unholy" and "wicked." This adds texture to the Toombs warning coming true: the call did not just trigger the Upper South's exit, it inflamed the Border. Non-blocking, but the contrast (four states OUT, border states furious but IN) sharpens the political picture.

---

### SC-5: The magnitude of Confederate fire — 3,000+ rounds

The fact pack notes (and the Wikipedia fetch confirms) that Anderson withstood "over 3,000 Confederate rounds" in 34 hours. The draft conveys the intensity well through scene and detail, but the raw number — 3,000 rounds to produce zero combat deaths — would land the "bloodless bombardment" point with added force. One number, one sentence. Non-blocking.

---

### SC-6: The ammunition magazine danger — 300 barrels of powder surrounded by fire

The Wikipedia fetch surfaces a detail not in the fact pack: by the morning of April 13, the fires had surrounded the main powder magazine, which contained 300 barrels of gunpowder. Anderson ordered the doors sealed and the remaining powder moved. The fort was one stray spark away from catastrophic explosion throughout the final hours — a detail that raises the stakes of the fire section considerably. Cinematic, but non-blocking if the fire narrative is already strong enough.

---

### SC-7: Anderson's April 14, 1865 return — more context

The draft's symmetry section (four years to the day) is one of the strongest passages. One small additional beat: Anderson was a "major general in failing health" (covered) but also — he had carried the specific flag he lowered in 1861. The same physical object. The draft says this, but could make the object itself more vivid: this was not "a" Union flag but the literal flag he had saluted and carried away on April 14, 1861. Some readers will miss that the flag is the same physical artifact.

## Fact-Pack Blind Spots

These are gaps in the fact pack itself — details that are well-attested in standard sources but absent from the pack. They are relevant to what the draft could or should say.

**1. Buchanan administration paralysis (SIGNIFICANT GAP)**
The pack jumps from South Carolina's secession (Dec 20) to Lincoln's inauguration (March 4) with no coverage of Buchanan's four months of inaction. This is the context that explains why the harbor was ringed with Confederate guns by the time Lincoln took office. Any treatment of "why Lincoln inherited a lit fuse" needs this. Sources: Wikipedia "Battle of Fort Sumter" (covers Buchanan section); Britannica.

The ledger flags this correctly as absent from the pack. It is well-attested (Wikipedia, ABT), and is the specific act that made Pickens's fury understandable. Should be added to the pack.

**3. The 3,000+ rounds fired during the bombardment**
Pack gives the gun count (43) and duration (34 hours) but not the total rounds fired. Adds force to the zero-combat-deaths fact.

**4. The powder magazine danger during the fires**
Not in pack. Well-attested (Wikipedia). Enriching, non-blocking.

**5. Anderson's father's Revolutionary War service / family connection to Fort Moultrie**
Not in pack. Well-attested (Wikipedia, ABT biographies). Enriching, non-blocking.

**6. Buchanan's specific "not immediately" language to Pickens**
Related to gap #1. Not in pack. Sourced (Wikipedia, ABT). Non-blocking if general Buchanan paralysis is covered.

## Summary

**MUST-ADD count: 4**

1. Anderson's personal identity (Kentucky-born Unionist former slaveholder — deepens the teacher-student irony and the moral stakes; in the fact pack, not used)
2. Anderson spiked Moultrie's guns — the act that made Pickens's fury legible (not in fact pack; coordinator must source)
3. The relief fleet's failure — why Fox could not enter (in fact pack, not written; closes Lincoln's trap)
4. Buchanan's four-month paralysis — why Lincoln inherited a fortified standoff (not in fact pack; significant fact-pack blind spot, coordinator must source)

**Most important MUST-ADD: #4 (Buchanan paralysis).** The draft's opening section frames the story as starting with Anderson's move, but leaves the reader with no explanation for why the ring of batteries was allowed to form for months, or why Lincoln faced a crisis on day one. The absence makes the Confederate fortification of the harbor seem almost natural — "all spring, a ring of guns rose" — when in fact it was the direct result of four months of presidential inaction that a one-paragraph treatment could explain and dramatize. It is also a fact-pack blind spot, meaning the coordinator needs to source it before it can be written.
