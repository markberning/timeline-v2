# STORYTELLING CRITIC · Battle of Stony Point draft (rev-stony-point)

*Critic pass 2026-06-12, against `rev-stony-point-draft.md`. Bar = `rev-germantown-final.md`.

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is a lean, well-shaped battle page for exactly what the subject is: a small, perfect raid,
not a flagship. The three-section structure is correct. The dossier note is the best single
paragraph in the draft — it could run standalone and the reader would know everything. Section 2
handles the assault's clockwork logic well: the unloaded-musket order, the three-role split
(two bayonet columns + Murfree's noisy center), the forlorn hopes, the twenty-five minutes. The
restraint beat in section 3 has its two good quotes and the Collier counter-testimony is exactly
right. The ending — Washington abandons the point, the raid's logic holds anyway, Paulus Hook
copies it — closes with stakes and doesn't moralize.

What keeps it from a clean PASS: Wayne is vivid in the dossier bio and nearly absent as a
character in the prose; the assault section dramatizes the plan's structure well but buries the
tension of the unloaded-musket order at the back of the paragraph where it belongs at the front;
and the restraint meaning-beat in section 3 is told correctly but opens with a sentence that
tells the reader what to think before the evidence lands. These are fixable. The bones are clean.

---

## BLOCKER

### B1. Wayne's wound arrives too late — the section's only lived moment is passive

Section 2's most human moment is Wayne going down at the inner abatis and going on anyway. The
draft places it correctly in the assault sequence, but renders it at a distance:

> "Near the inner abatis a musket ball grazed Anthony Wayne (American) across the scalp and
> knocked him down, stunned and bloodied. Tradition has him gasping to be carried into the fort
> to die at the head of his column; what the record shows is that he was helped up, went on in
> with his men, and minutes later was inside it."

The tradition-framing is right and required. But "what the record shows is that he was helped up,
went on in with his men, and minutes later was inside it" is a claims-management sentence, not
a story sentence. The paragraph immediately follows with the watchword going up and resistance
collapsing — the two events are juxtaposed but not connected. The reader never feels the thing
the scene is actually about: this is the man who was bayoneted in the dark at Paoli, his men
around him, and he goes forward with a bandaged head. The dispatch from inside the works at
2 a.m. is the punctuation on that, and the quote IS there, one paragraph later. The problem is
the wound-paragraph itself reads like a fact to be disclaimed rather than a moment to be felt.

The section needs one sentence — not dramatic editorializing, just the plain geometry — that
connects Wayne's wound to the Paoli memory that runs through the whole piece. The dossier bio
has it: "His own division had been bayoneted in the dark at Paoli two years before." That
connection exists in the reader's head from section 2's opening paragraph (Steuben drill,
Valley Forge). But it disappears entirely from the wound-moment, the one place it would land
hardest.

**Fix:** After "went on in with his men," add one clause that does the connective work without
editorializing. Something like: "It was the second time in two years he had taken the bayonet
end of a night assault; this time he was writing from inside the fort." Then the Wayne dispatch
quote follows as the evidence, not as a kicker stapled to an earlier paragraph. The transition
"That was Wayne's whole dispatch to Washington, written from inside the captured works at two
o'clock in the morning with a bandage on his head" can stay verbatim — it earns the quote.

---

### B2. The unloaded-musket order is introduced after the column structure — the tension is
backward

The unloaded-musket order is the entire emotional and dramatic premise of this battle. The reader
already knows it from the dossier note and the section title. But in section 2, the plan is
introduced in this order: the corps' pedigree (paragraph 1, the march) → paragraph 2 opens
with the three-piece structure, then midway through: "The order to go in unloaded was the whole
secret." That clause arrives inside a dense list of moving parts (two columns, the center,
the vans, the forlorn hopes, the white paper, the watchword, the cash rewards, the death
threat for firing). By the time the reader reaches "the order to go in unloaded was the whole
secret," they are already processing six other things and the sentence does not land.

> "The plan that the officers learned there was a piece of clockwork. Three pieces of the corps
> would do three different things. Two columns would make the real attack, both with their
> muskets unloaded and their bayonets fixed..."

The structure is correct — the unloaded order IS named near the top. But "a piece of clockwork"
is a frame sentence that promises mechanism, not tension, and what follows delivers mechanism.
The reader needs to feel the danger of the unloaded order before they get the plan's parts. What
is a soldier doing going into an abatis at night with an empty musket? Why does one nervous shot
throw the whole thing away? The draft answers this ("so no nervous shot in the dark would wake
the garrison") in passing, inside the plan-summary. It should be the lede of the assault logic,
not a subordinate clause.

**Fix:** Split the plan paragraph. Open with the unloaded-musket order as its own beat — one or
two sentences on what it means to assault a fortified garrison in the dark with your weapon
deliberately empty, and what a single accidental shot would cost. Then introduce the three-piece
structure as the mechanism built around that constraint. The plan's clockwork is more dramatic
if the reader understands the knife-edge first. The existing sentences mostly stay; the reorder
does the work.

## SHOULD-FIX

### S1. Section 3 opens by telling the reader what to think

Section 3's first sentence:

> "The restraint was not lost on anyone, least of all the enemy."

This is the meta-narrator announcing the meaning before the evidence arrives. The reader has
just read the restraint beat at the end of section 2 — the garrison asking quarter, the
quarter given, the ~20 killed and ~500 prisoners. The section 3 Wayne humanity quote and the
Collier counter-testimony are the evidence that the restraint "was not lost on anyone." They
should open the section, not be introduced by a sentence explaining what they will demonstrate.

The Germantown bar document does not do this. Its section 3 opens with the army's behavior in
retreat — what happened, not what it meant. The meaning accumulates from the evidence.

**Fix:** Cut "The restraint was not lost on anyone, least of all the enemy." Open section 3
directly with the Wayne July 17 humanity quote. It earns everything the cut sentence was
announcing. The Collier journal passage follows as the enemy confirmation. The argument is made
by the quotes, not by the framing sentence.

---

### S2. The Paoli grievance beat is in the right place but not quite landed

The restrained assault's meaning depends on the Paoli connection being felt, not just cited.
The draft handles this carefully and correctly — it names both Paoli and Old Tappan, frames
the restraint as discipline not softness, correctly avoids the blacklisted "Remember Paoli!"
battle cry. But the handling is slightly legalistic:

> "The same corps had grievances to settle. Two years before, Wayne's own division had been
> surprised in the dark and bayoneted at Paoli, in Pennsylvania, an attack Americans called a
> massacre, and there had been a second such affair at Old Tappan since. The men with the
> bayonets in their hands had Paoli in their memory. They used the bayonet, because that was
> the order and the only way in; and when the garrison cried for mercy, they stopped."

"The same corps had grievances to settle" — this is an authorial assertion that the grievance
was active, not the documented event that makes the contrast. The sentence does the same thing
as S1's opening: it tells rather than shows. Compare how the Wayne commander bio handles it:
"His own division had been bayoneted in the dark at Paoli two years before; at Stony Point his
men did the bayoneting, and then gave quarter." That is twenty words and it lands. The section 2
prose version takes five sentences to do less.

**Fix:** Cut "The same corps had grievances to settle." Start with the plain fact: "Two years
before, Wayne's own division had been bayoneted in the dark at Paoli." Then move directly to
"The men with the bayonets in their hands had Paoli in their memory. They used the bayonet,
because that was the order and the only way in; and when the garrison cried for mercy, they
stopped." Three sentences, no meta-framing. The reader does the arithmetic.

## TRIM

### T1. The 1779 stalemate context is correctly braided but slightly over-explained in section 1

Section 1 covers the post-Monmouth stalemate, the Hudson's strategic weight, King's Ferry,
West Point, and Clinton's seizure of the ferry in six paragraphs. This is the right material
and the pacing is mostly right for a battle with a significant setup. But the third paragraph —
Clinton's seizure of both ends of the ferry, the Tryon raids, and Washington's response — does
some work that the strategic locator and the MEANWHILE will also do. The Tryon material in
particular:

> "He even sent General William Tryon (British) with some 2,600 men to burn and plunder the
> Connecticut coast in early July, hitting New Haven, Fairfield, and Norwalk in a week, partly
> to punish, partly as more bait."

And then the MEANWHILE box at the end of section 1:

> "MEANWHILE: The Connecticut coast | The fires that did not work | While Wayne's corps was
> assembling, Tryon's men were still burning their way along the Connecticut shore, New Haven,
> then Fairfield, then Norwalk..."

The raids are listed twice — once in the prose, once in the MEANWHILE box — with overlapping
content. This is a small battle. The setup should be lean.

**Fix:** In the section 1 prose, compress the Tryon material to one sentence: "He sent raiders
to burn the Connecticut coast that same month — New Haven, Fairfield, Norwalk in a week — the
same bait on a longer leash." The MEANWHILE box then gets the fuller telling and the
Washington-refused-all-of-it beat, where it belongs. The section 1 prose saves ~60 words without
losing the beat.

### T2. Section 3's Fleury-Stewart-Wayne medals passage is slightly bottom-heavy

The medals paragraph is correct and the detail level is appropriate for a "what it meant"
section. But it runs through Fleury's silver, Stewart's silver, Wayne's gold, the three-of-
a-dozen figure, the prize money, and the celebration across the states before landing on
Washington abandoning the point — which is the section's structural pivot. The passage buries
its own turn:

> "Then Washington gave the place away."

This sentence, after the long medals run, reads as an afterthought rather than the pivot it is.
The Washington decision IS the point of the section (take the trophy, keep nothing the enemy
can besiege). The medals are evidence; the abandonment is the argument.

**Fix:** Shorten the medals paragraph by one sentence — the prize-money appraisal figure
("appraised at more than $150,000 in the inflated paper money of the day, divided among the
troops as prize money") is the compressible beat, since the medals already demonstrate Congress's
view — and put "Then Washington gave the place away" as the start of a new paragraph, not a
buried continuation. This gives the pivot the visual weight it earns.

## NIT

### N1. "a piece of clockwork" is mechanical, not evocative

> "The plan that the officers learned there was a piece of clockwork."

"Clockwork" works as a structural metaphor — the plan IS clock-like, with interlocking parts.
But it is a writer's frame, not the reader's experience of the thing. The plan was a piece of
clockwork that sent men into an abatis in the dark with unloaded muskets. "Clockwork" mutes the
danger. The B2 fix restructures this paragraph anyway; if it survives, reconsider the word.

### N2. "they had been assembled to attempt" is hedging where the draft should commit

> "A silent night bayonet assault is the single hardest thing the eighteenth-century manual asks
> of infantry, and this was the corps assembled to attempt it..."

"Assembled to attempt it" — they didn't attempt it, they did it in twenty-five minutes. The
hedge is factually safe but it deflates the setup. "Assembled to do it, and this was the night"
is the read; the prose shouldn't pre-undermine the outcome with "attempt."

### N3. The Wayne dispatch quote transition is slightly clunky

> "QUOTE: The fort & Garrison with Colo. Johnston are ours—Our Officers & men behaved like men
> who are determined to be free.
> 
> That was Wayne's whole dispatch to Washington, written from inside the captured works at two
> o'clock in the morning with a bandage on his head."

"That was Wayne's whole dispatch" is a strong close. But "with a bandage on his head" arriving
at the end of the attribution line is a detail-tag bolted to a summary sentence. If the B1 fix
integrates the wound into the assault narrative properly, this attribution can simplify: "Wayne's
dispatch to Washington, two o'clock in the morning." The bandage will already be established
in the prose above it; the attribution doesn't need to repeat it.

### N4. "the watchword, to be shouted only once the works were taken" — grammar

> "...the watchword, to be shouted only once the works were taken, was 'The fort's our own!'"

"Only once the works were taken" is ambiguous — it reads first as "only one time." "To be
shouted only after the works were taken" is cleaner.

## CHECKS THE DRAFT PASSES (for the record)

- **Voice ship-blockers: clean.** No em-dashes in original prose; no drama-bold; no
  meta-narrator ("as we'll see," "our story," etc.). Register is correct throughout — plain,
  direct, informal-informational.
- **Apocrypha blacklist: clean.** "Remember Paoli!" not in the prose. The "Carry me into the
  fort" line is correctly framed as tradition only, never as speech. "Little Gibraltar" is
  attributed to "the histories," not a named contemporary. Dog-slaughter omitted, civilian
  detention told instead. British dead given as official ~20; the 63 claim is mentioned as
  inflated and explicitly not used. Murfree's fire is in the prose as the planned diversion,
  so the "not a shot fired" absolute is correctly avoided.
- **Wayne as character:** the dossier bio is the strongest commander bio in the draft — the
  Paoli/court-martial/light-corps arc is exactly right. Section 2 gives him the dispatch; the
  B1 fix will give him a proper moment in the assault. His character is present; it just needs
  one connection point in the prose.
- **The three-section shape is lean.** This is a small battle and the draft treats it like one.
  No section is bloated. The MEANWHILE boxes are doing real work (the failed Verplanck's half
  is the right structural counterpoint; the Connecticut-coast MEANWHILE places the stalemate
  context without a lecture; the Paulus Hook MEANWHILE closes the "model" beat cleanly).
- **The ending works.** "Take the trophy, keep nothing the enemy could besiege" is the correct
  war-of-posts logic, stated plainly. Clinton's whole Hudson effort unwinding — West Point
  untouched, the ferry evacuated in October, the momentum draining south — closes the strategic
  frame without overclaiming the battle's significance. The lighthouse closer earns its place.
- **The stalemate context is braided, not lectured.** Section 1 builds the Hudson-seam logic
  from the ground up; the post-Monmouth context arrives as setting, not thesis paragraph. The
  T1 fix trims a minor redundancy but the approach is correct throughout.
- **The stats and dossier are clean.** Ranges used throughout; no bare precise numbers; British
  dead given as official ~20 with the American inflated claim noted. The 25-minute figure is
  the right clock for the piece.
