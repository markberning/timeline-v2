**CWSAC reference:** LA001 [NPS-LA001]
**Campaign:** Expedition to and Capture of New Orleans [NPS-LA001]

**Dates:**

- Chain/hulk barrier breached: night of April 20–21, 1862 [BATTLEFIELDS-ABT; MARINERSM] — *see Section 10, date contested*
- Main fleet passage past the forts: pre-dawn April 24, 1862 (~2:00–3:30 a.m.) [WP-BATTLE; ECCW; MARINERSM]

**Location:** Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana; Forts Jackson and St. Philip positioned ~75 miles below New Orleans (some sources say 75 miles, one says 120 km / ~75 miles [WP-BATTLE]), at a bend in the Mississippi River near Plaquemines Bend. Fort Jackson on the WEST (right descending) bank; Fort St. Philip on the EAST (left descending) bank, approximately half a mile upstream of Fort Jackson. [WP-BATTLE; ECCW]

**Belligerents:**
- Union: West Gulf Blockading Squadron (naval); Army of the Gulf (land)
- Confederate: Defenses of New Orleans and Lower Mississippi; Confederate States Navy; Confederate River Defense Fleet

**Union commanders:**

- Commander David Dixon Porter — mortar flotilla (semi-independent command) [WP-BATTLE; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT]
- Captain Theodorus Bailey — first division of gunboats [WP-BATTLE]
- Captain Henry H. Bell — second/third division of gunboats [WP-BATTLE]
- Major General Benjamin F. Butler — Union Army, 18,000 troops following the fleet [WP-BATTLE; 64PARISHES]

**Confederate commanders:**
- Brigadier General Johnson K. Duncan (born March 19, 1827, York County, Pennsylvania; West Point class of 1849, ranked 5th of 43 cadets) — commanding both forts and all lower river defenses [YORK-DUNCANBIOG; WP-BATTLE]
- Commander John K. Mitchell — Confederate naval forces (CSS Louisiana, River Defense Fleet coordination) [WP-BATTLE]
- Captain John A. Stephenson — River Defense Fleet (cottonclad rams) [WP-BATTLE]
- Major General Mansfield Lovell — commanding Confederate Department No. 1 (New Orleans department); he evacuated the city with ~3,000 troops before the fleet arrived [CAPTURE-WIKI; 64PARISHES; LOVELL-WIKI]

**Union forces:**
- 17 warships (sloops, gunboats, corvettes) [HISTORYNET-FARR; MARINERSM] — *see Section 10 for fleet count variants*

- Total ~47 vessels, ~310 guns [HISTORYCENTRAL]
- 18,000 Army troops under Butler [WP-BATTLE; 64PARISHES]
- Major vessels: USS Hartford (flagship), USS Pensacola, USS Mississippi, USS Brooklyn, USS Richmond, USS Oneida, USS Varuna, USS Cayuga, USS Sciota, USS Iroquois, USS Kennebec, USS Pinola, USS Itasca, USS Winona, USS Katahdin, USS Kineo, USS Wissahickon, USS Portsmouth [WP-BATTLE]

**Confederate forces (lower river):**

- Fort St. Philip: 52 guns; brick and stone earthwork, east bank, ~half mile upstream of Fort Jackson [CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH; WP-BATTLE]

- 3 Confederate naval vessels of note: CSS Manassas (ironclad ram), CSS Louisiana (large casemate ironclad, unfinished), CSS Mississippi (large ironclad, not yet operational)
- 2 converted warships: CSS McRae, CSS Jackson
- 2 Louisiana Provisional Navy vessels: CSS General Quitman, CSS Governor Moore
- 6 cottonclad rams of the River Defense Fleet: Stonewall Jackson, Warrior, Defiance, Resolute, General Breckinridge, General Lovell [WP-BATTLE; RDF-WIKI]
- Various fire rafts and tugs

**Outcome:** Decisive Union victory. New Orleans captured; Confederacy's largest city and primary port lost. [NPS-LA001; WP-BATTLE]

## 3. WHAT CAME BEFORE

**New Orleans' size and importance:**
- Population ~168,675–170,000 (1860 census range cited in sources), making it the 6th largest U.S. city and by far the Confederacy's largest — larger than Charleston (40,522), Richmond (37,910), Mobile (29,258), Memphis (22,623), and Savannah (22,292) combined [NPS-10FACTS; WP-NEWORLEANS; EMERGINGCW]
- More than half of all American cotton passed through the port [NPS-10FACTS; WP-NEWORLEANS]
- Second only to Richmond in the value of manufactured goods; hosted the Leeds Foundry, the South's second-most important ironworks after the Tredegar in Richmond [EMERGINGCW]
- Outlet for goods from the entire Mississippi River valley [ECCW; WP-NEWORLEANS]
- Strategic gateway to the Anaconda Plan — Union General-in-Chief Winfield Scott's strategy to strangle Confederate commerce by blockading ports and controlling the Mississippi [BATTLEFIELDS-ABT; ECCW]

**Why the defenses were stripped:**
- Confederate leadership (and most officials) did not believe the city was vulnerable to a naval attack from the south; they focused defenses upstream at Island No. 10, Fort Pillow, and Vicksburg [ECCW; EMERGINGCW]
- Troops were drained by Union victories at Forts Henry and Donelson (February 1862) and sent to reinforce other theaters [ECCW]
- The Confederate naval flotilla under Commodore George Nichols Hollins was reassigned upstream to Island No. 10 [ECCW]
- The Union victory at Shiloh (April 6–7, 1862) further diverted Confederate resources [ECCW; EMERGINGCW]
- Result: Lovell had only ~3,000 ninety-day troops in New Orleans itself, of which about 1,200 had muskets [EMERGINGCW]; forts held ~1,100 [64PARISHES]

**Confederate river defenses at the forts:**
- Fort Jackson (west/right bank): pentagonal bastioned brick fortification built 1822–1832, mounting ~74 guns. Many were worn smoothbores, too light for modern fleet engagement [CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH; WP-BATTLE]
- Fort St. Philip (east/left bank): older brick and stone earthwork covered with sod, mounting ~52 guns; located approximately half a mile upstream of Fort Jackson [WP-BATTLE; CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH]
- The forts together mounted 126 guns [HISTORYNET-FARR] or as many as 177 in total count including all emplacements [WP-BATTLE] — *see Section 10*
- A barrier chain stretched across the river between the forts, threaded through eight moored hulks (derelict vessels) [HISTORYNET-FARR; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT; MARINERSM], with fire rafts added as additional obstacles [WP-BATTLE; ECCW]
- The two unfinished Confederate ironclads:
  - CSS Louisiana: 1,400-ton casemate ironclad, 264 ft long, 62-ft beam, draft 12–13 ft; armed with 2×7-inch rifles, 3×9-inch shell guns, 4×8-inch shell guns, 7×32-pounder guns; propulsion unreliable — her four engines (2 paddlewheels + 2 screws) were not working properly; essentially moored as a floating battery [MILITARYFACTORY-CSS-LA; WP-BATTLE; ECCW]
  - CSS Mississippi: even less complete; only launched in late April with minimal machinery and just two cannons installed — never operational [ECCW; EMERGINGCW]
- CSS Manassas: older Confederate ironclad ram; 387-ton, 143 ft long, armed with 1×64-pounder Dahlgren gun (later a 32-pounder smoothbore), crew of 104 men; notable as one of the first ironclad vessels to serve in the Civil War [MANASSAS-SEARCH; WP-BATTLE]
- River Defense Fleet: 6 cottonclad rams at New Orleans (remainder — 8 vessels — sent to Memphis). Converted merchant steamers/towboats with reinforced bows, armed with 1–2 light guns, double-timber bulkheads packed with cotton bales [RDF-WIKI; COTTONCLAD-SEARCH]. The 6 at New Orleans: Stonewall Jackson, Warrior, Defiance, Resolute, General Breckinridge, General Lovell [RDF-WIKI]

## 4. THE BOMBARDMENT AND THE RUN (APRIL 18–24)

**The mortar bombardment (April 18–23):**
- Porter's mortar schooners each carried a 13-inch seacoast mortar [MARINERSM]; flotilla comprised 20 schooners (or 19/26 per other sources — see Section 10)
- Bombardment began April 18, 1862 [WP-BATTLE; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT]
- One source (ECCW) gives the start as April 15; the majority give April 18; *see Section 10*
- Shells fired during the bombardment: approximately 7,500 over the 5–6 day bombardment [WP-BATTLE; MARINERSM]; other sources say 13,000 over 96 hours [64PARISHES]
- First day alone: Porter fired ~1,400 shells [WP-BATTLE; MARINERSM]; one source counts 2,997 mortar shells on Day 1 by Confederate General Duncan's own count [WP-BATTLE]
- Confederate General Duncan's account of April 18: "The mortar fire was accurate and terrible, many of the shells falling everywhere within the fort and disabling some of our best guns." [WP-BATTLE, citing Duncan's official report]
- Damage to Fort Jackson was real but not disabling: drawbridge, hot-shot furnaces, cisterns destroyed; outer walls cracked; 4 guns dismounted, 11 carriages damaged; casemate floors flooded [WP-BATTLE]
- Fort St. Philip was minimally damaged [HISTORYNET-FARR; ECCW]
- The bombardment failed to reduce either fort sufficiently to prevent them from firing on the fleet [all sources agree]

**Breaking the chain barrier:**
- The barrier: a chain cable threaded through 8 moored hulks, stretching shore to shore [HISTORYNET-FARR; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT]
- USS Pinola and USS Itasca were assigned to break through the barrier [BATTLEFIELDS-ABT; MARINERSM]
- Date given: April 20 [MARINERSM says "April 20"; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT says "April 23"; USS Pinola Wikipedia confirms "April 1862" without a specific date; *see Section 10 for the discrepancy*]
- Result: a gap was opened in the chain/hulk barrier on the east side near Fort St. Philip [HISTORYNET-FARR]

**Farragut's decision:**
- After five to six days of bombardment with limited effect, Farragut convened a council of war. Porter (his stepbrother) favored continued bombardment; Farragut overruled him [ACWS-SEARCH]. Farragut decided to run the fleet past the forts in the pre-dawn dark rather than wait for their reduction [all sources agree]
- On April 23, Farragut ordered his captains to armor their vessels with anchor chains draped over the sides and sandbag protection [MARINERSM; ECCW]
- The fleet was divided into three divisions [WP-BATTLE; ECCW]:
  - First division (Captain Bailey): USS Cayuga (lead), Pensacola, Mississippi, Oneida, Varuna, Katahdin, Kineo, Wissahickon (8 vessels)
  - Second division (Farragut's center): USS Hartford (flagship), Brooklyn, Richmond (3 vessels)
  - Third division (Captain Bell): USS Sciota, Iroquois, Kennebec, Pinola, Itasca, Winona (6 vessels)
  - USS Portsmouth remained to protect the mortar schooners

**The passage (pre-dawn April 24):**
- Passage began approximately 2:00–3:30 a.m., April 24, 1862 [ECCW: 3:30 a.m.; WP-BATTLE: ~3:00 a.m.; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT: 2:00 a.m.] — *see Section 10*
- The Union fleet passed through the gap in the barrier under fire from both forts in a chaotic night battle lit by fire rafts and muzzle flashes [WP-BATTLE; ECCW; all sources]
- USS Hartford (Farragut's flagship) ran aground near Fort St. Philip after avoiding a fire raft; the CSS Mosher pushed a fire raft against her, she caught fire but the crew extinguished the flames and she worked free [WP-BATTLE; HISTORYNET-FARR; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT]
- CSS Governor Moore (cottonclad) rammed USS Varuna repeatedly; Varuna was sunk in shallow water — the only Union warship lost in the passage [WP-BATTLE; ECCW]
- CSS Manassas rammed USS Mississippi and USS Brooklyn without disabling either; she was driven ashore and set afire, later floated free, and sank [WP-BATTLE; MARINERSM; MANASSAS-SEARCH]
- CSS Louisiana (the large ironclad) was moored near Fort St. Philip; her guns engaged the fleet, but her non-functional engines made her unable to maneuver — she served only as a floating battery [ECCW; WP-BATTLE]
- CSS McRae engaged the Union fleet; her commanding officer Lieutenant Commander Thomas B. Huger was mortally wounded [WP-BATTLE]
- Three Union vessels turned back without completing the passage: USS Itasca (disabled by shot to boilers), USS Pinola, USS Winona (both turned back at dawn) [WP-BATTLE]
- Result: 13–14 of Farragut's warships passed successfully [ECCW says 13; WP-BATTLE says 14 passed; NPS-LA001 confirms success]; the Confederate naval flotilla was effectively destroyed — 12 Confederate vessels lost during the battle [WP-BATTLE]
- Historian John D. Winters on the Confederate naval performance: "made a sorry showing. Self-destruction, lack of co-operation, cowardice of untrained officers, and the murderous fire of the Federal gunboats reduced the fleet to a demoralized shambles." [WP-BATTLE, citing Winters]

## 5. THE FALL OF NEW ORLEANS (APRIL 25–29)

**April 25:**
- Farragut's fleet steamed upriver after passing the forts and arrived before New Orleans
- Farragut anchored at noon before the city [ABT-10FACTS; HISTORYNET-FARR]
- General Mansfield Lovell evacuated his ~3,000 troops northward toward Camp Moore, 78 miles away, rather than defend the city against naval bombardment [ECCW; EMERGINGCW; CAPTURE-WIKI]
- Mayor John T. Monroe and the city government refused to formally surrender: Monroe stated the city was undefended [ECCW; HISTORYNET-FARR]
- Confederate citizens burned cotton bales, sugar, and other supplies along the wharves to prevent Union capture; the unfinished ironclad CSS Mississippi was set afire and floated downstream as a burning hulk [ECCW; CAPTURE-WIKI]
- Captain Bailey was sent ashore by Farragut on April 25 to demand the city's surrender [CAPTURE-WIKI]

**April 26:**
- Marines from USS Pocahontas were sent ashore to raise the U.S. flag over the New Orleans Mint [WP-MUMFORD]
- William Bruce Mumford (b. December 5, 1819, North Carolina; resident of New Orleans, described as "a noted gambler") was part of a group of seven who tore the flag down [WP-MUMFORD; BATTLEFIELDS-10FACTS]
- The Pocahontas fired, injuring Mumford with flying brick debris; he and the group carried the flag to City Hall, where onlookers tore it "reducing it to a stub" [WP-MUMFORD]
- Butler reportedly identified Mumford wearing a piece of the flag later — *this identification is noted as possibly disputed in sources* [WP-MUMFORD]

**April 28:**
- Fort Jackson garrison mutinied the night of April 27–28; approximately 300 soldiers spiked guns and deserted [YORK-DUNCANBIOG; ECCW]
- No mutiny occurred at Fort St. Philip [YORK-DUNCANBIOG]
- Brigadier General Duncan, cut off, his boats destroyed, supplies cut off, and the garrison half-deserted, initiated surrender negotiations [YORK-DUNCANBIOG; WP-BATTLE]
- Both forts surrendered April 28, 1862 [NPS-LA001; WP-BATTLE] — *Wikipedia's infobox gives surrender as April 30 for the final capitulation; the NPS gives April 28; see Section 10*
- Commander Mitchell ordered CSS Louisiana set afire rather than surrender her as part of the truce; she blew up passing Fort St. Philip, killing one Confederate soldier in the fort [WP-BATTLE]

**April 29:**
- Farragut and 250 marines went ashore; they removed the Louisiana State flag from City Hall and raised the U.S. flag over the Customs House, signifying official occupation [CAPTURE-WIKI; WP-BATTLE]
- One source dates this act to April 29 [CAPTURE-WIKI]; ABT gives "April 25" for the initial anchoring, with the flag-raising at Customs House on April 29 [ABT-NOLA; WP-BATTLE]

**May 1, 1862:**
- Butler's army occupied New Orleans; 5,000 troops entered without resistance [CAPTURE-WIKI; ECCW]
- Butler declared martial law; demanded loyalty oaths; confiscated weapons [64PARISHES; ECCW]
- Secretary of State William H. Seward declared New Orleans "recovered" on May 2 [CAPTURE-WIKI]

**Mumford execution:**
- Trial: May 30, 1862, military tribunal convicted Mumford of treason (despite questions about whether the city was legally occupied at the time of the April 26 incident) [WP-MUMFORD; HOUSEDIVIDED]
- Butler's Special Order No. 70 (issued June 5, 1862): Mumford convicted of "treason and an overt act thereof, tearing down the United States flag from a public building of the United States, after said flag was placed there by Commodore Farragut" [EXECUTEDTODAY]
- Execution: June 7, 1862, hanged at noon from a flagstaff protruding from the New Orleans Mint [WP-MUMFORD; ABT-10FACTS; EXECUTEDTODAY; HOUSEDIVIDED]
- Confederate President Jefferson Davis declared Butler deserved capital punishment in response [EXECUTEDTODAY]
- British Prime Minister Viscount Palmerston publicly condemned the execution [64PARISHES]

## 6. KEY PEOPLE (SIDE-TAGGED)

**David Glasgow Farragut (Union)**
- Born: July 5, 1801, near Knoxville, Tennessee [FARRAGUT-WIKI]
- Raised by naval officer David Porter; adopted his foster father's first name in 1812 (making Commander David Dixon Porter his foster brother) [FARRAGUT-WIKI]
- Assigned February 1862 in secret orders to command the Gulf Blockading Squadron [FARRAGUT-WIKI]
- Sailed from Hampton Roads aboard USS Hartford [FARRAGUT-WIKI]
- Key decision: overruled Porter's preference for continued bombardment and chose to run the fleet past the forts [ACWS-SEARCH; all sources]
- Promotion: Congress created the rank of Rear Admiral on July 16, 1862; Farragut was among the first recipients [FARRAGUT-WIKI]
- Note: "Damn the torpedoes, full speed ahead!" is from the Battle of Mobile Bay, August 1864 — NOT from New Orleans [HISTORYNET-FARR; USNI-TORPEDOES] [VERIFY before any use in New Orleans narrative]

**Commander David Dixon Porter (Union)**
- Foster brother of Farragut; commanded the semi-independent mortar flotilla of 20 schooners [WP-BATTLE; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT]
- Favored the strategy of reducing the forts by bombardment before the fleet ran past; Farragut overruled him [ACWS-SEARCH]
- Each mortar schooner carried a 13-inch seacoast mortar [MARINERSM]

**Major General Benjamin F. Butler (Union)**
- Commanded the Army of the Gulf, 18,000 troops [WP-BATTLE]
- Occupied New Orleans May 1, 1862 [CAPTURE-WIKI]
- Issued General Order No. 28 (the "Woman Order"), May 15, 1862: any woman showing disrespect to Union soldiers would be treated as "a woman of the town plying her avocation" — provoked international outrage [CAPTURE-WIKI; 64PARISHES; ECCW]
- Ordered and carried out the execution of William Mumford, June 7, 1862 [WP-MUMFORD; BATTLEFIELDS-10FACTS]
- Earned the nickname "Beast Butler" from white New Orleans civilians [64PARISHES; ECCW]
- Replaced December 17, 1862, by Major General Nathaniel P. Banks [CAPTURE-WIKI]

**Brigadier General Johnson K. Duncan (Confederate)**
- Born: March 19, 1827, Chanceford Township, York County, Pennsylvania (a Northerner commanding Confederate defenses) [YORK-DUNCANBIOG]
- West Point class of 1849; ranked 5th of 43 cadets [YORK-DUNCANBIOG]
- Resigned U.S. Army January 31, 1855; entered private life in Louisiana [YORK-DUNCANBIOG]
- Pre-war: Superintendent of Construction and Repairs in New Orleans, overseeing the branch mint, marine hospital, and other federal properties [YORK-DUNCANBIOG]
- Promoted Brigadier General January 7, 1862; assigned to command all lower river defenses including both forts [YORK-DUNCANBIOG]
- Widely regarded as one of the finest artillerists in the region [YORK-DUNCANBIOG]
- Surrendered both forts April 28, 1862; taken prisoner of war; subsequently exchanged [YORK-DUNCANBIOG; NPS-LA001]
- Post-exchange: Chief of Staff to General Braxton Bragg [YORK-DUNCANBIOG]
- Death: contracted malaria; died December 18, 1862, in Knoxville, Tennessee, age 36 [YORK-DUNCANBIOG]

**Commander John K. Mitchell (Confederate)**
- Commanded Confederate naval forces (CSS Louisiana and coordination of naval vessels) [WP-BATTLE]
- Ordered CSS Louisiana burned rather than surrendered under the truce; the explosion killed a Confederate soldier in Fort St. Philip [WP-BATTLE]

**Major General Mansfield Lovell (Confederate)**
- Commanded Confederate Department No. 1 (New Orleans department) [CAPTURE-WIKI; LOVELL-WIKI]
- Neither he, local officials, nor Richmond's Navy Department expected a serious naval assault from the south — they anticipated attack from the north [ECCW; LOVELL-WIKI]
- Evacuated his ~3,000 troops to Camp Moore, 78 miles away, rather than attempt an indefensible street fight against a naval fleet [EMERGINGCW; ECCW; CAPTURE-WIKI]
- Faced fierce criticism for the loss; demanded a court of inquiry, which met April 1863 and declared him innocent of incompetence [LOVELL-WIKI]
- Received no further command assignments during the war [LOVELL-WIKI]
- Historian Charles L. Dufour later argued Jefferson Davis and Naval Secretary Mallory bore primary responsibility for the loss [LOVELL-WIKI]

## 7. WHAT IT MEANT

**Immediate strategic impact:**
- New Orleans lost after only 455 days of Confederate control; the first major Confederate city to fall [MARINERSM; ABT-10FACTS]
- No effective Confederate naval or land defense remained between the Gulf of Mexico and Memphis, Tennessee [WP-BATTLE]
- Farragut subsequently captured Baton Rouge (May 9, 1862) and Natchez (May 14, 1862); Vicksburg became the only remaining Confederate stronghold on the lower Mississippi until July 4, 1863 [ECCW; WP-BATTLE]
- The loss shut down the Confederacy's largest port and eliminated its most important manufacturing base outside of Richmond [EMERGINGCW; ECCW]
- The Leeds Foundry — the South's second-most important ironworks — was captured [EMERGINGCW]
- Confederate ironclad-building program dealt a crippling blow: CSS Mississippi destroyed, CSS Louisiana blown up [ECCW; EMERGINGCW]
- The Anaconda Plan tightened: the Union gained control of the river's mouth, critical to strangling Confederate commerce and cotton exports [BATTLEFIELDS-ABT; ECCW]

**Diplomatic impact:**
- European powers (Britain, France) received news of the loss with decreased willingness to recognize the Confederacy [WP-BATTLE, citing historian Allan Nevins]
- Historian Allan Nevins noted the loss demonstrated Confederate "poverty, disorganization, lack of skilled engineers and craftsmen, friction between State authorities and Richmond, and want of foresight." [WP-BATTLE, citing Nevins]

**Butler's occupation:**
- Butler imposed martial law, loyalty oaths, and confiscated weapons [64PARISHES; ECCW]
- Controversial measures: General Order No. 28 (Woman Order, May 15); execution of Mumford (June 7) [see Section 5]
- Butler raised three Black regiments (3,122 soldiers total) during occupation [CAPTURE-WIKI]
- His administration also provided food relief: Order No. 25 created 2,000 relief jobs; Order No. 55 placed 11,000 families on relief [64PARISHES]
- Sewer improvements under Butler significantly reduced annual yellow fever deaths [ABT-10FACTS]
- Value of goods through port: pre-war ~$500 million (1860); dropped to ~$52 million (1862) [CAPTURE-WIKI]

**Assessment of the battle's significance:**
- Described as "a serious, even fatal, blow from which the Confederacy never recovered" [WP-BATTLE]
- Union Assistant Secretary of the Navy Gustavus Fox called it the "great event second only to the final surrender of Lee." [EMERGINGCW]
- Confederate diarist Mary Chesnut wrote: "New Orleans gone and with it the Confederacy." [EMERGINGCW] [VERIFY — spelling variant: Chesnut/Chestnut; confirm attribution]
- Richmond Examiner: the loss "annihilated us in Louisiana" and cost the Confederacy "the commercial capital of the South." [EMERGINGCW] [VERIFY — confirm original publication date and exact text]

Monroe of New Orleans** (to Farragut, on surrendering the city): reportedly stated the city was undefended and effectively invited Union entry — specific phrasing: "Come and take the city; we are powerless." — [ECCW] [VERIFY: Monroe did refuse formal surrender but this quotation needs original source confirmation]

## 9. GEOGRAPHY FOR A MAP

**Orientation:**
- North is UP; the Mississippi River flows roughly south-to-north in this stretch, from the Gulf toward New Orleans. Farragut's fleet came FROM THE SOUTH (from the Gulf side), running NORTHWARD past the forts toward the city. [WP-BATTLE; all sources]

**Key locations (north to south):**

1. **New Orleans** — upstream/north; ~75 miles above Forts Jackson and St. Philip [MARINERSM; HISTORYNET-FARR; various]
2. **Chalmette** — Confederate shore batteries between the forts and New Orleans; the fleet passed these as well [FARRAGUT-WIKI]
3. **Fort Jackson** — on the WEST (right descending/right ascending) bank of the Mississippi; a pentagonal bastioned brick fort ~100 yards from the levee [WP-BATTLE; CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH]; the larger and more formidable of the two forts [CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH]
4. **Fort St. Philip** — on the EAST (left descending/left ascending) bank, approximately half a mile UPSTREAM (north) of Fort Jackson [WP-BATTLE; CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH]; older brick and stone earthwork
5. **The chain/hulk barrier** — stretched across the river between the two forts; 8 derelict hulks threaded with chain cable [HISTORYNET-FARR; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT]; the gap breached by Pinola and Itasca was on the east side near Fort St. Philip [HISTORYNET-FARR]
6. **Head of Passes** — ~25 miles south of the forts (where the river splits into multiple passes toward the Gulf) [WP-BATTLE]
7. **Gulf of Mexico** — farther south; Farragut's fleet assembled near Ship Island, then moved up to the forts [ECCW; CAPTURE-WIKI]

**River bend context:**
- The forts sit at a bend in the river called Plaquemines Bend (or near it) [WP-BATTLE]; the river is not straight here — ships rounding the bend came under fire from both forts near-simultaneously as they passed through the gap [WP-BATTLE; CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH]

**Map note:** The river here runs roughly south-to-north with a curve. Fort Jackson (west bank) and Fort St. Philip (east bank, slightly upstream) form a natural chokepoint. The chain barrier connected them across the river. North of the forts, the river runs approximately 75 miles to New Orleans with the Chalmette batteries as the next defensive position.

## 10. CONTESTED / UNCERTAIN FACTS

The following items have conflicting data across sources, or are flagged as requiring original source verification:

**A. Casualty figures — high uncertainty**
- The NPS/CWSAC total of 229 Union / 782 Confederate (1,011 total) [NPS-LA001; WP-BATTLE] appears to encompass all casualties for the full April 18–28 engagement, including surrendered garrison personnel counted in Confederate totals. The narrower passage-only figures (37–39 Union killed, 135–171 Union wounded; ~140 CS flotilla casualties) appear in ECCW, HISTORYCENTRAL, and 64PARISHES. These are not contradictory but measure different things. A writer should be specific about which phase they are describing.
- Confederate fort casualty figures during the mortar bombardment alone range from 2 KIA [WP-BATTLE] to 4 KIA / 14 WIA [64PARISHES] to 11 KIA / 39 WIA [ECCW]. These may reflect different time periods of counting.
- **Recommendation:** Use "approximately 229 Union and 782 Confederate total for the full battle [NPS]" for the stat block, and note the narrower passage-night figures separately.

**B. Fleet count — minor uncertainty**
- "17 warships + 20 mortar schooners" is the most commonly cited figure [HISTORYNET-FARR; MARINERSM; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT]
- WP-BATTLE describes 6 ships + 12 gunboats initially = 18 vessels, plus 26 mortar schooners
- HISTORYCENTRAL gives "47 vessels total, mounting 310 guns" with 17 warships + mortar flotilla
- The ECCW says "17 ships divided into three divisions" for the assault fleet; HISTORYNET-FARR says "24 gunboats and 19 mortar boats"
- **Likely resolution:** The fleet's composition evolved; the 17-warship figure for the passing fleet is most consistent. "20 mortar schooners" is the most common figure. "47 total vessels" appears to include all types.

**C. Total fort gun count — minor uncertainty**
- Fort Jackson: 74 guns [CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH; HISTORYCENTRAL]; or "~70 of assorted types" [CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH narrative]; some sources count only guns that were operable
- Fort St. Philip: 52 guns [CW-JOURNEYS-SEARCH; MARINERSM]
- Combined totals given as 126 [HISTORYNET-FARR] or 177 [WP-BATTLE] — the 177 figure may include all emplacements including water batteries and exterior positions; 126 may reflect the main fortress guns only
- **Recommendation:** Use "totaling approximately 126–177 guns [sources vary]" or pick 126 and note it

**D. Date the chain barrier was breached — moderate uncertainty**
- MARINERSM gives "April 20" for Pinola and Itasca breaching the chain
- BATTLEFIELDS-ABT gives "April 23"
- The USS Pinola Wikipedia article does not specify a date within April 1862
- **Likely resolution:** An initial breach was made April 20–21; the gap was widened or confirmed viable by April 23 before the main fleet passage. Use "the night of April 20–21" and note the April 23 operation as the final confirmation, or flag as [VERIFY against OR records]

**E. Start date of mortar bombardment — minor uncertainty**
- Most sources: April 18 [WP-BATTLE; BATTLEFIELDS-ABT; 64PARISHES; HISTORYNET-FARR]
- ECCW gives April 15 for start of bombardment; April 16 in BATTLEFIELDS-ABT for initial positioning
- **Recommendation:** Use April 18 as the bombardment start (majority of sources; consistent with NPS); April 16 may be fleet positioning/initial approach

**F. Exact time of fleet passage — minor uncertainty**
- ECCW: 3:30 a.m. [ECCW]
- WP-BATTLE: ~3:00 a.m. [WP-BATTLE]
- BATTLEFIELDS-ABT and CAPTURE-WIKI: 2:00 a.m. [BATTLEFIELDS-ABT]
- **Recommendation:** "In the pre-dawn hours of April 24 (approximately 2:00–3:30 a.m.)" covers the range

**G. Surrender date of forts — minor uncertainty**
- NPS-LA001: April 28, 1862 [NPS-LA001]
- WP-BATTLE infobox: April 30 [WP-BATTLE]
- Body of WP-BATTLE: "April 29: mutiny; April 30: Duncan capitulated both forts" [WP-BATTLE body text]
- 64PARISHES, ECCW, and YORK-DUNCANBIOG all give April 28
- **Recommendation:** April 28 (NPS + multiple secondary sources); the April 30 figure in WP-BATTLE body text may reflect a separate formal capitulation document date

**H. Number of shells in mortar bombardment**
- ~7,500 over 5–6 days [WP-BATTLE; MARINERSM]
- 13,000 over 96 hours [64PARISHES]
- **Recommendation:** Use "approximately 7,500–13,000 shells" or the 7,500 figure with NPS/Wikipedia citation; flag the discrepancy

**I. Mumford details — mostly verified but one disputed element**
- The flag incident date (April 26) is consistent across sources [WP-MUMFORD; CAPTURE-WIKI; 64PARISHES]
- Location (the New Orleans Mint) is consistent [WP-MUMFORD; ABT-10FACTS; EXECUTEDTODAY]
- Execution date (June 7, 1862) is consistent [WP-MUMFORD; EXECUTEDTODAY; HOUSEDIVIDED]
- Execution location (hanged from the Mint) is consistent [WP-MUMFORD; ABT-10FACTS]
- The trial conviction included the question of whether New Orleans was legally occupied at the time of the April 26 incident — this legal ambiguity is noted in WP-MUMFORD
- Butler's reported identification of Mumford wearing a piece of the flag is noted as possibly disputed [WP-MUMFORD]
- **Recommendation:** The core facts (what, who, where, when) are solid. Flag the legal ambiguity in the trial as a detail worth noting.

**J. Mary Chesnut quote provenance**
- "New Orleans gone and with it the Confederacy" is widely attributed to Chesnut (note: correct spelling is "Chesnut" — she signed her diary this way)
- EMERGINGCW cites this without a volume/page reference
- **Recommendation:** [VERIFY] — confirm against C. Vann Woodward's edited edition of the Chesnut diary before using as a direct quote

**K. CSS Manassas as "first ironclad"**
- The CSS Manassas search result states she "predates the Virginia as the first ironclad vessel to serve in the American Civil War" [MANASSAS-SEARCH]
- This claim is contested — the CSS Manassas was converted and first saw action at the Head of Passes (October 1861), which is earlier than the Virginia/Monitor battle (March 1862), but whether she truly counts as "the first" is disputed
- **Recommendation:** Do not assert this claim without additional verification; simply call her "an early Confederate ironclad ram"
