## 1. THE STRUCTURAL FACT — Native nations were the decisive military factor; the war's own name admits it

- **The name reflects the enemy.** British colonists named the conflict the "French and Indian War" after the opponents they faced — the French Empire AND its Indigenous allied nations. (King George's War had already used the monarch's name, so they named this one for the enemies.) The name itself encodes that Native nations were central to the war, not a sideshow.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *French and Indian War*; history.state.gov ("the importance of English and French alliances with Indian tribes").
- **Neither empire could hold the interior without Native allies.** Large areas of the North American interior — the Great Lakes country and the Ohio Country — had essentially no colonial settlements; control there ran through Native nations. Neither European power could campaign in the interior without recruiting Native warriors and maintaining the alliances that supplied them.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *French and Indian War* ("Large areas had no colonial settlements"; French recruited fighters "from tribes in western portions of the Great Lakes region").
- **The numbers prove the point at the decisive early battles** (detailed in §6–7): at the Monongahela (1755) Native warriors were ~70%+ of the attacking force; at Fort William Henry (1757) Montcalm's army included nearly 2,000 Native warriors. The French won the interior's opening battles BECAUSE of Native allies, and lost the Ohio when those allies walked away (Treaty of Easton, §8).

## 2. FRANCE'S ALLIANCE SYSTEM — who, and WHY it was durable

### The nations allied with France
- **The pays d'en haut ("upper country") — the Great Lakes nations:** Ottawa (Odawa), Ojibwe (Chippewa), Potawatomi, Huron-Wyandot (Wyandot), plus Mississauga, Winnebago, and others.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *French and Indian War* (lists "Wyandots, Mississaugas, Ojibwas, Winnebagos, and Potawatomi"); Wikipedia, *Battle of the Monongahela* (Ottawas, Ojibwa/Chippewa, Potawatomis, Hurons).
- **The mission / "domiciled" nations of the St. Lawrence — the Seven Nations of Canada.** A confederation of seven Catholic mission villages along the St. Lawrence allied to New France. The seven member communities:
  1. **Oswegatchie** (Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga)
  2. **Akwesasne** (Mohawk)
  3. **Kahnawake** (Mohawk) — "near the rapids," opposite Montreal; the most reliable French allies
  4. **Kanesatake / Lake of Two Mountains** (Mohawk + Algonquin and Nipissing)
  5. **Odanak** (Abenaki)
  6. **Bécancour / Wôlinak** (Abenaki)
  7. **Jeune-Lorette / Wendake** (Huron)
  - These were "domiciled" Indians: Indigenous people who had relocated to mission villages, converted (often while keeping traditional practice), and become New France's most dependable allies.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Seven Nations of Canada*.
  - **Kahnawake Mohawk origin:** After the French attacked Mohawk villages in New York (1660s–1667), some Mohawk converted to Catholicism and relocated to Kahnawake on the St. Lawrence; their descendants ("Caughnawaga"/Kahnawake Mohawk) became France's most reliable Indigenous allies. NOTE the irony for the author: Mohawk fought on BOTH sides — Kahnawake Mohawk for France, Johnson's Canajoharie/valley Mohawk for Britain (see §5, §6).
  - Source: Britannica, *Canada — The French and Indian/Seven Years' War*; Wikipedia, *Seven Nations of Canada*.
- **The Abenaki / Wabanaki** (St. Lawrence mission villages Odanak and Wôlinak; also Maritime/maritime allies the Mi'kmaq). Long-standing French allies on the New England frontier.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *French and Indian War*; *Siege of Fort William Henry* (Abenaki present).

### WHY the French model built durable alliances
- **Fur trade + intermarriage + cultural integration.** French traders (*voyageurs*, *coureurs de bois*) lived among Native nations far from forts, married Native women, adopted Native ways, and built networks that were political as well as commercial. Relations in the pays d'en haut were comparatively harmonious because France depended on Native trade and dealt through kinship.
  - Source: Britannica, *Canada — French and Indian/Seven Years' War*; Wikipedia, *Franco-Indian alliance*.
- **Gift-giving as the core of diplomacy.** Annual gifts (powder, shot, cloth, goods) were not bribes; they were the obligatory substance of the alliance. In Native diplomatic logic, accepting gifts created reciprocal obligation; withholding them broke the relationship. This is the mechanism the author should explain — and it is exactly what Amherst later destroyed (§10).
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War* (gift-giving "an integral part of the relationship between the French and the tribes," seen as "a necessary part of diplomacy"); Richard White, *The Middle Ground*.
- **Catholic missions.** Jesuit missionaries converted many St. Lawrence nations to Catholicism while preserving Indigenous languages and clan systems rather than forcing assimilation — binding the mission nations to New France religiously as well as economically.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Seven Nations of Canada*.
- **The "Onontio" / middle-ground frame (analytical, Richard White).** The French governor played "Onontio," a father figure who was patriarchal but REciprocal — he distributed gifts and mediated inter-tribal disputes to keep the peace that trade required. Both sides negotiated and accommodated on a "middle ground." This is the model that explains French durability; its collapse explains Pontiac's War.
  - Source: Richard White, *The Middle Ground: Indians, Empires, and Republics in the Great Lakes Region, 1650–1815* (1991), via Videri/DailyHistory syntheses.

## 3. BRITAIN'S SYSTEM — the Covenant Chain, William Johnson, and the alienated Ohio nations

### The Covenant Chain
- **What it was:** the alliance system between the British colonies (above all New York) and the Iroquois Confederacy / Six Nations (Haudenosaunee). Grew out of 17th-century Dutch-Iroquois trade ties; formalized after England took New Netherland (1664); centered diplomatically on Albany.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Covenant Chain*.
- **How it worked:** metaphor of a silver chain linking the English ship to the Haudenosaunee Tree of Peace; the chain had to be periodically "polished"/renewed at councils. The Iroquois insisted on a brother-to-brother relationship, NOT subordination.
  - **QUOTE (Iroquois leaders, 1692):** "We will not be like Father and Son, but like Brothers." — source: Wikipedia, *Covenant Chain*.
- **It was fraying by the 1750s** under colonial land seizures. (See Hendrick "broken" declaration, §4.)
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Covenant Chain*; *French and Indian War*.

### Sir William Johnson — Britain's broker
- **Who:** Sir William Johnson, 1st Baronet (c. 1715 – 11 July 1774), Irish-born; settled in the Mohawk Valley (New York) managing his uncle's estate; learned the Mohawk language and Iroquois custom.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Sir William Johnson, 1st Baronet*.
- **Adopted by the Mohawk (~1742)** as an honorary sachem, given the name **Warraghiyagey** ("a man who undertakes great things").
- **Appointed Superintendent of Indian Affairs for the Northern District (1756)** — a Crown post reporting directly to London, bypassing colonial governments. His charge: win the wary Iroquois to Britain by reviving the Covenant Chain.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Sir William Johnson*; The Canadian Encyclopedia.
- **Mohawk family ties.** After his first wife's death, Johnson's household partner was **Molly Brant (Konwatsi'tsiaienni, c. 1736–1796)**, a Mohawk clan mother whose standing among the Six Nations made her a power broker; her diplomacy and intelligence-gathering were central to Johnson's influence.
  - Source: Wikipedia / Encyclopedia.com, *Molly Brant*.
  - **CAUTION for author:** sources loosely call Molly Johnson's "wife"; they were partners in a long consensual union and she ran his household, but the marriage was not a formal colonial-church marriage. Safer phrasing: "his Mohawk partner / consort," or "the woman who ran his household and was his closest link to the Six Nations." Do not assert a formal wedding.
- **Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea, "he places two bets together"), Molly's younger brother**, born March 1743 in the Ohio Country (Cuyahoga River). **At about age 13 he accompanied Johnson to the Battle of Lake George in 1755** — his first action. (His fame as a war leader and diplomat comes LATER, in the Revolution; in THIS war he is a teenager just entering the picture. Do not overstate his 1754–63 role.)
  - Source: Wikipedia / NPS, *Joseph Brant*; Mount Vernon.

### The alienated Ohio nations
- **The problem:** the very nations Britain most needed in the Ohio — the Ohio Delaware (Lenape) and Shawnee — had the strongest reasons to distrust it, because British/Virginian land hunger threatened them directly.
- **The land grab:** the **Ohio Company of Virginia** received a royal grant of **200,000 acres** (granted/promised **July 12, 1749**), with a further 300,000 acres to follow if it settled 100 families in seven years — a corporate claim on the upper Ohio Valley. This, plus colonial trade pushing into the Ohio, is what brought French troops (Céloron's 1749 expedition) and ultimately war.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Ohio Company*; America's Best History.
- **Tanaghrisson ("the Half King"),** a Mingo (Ohio Iroquois) headman empowered by the Six Nations to speak for the Ohio nations, is a key early figure: he leaned British against French expansion that threatened his authority, and at Logstown (1752) agreed to British settlement — over the heads of the Delaware, whose own leaders (e.g., Shingas) he claimed had no independent authority. This top-down Iroquois claim over the Ohio Delaware is part of WHY the Ohio Delaware later made their OWN peace at Easton (§8).
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Ohio Company*; *French and Indian War* (Tanaghrisson); Scioto Historical.

## 4. THEYANOGUIN / HENDRICK — the Mohawk at Albany (1754) and his death at Lake George (1755)

### IDENTITY — CRITICAL, do not conflate (this error was caught once already)
- **This is Theyanoguin, a.k.a. Hendrick Peters / "King Hendrick" (c. 1691/1692 – 8 September 1755), BEAR CLAN, based at Canajoharie (the Upper Castle).**
- He is **NOT** the earlier **Hendrick Tejonihokarawa (c. 1660 – c. 1735), WOLF CLAN, of Tiononderoge (Lower Castle)** — one of the **"Four Mohawk Kings"** who visited Queen Anne in **London in 1710**. That was a different, older man.
- Until the late 20th century historians conflated the two into one figure; they are now firmly distinguished (Eric Hinderaker, *The Two Hendricks: Unraveling a Mohawk Mystery*, 2010).
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Hendrick Theyanoguin* (explicit on the conflation); New York Almanack, "The Two Hendricks."
- He was a Christian convert, a leading Mohawk orator and diplomat for the Iroquois in dealings with the British.
  - Source: American History Central, *King Hendrick (Theyanoguin)*.

### The Covenant Chain "broken" (1753)
- In **1753**, frustrated by colonial land fraud and British neglect, Theyanoguin declared before New York's governor that the **Covenant Chain was broken.**
  - Land grievance cited: roughly **800,000 acres** of Mohawk land taken through fraudulent dealing.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Covenant Chain* / *French and Indian War* (Hendrick declares the chain broken; ~800,000 acres). [CONTESTED note: the "800,000 acres" figure travels via Howard Zinn's framing ("intricate swindling"); treat as the order-of-magnitude grievance Hendrick voiced, not an audited survey.]

### The Albany Congress (June 1754) — his great speech
- At the **Albany Congress of 1754**, Theyanoguin was the lead speaker for the Mohawk/Iroquois delegation and delivered a stinging rebuke of British inaction while France fortified the frontier. He spoke on/around **19 June 1754**.
  - Source: History Matters (GMU); New York Almanack, "Albany Congress of 1754"; American History Central.
- **QUOTE (Theyanoguin / Hendrick, Albany Congress, 1754):**
  > "Brethren, you were desirous we should open our minds and our hearts to you; look at the French, they are men; they are fortifying everywhere; — but, we are ashamed to say it, you are all like women, bare and open, without any fortifications."
  - Speaker: Theyanoguin (Hendrick), Mohawk. Source: History Matters (GMU), citing the colonial record; text also in Jeptha R. Simms, *History of Schoharie County and the Border Wars of New York* (1845), pp. 126–129. (Two slightly variant transcriptions exist — "you are like women" vs "you are all like women bare and open"; use the wording above.)

### Death at the Battle of Lake George — 8 September 1755
- Theyanoguin (then ~64) helped recruit Mohawk warriors for **William Johnson's 1755 expedition against Crown Point** and went with Johnson personally at the head of a Mohawk force.
  - Source: American History Central; New York Almanack.
- He was killed on **8 September 1755** in the **"Bloody Morning Scout"** — the opening ambush of the **Battle of Lake George.** Johnson had sent a detachment (Col. Ephraim Williams's ~1,000 provincials + ~200–250 Mohawk under Hendrick) south; **Baron Dieskau's French-and-Indian force ambushed it in a ravine.** Williams and Hendrick were both killed there.
  - **How he died:** his horse was shot from under him and he was bayonetted (he was elderly and could not get clear). [CONTESTED in detail: Wikipedia, *Hendrick Theyanoguin* says "bayonetted after his horse was shot dead"; some popular accounts say tomahawked/shot — use "killed in the ambush; his horse was shot and he was bayonetted," which is the Wikipedia statement.]
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Battle of Lake George* and *Hendrick Theyanoguin*; Schenectady History (Mohawk Valley Gateway).
- **QUOTE (Theyanoguin, on the eve of the battle, objecting to Johnson's plan to split the force):**
  > "If they are to fight, they are too few; if they are to die, they are too many."
  - Speaker: Hendrick (Theyanoguin). Context: he criticized dividing the detachment; he is also said to have made the point with a bundle of sticks (one stick breaks, a bundle does not). Source: crossroadsofacontinent / Schenectady History (Mohawk Valley Gateway, ch. 42); widely attributed. [CONTESTED: this is a well-attested traditional quote but its exact wording varies across sources ("if they are to be killed, they are too many; if they are to fight, they are too few"); flag as traditional/attributed, not a signed document. The companion "bundle of sticks" anecdote is also traditional.]

### Forces at Lake George (context for §1's "decisive" claim)
- French side (Dieskau): ~220 French regulars/grenadiers, ~600 Canadian militia, and ~600–700 Native warriors — including **Kahnawake (Caughnawaga) Mohawk, Abenaki, and Nipissing on the FRENCH side.** Mohawk fought Mohawk.
- British/allied side (Johnson): ~1,500 provincial militia + ~200–250 Iroquois (mostly Mohawk).
- Source: Wikipedia, *Battle of Lake George*. [CONTESTED: casualty totals vary widely across sources, ~154–262 British dead; give as a range or omit.]

## 5. THE IROQUOIS CONFEDERACY — balancing, partial neutrality, leaning British via the Mohawk

- The **Iroquois Confederacy / Six Nations (Haudenosaunee)** — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, Tuscarora — pursued their OWN strategic interest: **not to be conquered or dominated by either empire,** and to keep trade flowing from both. For most of the war the Confederacy as a whole held to a **strategic neutrality / balancing posture,** preserving leverage by not committing fully.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *French and Indian War* ("The Iroquois initially maintained strategic neutrality to preserve beneficial trade with both sides").
- **The Mohawk (especially Johnson's valley Mohawk) leaned British** through Johnson's Covenant-Chain diplomacy and family ties — but even they were divided (Kahnawake Mohawk were French allies), and Hendrick's "chain is broken" speeches show the alliance was conditional and aggrieved, not loyal.
- **Western Iroquois (Seneca) and Ohio Iroquois (Mingo)** had their own Ohio interests and at times sided differently; the Confederacy was never a single bloc.
- **Framing point:** the Iroquois were playing the empires against each other — exactly the balance-of-power leverage that France's defeat would destroy (§10).

## 6. HOW NATIVE ALLIES DELIVERED FRANCE'S EARLY VICTORIES — the Monongahela (1755)

- **Date:** **9 July 1755,** at Braddock's Field, ~10 miles east of present-day Pittsburgh, as Gen. **Edward Braddock's** British column advanced on **Fort Duquesne.**
- **Force composition (the decisive point):** the French/Indian party totaled roughly **850–900,** of which the great majority were **Native warriors — ~637 by Wikipedia's count** — alongside ~108 French troupes de la Marine and ~146 Canadian militia. Native nations present: **Ottawa, Ojibwe (Chippewa), Potawatomi, Huron, Lenni Lenape (Delaware), Shawnee, Mingo, Abenaki** — i.e., the pays d'en haut Great Lakes nations plus Ohio nations.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Battle of the Monongahela*. [CONTESTED: a second figure set (891 total / ~900 Native) circulates; Wikipedia's breakdown (108 + 146 + 637 ≈ 891) is the cleanest — use "around 250 French and Canadians and roughly 640 Native warriors."]
- **The French field commander, Capt. Daniel Liénard de Beaujeu, was killed early** — after which Native warriors effectively carried the fight, fighting from forest cover against Braddock's tight European formations in the open.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Battle of the Monongahela*; Britannica.
- **Result — a rout:** of Braddock's ~1,300–1,460 engaged, roughly **900+ were killed or wounded** (Wikipedia: ~457 killed, ~450+ wounded). French/Indian losses were trivial by comparison (~39 killed, ~57 wounded). **Braddock was mortally wounded and died 13 July 1755.** A young George Washington helped organize the retreat.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Battle of the Monongahela*; American Battlefield Trust.
- **Author's takeaway:** France did not "beat Braddock"; France's Native allies did. This is the cleanest illustration of §1's structural claim.

## 7. FORT WILLIAM HENRY (1757) — the victory, then the rupture

### The siege
- **Siege dates: 3–9 August 1757.** **Louis-Joseph de Montcalm** besieged the British-held **Fort William Henry** (south end of Lake George), garrisoned by **Lt. Col. George Monro** (~2,300–2,500, many sick with smallpox).
- **Montcalm's army (~8,000):** ~3,000 French regulars, ~3,000 Canadian militia, and **nearly 2,000 Native warriors** — drawn from BOTH France's alliance circles: **Great Lakes / pays d'en haut nations (Odawa/Ottawa, Potawatomi, Ojibwe, Nipissing, and more)** AND **St. Lawrence domiciled/mission nations (Abenaki and others).**
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Siege of Fort William Henry*.
- Monro surrendered **8 August 1757.** Terms (a "parole of honour"): the garrison would march out under French escort to Fort Edward with the honours of war, keeping muskets (but no ammunition) and one symbolic cannon, on condition of not fighting for 18 months; French to release prisoners within three months.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Siege of Fort William Henry*.

### Why the Native allies felt cheated — and the attack on the surrendering column
- **The grievance:** Montcalm and his officers had repeatedly promised the warriors the trophies of war — **plunder, scalps, and captives.** The surrender terms STRIPPED those: war provisions went to the French army, British personal effects stayed with the British, leaving the warriors — especially the Great Lakes nations who had travelled vast distances expecting return — with **nothing.** To them it looked like the French had conspired with the enemy against their own allies.
  - **QUOTE (historian Ian K. Steele, paraphrase-sourced):** the terms created "resentment, as it appeared that the French were conspiring with their enemies, the British, against their friends, the Indians."
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Siege of Fort William Henry*, citing Ian K. Steele, *Betrayals: Fort William Henry and the "Massacre."*
- **The attack:** on the morning of **9 August 1757,** warriors (Abenaki and others entered first) fell on the sick and wounded left in the camp, then on the marching column — killing, scalping, stripping, and seizing women, children, servants, and the enslaved as captives.
  - **QUOTE (Col. Joseph Frye, Massachusetts, eyewitness):** "...the savages still carrying away officers, privates, women and children, some of which later they kill'd & scalpt in the road."
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Siege of Fort William Henry*, quoting Frye's account.
- **Scale — [CONTESTED], and the author MUST use the modern range, not the legend:**
  - Early/inflated accounts (and Fenimore Cooper's *Last of the Mohicans*) claimed up to ~1,500 dead.
  - **Modern scholarship (Ian K. Steele) estimates ~69 to ~184 British killed or missing** — under 8% of the ~2,300+ who surrendered. Forensic work (Maria Liston & Brenda Baker, 1990s) found disease (smallpox, etc.) killed more of the dead than combat did.
  - Use: "the modern estimate is well under 200 killed — not the thousand-plus of legend." Source: Wikipedia, *Siege of Fort William Henry*; Penn Museum, *Expedition* ("The 'Massacre' at Fort William Henry").

### The smallpox the warriors carried home
- The garrison was riddled with **smallpox.** Great Lakes warriors, taking captives and plunder (including, in some accounts, infected goods) **back to distant villages, carried smallpox home, sparking epidemics that devastated their own communities.** This is both a human-cost fact and a reason the western nations soured on the alliance.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Siege of Fort William Henry*; Penn Museum *Expedition*.

### The lasting damage to FRANCE's alliances (the theme's hinge)
- The Fort William Henry aftermath **poisoned France's own alliance system:** the western/Great Lakes nations felt betrayed (denied their due, then sickened), and many were less willing to campaign for France afterward. France won the battle and lost trust.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Siege of Fort William Henry* (the episode "profoundly damaged French-Indian alliances").
- **Montcalm's culpability is [CONTESTED]:**
  - Francis Parkman: French officers "did what they could" but were powerless.
  - Francis Jennings: Montcalm "anticipated what was going to happen, deliberately ignored it... and stepped in only after the atrocities were well under way."
  - **Louis Antoine de Bougainville** (Montcalm's aide) left on 9 August before the worst and wrote an account some scholars read as protective of Montcalm. (NOTE: I did NOT find a clean, verifiable Bougainville verbatim quote ON the massacre to anchor; do not invent one — see GAPS.)
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Siege of Fort William Henry*.

## 8. THE TREATY OF EASTON (1758) — the Ohio nations make a separate peace, dooming Fort Duquesne

- **Date & place: 26 October 1758, Easton, Pennsylvania** (a large outdoor council, **500+ Native people attending**).
- **Parties:** British colonial governors of Pennsylvania and New Jersey with representatives of **~13 Native nations,** including the **Ohio Delaware/Lenape (Eastern and Western), Shawnee, the Iroquois (three nations), and others.**
  - Key figures: **Teedyuscung** (Delaware leader), Conrad Weiser (interpreter), Gov. William Denny (PA), Charles Thomson (secretary/advisor).
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Treaty of Easton*.
- **Terms:**
  - Britain pledged to recognize Iroquois and other nations' rights to their Ohio Valley hunting grounds and **to refrain from settling west of the Allegheny/Appalachian Mountains after the war.**
  - Pennsylvania returned previously ceded lands (addressing the 1737 **Walking Purchase** fraud); the Lenape received **1,000 Spanish dollars** for remaining New Jersey land claims.
  - Gov. Denny agreed to deal **directly** with the Lenape, without Iroquois intermediation — restoring Delaware standing.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Treaty of Easton*.
- **The military payoff — Fort Duquesne falls:** with the Ohio Delaware and Shawnee withdrawing from the French and pledging neutrality, the French at **Fort Duquesne lost their Native shield.** Days later, as **Brig. Gen. John Forbes's** expedition closed in (late **November 1758**), the French — now badly outnumbered AND abandoned by their Native allies — **blew up and abandoned Fort Duquesne;** the British occupied the site and built **Fort Pitt** (later Pittsburgh).
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Treaty of Easton* and *Forbes Expedition*; the "without the support of the Ohio Indians, the French abandoned Fort Duquesne to the British — 'a pivotal moment in American history'" framing (TeachingHistory.org / standard scholarship).
- **Significance:** Easton is **the diplomatic turning point of the war in the Ohio.** The Ohio nations acted for THEIR OWN interest — land security and getting the Iroquois off their backs — and in doing so changed the war. They were not handed peace; they chose it as the better deal. It also fed directly into the 1763 **Royal Proclamation** line.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Treaty of Easton*.

## 9. THE FRAMING & HUMAN COST — sovereignty, not pawns

- **Their own aims, not anyone's instruments.** Across every episode, Native nations fought for **their own sovereignty, land, trade access, and balance-of-power leverage.** The Ohio Delaware/Shawnee distrusted Britain over land (§3) and made peace when it served them (§8); the Great Lakes nations turned on France when cheated (§7); the Iroquois balanced to avoid conquest by either side (§5). Tanaghrisson, Teedyuscung, Theyanoguin, Pontiac — each pursued a Native strategy, not a French or British one.
  - Source: synthesized across the cited articles; explicit in Wikipedia, *French and Indian War* (Native agency: Céloron's report that Ohio nations "are entirely devoted to the English" despite French threats; Tanaghrisson's own strategic choices).
- **The alliances were diplomacy among powers, not loyalty of subjects.** Gift-giving, the Covenant Chain, "brother not son," Onontio-the-father-not-master — every protocol assumed Native nations were independent powers to be courted. The author should never write "Britain's Indians" / "France's Indians" as if they were owned.
- **The human cost:**
  - **Smallpox** ripped through both garrisons and Native communities — and Fort William Henry literally exported the disease into the Great Lakes villages (§7).
  - War, captivity, and dispossession devastated communities; the land cessions and frauds (Walking Purchase 1737; the ~800,000 acres of Mohawk land; the Ohio Company's 200,000-acre grant) were the slow-motion catastrophe the war sat on top of.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Siege of Fort William Henry*, *Ohio Company*, *Covenant Chain*.

## 10. THE POST-CONQUEST COLLAPSE — Amherst kills gift-giving; Pontiac's War (1763)

- **France's defeat removed the counterweight.** Once New France fell (Quebec 1759, Montreal 1760; formalized by the **1763 Treaty of Paris**), Native nations could no longer **play the two empires against each other.** The leverage that the balance of power had given them was gone.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*; Richard White, *The Middle Ground* ("Indian republics no longer answered to Onontio... the French were replaced with English 'fathers' who... saw less need to mediate").
- **Jeffery Amherst (British C-in-C, North America) ended the alliance system on purpose.** Believing Native nations were now **conquered subjects, not allies,** he:
  - **Ended the customary gift-giving (from ~February 1761)** — to him a waste/bribe; to Native nations the rupture of the relationship itself.
  - **Restricted gunpowder and ammunition** sales to Native traders — crippling for hunting and the fur economy.
  - Made little effort to hide his contempt; Native participants complained the British "treated them no better than slaves or dogs."
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.
- **That contempt lit Pontiac's War.** In **May 1763,** a broad coalition of nations — under the **Odawa (Ottawa) leader Pontiac (Obwandiyag)** and others — rose against British posts across the Great Lakes and Ohio. The trigger was Amherst's policy and the news that France had ceded the region to Britain.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Pontiac's War*.
- **The smallpox-blanket episode (Fort Pitt, 1763):** during Pontiac's War, with Fort Pitt under siege, British officers (Capt. Simeon Ecuyer; traders' account, William Trent's journal) gave besieging Delaware **two blankets and a handkerchief from the fort's smallpox hospital** (24 June 1763). Amherst, separately and slightly later, wrote to **Col. Henry Bouquet** endorsing the idea.
  - **QUOTE (Jeffery Amherst to Henry Bouquet, July 1763):** "Could it not be contrived to send the Small Pox among those disaffected tribes of Indians? We must on this occasion use every stratagem in our power to reduce them."
  - **QUOTE (Amherst to Bouquet, follow-up):** "You will do well to try to inoculate the Indians by means of blankets, as well as to try every other method that can serve to extirpate this execrable race."
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Jeffery Amherst* / *Pontiac's War*; UMass (Derrico) documentary transcription of the Amherst–Bouquet correspondence and Trent's journal.
  - [CONTESTED: historians debate whether the Fort Pitt blankets actually caused an outbreak; the INTENT (Amherst's letters) is documented and not in dispute — frame it as intent + a documented attempt, effectiveness uncertain.]
- **Author's takeaway:** Pontiac's War is the proof of the theme. The thing that had made France's alliances work — reciprocity, gifts, treating nations as powers — was exactly what Amherst threw away, and the explosion followed within months. The "contest for Native alliances" didn't end in 1760; the British WON the war and then lost the peace by forgetting why the alliances had mattered.

## SUPPLEMENT — Jumonville Glen/Tanaghrisson, the 1755-58 Ohio frontier war, and Easton/Walking Purchase specifics (added in reconcile)

**Why this supplement exists:** a comprehensiveness critic flagged two ESSENTIAL missing beats — (S1) Jumonville Glen as the clearest case of NATIVE agency steering both empires to war, and (S2) the Ohio Delaware/Shawnee pro-French frontier war of 1755–58 (the "missing middle act" that makes the §8 Easton turn legible) — plus three should-fix specifics (S3). All items below are web-verified to the sources named; [CONTESTED] items carry the range/hedge.

### S1. JUMONVILLE GLEN (28 May 1754) — a Native leader steering two empires to war for his own aim

- **The skirmish.** The Battle of Jumonville Glen was fought **28 May 1754**, in Fayette County, Pennsylvania (between present-day Hopwood and Farmington). Virginia Regiment Lt. Col. **George Washington**, with ~40 provincial troops, was **guided by Tanaghrisson ("the Half King") and his Mingo warriors** (Wikipedia gives ~12 Mingo warriors with him). At dawn they surrounded and ambushed a French detachment of ~35 men under Ensign **Joseph Coulon de Villiers de Jumonville.** **About 10 French were killed** (Jumonville among them) and ~21 captured. This is the opening skirmish of the French and Indian War.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Battle of Jumonville Glen*; George Washington's Mount Vernon, "Jumonville Glen Skirmish"; American Battlefield Trust.
  - **TAKEAWAY for author:** France did not stumble into this and Washington did not engineer it alone — a Native leader put him there. This is the clearest case of Native agency at the war's very OUTBREAK.

- **[CONTESTED] — how Jumonville died (HEDGE; do not state as settled fact).** The most-repeated account holds that after the firing stopped, Tanaghrisson stepped up to the wounded Jumonville, said in French **"Tu n'es pas encore mort, mon père"** ("Thou art not yet dead, my father"), and killed him with hatchet/tomahawk blows to the head. **Wikipedia itself flags this as disputed:** sources conflict on whether Jumonville was killed by musket fire or by the hatchet, and on whether Washington's men or Tanaghrisson's warriors were responsible; the *Tanacharison* article calls these "disputed and contradictory accounts" and alludes to "another (and more accurate) account" without specifying it.
  - **Author's hedge:** write it as the widely-reported-but-contested version — e.g. "by the most-repeated account, Tanaghrisson killed the wounded Jumonville himself, reportedly saying 'Thou art not yet dead, my father' — though exactly who killed him, and how, is disputed." Do NOT assert the hatchet killing flatly.
  - The **"father"** address is load-bearing for the theme: Tanaghrisson was casting Jumonville as the stand-in for ALL French power in the Ohio. (NPS frames the killing as symbolic: "he was using Ensign Jumonville as a symbol for all the French and he wanted the French to leave.")
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Battle of Jumonville Glen* and *Tanacharison*; NPS, "Tanaghrisson, the Half King."

- **Tanaghrisson's OWN AIM (the load-bearing point — verified).** Tanaghrisson had been empowered/sent by the Iroquois Council (c. 1750) to restore Six Nations control over the Ohio Valley, but **his authority over the local Ohio nations — especially the Delaware — was waning.** Historian **Fred Anderson** (*Crucible of War*) theorizes he had **lost influence over some local tribes (specifically the Delaware) and believed that conflict between Britain and France would restore his authority** by bringing those nations back under him as British-aligned allies. To that end he sided with the British and **took steps to provoke a Franco-British war** — the killing of the French at the Great Meadows being the provocation. **The strategy FAILED:** by 1754 the Ohio Shawnee had already declared "perpetual war" on the English and other Delaware/Iroquois bands were committed to the French; Tanaghrisson had misjudged the geopolitics. He fell ill after Jumonville Glen and **died of pneumonia on 4 October 1754.**
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Battle of Jumonville Glen* (cites Anderson on the motive); NPS, "Tanaghrisson, the Half King"; Dictionary of Canadian Biography, *Tanaghrisson*.
  - **[CONTESTED — Tanaghrisson's nation/identity, NOTE for author]:** the existing factpack §3 (and Mount Vernon / DCB tradition) call him a **Mingo (Ohio Iroquois)**; the NPS page calls him **"a Seneca."** Wikipedia says he was possibly born **Catawba** and emerged as a **Mingo leader.** Safest phrasing: "a Mingo (Ohio Iroquois) leader the British called the Half King." Don't over-specify his birth nation.

### S2. THE OHIO DELAWARE/SHAWNEE PRO-FRENCH FRONTIER WAR, 1755–1758 (the missing middle act)

- **After Braddock's defeat (Monongahela, 9 July 1755), the Ohio frontier exploded.** **Beginning about October 1755, Ohio Delaware (Lenape) and Shawnee war parties, allied with / cooperating with France, launched devastating raids across the Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia backcountry (1755–1758).** Notable early massacres of mostly-unarmed settlers: **Draper's Meadow, Penn's Creek, Great Cove, and Gnadenhütten (all 1755).** Hundreds-to-thousands of settlers were killed, captured, or driven east; Pennsylvania and Virginia put **scalp bounties** on the Delaware war leaders.
  - **Leaders: Shingas and Captain Jacobs (Delaware)** — both based at **Kittanning**, which served as a staging point for the raids and a holding center for captives.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Kittanning Expedition* (background).
  - **TAKEAWAY for author (the load-bearing framing):** this was **the Ohio nations fighting their OWN war** — against the colonists who had taken their land — not merely "doing France's fighting." It is the middle act that makes the 1758 Easton turn legible: they fought Britain until Britain gave them a land deal worth more than the French alliance (§8).

- **The Kittanning Expedition (8 September 1756) — the Pennsylvania counterstrike.** Lt. Col. **John Armstrong** led ~300 Pennsylvania provincials from Fort Shirley (set out 31 Aug; reached Kittanning area by 7 Sept). At dawn **8 September 1756** they attacked and **destroyed the Delaware town of Kittanning,** burning it; **Captain Jacobs was killed and scalped** (he refused to surrender, his house was fired, stored gunpowder exploded). Armstrong became "the Hero of Kittanning."
  - **Mixed/limited outcome (don't overstate it as decisive):** Armstrong lost ~17 killed and ~13 wounded (more casualties than he inflicted by some counts); he killed an estimated 30–40; **most villagers escaped, taking most captives with them,** and raids actually **intensified that autumn.** But the strike did **embolden a Pennsylvania peace faction** and fed toward the Easton conferences.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Kittanning Expedition*; American Battlefield Trust, "Kittanning Expedition"; SCWPA.

- **Teedyuscung and the Easton conferences (1756–1758) — the diplomatic path out.** **Teedyuscung** (Delaware, called "King of the Delawares") was the central Delaware spokesman. At an Easton conference **13 November 1756** he aired the land grievance directly: the Delaware had been "unjustly dispossessed and defrauded," and he **accused the Penn proprietors of forging the 1686 deed behind the Walking Purchase.** His complaints prompted Sir William Johnson to send George Croghan to the **1757** Easton conference (July 1757, where Teedyuscung asked that the disputes be referred to the King) and on to the decisive **1758** treaty (§8). So Easton was not one event but a **1756 → 1757 → 1758 sequence** driven by Teedyuscung's land claim.
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Teedyuscung*; Wikipedia, *Treaty of Easton*; Founders Online (Charles Thomson / Pennsylvania Assembly Easton-conference reports).
  - **NOTE (irony to flag, not assert as triumph):** despite all this, on **23 June 1762** Teedyuscung signed a statement **acknowledging the legality of the Walking Purchase** — the fraud was officially "declared authentic" at the councils. The redress at Easton was real (direct dealing restored, NJ claims paid) but the original swindle was never legally overturned. Source: Wikipedia, *Teedyuscung*; Walking Purchase collection (OAC).

### S3. SHOULD-FIX SPECIFICS (so the author can name them, not gesture at "an earlier fraud")

- **(a) The Walking Purchase — 1737 (verified year + mechanism).** In **1737**, the **Penn family / Pennsylvania proprietors** (orchestrated by **Thomas Penn**) defrauded the **Lenape (Delaware)** using a claimed **1686 deed** granting land "as far as a man could walk in a day and a half." The Lenape understood this as roughly **40 miles** through forest. Instead, Penn **had a straight path cleared in advance and hired three of the fastest runners** in the province (incentivized with cash + 500 acres). On **19 September 1737**, the one runner who finished, **Edward Marshall, covered ~70 miles**; a line drawn back to the Delaware River then claimed everything between. The swindle seized **~1,200,000 acres (~1,100 sq mi by another count), about the size of Rhode Island,** forcing the Lenape into the Shamokin/Wyoming valleys and ultimately west — the root of their distrust of Pennsylvania.
  - **Author can now write:** "the Walking Purchase of 1737 — a deed measured by a rigged day-and-a-half 'walk' that trained runners turned into a 70-mile sprint" — instead of "an earlier fraud."
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Walking Purchase*; Bucks County PA; OAC Walking Purchase collection.
  - **[CONTESTED — acreage]:** sources give ~1,200,932 acres (Wikipedia) vs "nearly 1,100 square miles" (~704,000 acres; Huntington/Founders framing). Use "more than a million acres — about the size of Rhode Island" or hedge "roughly 1,100 sq mi to 1.2 million acres."

- **(b) The 1,000 Spanish dollars at Easton 1758 — CONFIRMED.** Verified verbatim against Wikipedia, *Treaty of Easton*: **"By the treaty, the Lenape ceded all remaining claims to land within the Province of New Jersey for the sum of one thousand Spanish dollars. They received payment immediately."** So the figure checks out — but note the **scope: it was payment for the Lenape's remaining NEW JERSEY land claims specifically, not for the Walking Purchase / Pennsylvania lands.** The author should keep that precision (the existing §8 already states this correctly).
  - Source: Wikipedia, *Treaty of Easton*.

- **(c) Seneca-vs-Mohawk divergence — the Iroquois were never a single bloc (concrete verified line).** The clearest documented split for THIS war: **the western Seneca leaned French while Johnson's valley Mohawk leaned British.** Verbatim, West Virginia Encyclopedia (*Seneca*): **"As allies of the French, Senecas participated in the defeat of Braddock's army at the Battle of the Monongahela (1755) and in the raids on the Virginia frontier that followed."** This is corroborated at Fort Niagara: in the 1759 siege, the French commander **Pouchot's Seneca allies withdrew from the fort rather than fight the British-allied Iroquois** (Old Fort Niagara) — i.e., Seneca were inside the French fort. And in **1763 a Seneca war party ambushed a British supply train at the Battle of Devil's Hole** during Pontiac's War (Wikipedia, *Seneca people*). Set against the Mohawk leaning British (factpack §4–5, Johnson's Covenant-Chain diplomacy; Kahnawake Mohawk on the French side at Lake George), this is the concrete proof that the Six Nations were not one bloc.
  - Source: West Virginia Encyclopedia, *Seneca*; Old Fort Niagara; Wikipedia, *Seneca people*.
  - **[CONTESTED / nuance]:** general-survey sources (American Battlefield Trust; Wikipedia, *Seneca people*) describe the Iroquois Confederacy as broadly British-aligned/neutral and note the Seneca helped the British take Fort Niagara in 1760. Both are true: the Confederacy's official posture tilted British, but the WESTERN Seneca acted French on the ground early in the war. Frame as "the western Seneca leaned French even as the Confederacy as a whole tilted British" — not "the Seneca were France's allies" flatly.
