## ESSENTIAL omission — MUST-ADD (needs facts first)

### Pickawillany, Memeskia ("Old Briton"), and the Miami nation (1752)

This is the section's one real comprehensiveness failure, and it is a **fact-pack blind spot**, not just a drafting choice. Neither the draft nor the fact pack mentions the **Miami nation, Memeskia (La Demoiselle / "Old Briton"), or the destruction of Pickawillany on 21 June 1752.**

Why it is essential to THIS section specifically (not encyclopedia-padding):

- **It is the section's own thesis, dramatized in blood.** The draft argues the Ohio nations were first-class actors choosing British *trade* over French *masters*, and that dispossession-by-force was "already in motion before the war was even lit." Pickawillany is the cleanest single proof of exactly that: a Native nation (the Miami) deliberately broke with the French, hosted British/Pennsylvania traders because their goods were better, and France answered by having a war party destroy the town and kill (and, per the standard account, ritually eat) its chief. That is the dispossession-already-in-motion claim made concrete, and the section currently asserts it without its best evidence.
- **It is conventionally called "the opening action of the French and Indian War"** (American Battlefield Trust; Ohio History Connection), and it happened **days after the Treaty of Logstown council adjourned (June 13 → June 21, 1752)** — i.e., it sits in the exact 1752 window the section already narrates. The section walks right up to it at Logstown and then steps over it.
- **The Miami are a resident Ohio-Country nation the section omits entirely.** The draft's roster is "Shawnee, Delaware, Mingo, Wyandot," repeated as a refrain four+ times. Standard scholarship treats the Miami (with Shawnee, Wyandot, Delaware) as one of the "big four" of the mid-1700s Ohio Country. Omitting them while making "who lived there" the moral center of the piece is a real gap, and the repeated four-name refrain actively advertises the absence.

**This needs the fact pack reopened first.** The current pack licenses nothing about the Miami or Pickawillany, and the section's own rule is "write ONLY from verified facts." Facts to pull and verify before authoring:
- Memeskia (Miami/Piankeshaw war chief; English nickname "Old Briton," French "La Demoiselle"); his ~1747 break with the French and relocation to Pickawillany (near present Piqua, OH).
- Pickawillany as a British-trader-hosting town and why (better Pennsylvania trade goods/prices).
- The 21 June 1752 raid: Charles Michel de Langlade leading ~240 French-allied Ottawa/Ojibwe; Memeskia killed, traders captured, town burned; the post-raid Miami drift back toward the French.
- Its standard billing as an opening action / cause-stage flashpoint of the war.

A tight 1–2 paragraph beat is enough — storytelling-first, not a battle write-up (it is a theme section, no stat block). Best placement: as a hinge between the Logstown council (§"The land does not belong to either of you") and the fort race, or folded into the land-rush section to show the Pennsylvania/trade axis the Virginia/grant axis is currently crowding out.

## SHOULD-FIX (underweighting, not ship-blocking)

- **Pennsylvania's claim is nearly invisible.** The whole section is a Virginia story (Ohio Company = Virginia syndicate, Virginia commissioners, Virginia's Dinwiddie, Virginia's Washington). In reality the Ohio Country was claimed by **both** Pennsylvania and Virginia, and Pennsylvania-based traders (Croghan, and the men at Pickawillany) were the British commercial presence on the ground. The draft gestures at this once — Croghan's deed being void under Pennsylvania law — but never states that two British colonies were both pushing claims. One clause would fix it and would also set up the Pickawillany beat (those were Pennsylvania traders).
- **The Logstown back-room deal.** Sources note that after the public council disputed the Lancaster treaty, Croghan/Montour/Gist met Native leaders privately and secured the concession the open council had refused. This sharpens the section's existing point about the British preferring to deal around the resident nations. Optional enrichment to §"The land does not belong to either of you," not required.
- **Andrew Montour at Logstown.** The draft names Montour only as a guide on Gist's 1750 tour; he was also the **interpreter at the 1752 Logstown treaty**. Minor, but if the Logstown scene is expanded he belongs in it.

## Native perspective and aims — weighting assessment (the brief's special focus)

**Strong, and the strongest part of the draft.** The section does the thing most cause-of-the-war narratives fail to do: it makes the resident nations a *third power with its own strategy*, not scenery. Specifically well-handled —
- The migration-to-escape-colonists frame is established early and used as the moral spine.
- The Iroquois-by-right-of-conquest vs. resident-nations tension is dramatized, not flattened, and the British preference for dealing through the Iroquois "precisely because it produced cessions the resident nations never agreed to" is named as the mechanism of dispossession.
- Tanaghrisson's own words carry the climax, with the transcription-through-Washington provenance honestly flagged.
- The closing reframes the war's very name as a colonists'-eye-view artifact.

**The one weighting flaw is the Pickawillany omission (above):** the section gives the Ohio nations a strong *voice* (Tanaghrisson refusing cessions, holding both empires off) but is missing the strongest single instance of the Ohio nations *acting and being punished for it* — the Miami choosing British trade and France destroying their town for it. Adding it doesn't fix a wrong weighting so much as supply the missing concrete proof of a claim the section already makes in the abstract.

No factual errors found in the Native-perspective material as written; the contested items (Tanaghrisson's origin, the "Half King" title) are correctly presented as unresolved.
