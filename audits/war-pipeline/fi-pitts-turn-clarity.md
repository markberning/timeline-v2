## Unexplained Leaps / Comprehension Breakers

### MUST-FIX

**1. WHY Louisbourg "opens the St. Lawrence" — not explained**
The draft states it three times ("whoever held it held the door to Quebec," "the mouth of the St. Lawrence stood open," "the single greatest obstacle on the road to Quebec was gone") but never explains WHY controlling a coastal fortress unlocks an entire river. A zero-knowledge reader has no mental model of how sailing up a river works or why a fort at its mouth would block a fleet. One sentence — "the St. Lawrence is Canada's only deep-water entry from the sea; a fortress at its mouth could refuse passage to any ship trying to reach Quebec, 800 miles upriver" — fixes this completely.

**2. WHY the Treaty of Easton made the French abandon Fort Duquesne — causal gap**
The logic chain is: Easton → Native allies withdraw → French have no warriors → French blow up the fort. The draft gestures at this ("stripped away the Native warriors") but never explains WHY French regulars, even a few hundred of them, could not simply hold a fort without Native allies against 6,000 approaching British. Specifically: what had the Native warriors been doing? Raiding supply lines? Providing the scouting the French couldn't otherwise do? The Grant ambush (p84) shows Native warriors protecting the fort, which is the right setup — but the reader needs one sentence connecting that to why their departure made the fort militarily untenable (not just undersupplied). Currently feels like the Native allies are described as an abstract political bargaining chip rather than a concrete military necessity.

**3. Regulars vs. Provincials — the rank insult is the engine of the whole Pitt-strategy section**
Pitt's colonial partnership pivot (reimbursements, rank reform) is the most important part of his strategy section, but a reader who doesn't know what a "regular" is vs. a "provincial" won't understand why the rank order was humiliating or why fixing it brought men into the army. The contrast must be defined at first use (p15), not just implied. MUST-FIX.

### SHOULD-FIX

**4. "Litter" — wrong mental image for modern readers**
P80: "carried west in a litter." Modern readers hear "garbage litter." Change to "stretcher" or "field stretcher."

**5. "Powder magazine" — not "magazine"**
P106: "the French blew up the fort's magazine." Use "powder magazine" to distinguish from the modern meaning.

**6. "Half-Acadian" (Bradstreet) — unexplained**
P69: readers don't know what being Acadian means in this war's context. One-liner: "of partly Acadian descent — the French-speaking settlers of Nova Scotia Britain had forcibly expelled a few years earlier."

**7. "Secretary of State for the Southern Department"**
P11: this title appears with zero explanation of what a "Southern Department" is. Change to "Secretary of State (the cabinet post that made him war minister)" or just omit the formal title since the next sentence explains the actual power anyway.

**8. "Bastioned fortress"**
P112: "five-sided bastioned fortress" — drop "bastioned" or replace with "star-shaped" which readers recognize.

## Overwhelming / Wall-of-Text Passages

None. Paragraph lengths and pace are well-controlled throughout. The Easton section (p91–99) is dense with names (13 nations listed, multiple negotiators) but the narrative doesn't lose the thread.

## Overall Assessment

The draft is largely accessible. Voice, scene-setting, and structural signposting are strong. The three MUST-FIX items are genuine comprehension breakers for a zero-knowledge reader — the St. Lawrence geographic explanation is the biggest single gap. The SHOULD-FIX items are one-word or one-phrase fixes.
