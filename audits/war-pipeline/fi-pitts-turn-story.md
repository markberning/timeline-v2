# Storytelling Critic Gate — Pitt's Turn (French & Indian War)
> Draft: `audits/war-pipeline/fi-pitts-turn-draft.md`
> Voice target: `improvising-a-war` (popular-history, informal, stakes-first)
> Date: 2026-06-02

---

## Overall Grade: GOOD (borderline STRONG)

Strong bones. The narrative through-line is clear, the four-part strategy lands cleanly, and the Easton section is genuinely the best work in the draft — it earns its pivot. No house-voice violations (no em-dashes, no meta-narrator asides, no over-bolding). The main problems are a flat opening, a Carillon section that rushes past the human stakes once Howe dies, and a Pitt-as-administrator risk that materializes briefly before the prose rescues itself.

---

## Section-by-Section Grades

### Intro (paragraphs 1–2)
**NEEDS WORK**

The opening sentence is competent but doesn't grab. "For three years Britain had been losing this war" is correct and contextual, but it's a situation summary, not a story hook. The *Improvising a War* target opens with a single declarative about what the war *was actually about* — moral stakes named immediately — and then pivots to the paradox. This intro gives you a chain of losses and then tells you what the chapter will cover. That second paragraph ("This is the strategic spine…") is pure meta-narration — it's the author talking *about* the draft rather than the story. The target voice never does this.

> **Quoted problem:** "This is the strategic spine of the turning year. It does not re-tell each siege and slaughter blow by blow; it links down to those." — This is a table of contents, not an opening. Cut or collapse to one sentence, and lead with a human image or a single charged fact.

**Fix:** Open on a sensory or human detail that concretizes the losing-war feeling — a frontier farm burned, a column of Braddock's men still unburied in the forest — *then* introduce Pitt arriving in London. The structural preview can shrink to a single sentence buried mid-intro.

### A Slaughter at the Log Wall (Carillon)
**NEEDS WORK**

The setup is excellent. Howe's death and the quote ("the soul of General Abercromby's army seemed to expire") land hard. The abatis explanation (the "band of felled trees with their sharpened branches pointed outward… functioning like a tangle of barbed wire") is a model of inline definition done right.

The problem is the battle itself. Once Howe is dead and the attack begins, the prose speeds past what should be the draft's most visceral passage: thousands of men snarled in sharpened branches, being cut apart from behind eight feet of timber, repeating the charge for *hours*. "One observer compared the ground to a slaughterhouse" — that's a single clause, not a scene. The target voice earns emotion through specificity, not adjectives. This is the bloodiest battle of the whole war. The reader should feel it for a sentence or two more.

The Abercromby-as-culprit setup — the engineer's faulty scout report, the decision to go without artillery — is clean and satisfying. The retreat and aftermath paragraph is good. The fault is specifically in the attack passage (four sentences from "On the afternoon of July 8" to "each time").

> **Quoted problem:** "The British came on again and again for hours, and were cut down each time." — This is telling. Give us one specific regimental detail (the Highlanders making four charges in full sight of each other's dead; a surviving officer's account of the noise; anything concrete) before the retreat.

The "For all its horror, Carillon could not change the war" bridge is strong — it earns the strategic pivot cleanly.

The contrast between the tiny fight (two French dead, eleven British wounded) and the enormous strategic haul is the story here, and the draft makes that contrast explicit and punchy. "Dangling at the end of a severed supply line" is good.

> Minor: "After the catastrophe at Carillon, the news also did something for British morale that no amount of casualties at the log wall could undo" — this sentence ends the section on an abstraction. One concrete detail (a letter home, a dispatch, a number that shifted) would land it harder.

### The Slow Road West (Forbes)
**GOOD**

Forbes dying in a litter while winning the war's greatest victory is the human story here, and the draft doesn't fully lean into it. "Forbes was also dying" is introduced as a logistical aside rather than a character note. The opportunity: make his physical deterioration a counterpoint to his methodical, unhurried strategy — the man who moved slowly because he had to, building a road that couldn't be ambushed, even as his body gave out.

The Washington-arguing-for-the-Virginia-route detail is good texture. The Grant's Hill disaster (September ambush, 342 casualties) is handled correctly — enough to establish that Native alliance still mattered, not so much it hijacks the Forbes thread.

> House-voice note: No violations, but the section lacks a single vivid human detail in Forbes himself beyond "dying" and "methodical." One specific — what he wrote, how he gave orders, one image of the litter — would make him a person.

## MUST-IMPROVE

**1. Intro: cut the meta-narration paragraph entirely; replace the opening with a hook that puts a person or place before the strategy.**
The two-paragraph intro leads with a five-clause situation summary and then a table-of-contents paragraph. The voice target never does this. Specifically: "This is the strategic spine…" and "What it carries is the shape" are the author narrating at the reader. Cut the second paragraph. Open the first with something concrete — the smoking bones west of the Appalachians, the burned farmhouse, Braddock's ghost — and land on Pitt in London in the second or third sentence.

**2. Carillon attack passage: give the assault one specific concrete detail before the retreat.**
"The British came on again and again for hours, and were cut down each time" is the bloodiest moment in the whole war, told in thirteen words. One regimental detail — a specific charge, a specific officer's account of what the abatis felt like, a specific time gap between attacks — makes it visceral without slowing the section. This is the draft's most under-written moment relative to its historical weight.

**3. Forbes: make his dying-in-a-litter a character counterpoint, not a logistical note.**
"Forbes was also dying" buried mid-paragraph misses the irony the story earns: a man building the most careful, methodical road in the war because he literally cannot afford to rush, carried west in a litter, dictating orders he will not live to see completed. That's a story. One sentence doing that work — early in the section — would give Forbes the human dimension Bradstreet already has.

---

## NICE-TO-HAVE

**A. Intro: consider opening on a specific image from the losing years.**
Rather than "For three years Britain had been losing this war," consider opening on one specific thing from those three years — a fort name, a dead column, a burned settlement — that a reader can picture. Then pull back to the strategic picture.

**B. Fort Frontenac close: land on a concrete morale detail.**
"No amount of casualties at the log wall could undo" ends on abstraction. A brief specific (a letter, a newspaper line, a dispatch that named Frontenac as the turning point) would close the section on something felt rather than described.

**C. Easton: consider whether the Pontiac's War paragraph is a sidebar.**
It's accurate and important, but it breaks the narrative's present tense mid-section. A "Meanwhile" or linked piece would carry it without interrupting the Easton payoff.

**D. Pitt's four-part strategy: consider varying sentence rhythm in part 2–4.**
Each part opens "The second part was…" / "The third part was…" / "The fourth was…" — parallel structure is intentional and defensible, but the repetition grows slightly mechanical by the fourth. A small variation in the fourth entry's opening would release the pressure.
