## HERO

**Subject:** Bombardment of Fort Sumter, Charleston Harbor — the opening shots of the Civil War, April 12–13, 1861.

**Why hero:** The most iconic single image of the chapter's climax — Currier & Ives's hand-colored lithograph shows the night bombardment from the Confederate batteries, shells bursting over the fort, harbor and city silhouetted behind. Instantly recognizable, dramatically lit, visually rich at full bleed. It anchors the moment the talking stopped.

### Figure 4 — Abraham Lincoln portrait, April 1861

**Subject:** Abraham Lincoln, head-and-shoulders portrait, facing left — photographed by Mathew Brady, April 6, 1861.

**Why it fits:** The chapter opens with the "two presidents inaugurated that year" framing and makes an extended Davis-vs-Lincoln contrast. ### Figure 6 — Fort Sumter interior after bombardment, 1861

**Subject:** Interior of Fort Sumter after the April 1861 bombardment — stereoscopic photograph by Osborn & Durbec, Charleston.

**Why it fits:** "The Fort in the Harbor" describes thirty-four hours of bombardment, three thousand shells, and the fort catching fire. A photograph of the actual ruined interior — taken in 1861 by Charleston photographers — grounds the narrative's physical description of the bombardment's aftermath.

**Note on image:** This is a stereoscopic half-frame (1024 × 529 px, wide crop, low resolution). It will display as a wide landscape strip. If a cleaner image is preferred, the Currier & Ives colored lithograph of the bombardment (LCCN90711987 version — see alternate below) is a more visually rich option for this slot; the interior photo is the more historically precise choice. ### Figure 7 — Major Robert Anderson portrait, February 1861

**Subject:** Major Robert Anderson, U.S. Army, commander of Fort Sumter — half-length portrait in uniform, taken at Fort Sumter, February 8, 1861.

**Why it fits:** Anderson is the chapter's key Union figure inside the fort. The narrative names him, describes his bold move from Moultrie to Sumter, and follows him through the surrender. A portrait taken *at Fort Sumter* two months before the bombardment is precisely sourced and gives the reader a face for the man who held out for thirty-four hours.

### Figure 10 — 1861 Secession crisis map

**Subject:** Map of the secession crisis of 1860–1861, showing seceding states, border states, and Union states — Charles O. Paullin, from *Atlas of the Historical Geography of the United States* (1932).

**Why it fits:** The chapter's final section "The Hinge" describes the four border states and why their geography was decisive — "look at a map and notice where they sit. They nearly surround Washington, D.C." This map gives the reader exactly that view: the seceded states in the Deep South, the four border states wedged between, Washington surrounded by Maryland.

The 1864 Morris Island wood engraving (LCCN2002706069) is higher quality but depicts the Second Battle of Fort Sumter, not the April 1861 bombardment that this chapter covers. **Editorial decision needed:** use the low-res 1861 stereograph (Figure 6 above), substitute the second Currier & Ives bombardment print (LCCN90711987 version), or leave this slot open.
