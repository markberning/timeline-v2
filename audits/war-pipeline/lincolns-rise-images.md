## HERO IMAGE

### Mathew Brady, "Cooper Union" standing portrait of Lincoln (February 27, 1860)

**Why this spot:** This is the photograph that made Lincoln a national figure. Brady took it the afternoon of the Cooper Union speech, before Lincoln delivered the address that turned him from a prairie curiosity into a serious presidential contender. It is Lincoln beardless, lean, and serious — the man the East first had to reckon with. It is the definitive visual document of the moment this section is built around. Lincoln himself is often quoted (secondhand, as the narrative notes) as crediting Brady and the Cooper Institute with making him president. Whether or not he said it, the image did exactly that.

### Figure 3 — "The Political Quadrille" cartoon (additional proposed figure)

**Narrative position:** "Four men, one ballot, no center" section — at the explanation of the four-way race and the Democratic split.

**Why this spot:** This is the most famous period political cartoon of the 1860 election. Published September 1860 by Currier and Ives (or in the same style), it depicts all four candidates dancing to Dred Scott's fiddle, each paired with a figure representing their constituency — Lincoln with an African American woman (mocking Republican abolitionism), Douglas with an Irish immigrant, Breckinridge with Buchanan (depicted as a goat/"Buck"), Bell with a Native American. It captures the exact four-way chaos the section explains, and it is unmistakably a period artifact from the campaign itself, not a retrospective illustration.

### Figure 4 — "The National Game. Three Outs and One Run" cartoon (additional proposed figure)

**Narrative position:** "A clear majority, a divided country" section — immediately following the electoral vote results, where the winner-take-all mechanism is explained.

**Why this spot:** This Currier and Ives lithograph (drawn by Louis Maurer, deposited for copyright September 15, 1860, weeks before the election) is the most celebrated single visual artifact of the four-way 1860 race. It shows Lincoln with his foot on home plate having scored the "run" while Bell, Douglas, and Breckinridge are struck out, each holding a bat labeled with their political position. Lincoln's bat reads "Equal Rights and Free Territory." It perfectly illustrates the winner-take-all logic the narrative is explaining — Lincoln scored; everyone else got nothing.

### Figure 7 — Charleston Mercury "Union Is Dissolved" broadside (additional proposed figure)

**Narrative position:** "Secession winter" section — at South Carolina's secession on December 20, 1860.

**Why this spot:** This is the most iconic single document of the secession winter — the Charleston Mercury newspaper's special "Extra" edition of December 20, 1860, with the headline "THE UNION IS DISSOLVED!" in massive type, printed and distributed the same day South Carolina voted 169–0 to secede. The narrative describes that day directly. This broadside is the period artifact that makes the reader feel the speed and drama of the moment: South Carolina did not wait. They printed it the same day.
