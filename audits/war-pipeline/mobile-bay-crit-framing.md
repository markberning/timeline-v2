# Mobile Bay — LOST CAUSE / FRAMING GATE

**Verdict: FAIR / PASS — slavery is named as the cause in the draft's OWN prose, repeatedly and structurally. No Lost Cause tilt. 0 MUST-FIX, 1 SHOULD-FIX (the upstream statistic shared with the fact gate).**

---

## FIRST CHECK — is slavery named as the cause, in the draft's own voice? YES.

This is the gate's automatic-fail trigger, and the draft clears it decisively — not in a buried clause but as a
structural spine:

- **§1, author's own voice:** "The Confederacy existed to preserve and extend slavery — that was the cause the whole war turned on, and the blockade-runners feeding through Mobile were the supply line of a republic built on enslaved labor."
- **§5 / "what-it-won":** "The Confederacy fought to preserve and extend slavery. That is not editorializing; it is the plain reason eleven states left the Union and built armies." And: "a society built on owning human beings."
- **Closing MEANWHILE:** "the cotton and the cargo of a republic founded to keep four million people in bondage."

Slavery is named, unhedged, in the narrator's own voice — not attributed to a third party, not softened to
"states' rights" or "the Southern way of life," not deferred to a quote. The "name the cause" requirement is met
the way the locked rule demands.

## The cause is not just named — it is the THESIS
The draft does something stronger than satisfy the gate: it makes slavery the organizing argument of the piece.
The recurring "the port was the *object*, slavery was the *reason*" refrain, the blockade-as-instrument-of-
emancipation throughline ("the blockade and emancipation were two halves of one policy"), and the framing of the
door as "a door *for* something" all keep the cause load-bearing rather than ornamental. This is the opposite of
the Lost Cause default (a "daring fleet action" told with the cause filed off).

## The Brown / Lawson cause-hook — accurately stated
- **Wilson Brown — "born enslaved on a plantation near Natchez, Mississippi."** VERIFIED accurate (Botany Bay Plantation, ~1841; he enlisted as "contraband"). The draft's emotional anchor — "A man born a slave on a Mississippi plantation was, that August morning, working the guns of the fleet that was shutting the Confederacy's last Gulf door" — is factually sound and not overstated.
- **John Lawson — "free-born Black sailor from Philadelphia."** VERIFIED accurate.
- Both citations are PARAPHRASED in house voice, not printed as contested verbatim — and both paraphrases match the real NHHC/cmohs citation text. The cause-hook is concrete and documented, not a generality. PASS.

## Lost Cause / romanticization hunt

The fact-pack correctly warned that **naval gallantry lore can drift into romanticizing the cause.** I checked the
three danger zones:

1. **Buchanan's lone stand of CSS Tennessee.** This is the single biggest romanticization risk — a doomed
   Confederate ironclad fighting a whole fleet is exactly the kind of beautiful-loser set-piece the Lost Cause
   feeds on. The draft admires the *seamanship and nerve* ("turned a one-sided fight into a legend on the losing
   side") but never lets that admiration migrate to the *cause*. Crucially, the draft's structure subordinates the
   gallantry to the thesis: the very next major beat reasserts that the thing this fleet was shutting down was a
   slave economy, and that a formerly enslaved man was firing the flagship's guns. The valor is rendered without
   ennobling what Buchanan was fighting *for*. PASS — this is the hard case and it's handled correctly.

2. **Farragut hero-worship.** "Damn the torpedoes," the rigging, "the most famous sailor in the country" — all
   present, but each is explicitly de-mythologized in-prose (the order is "stitched together and polished in the
   retelling"; the rigging was his captain's safety precaution, "not out of bravado"). The draft actively resists
   the swashbuckler myth rather than indulging it. No false grandeur.

3. **"After you, pilot."** A genuinely moving death — held at arm's length as survivor-reported lore, not milked.
   Human reality without sentimentality. PASS.

## Soft-pedaling / false balance / euphemism — none found
- No "states' rights," no "both sides were brave so the cause is a wash," no sectional-tragedy framing that
  launders secession's purpose.
- The human cost is neither sanitized (the ~90 men dead in 30 seconds inside Tecumseh; Buchanan's broken leg) nor
  gratuitous. Proportionate.
- No presentism: the integrated-crew point is made with period statistics and named men, not anachronistic
  editorializing.

## Tilt CHECK — does it tilt unfairly against the South? No.
The Confederate commanders are named and given their due (Buchanan's courage, the garrisons' surrenders dated
factually). The draft condemns the *cause*, not the *men* gratuitously. Balanced where balance is honest, unbalanced
only where the historical record is (the cause).

## SHOULD-FIX (shared with the fact gate)
- **The "roughly one in four… in Farragut's own West Gulf Blockading Squadron" statistic is wrong** (NARA Table 2:
  West Gulf was ~20%, one in five; "one quarter" is the Navy-wide rule-of-thumb). This is a *framing-adjacent*
  fix as well as a factual one: the Black-crew proportion is doing argumentative work in the cause section, so the
  number should be exactly right. Fix to "roughly one in five" (squadron) or attribute "one in four" to the Navy
  as a whole. Not a Lost Cause problem — it slightly *over*states the integration on this specific battlefield —
  but precision matters most exactly where the prose leans hardest. SHOULD-FIX (MUST-FIX in the fact gate).

**Bottom line:** No Lost Cause tilt, no soft-pedaling, no romanticization of the cause despite a gallantry-heavy
naval story. Slavery is named in the draft's own voice and made the thesis. The only correction is the shared
statistic. FAIR / PASS.
