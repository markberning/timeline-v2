# NEWCOMER-CLARITY CRITIC · The Siege of Savannah draft

*Critic pass on `audits/war-pipeline/rev-savannah-draft.md`, 2026-06-12. Reader model:
zero prior knowledge — does not know the Revolution's geography, has never seen Savannah
on a map, does not know there was a prior British capture of the town, cannot picture a
redoubt or an abatis, has not read any other article in this vertical, has no frame for
why a French fleet is sailing off Georgia.*

## VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FIXES

This is a clean, well-structured draft. The hurricane-clock driver is explained early and
mechanically. The diversity of the army is richly set up. The Chasseurs-Volontaires
Saint-Domingue context (who they were, where they came from, what the contradiction was)
is the single best clarity passage in the draft and fully passes. The 24-hour stall logic
is legible. The section structure (fleet/clock → stall/betrayal → assault/aftermath) is
clean. The main clarity problems are: two BLOCKERS (abatis never glossed; the December
1778 capture — the essential backstory — is not clearly anchored before the siege opens)
and a cluster of SHOULD-FIX items (sortie unexplained; truce vs parley undefined; Maitland
cold on first DOSSIER use; Beaufort/Port Royal location set only in Section 2, not when it
is first needed; the Caribbean-to-Georgia arc not given a simple mileage/distance anchor).
No structural BLOCKERS on the French-fleet logic, the siege geography, or the Saint-Domingue
framing — those all pass.

**B1. The December 1778 Savannah capture is mentioned in Section 1 but never anchored as
a clear, dated, settled backstory before the siege narrative opens.**
The DOSSIER NOTE says "a town Britain had captured almost for free the winter before."
Section 1 then unpacks the story of Campbell's December 29, 1778 capture midway through
the section, after the strategic pivot and the French-fleet setup. But the article OPENS
with an army that has come to "take back a town" — and the newcomer does not yet know the
town was taken. The dossier note is the first signal, but the dossier note is metadata:
phone readers often skip it or read it after tapping into Section 1. By the time "take
back" is meaningful, the reader is already in the opening clause of Section 1 and the
backstory has not landed yet.

More critically: Section 1's opening sentence — "By the summer of 1779, Britain had
decided to win the war from the bottom up" — launches into the strategic pivot WITHOUT
telling the reader that Savannah is already in British hands. The newcomer's question
("wait, so who holds Savannah right now?") is not answered until the middle of Section 1.

- FIX (Section 1, opening paragraph or immediately after the strategic-pivot paragraph):
  surface a single orienting sentence at the TOP of Section 1 that tells the reader
  Savannah is a captured city. Something like: "Britain had taken Savannah, Georgia's
  colonial capital, on December 29, 1778 — a small force, an enslaved guide who showed
  the path through the swamp, and two dozen British casualties to rout the American
  defenders — and the siege that follows is the allied attempt to take it back." That
  sentence, placed before or at the top of the strategic-pivot paragraph, would make every
  subsequent "take it back" and "southern strategy" reference land cleanly. The fuller
  Campbell backstory later in Section 1 then serves as a richly-earned expansion, not the
  reader's first orientation.

**B2. "Sortie" is never defined.**
Section 2: "A British sortie on September 24 hit the allied working parties in the
trenches." A sortie is a military term (a sudden attack launched by defenders from inside
a fortification to disrupt the besiegers) that the newcomer will not know. It appears once,
in a pivotal sentence that establishes the Chasseurs-Volontaires' documented combat role.

- FIX: "A British sortie (a sudden attacking raid launched from inside the fortifications)
  on September 24 hit the allied working parties in the trenches..."

## SHOULD-FIX

**S1. "Truce" vs context — what a siege truce means is not explained.**
Section 2: "Prevost asked for twenty-four hours to consider. D'Estaing granted it." And
then: "While the truce held, the Royal Engineer James Moncrief (British) kept his crews
digging." The newcomer may not know that a siege truce means fighting formally stops while
an answer is awaited — and therefore that BOTH sides were bound not to shoot. The draft
relies on that to make the betrayal implicit ("he used every minute"), but "truce" is never
defined. The reader may not understand why d'Estaing could not simply attack while Prevost
was buying time.

- FIX (first "truce" mention): "While the truce held (the formal ceasefire both sides
  observed while a surrender demand was under consideration)..." or add a clause at
  "D'Estaing granted it": "D'Estaing granted it — standard siege protocol: both sides
  stopped fighting while the garrison considered."

**S2. Maitland is cold on first substantive DOSSIER use; Beaufort's location is not set
until Section 2 prose.**
The DOSSIER SIDES note (British) mentions: "a relief column of about 800 men that
Lieutenant Colonel John Maitland marched through the coastal creeks from Beaufort during
the truce." This is the FIRST time Maitland appears and the first time Beaufort appears.
Neither has been established yet. Beaufort is identified as being "on Port Royal Island
up the coast" — but only later, in Section 2 prose. The dossier sides note is a reader
surface; phone users who read it before tapping into the sections will be confused by
"Beaufort" as a cold place-name.

- FIX (DOSSIER SIDES note, British entry): gloss Beaufort on first mention in the dossier:
  "from Beaufort (a British-held port about 40 miles up the South Carolina coast, on Port
  Royal Island) during the truce." This makes the dossier self-contained.

**S3. "Free men of color" is defined but the LEGAL STATUS within Saint-Domingue is not
fully explained for the newcomer.**
Section 1: "roughly 545 men raised that spring in the French sugar colony of Saint-Domingue
(the colony that is now Haiti) from free men of color and enslaved men promised their
freedom for service." The draft says "free men of color," which is the period term, and
later explains "a colony built on enslaved sugar labor, where their own status was hedged
about with restrictions." The word "restrictions" is vague. A newcomer has no frame for
what "free man of color" meant in 1779 Saint-Domingue — legally free, but barred from
most civic rights, excluded from many professions, unable to sit at the same table as
white colonists, legally required to defer. The MEANWHILE box in Section 1 carries the
right tone ("a freedom their own home denied them") but does not name the actual constraint.

- FIX (MEANWHILE box, Section 1): add one phrase — "free men of color in Saint-Domingue
  were legally free but barred from full civic rights, excluded from most public roles,
  and legally required to defer to white colonists." This makes the contradiction visceral,
  not abstract.

**S4. The Caribbean-to-Georgia arc has no distance or travel-time anchor.**
Section 1 explains the hurricane deadline clearly: exposed coast, scurvy, dysentery, orders
home. But it never tells the newcomer how far the fleet sailed to get there. The MEANWHILE
box mentions "Cap-Français (today Cap-Haïtien, in Haiti)" but gives no sense of the
distance or the journey. A phone reader has no mental map of the Caribbean-to-Georgia gap.

- FIX (Section 1, when the fleet "appearing off the coast in the first days of September"
  is mentioned): one sentence — "The fleet had sailed roughly 1,200 miles (about 1,900 km)
  north from its Caribbean base at Cap-Français, the main port on the north coast of
  Saint-Domingue (today Haiti)." (The mileage is approximate but within range; adjust to
  whatever the factpack's §10 geography gives.) This makes the hurricane-deadline mechanic
  visceral — a fleet that far from port, in hurricane season, needs to be home.

**S5. "Grapeshot" is glossed in Section 3 at first use — PASS. But "rifle fire" vs musket
fire distinction is assumed.**
Section 3: "Grapeshot (a cannon round that sprays a cloud of iron balls) and rifle fire
poured out of the works." The newcomer does not know the difference between a rifle and a
musket in 1779 terms — a rifle had a much longer accurate range, which is why Morgan's men
and the defenders at Spring Hill could pick off attackers at distances a smoothbore musket
could not reach. The narrative implies range advantage but never explains the mechanism.
This matters because "rifle fire" from redoubt defenders is slightly odd (rifles were
expensive, slow to load, rare in regular formations) — the draft might mean musket fire
with some rifle detachments mixed in.

- FIX (POLISH level): if the defenders at Spring Hill were primarily musket-armed
  (standard infantry), change "rifle fire" to "musket fire" or "concentrated infantry
  fire" to avoid a possible factual slip. If rifles were genuinely present, add a brief
  parenthetical. The factpack §4 should clarify.

**S6. The "24-hour stall" mechanism — why d'Estaing was OBLIGATED to grant it — is not
stated.**
Section 2 says d'Estaing "granted" the 24 hours, and the draft correctly frames this as
a blunder rather than simply following protocol. But a newcomer may wonder: why did he have
to say yes? The answer (standard siege etiquette: a commander who received a summons could
ask for time to consult; refusing to grant it was considered poor form and d'Estaing
believed the garrison would capitulate anyway) is implied but never stated.

- FIX (Section 2, after "D'Estaing granted it"): one clause — "standard siege custom gave
  a garrison the right to ask for time to consult, and d'Estaing, certain the town would
  yield, saw no harm in it." This closes the logic gap.

**S7. Maitland's route through "Wall's Cut and Skull Creek" — the geography is plausible
but opaque.**
Section 2: "he brought them out of Port Royal and threaded them through the half-charted
tidal creeks of the inside passage, behind Daufuskie Island, by Wall's Cut and Skull Creek,
with local fishermen as guides." This is excellent detail but the newcomer has no map in
their head. The strategic locator (Locator 2) carries this geography, but a reader who
taps straight into Section 2 needs at least a minimal orientation: is this route BEHIND
the allied army? Hidden from the French fleet? What made it unwatched?

- FIX (Section 2, Maitland's march): add one orienting clause — "threading through the
  half-charted tidal creeks of the inside passage, behind Daufuskie Island, by Wall's Cut
  and Skull Creek — a water route that ran between the barrier islands and the mainland,
  hidden from the open sea where the French fleet lay — with local fishermen as guides."
  This makes "the allies left the inside-water door unguarded" fully legible.

## NIT

**N1. "Redoubt" — the gloss arrives late.**
Section 2 end: "the main blow falling on the Spring Hill redoubt (a redoubt being a small
earthwork fort) on the British right." The word "redoubt" appears EARLIER in the DOSSIER
SIDES note: "about 13 redoubts and more than 100 guns." That is the reader's first
encounter, and there is no gloss there. The gloss in Section 2 is correct and useful but
arrives after the dossier has already used the term three times.

- FIX (DOSSIER SIDES note, British entry, first "redoubts" mention): add "(small earthwork
  fortifications)" — "about 13 redoubts (small earthwork fortifications) and more than
  100 guns." The Section 2 gloss can then be dropped or kept as a reminder.

**N2. "Parapet" in Section 3 is used twice without definition.**
"Two lieutenants of the 2nd South Carolina, Hume and Bush, planted the regiment's colors
on the rampart." And earlier in the battle sequence the "parapet" is the lip of the
earthwork where the men stood to fire. The newcomer may not know what a parapet or rampart
is. Given that a redoubt is glossed, parapet/rampart should also be, at least once.

- FIX: at first use in Section 3, change "planted the regiment's colors on the rampart" to
  "planted the regiment's colors on the rampart (the earth wall at the top of the
  redoubt)." The following uses inherit the gloss.

**N3. "The evening gun" — a cold idiom.**
Section 2: "the evening gun would be the signal that hostilities had resumed." The
newcomer does not know what "the evening gun" was (a ceremonial cannon fired at sundown
in garrison towns; here, the garrison's signal that the truce was over and they were
ready to fight). The phrase reads as slightly opaque color.

- FIX (POLISH): "Prevost sent his answer: at the evening gun — the cannon fired at
  sundown each night — hostilities would resume." Or simply: "he would fire a cannon at
  sundown to signal the truce was over and fighting had resumed."

**N4. "Scuttled" for the newcomer.**
DOSSIER bio for Moncrief: "had HMS Rose scuttled in the channel to block the French ships
from coming upriver." Section 1 also alludes to the blocked channel. "Scuttled" (sinking
a ship deliberately) may not be known to every reader.

- FIX (POLISH, at first use): "HMS Rose sunk in the channel (deliberately, to block the
  French ships from sailing upriver to bombard the town)." Or add a parenthetical:
  "scuttled (deliberately sunk)."

**N5. "Feint" in Section 3 — never defined.**
"A feint by several hundred militia on the far left was easily stopped." A feint is a
deliberate fake attack meant to draw defenders away from the real blow. The newcomer does
not know this.

- FIX: "A feint (a fake attacking move meant to pull defenders away from the real blow) by
  several hundred militia on the far left was easily stopped."

**N6. "Piecemeal" — military jargon.**
Section 3: "the main columns went in piecemeal at first light." "Piecemeal" in military
context means arriving in disjointed fragments rather than together; it is not opaque, but
a newcomer may read it as simply "slowly."

- FIX (POLISH): "the main columns went in piecemeal — fragmented, not together — at first
  light, against works whose defenders were awake and waiting."

**N7. "Parley" / "summons" distinction — not a BLOCKER but worth noting.**
The draft correctly uses "summons" for the formal surrender demand and "truce" for the
ceasefire — it never uses "parley." This is clean. No fix needed. Flagged only because
the brief calls it out.

**N8. "Recrimination" in the outcome sentence.**
Section 3: "the second straight joint operation, after the wreck at Newport the year
before, had ended in failure and recrimination." "Recrimination" (mutual blame, accusations
traded between allies) may not land for all readers.

- FIX (POLISH): "ended in failure and mutual blame, with the French and Americans pointing
  fingers at each other."

**N9. The Pulaski burial paragraph is long and has one potentially confusing antecedent.**
Section 3: "Then, in 2019, a Georgia Southern-led team reported that the skeleton was
consistent with Pulaski's known injuries and that its mitochondrial DNA matched a
grandniece's, concluding the Monterey Square remains are most likely Pulaski's, which would
settle the question for burial at Greenwich over burial at sea." The "which would settle
the question for burial at Greenwich" clause reads ambiguously: settle it IN FAVOR of the
Greenwich burial, or settle the question OF the Greenwich burial? The draft means the
former (the remains came from Greenwich, so a positive ID confirms the sea-burial story is
wrong). Worth tightening.

- FIX: "concluding the Monterey Square remains are most likely Pulaski's — which, if so,
  would confirm the Greenwich burial over the burial-at-sea story."

**N10. "Mitochondrial DNA... matched a grandniece's" — newcomer may not know why a
grandniece's DNA would be relevant.**
The Pulaski DNA identification works through the maternal line (mtDNA passes from mother
to children of either sex; a grandniece on the maternal side shares the same mtDNA
lineage). A newcomer reads "matched a grandniece's" and may wonder why a grandniece would
be the comparator.

- FIX (POLISH): "its mitochondrial DNA (inherited through the female line) matched a
  female-line grandniece's of Pulaski's."
