## Unexplained leaps and structure issues

### 1. "The exchanges stopped — why does that fill the camps?" [NEEDS-GLOSS]
The intro says the camps filled because a decision broke the exchange system. The mechanism is: before the break, captured men were sent to camp but traded back quickly, so the camps never swelled. After the break, new prisoners kept arriving but nobody left. This chain is *implied* but never stated in a single plain sentence. A first-timer may absorb the parts but not assemble the logic. One sentence at the end of "The ledger breaks" would close it: "Every man captured from August 1863 onward went into a camp and stayed there, with no mechanism left to move him out — and both sides kept sending more."

### 2. The USCT cross-reference [NEEDS-GLOSS — minor]
The intro refers back to "the previous section ('Freedom Seekers & the USCT')" and the parallel section "Fort Pillow & the Guerrilla War." A reader who hasn't read those sections (or who is reading this standalone) doesn't know what USCT stands for. "USCT" is never expanded in this piece. Should be "(United States Colored Troops, the Black Union regiments)" on first mention.

### 3. "Paroled" men and the holding camps [NEEDS-GLOSS]
The parole mechanic is explained well in section 1. But the piece then says paroled men "piled up in holding camps with no way to be cleared." A first-timer will wonder: if paroled men were sent home, why are they in a holding camp? The answer — that "home" for a parolee meant a Union holding depot, not his actual house, because the army had to track him and clear his exchange debt — is implied but not stated. One sentence of clarification needed.

### 4. Grant's manpower math — the Confederate side of the equation [CLEAR but tight]
The piece says Grant's logic was that an exchanged Confederate "becomes an active soldier against us at once." A 15-year-old may not immediately see why the same logic didn't apply to exchanged Union men. The piece briefly says "the Union, larger, could better absorb" — this is fast. Probably clear enough, but borderline.

### 5. "The Lost Cause" monument — why it's still contested [CLEAR]
The final paragraph says the Wirz monument "is still contested." The piece doesn't explain the current controversy in detail, but it doesn't need to — the narrative has earned the reader's understanding that the framing war is ongoing. This is fine.

### 6. Transition to the Fact Ledger [NOT AN ISSUE]
The Fact Ledger at the end is clearly for the pipeline, not the reader. No concern.

## Overwhelming passages (too much at once)

### The rank-exchange table (section 1)
The sliding scale of rank-to-private equivalencies is genuinely interesting but dense: general = 60, major general = 40, brigadier = 20, colonel/navy captain = 15, lieutenant = 4, sergeant = 2. This is a lot of numbers in quick succession. The piece would benefit from one anchor ("A captured general was worth sixty ordinary soldiers — and both sides agreed on the math") and then softer treatment of the middle ranks, rather than listing all of them. Not a MUST-FIX but noted as a minor readability hit.

### The causes-of-death paragraph
The paragraph that lists scurvy, dysentery, starvation, exposure, contagious disease, and hookworm is already compressed. It works, but it's at the edge of density. Fine as-is.

## MUST-FIX (genuine comprehension breakers)

1. **"stockade" undefined on first use (intro paragraph).** Used three times before it is ever defined as a log-wall enclosure. Readers picture a military jail or a western fort, not an open pen of 30,000 men. The misimage persists through the first two sections. Fix: add "(a pen ringed by a wall of pine logs)" on the first appearance in the intro.

2. **"USCT" never expanded.** The acronym appears in a cross-reference in the intro paragraph and is never spelled out in this piece. A standalone reader has no referent. Fix: "(United States Colored Troops, the Black Union regiments formed by the Union after 1862)."

3. **Military commission fairness — the "mattering" is never explained.** The piece says the military-court framing "mattered to the fairness questions later" but never says how. This is the one genuine IOU the piece issues and doesn't pay. Fix: one clause — "(a military court run by officers, not civilian judges or a jury, which meant the legal protections of a civilian trial didn't apply — a point Wirz's defenders raised repeatedly)."

4. **"shebangs" undefined.** The word appears in quotes and is never glossed. It looks like filler slang. Fix: add "(crude lean-tos — a hole scraped in the dirt, a blanket stretched over sticks)" immediately after.

5. **"Commissary-General of Prisoners" (Hoffman) — title unexplained.** A reader cannot tell if this person is a logistics officer, a general, or something else. Fix: one clause — "William Hoffman (Union), the Union officer in charge of managing all Northern prisoner-of-war camps."

## SHOULD-FIX (clarity upgrades, not comprehension breakers)

6. **"shebangs"** — listed above as MUST-FIX, but its severity is debatable; some readers will guess from context. Flagged in both places.
7. **"sexton"** — define as "(the official in charge of church burials and grounds)" next to John W. Jones.
8. **"military commission"** — expand on the fairness distinction (listed as MUST-FIX #3 above; also applies here for completeness).
9. **"ship-chandlery"** — define as "(nautical-supply warehouse)" or drop; adds nothing narratively.
10. **"tidewater Maryland"** — one clause of coastal geography context at Point Lookout.
11. **"Lost Cause" definition** — currently one sentence; worth one more sentence noting it shaped popular Civil War history for generations.
12. **Gen. Benjamin Butler — side tag.** Already called "Union Gen." in text but lacks the consistent "(Union)" parenthetical format. Minor, but jarring given how strictly the rest of the piece tags people.
13. **Brig. Gen. John H. Winder — side tag.** Identified as running the Confederate prison system in prose but not tagged "(Confederate)" in the parenthetical format.
14. **The paroled-men-in-holding-camps confusion.** A sentence clarifying that parole meant supervised limbo at a Union depot, not going home free, would prevent a first-timer from wondering why paroled men need camps.
15. **The rank-equivalency list.** Consider anchoring on the most vivid number (general = 60 privates) and summarizing the middle ranks more loosely.

## Overall assessment

The draft is remarkably clear for material this dense. The best-written glosses in the piece — parole, the cartel, the deadline, germ theory, scurvy, dysentery — are all excellent: definitions land naturally in the prose without slowing it down. The structure is logical and the voice is accessible throughout.

The MUST-FIX items are mostly sins of omission (terms assumed, an IOU not paid) rather than confusing writing. None of them would make a reader put the piece down, but items 1 ("stockade") and 3 (military commission fairness) will leave a first-timer with a persistent wrong image or an unresolved promise.
