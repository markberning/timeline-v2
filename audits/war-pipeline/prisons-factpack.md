## THE HEADLINE NUMBERS (the scale)

- **~410,000 men were held as prisoners of war** across both sides during the war (American Battlefield Trust rounds to "approximately 400,000"; the Official Records cite ~347,000 men formally recorded as POWs — 220,000 Confederate, 127,000 Union; the higher ~410,000 figure includes men captured-and-paroled in the field who never reached a camp). **USE: "more than 400,000 men passed through captivity."**
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Prison Camps," https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/civil-war-prison-camps ; Wikipedia, "American Civil War prison camps," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War_prison_camps

- **~56,000 prisoners died in captivity (ESTIMATE).** Roughly 30,000 in Confederate prisons, ~26,000 in Union prisons. This is ~10% of ALL Civil War deaths. **A lower, conservative scholarly tally is ~49,000+ deaths (≥26,440 Confederate POWs died in Union camps, ≥22,580 Union POWs died in Confederate camps), an overall ~14% mortality.** Give the **range: ~49,000–56,000, labeled estimate.**
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust (56,000); Wikipedia, "American Civil War prison camps" (49,000+ figure); Warfare History Network, "Prisons of the Civil War: An Enduring Controversy," https://warfarehistorynetwork.com/article/prisons-of-the-civil-war-an-enduring-controversy/

- **Mortality split:** death rate ~14–15% in Confederate camps overall vs. ~5.6–8% in Union camps overall — but the worst single camps on EACH side were comparable (Andersonville ~29%, Elmira ~24.5%). The North-vs-South gap is real at the aggregate level; it is NOT a clean morality story at the level of the worst camps.
  — Source: Warfare History Network; Wikipedia, "American Civil War prison camps"

- **The combat comparison (verify framing, numbers solid):** Andersonville alone killed ~13,000 prisoners. For scale: **Union killed at Antietam ≈ 2,100 (12,401 total Union casualties incl. wounded/missing); Union killed at Gettysburg ≈ 3,150 (≈7,058 total killed both sides).** The "more than died at Gettysburg, Antietam, and several major battles combined" framing works **only if you compare deaths-in-camp to battle DEATHS (not total casualties)** — 13,000 dead at one prison genuinely exceeds the *killed* at most single battles. Across all camps, the ABT notes the ~56,000 prison deaths **"exceeded American combat losses in World War I, Korea, and Vietnam combined."** That WWI/Korea/Vietnam line is the cleaner, ABT-sourced version of the comparison — **prefer it.**
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Prison Camps"; NPS, Antietam casualties, https://www.nps.gov/anti/learn/historyculture/casualties.htm ; American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Casualties," https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/civil-war-casualties

## BEAT 1: The Exchange System — The Dix–Hill Cartel (the policy hinge)

- **Before the cartel:** Early in the war neither side wanted a formal exchange system — the Union feared that signing a cartel would amount to recognizing the Confederacy as a legitimate government. Informal field paroles happened, but there was no agreed framework.
  — Source: History Is Now Magazine, "Prisoner of War Exchanges in the U.S. Civil War"; Wikipedia, "Dix–Hill Cartel," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dix%E2%80%93Hill_Cartel

- **Signed: July 22, 1862**, at Haxall's Landing on the James River, Virginia, by **Union Maj. Gen. John A. Dix** and **Confederate Maj. Gen. Daniel Harvey (D.H.) Hill**.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Dix–Hill Cartel"; Legal Legacy, "July 22, 1862 – Dix-Hill Cartel Agreement"

- **The equivalency scale (the mechanics — own this here):** Prisoners were exchanged man-for-man by equal rank, with a sliding scale for unequal ranks. **A general was worth 60 privates; a major general 40; a brigadier 20; a colonel (or naval captain) 15 privates/common seamen; a lieutenant 4; a sergeant 2; etc.** Rank had a literal exchange rate in human bodies.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Dix–Hill Cartel" (privates-per-rank scale); Legal Legacy (cartel text)

- **The parole mechanic:** A prisoner not formally exchanged within **10 days** of capture could be released **on parole** — sent home, but bound by oath not to perform "field, garrison, police, or guard, or constabulary duty" until formally *exchanged* (matched against an enemy prisoner of equal value on the ledger). A paroled man was a soldier on pause, not a free man. This is why "exchange" and "parole" are different things — and why stopping exchanges stranded paroled men in limbo camps too.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Dix–Hill Cartel"

- **It worked at first (mid-1862 into early 1863):** The cartel functioned well for several months and largely emptied both sides' prison populations — which is exactly why the camps were small and survivable in 1862–63 and catastrophic in 1864.
  — Source: Legal Legacy; History Is Now Magazine

### Why it collapsed (the hinge — three stacked causes)

1. **December 1862 — Davis's retaliation order.** Confederate President Jefferson Davis suspended the parole/exchange of captured Union officers in response to Union Gen. Benjamin Butler's actions in occupied New Orleans (the execution of civilian William Mumford for tearing down a U.S. flag; Davis declared Butler an outlaw). First friction.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Dix–Hill Cartel"

2. **May 1, 1863 — the racial-POW policy (THE central cause).** The Confederate Congress passed a joint resolution classifying captured **Black Union soldiers as fugitive/recaptured slaves** — to be returned to claimed owners or dealt with under state slave law (i.e., re-enslavement or death), **not exchanged as prisoners of war.** The same resolution declared captured **white officers commanding Black troops** liable as instigators of "servile insurrection," punishable by military tribunal up to **death.** (DEDUP: the USCT section owns what this meant for the soldiers; here it is the line item that broke the ledger.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Dix–Hill Cartel"; NPS Andersonville, "Grant and the Prisoner Exchange," https://www.nps.gov/ande/learn/historyculture/grant-and-the-prisoner-exchange.htm

3. **July 30, 1863 — Lincoln's General Order 252.** Lincoln issued **General Orders No. 252**, suspending the cartel until the Confederacy agreed to treat Black prisoners the same as white ones, and threatening retaliation (a rebel prisoner placed at hard labor for every U.S. prisoner enslaved or killed). The U.S. position: no exchanges unless ALL Union soldiers, Black included, are exchanged on equal terms. **Large-scale exchanges had effectively ceased by August 1863** — months *before* Grant took overall command.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Dix–Hill Cartel"; NPS Andersonville, "Grant and the Prisoner Exchange"

- **1864 — Grant formalizes the halt, and the camps fill.** When Grant took command of all Union armies (spring 1864), he kept exchanges stopped, on **two** grounds: (a) the Black-soldier equal-treatment demand, and (b) cold manpower math — exchanged Confederates went straight back into the ranks, while the Union's larger population could absorb the loss. Grant's own words (testimony / Aug. 18, 1864 letter to Butler):
  > "It is hard on our men held in Southern prisons not to exchange them, but it is humanity to those left in the ranks to fight our battles. Every man we hold, when released on parole or otherwise, becomes an active soldier against us at once either directly or indirectly."
  And: *"At this particular time, to release all Rebel prisoners North would insure Sherman's defeat, and would compromise our safety here."*
  — Source: NPS Andersonville, "Grant and the Prisoner Exchange"; Wikisource, *Southern Historical Society Papers* Vol. 1, "Testimony of General U.S. Grant"

- **IMPORTANT BALANCE (NPS, ship-blocking nuance):** The Lost Cause later weaponized Grant's quote to blame the North for Andersonville. NPS states plainly: *"It is therefore inaccurate to attribute the breakdown of the prisoner exchange and all of the sufferings of prisoners of war to a callous military directive by General Ulysses S. Grant."* The exchange had already collapsed over the Black-prisoner issue **a year before** Grant's directive. **Author MUST present both: Grant's manpower logic was real AND was not the originating cause.**
  — Source: NPS Andersonville, "Grant and the Prisoner Exchange"

- **Resumption:** Grant permitted exchanges to resume in **January 1865** once the Confederacy (now desperate and losing) agreed to include all prisoners — but by then Andersonville's worst was already done.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Dix–Hill Cartel"

**THE HINGE IN ONE LINE (for the author):** The cartel emptied the camps in 1862; the refusal to exchange Black soldiers as men broke the cartel in 1863; the broken cartel is *why* there was an Andersonville at all. The camps were a policy consequence, not just a logistics failure.

## BEAT 2: Andersonville (Camp Sumter) — the turning point

- **Official name:** Camp Sumter. **Location:** near the village of Andersonville, Sumter/Macon County, southwest Georgia (sited deliberately deep in the interior, away from raids and the rail lines that fed exchanges).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Andersonville National Historic Site," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andersonville_National_Historic_Site

- **Opened:** **February 1864.** Operated ~14 months, to **April/May 1865.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Andersonville NHS"

- **Built for ~10,000; held ~32,000 at peak.** Original stockade enclosed **16.5 acres** (1,620 ft × 779 ft), 15-foot pine-log walls; expanded to **26.5 acres** in late June 1864. **Peak population: 31,695 on August 31, 1864** (often rounded to "32,000" / "33,000" — Riddle's photo captions say "thirty-three thousand"). Space per man fell to roughly **5 × 6 feet.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Andersonville NHS"

- **Total held:** ~**45,000** Union prisoners passed through (often cited as 49,485 received). **Deaths: nearly 13,000** (commonly 12,920; ~28–29% of those held). It was the single deadliest site of the war.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Andersonville NHS"; New Georgia Encyclopedia, "Andersonville Prison"

- **The "deadline":** A light wood railing on posts ~3–4 ft high, set **~19 feet inside** the stockade wall. Crossing or even reaching past it meant being **shot without warning** by sentries in the "pigeon roost" guard posts. The term appears in a Confederate inspection report by **Lt. Col. D.T. Chandler (August 1864):** *"A railing around the inside of the stockade and about 20 feet from it constitutes the 'dead-line,' beyond which the prisoners are not allowed to pass."* (This is the literal origin of the modern word "deadline.")
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Andersonville NHS"; Grammarphobia, "We're on deadline"; Civil War Picket, "Crossing the 'deadline'"

- **Water — Stockade Branch:** The only water source was a small creek (Stockade Branch) running through the camp. It also served as the camp's **sink (latrine)**, and was fouled by the **Confederate guards' cookhouse/camp upstream** before it even entered the stockade — so prisoners drew drinking water from what was already sewage. Dysentery and diarrhea followed directly.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Andersonville NHS"; NPS, "Andersonville: Prisoner of War Camp (Teaching with Historic Places)"

- **Providence Spring (the one mercy):** During heavy rains in **mid-August 1864** (commonly Aug. 14), a fresh-water spring burst from the hillside inside the stockade, above the deadline near the north gate, giving clean water. Prisoners called it providential — many believed lightning had opened it; NPS rangers explain it as a natural spring whose outlet had been buried when the stockade trench was dug and which the rains re-opened. A memorial spring house was built over it in 1901 by the Woman's Relief Corps. **John Ransom's diary, plainly:** *"A nice spring of cold water has broken out in camp."*
  — Source: Gettysburg Compiler, "Andersonville's Providence Spring"; HMDB Providence Spring marker; M.B. Henry, "Providence Spring"

- **Causes of death:** scurvy (vitamin-C deficiency from a corn-meal-and-beans diet), dysentery/diarrhea (the fouled water), starvation, exposure (no barracks — men lived in "shebangs," holes and scrap-blanket tents), and contagious disease. Later analysis flags **hookworm** as a major unrecognized contributor. **No germ theory existed yet** — sanitation failures weren't understood as lethal until far too late.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Andersonville NHS"; Statista, "Union deaths in Andersonville POW Camp 1864"

- **The Andersonville Raiders (internal horror beat):** A gang of Union prisoners ("Raiders") robbed and murdered fellow prisoners for food and valuables. Other prisoners organized as the "Regulators," tried them, and on **July 11, 1864**, hanged **six** Raider ringleaders (Sarsfield, Collins, Curtis, Delaney, Munn, Sullivan) on a gallows built that day, with Wirz's permission. A vivid, true detail that the suffering was not only inflicted from outside.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Andersonville Raiders"; ExecutedToday, "1864: The Andersonville Raiders"

## BEAT 3: Henry Wirz — the commandant, the trial, the only execution

- **Identity:** Hartmann Heinrich ("Henry") Wirz, born **November 25, 1823, Zurich, Switzerland.** A Swiss immigrant; had a wounded right arm. Commanded the **interior of the stockade** at Andersonville from **late March/April 1864** to the war's end. (Brig. Gen. John H. Winder ran the broader prison system and died in February 1865, leaving Wirz the surviving face of it.)
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Henry Wirz," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wirz

- **The trial:** A **military commission, Washington, D.C., August 23 – October 18, 1865**, presided over by **Maj. Gen. Lew Wallace** (later author of *Ben-Hur*). Charges: (1) **conspiracy** to injure the health and destroy the lives of Union prisoners, and (2) **13 specifications of personal murder/cruelty** (shooting prisoners, the stocks, ordering guards to fire, setting dogs). **160 witnesses** testified.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Henry Wirz"; Famous Trials (UMKC), "The Andersonville (Henry Wirz) Trial (1865)"

- **Verdict:** Guilty of conspiracy and **10 of the 13 specifications.** Sentenced to death.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Henry Wirz"

- **Execution:** Hanged **November 10, 1865, ~10:32 a.m., in the courtyard of the Old Capitol Prison, Washington, D.C.**, before ~200 spectators and soldiers who chanted "Wirz — remember Andersonville." His neck did not break; he strangled. **He was the only Confederate officer executed for war crimes after the war** (two Confederate *guerrillas*, Champ Ferguson and Henry C. Magruder, were also executed — Wirz was the highest-ranking, and the only one tried specifically for the treatment of prisoners).
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Henry Wirz"; History.com, "Henry Wirz hanged"; NPS Andersonville, "Wirz tribunal" myth page

- **Last words (CONTESTED — give both):** Widely quoted scaffold line to the officer in charge: *"I know what orders are, Major. I am being hanged for obeying them."* A competing account gives: *"I have nothing to say... only that I am innocent, and will die like a man."* Treat the first as the famous/popular version, flagged as not definitively documented.
  — Source: Emerging Civil War, "The 150th Anniversary of the Execution of Henry Wirz"; UMKC Famous Trials

- **The scapegoat controversy (BALANCE — required):** Historians have long debated whether Wirz received a fair trial and whether he was a **scapegoat** for a systemic Confederate failure (collapsing supply, a system designed by Winder, conditions partly beyond one captain's control) — versus a man who personally chose cruelty he could have eased. **Author MUST hold both:** the trial had real procedural problems (a hostile post-war climate, a conspiracy charge that implicated dead/absent men) AND the conditions and specific acts were real. Do not adopt the Lost Cause "innocent martyr" frame; do not pretend the trial was clean.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Henry Wirz"; UMKC Famous Trials, "The Andersonville Trial"

## BEAT 4: The Northern camps (NOT one-sided — the North had resources and still let men die)

**Framing for the author:** This is the section's fairness spine. The North won, wrote the histories, and tried Wirz — but its own camps killed men by the thousands, sometimes through *deliberate* retaliation. Andersonville is the worst; it is not the whole story.

- **Elmira, NY — "Hellmira":** Opened **July 6, 1864** (on the former Camp Rathbun); closed July 1865. Designed for ~4,000; held **>12,100 within a month.** **~2,970 of ~12,100 prisoners died — a ~24.5% death rate, essentially Andersonville-class.** Killers: **Foster's Pond**, a stagnant pool used as both water and latrine; **no winter shelter** (2,000+ men still in tents in late November as temperatures hit **−18°F**); smallpox; and **deliberately reduced rations** ordered by Union Commissary-General of Prisoners **William Hoffman** in retaliation. Sexton **John W. Jones**, a formerly enslaved man, buried and meticulously recorded the dead — only 7 of ~3,000 graves are unidentified. A civilian observation tower charged 15 cents to gawk at the prisoners.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Elmira Prison"; American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Prison Camps"

- **Point Lookout, MD:** The **largest** Union camp (opened Aug. 1, 1863, tip of southern Maryland). Designed for ~10,000; routinely held **12,600–20,000**; **~52,000 total passed through; ~3,000–4,000 died.** Exposure (a wind-blasted tidewater spit, tents only), bad water, scurvy.
  — Source: MyCivilWar, "Point Lookout"; National Museum of Civil War Medicine, "Point Lookout"

- **Camp Douglas, Chicago, IL:** "The North's Andersonville." Held ~**18,000+**; **~4,200 deaths (~1 in 7).** Brutal Chicago winters, smallpox, exposure, punitive discipline.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Prison Camps"

- **Rock Island, IL:** ~**12,400 held over 20 months; ~1,964 prisoners (+171 guards) died (~16%)**, mostly to a smallpox epidemic in the camp's first winter (1863–64).
  — Source: MyCivilWar, "Rock Island Prisoner of War Camp"

- **Johnson's Island, OH** (Lake Erie, near Sandusky): an **officers'** camp, comparatively well-run; **>15,000 passed through, ~200 died** — the low-mortality counterexample that proves resources + barracks + restraint could keep men alive.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Johnson's Island"

- **The retaliation policy (KEY moral beat):** After Northern newspapers and returning skeletal prisoners exposed Andersonville (mid-1864), the U.S. War Department under Secretary **Edwin Stanton** and Commissary-General **Hoffman** **cut rations to Confederate prisoners in retaliation** — explicitly punishing captured men for what the Confederacy did to Union men. This is the hardest fact for the triumphalist version: Northern deaths at Elmira et al. were partly a *chosen* policy, not just scarcity. The North had food and blankets and withheld them.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Elmira Prison" (Hoffman retaliatory rations); American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Prison Camps"

## BEAT 5: Confederate camps beyond Andersonville

- **Libby Prison (Richmond, VA):** A converted tobacco/ship-chandlery warehouse holding **Union OFFICERS** from 1862. Notorious for overcrowding, vermin, and a famous **February 1864 tunnel escape** (109 officers out, 59 got away). High disease/malnutrition mortality though far smaller than Andersonville.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Libby Prison"; Encyclopedia Virginia, "Libby Prison"; NPS Richmond, "Prisoners in Richmond"

- **Belle Isle (Richmond, VA):** An island in the James River for Union **enlisted men** (privates/NCOs — the rank counterpart to Libby's officers). **~20,000 held over ~18 months; nearly 1,000 died.** Mostly a tent/open-ground camp; men evacuated south to Andersonville/Salisbury/Danville starting Feb. 1864 to relieve Richmond. Emaciated Belle Isle survivors produced some of the war's most shocking "starvation" photographs.
  — Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, "Belle Isle Prison"; The Valentine Museum, "Belle Isle Prison"; NPS Richmond

- **Salisbury, NC:** Opened 1861, designed for ~2,500. As overcrowding hit in late 1864 it held **8,740 (Nov. 6, 1864), rising toward 10,000.** **Death rate spiked from ~2% to ~28%; ~3,500–4,000 died**, buried in **eighteen 240-ft trench graves** in a former cornfield. The second-worst Confederate camp after Andersonville.
  — Source: NCpedia, "Confederate Prison (Salisbury)"; Wikipedia, "Salisbury National Cemetery"

- **Cahaba (Castle Morgan), AL:** The **low-mortality Confederate counterexample** — ~5,000 held, only ~147 died (**<3%**), credited to a relatively humane commandant. Bitter coda: hundreds of paroled Cahaba (and Andersonville) survivors died weeks later when the steamboat **Sultana** exploded on the Mississippi, **April 27, 1865** (~1,700–1,800 dead, the worst maritime disaster in U.S. history) — men who survived the camps and died going home. (Sultana = brief tail beat only.)
  — Source: Encyclopedia of Alabama, "Cahaba Federal Prison"; Wikipedia, "Cahaba Prison"

- **Florence Stockade, SC:** An Andersonville overflow camp (late 1864); ~15,000–18,000 held, roughly **1-in-3 died** — Andersonville-class mortality, far less remembered.
  — Source: American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Prison Camps"

## BEAT 6: Why the camps killed — the mechanism (not just the outcome)

For the author — explain the *machine*, per the writing rules:

1. **No germ theory.** Neither side understood that fouled water and crowded filth *transmitted* disease. Sanitation was a matter of smell and decency, not understood as life-or-death. Dysentery, typhoid, and diarrhea — not bullets — were the great killers.
  — Source: Wikipedia, "Andersonville NHS"; NPS, "Teaching with Historic Places: Andersonville"
2. **The exchange collapse → overcrowding.** With exchanges frozen (Beat 1), populations meant to flow through in days stagnated into tens of thousands. Camps built for thousands held many times that. **This is the through-line: the policy in Beat 1 IS the cause of the bodies in Beats 2–5.**
3. **Exposure.** Andersonville, Belle Isle, Point Lookout, and Elmira all left men in the open or in tents — no barracks — through summer heat or sub-zero winter.
4. **Starvation + scurvy.** A monotonous, vitamin-poor diet (cornmeal, beans) caused scurvy even when calories existed; when calories *didn't*, men starved.
5. **Contaminated water** (Stockade Branch; Foster's Pond) — the same creek for drinking and sewage.
6. **Southern supply collapse (CONTEXT, not exoneration).** Late-war Confederate logistics genuinely failed — the South often could not feed its *own* army, let alone prisoners. This is real context for Southern camps. It does **not** explain Andersonville's specific cruelties (the deadline, refusing to use available timber for barracks, the fouled-water siting), and it does **not** apply to the North at all, which had abundant resources and chose retaliation. **Author: deploy as context, refuse it as a full excuse.**
  — Source: Wikipedia, "American Civil War prison camps"; American Battlefield Trust

## BEAT 7: The reckoning — Clara Barton, the graves, and the memory war (consequence)

### IMPORTANT CORRECTION FOR THE AUTHOR
The brief's name "**Dorando Clarke**" is an error. The Andersonville clerk who copied the death register is **DORENCE ATWATER** (1845–1910), a Union prisoner paroled to hospital clerk duty who secretly kept his own copy of the death rolls. **Use "Dorence Atwater."**

- **Dorence Atwater's list:** As a prisoner-clerk recording Andersonville deaths, Atwater secretly copied the death register, fearing the Confederacy would hide the toll. After the war he brought the list to the government and to **Clara Barton.**
  — Source: Clara Barton Museum, "The Fate of Your Loved Ones – Dorence Atwater's List"; National Museum of Civil War Medicine, "The Incredible Dorence Atwater Story"

- **The July–August 1865 expedition:** Clara Barton (already running a Missing Soldiers Office to answer families' letters) got War Secretary **Edwin Stanton's** approval to join the Army expedition to Andersonville. With Atwater's list and the captured hospital records, the team **marked 12,461 graves with the soldier's name, rank, unit, and date of death; only 451 were left "unknown."** Barton was given the honor of **raising the U.S. flag** at the dedication of **Andersonville National Cemetery** (August 17, 1865). It is one of the most completely *identified* mass-death sites of the 19th century — because one prisoner refused to let the names be lost.
  — Source: NPS, "Clara Barton and Andersonville," https://www.nps.gov/articles/cb_and_andersonville.htm ; Clara Barton Museum
- By the time Barton's Missing Soldiers Office closed in **1867**, she and her staff had answered ~63,000 letters and identified **>20,000 missing men**, including the ~13,000 Andersonville dead.
  — Source: NPS, "Clara Barton Missing Soldiers Office Museum"

### The Lost Cause blame fight (Reconstruction-era memory)
- **The North's frame:** Andersonville = deliberate Confederate atrocity; Wirz's execution and the immaculate national cemetery were the proof, monuments to Confederate guilt.
- **The Lost Cause counter-frame:** Southern apologists recast Wirz as a martyr and shifted blame to the **North** — arguing it was Grant's refusal to exchange that starved the camps, and that the South simply lacked supplies. This is the same argument the NPS labels a myth (Beat 1).
- **The Wirz Monument (1909):** The **United Daughters of the Confederacy** erected a granite obelisk to Wirz in the town of Andersonville, **dedicated May 12, 1909** — to "rescue his name from the stigma attached to it by embittered prejudice." Its panels quote **Grant's exchange letter** to pin the deaths on the Union. Union veterans were enraged; the monument still stands and is still contested. A symbol of how the war over the war's *memory* outlasted the war.
  — Source: New Republic, "Why Does This Georgia Town Honor One of America's Worst War Criminals?"; John Banks' Civil War Blog, "1909 dedication of monument to Henry Wirz"; HMDB, "Wirz Monument"

## CONTESTED / FLAGGED

1. **Total POWs held — 410,000 vs 347,000 vs 400,000.** The Official Records' formal count is ~347,000; the ~400,000–410,000 figures add field-paroled men. **USE "more than 400,000," note the OR's 347,000 as the recorded-in-camp count.** Author should not present a single hard number as gospel.

2. **Total deaths — RANGE.** ~49,000 (conservative scholarly tally, ~14% mortality) to ~56,000 (ABT/standard). **USE "an estimated 50,000–56,000 prisoners died," labeled estimate.**

3. **North-vs-South mortality.** Aggregate Southern-camp mortality (~14–15%) exceeds Northern (~6–8%) — TRUE at the aggregate, but Elmira (~24.5%) and Camp Douglas (~14%) show the worst Union camps rivaled Confederate ones. Do not let the aggregate become "the North's camps were humane." They were not.

4. **The "more than Gettysburg + Antietam combined" line.** Works only comparing Andersonville's ~13,000 *deaths* to battle *killed* (not total casualties). **Safer:** use the ABT line that total prison deaths "exceeded American combat losses in World War I, Korea, and Vietnam combined."

5. **Wirz's last words.** Two competing accounts (Beat 3). Attribute the "obeying orders" line as "reportedly" / popularly quoted; do not present as verified verbatim.

6. **Wirz as "only Confederate executed for war crimes."** Precisely: only Confederate **officer** executed for prisoner war crimes; two Confederate **guerrillas** (Champ Ferguson, Henry Magruder) were also executed. **USE: "the only Confederate official executed for war crimes" or "for the treatment of prisoners" — with that nuance available.**

7. **Providence Spring origin.** Prisoners' lightning belief vs. NPS natural-spring explanation. Tell both; don't assert lightning as fact.

8. **Peak population number.** 31,695 (Aug. 31, 1864) is the precise documented peak; Riddle's captions say "thirty-three thousand." Use ~32,000–33,000 and cite 31,695 as the documented figure.

9. **Brief's "Dorando Clarke" = Dorence Atwater** (corrected, Beat 7). Do not use "Dorando Clarke."

10. **Andersonville total received: 45,000 vs 49,485.** ~45,000 is the round figure; 49,485 is the often-cited register count. Either is defensible; note both.
