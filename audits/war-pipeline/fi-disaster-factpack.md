# Fact Pack: French and Indian War — Phase 2, "Years of Disaster" (1755–1757)

**Chapter scope:** Strategic narrative of Britain's catastrophic early years — Braddock's expedition and defeat, the four-pronged 1755 campaign, Battle of Lake George, the Acadian Expulsion, fall of Fort Oswego, and the Fort William Henry siege and massacre. Sets up the pivot to Pitt.

**Note on side-tags:** Per house rule, Braddock, Montcalm, and Washington take NO side-tag on first mention (marquee names); all other named individuals are tagged British / French / Native Nation on first mention per chapter.

## 1. The 1755 Four-Pronged British Plan

**Source:** Edward Braddock Wikipedia (raw), French and Indian War Wikipedia, multiple secondary searches.

At the Congress of Alexandria (April 14, 1755), Major General Edward Braddock coordinated a four-simultaneous-front offensive against New France:

1. **Ohio Valley prong (Braddock himself):** March on Fort Duquesne at the Forks of the Ohio River (modern Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania).
2. **Niagara prong:** Governor William Shirley (British), Governor of Massachusetts, assigned to capture Fort Niagara on Lake Ontario.
3. **Crown Point prong:** Sir William Johnson (British) assigned to capture Fort Saint-Frédéric at Crown Point, Lake Champlain.
4. **Acadia prong:** Colonel Robert Monckton (British) assigned to capture Fort Beauséjour on the Bay of Fundy, Nova Scotia.

**Outcome:** Only Monckton's Acadia expedition fully succeeded. Braddock's ended in catastrophe at the Monongahela (July 9). Shirley's Niagara push stalled at Oswego (logistical failure, supply shortages, interprovincial rivalry with Johnson; French reinforcement of Fort Frontenac made advance too risky). Johnson's Crown Point prong ended in a qualified victory at Lake George (September 8, 1755) — the French advance was stopped, but Crown Point itself was never taken.

## 2. Braddock's Expedition and the Battle of the Monongahela (July 9, 1755)

**Sources:** Battle of the Monongahela Wikipedia (raw), Braddock Expedition Wikipedia (raw), Edward Braddock Wikipedia (raw), Mount Vernon Encyclopedia (attempted, blocked), web searches on Washington quotes.

### The March

- Expedition departed Fort Cumberland, Maryland, May 29, 1755.
- Route: approximately 110 miles west through the Allegheny Mountains to Fort Duquesne, modern Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania.
- Progress was deliberately slow — sometimes only 2 miles per day — as Braddock prioritized road construction (this road, "Braddock's Road," became a lasting artery of westward expansion).
- To accelerate the final approach, Braddock divided his ~2,100-man force: a "flying column" of ~1,300 men under his direct command; a slower supply column of ~800 under Colonel Thomas Dunbar (British).
- Washington served as a volunteer aide-de-camp; he had suffered from dysentery during the march and was carried in a wagon for much of it.

### The Engagement

- **Date:** July 9, 1755
- **Location:** Near present-day Braddock, Pennsylvania, approximately 10 miles (16 km) east of Pittsburgh.
- Braddock's advance column crossed the Monongahela River at a ford about 10 miles south of Fort Duquesne — unopposed, which seemed a good sign.
- Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Gage (British) led the ~300-man advance guard. Around 1 pm, it encountered the French sortie in the forest.

### The French / Native Force

- **Total French/Native strength:** 891 (sources: Wikipedia Battle of the Monongahela raw)
  - 637 Native American warriors (Ottawa/Odawa, Huron/Wyandot, Ojibwa/Chippewa, Potawatomi, Abenaki, others)
  - 108 troupes de la Marine (French colonial regulars)
  - 146 Canadian militia
- **French commanders:** Captain Daniel-Hyacinthe-Marie Liénard de Beaujeu (French), Captain Jean-Daniel Dumas (French), Captain Charles Michel de Langlade (French-Ojibwa)
- **Notable Native commanders present:** Pontiac (Ottawa) — likely present; Shingas (Lenape) — present at or near the battle
- Beaujeu had been rushing to intercept — the French had only learned of Braddock's advance crossing and sortied in haste.

### Beaujeu's Death / Dumas Takes Command

- Beaujeu was born August 9, 1711, Montreal, New France. Officer in the troupes du détachement de la marine. Commander of Fort Duquesne in 1755 (just assigned; had previously commanded Fort Niagara).
- He was shot dead in the opening minutes — killed by British musket fire from Gage's advance guard as the two forces collided.
- Command passed to Captain Jean-Daniel Dumas (French), who rallied the Native warriors and continued the engagement.
- **Source:** Daniel Liénard de Beaujeu Wikipedia, NPS biography, battlefields.org biography (searches confirmed).

### The Collapse of the British Column

- When Gage's vanguard fell back, it collided with Braddock's main column advancing up the narrow forest road, creating catastrophic disorder.
- British regulars had been trained for European-style linear warfare in open fields. In the dense forest, flankers couldn't spread out, formations became compressed, and the Native warriors fired from concealed positions in the trees.
- French and Native fighters spread along both flanks and poured fire into the stationary British mass from cover they couldn't see.
- Officers on horseback were visible and priority targets — 26 of 86 British officers were killed; 37 more wounded.
- Confused British regulars mistakenly fired on provincial troops in the smoke.
- Washington later wrote of his men: "They behaved with more cowardice than it is possible to conceive." [Source: Braddock Expedition Wikipedia raw — attributed to Washington's private correspondence.]

### Casualties

⚠️ **Note on numbers:** Different sources give slightly varying figures for the French/Native side; treat the Native breakdowns as approximate.

| Side | Killed | Wounded |
|------|--------|---------|
| British/Colonial | 457 | 450+ |
| French/Native | 23–39 killed | 15–57 wounded |

- British: ~1,300 engaged; 457 killed, ~422–450+ wounded — roughly 70% casualties among those engaged.
- 26 of 86 officers killed; 37 wounded (officer casualty rate near 73%).
- French/Native: Wikipedia raw gives 39 killed, 57 wounded. Braddock Expedition raw gives 8 killed and 4 wounded for French, 15 killed and 12 wounded for Native allies (total ~23 killed, ~16 wounded). ⚠️ These figures conflict significantly; the Braddock Expedition article likely reflects French regulars and militia only in one count. The higher figure (39 total killed, 57 wounded) is from Battle of the Monongahela article.

### Braddock's Death

- Braddock was shot through the chest (⚠️ one source says "lung," another "chest") during the battle on July 9.
- He died four days later, July 13, 1755, of his wounds.
- Buried west of Great Meadows, Pennsylvania, in the middle of the road his men had cut — wagons were rolled over the grave to prevent desecration.
- **Last words attributed to him:** "Who would have thought?" and "we shall know better another time." [Source: Edward Braddock Wikipedia raw — attributed as "reportedly."] ⚠️ Mark [ATTRIBUTED — SOURCED BUT UNVERIFIED AS VERBATIM]
- He bequeathed to Washington his ceremonial sash and two pistols — items Washington carried throughout his later military and presidential service.

### Washington's Role and Quotes

- Washington had two horses shot from under him; his coat was pierced by four bullets. He was unharmed.
- He organized and led the rear guard, allowing the shattered force to disengage and retreat in relative order. Without his intervention, the retreat could have become a total rout.
- Presided at Braddock's burial service per Braddock's own request.
- After the retreat, Colonel Thomas Dunbar destroyed 150 wagons and excess supplies and withdrew the remaining force entirely, abandoning the frontier.

**Washington's letter to his brother John Augustine Washington, July 18, 1755:**
> "But by the All-powerful Dispensations of Providence, I have been protected beyond all human probability or expectation; for I had four bullets through my coat, and two horses shot under me, yet escaped unhurt, although death was levelling my companions on every side of me!"
[Source: Multiple secondary sources citing Washington's papers; consistent across sources — treat as verified primary quote.]

**Washington's earlier quote (1754, Battle of Jumonville Glen):**
> "I heard the bullets whistle, and, believe me, there is something charming in the sound."
[Source: Search result citing Washington's 1754 letter — this is from the previous year; ⚠️ Do NOT attribute to the Monongahela battle; it's from his 1754 baptism of fire. Useful color for the chapter but must be correctly dated.]

**Washington's letter to Robert Jackson, August 2, 1755:**
> "It is true, we have been beaten, shamefully beaten, by a handful of men, who only intended to molest and disturb our march... had I not been witness to the fact on that fatal day, I should scarce have given credit to it even now."
[Source: Multiple secondary sources; appears consistent — treat as verified primary quote.]

### Why British Regulars Failed: The Structural Analysis

- **Linear tactics in non-linear terrain:** British Army doctrine of the 1750s — close-order ranks, volley fire, bayonet charge — required open ground to function. The Ohio Valley forest offered none.
- **No adaptation protocol:** British officers had no doctrine for irregular warfare. Provincial fighters like the Virginia companies were more adaptable, but their suggestions were largely dismissed by Braddock.
- **Command visibility:** Officers on horseback in forest combat are high-value targets. The French and Native fighters knew this.
- **Intelligence failure:** Braddock had no reliable scouts ahead of his column at the point of contact. The Iroquois guides had largely withdrawn after Braddock's dismissal of Native concerns (see below).
- **Alienation of potential Native allies:** See Shingas exchange below.

### The Braddock-Shingas Exchange: A Decisive Political Failure

Before the campaign, Shingas (Lenape/Delaware chief) and five other chiefs met Braddock and asked what would happen to Native land once the French were expelled. The exchange, as recorded in colonial sources:

- **Shingas asked** whether Native friends of the English might be "Permitted to Live and Trade Among the English and have Hunting Ground sufficient to Support themselves and Familys."
- **Braddock replied:** "No Savage Should Inherit the Land."
- The chiefs said they would not fight with him. Braddock answered that he "did not need their Help and had No doubt of driveing the French and their Indians away."
[Source: Pennsylvania Historical Society document via explorepahistory.com, cross-confirmed in web search secondary sources — VERIFIED.]

After reporting this to their people, many Delaware and Shawnee joined the French immediately. After Braddock's defeat demonstrated French power, the remaining wavering nations followed. The frontier of Pennsylvania and Virginia was then ravaged by raids Shingas led personally throughout 1755–1758 — he earned the settler epithet "Shingas the Terrible."

## 3. The Battle of Lake George (September 8, 1755)

**Sources:** Battle of Lake George Wikipedia (raw), web search results with primary source details.

### Context

William Johnson (British), Superintendent of Indian Affairs for the Northern Colonies, led the Crown Point prong of Braddock's four-part plan. His objective was Fort Saint-Frédéric at Crown Point on Lake Champlain. He advanced from Albany northward along the Hudson River corridor toward Lake George.

### Commanders

**British/Mohawk side:**
- Sir William Johnson (British) — overall commander, Superintendent of Indian Affairs, known to the Mohawk as "Warraghiggey" ("he who does great things"); spoke Mohawk; in a long-term relationship with Molly Brant (Mohawk), later formalized
- Hendrick Theyanoguin (Mohawk, Bear Clan) — also known as "King Hendrick" or "Hendrick Peters"; approximately 64 years old; paramount war chief, instrumental in maintaining the Mohawk-British alliance; killed in action
- Ephraim Williams (British colonial) — colonel commanding advance column; killed in action
- Phineas Lyman (British colonial) — colonel

**French side:**
- Jean-Armand, Baron Dieskau (French) — commander, field general; wounded and captured
- Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre (French) — killed in action (⚠️ same officer who had met Washington in 1753 at Fort Le Boeuf — confirm identity carefully)

### Forces

- **British/Iroquois:** ~1,720 provincial troops + ~200 Iroquois allies (Mohawk-led)
- **French:** ~200 regular grenadiers + ~600 Canadian militia + ~700 Native allies (Abenaki, Nipissing, Caughnawaga/Catholic Mohawk)

### The Three Phases

**Phase 1 — "The Bloody Morning Scout":**
Johnson sent Hendrick and ~1,000 men (Williams and Hendrick co-commanding) south along a forest road to intercept a reported French movement. Dieskau had set an ambush. The column walked into it.
- Colonel Ephraim Williams (British colonial) was killed early in the ambush.
- Hendrick Theyanoguin (Mohawk) — his horse was shot from under him; he was bayoneted by French-allied Native warriors while trying to rise. Age ~64.
- ⚠️ Some sources say he was simply bayoneted; others say he was stabbed and scalped. The Wikipedia raw says "bayonetted by French forces"; other sources say bayoneted by one of the French Indians. The consistent fact is: killed in the ambush, not in stand-up combat.
- Estimated ~80 Mohawk warriors killed in this phase, along with many colonial soldiers.
- The survivors conducted a fighting retreat to Johnson's fortified camp at the lake's south end.

**Phase 2 — Assault on Johnson's Camp:**
Dieskau led his French grenadiers in a frontal assault against Johnson's entrenchments and artillery. Johnson's cannon loaded with grapeshot devastated the attackers in the open.
- Dieskau pressed forward and was shot through the bladder — seriously wounded and captured near Johnson's artillery position.
- Johnson himself was wounded in the thigh by a musket ball.
- Without Dieskau's leadership, the French assault collapsed.

**Phase 3 — "Bloody Pond":**
New Hampshire and New York provincial forces under Folsom intercepted the retreating French column at their baggage train — a brief, bloody engagement. The site, a pond stained red with blood, gave this phase its name.

### Casualties

⚠️ Sources vary significantly.
- **British/Allied:** Ian Steele's corrected figures: ~223 dead, ~108 wounded. One source reports ~216 killed, 96 wounded.
- **French/Allied:** Steele: 149 dead, 163 wounded, 27 captured. One French letter reports grenadier losses of 21 killed/missing + 30 wounded (Régiment de la Reine) and 5 killed + 21 wounded (Régiment de Languedoc) — "more than a third" of the regular grenadier force. Other estimates put total French losses at 500–800.

### Outcome and Significance

- British/Mohawk victory: Dieskau captured, French advance repulsed.
- Johnson advanced south along Lake George and built **Fort William Henry** to consolidate gains — the fort that would be besieged two years later.
- Had Dieskau succeeded in reaching Fort Edward, he could have "rolled back New York's and New England's defenses to Albany itself." The victory at Lake George saved the Hudson corridor.
- ⚠️ The strategic objective — Crown Point — was NOT taken. Johnson and the provincials did not follow up with a march on Fort Saint-Frédéric.

### Theyanoguin (King Hendrick) — Significance Note

Theyanoguin was perhaps the most significant Native ally Britain had in the northeast. He had traveled to London in 1710 as one of the "Four Mohawk Kings" to meet Queen Anne, cementing Mohawk-British diplomatic ties. He was central to maintaining Six Nations neutrality/alignment in Britain's favor. His death at Lake George was a serious blow to that relationship. The Albany Congress of 1754 — where colonial delegates (including Benjamin Franklin) had debated a plan of colonial union — was partly convened in response to Mohawk concerns about the flagging British alliance.

## 4. The Acadian Expulsion / Le Grand Dérangement (1755–1764)

**Sources:** Expulsion of the Acadians Wikipedia (raw), web searches including Canadian Encyclopedia, New England Historical Society, acadian.org.

### Background

The Acadians were French-speaking Catholic settlers of long standing in the region Britain called Nova Scotia (and they called Acadie/Acadia) — present-day Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, and parts of Maine. Britain had controlled mainland Nova Scotia (formerly French Acadia) since 1713, under the Treaty of Utrecht.

The Acadians had maintained a studied neutrality — refusing full oaths of allegiance to Britain while also avoiding active support of France. In 1730, Britain had formally agreed to allow them this "French neutral" status, protecting their property and religious rights. This arrangement worked during peacetime.

### The Oath Crisis

In July 1755, after the fall of Fort Beauséjour (Monckton's successful prong), British authorities pressed the Acadian population to take an unqualified oath of allegiance to the British Crown.

**Why the Acadians refused:**
1. Swearing to a Protestant monarch contradicted their Catholic faith (including forswearing loyalty to the Pope).
2. An unqualified oath would end their neutral status and make them enemies of France and the Mi'kmaq — their neighbors and longtime allies.
3. They had a prior agreement (1730) guaranteeing neutral status and demanded it be honored.
4. They feared conscription into a British war against France.

### The Expulsion Order

Lieutenant Governor Charles Lawrence (British) of Nova Scotia, together with the Nova Scotia Council, issued the deportation order on **July 28, 1755**, without distinguishing between Acadians who had been genuinely neutral and those who had actively supported France. Lawrence framed the decision as a military necessity — a disloyal or potentially disloyal population in a war zone was too dangerous to leave in place.

**The deportation campaigns began August 11, 1755.** British soldiers rounded up Acadian men, then their families, loaded them onto ships, and transported them to destinations across the Atlantic world.

### Scale and Human Cost

Of approximately **14,100 Acadians**, roughly **11,500 were deported** (expelled).

**Estimated deaths:**
- Approximately **5,000 Acadians died** from disease, starvation, or shipwrecks during the deportation process and in the years following.
- Of ~3,100 Acadians deported after the fall of Louisbourg in 1758, an estimated **1,649 died by drowning or disease — a 53% fatality rate** for that wave alone.
- Some 2,600 Acadians evaded capture and remained in Nova Scotia by 1764.

⚠️ **Total numbers are contested** — estimates of total displaced range from 10,000 to 18,000 depending on how subsequent waves and refugees are counted. The 11,500 expelled figure from Wikipedia (raw) is the most specific scholarly estimate but represents only the deportees, not refugees or those who fled voluntarily.

### Destinations of Deportation (First Wave, 1755–1758)

| Colony | Number |
|--------|--------|
| Massachusetts | ~2,000 |
| Virginia | ~1,100 |
| Maryland | ~1,000 |
| Connecticut | ~700 |
| Pennsylvania, Carolinas, Georgia, New York | smaller numbers |

Second wave (post-1758): ~3,500 sent to France; ~866 to Britain (as prisoners).

### The Cajun Diaspora

Acadians deported to France subsequently emigrated to **Spanish Louisiana**, settling along the Mississippi River and Atchafalaya Basin (modern-day south-central Louisiana). The French term "Acadien" evolved into "Cadien," which was anglicized as **"Cajun."** Cajun language, music, and food culture are direct descendants of the Acadian diaspora. [Source: Expulsion of the Acadians Wikipedia raw.]

### Le Grand Dérangement as War Crime Debate

⚠️ The moral and legal status of the Acadian Expulsion is a live scholarly and political question. Contemporary French officer Lotbinière wrote in November 1756 that Britain had strategically removed French families to weaken supply lines — framing it as a military-strategic action. Modern Canadian and Acadian scholars have called it ethnic cleansing. In 2003, the Canadian government issued a Royal Proclamation acknowledging the deportation and expressing regret — stopping short of a formal apology. The word "genocide" is used by some Acadian advocacy organizations; most mainstream historians use "ethnic cleansing" or "forced displacement." The author should handle this with full moral weight without overstating the historical consensus.

## 5. Fort Oswego (August 10–14, 1756)

**Sources:** Battle of Fort Oswego Wikipedia (raw), web searches.

### Context

By 1756 the war had officially widened: Britain declared war on France on **May 17, 1756**, nearly two years after fighting began in North America. (The formal trigger in Europe was France's attack on British-held Minorca in the Mediterranean, combined with the "Diplomatic Revolution" — Prussia's alliance with Britain, which pushed France into alliance with Austria, inverting the traditional European lineup.)

In North America, the new French commander Major General Louis-Joseph de Montcalm-Gozon, Marquis de Montcalm arrived in New France in 1756, sent by Louis XV to lead the colony's defense. He immediately went on offense.

### The Battle

- **Dates:** August 10–14, 1756
- **Location:** Fort Oswego, southeastern shore of Lake Ontario (modern Oswego, New York)
- Fort Oswego was Britain's only base on Lake Ontario — its strategic gateway to French positions at Fort Frontenac (Kingston, Ontario) and the western Great Lakes.

**French force:** ~3,000 (regulars, troupes de la Marine, Canadian militia, Indigenous allies)
- Commander: Louis-Joseph de Montcalm (French)

**British garrison:** ~1,100 fit for duty (nominally ~2,000; the rest sick or non-combatant). Three interconnected forts: Fort Ontario (~370 men), Fort George (~150 New Jersey militia), and the main Fort Oswego.

**British commander:** Colonel James Mercer (British) — killed in action on August 13 when a cannon ball decapitated him while he was inspecting Fort Oswego's walls. Command devolved to Lieutenant Colonel John Littlehales (British), who surrendered.

**Casualties:**
- French: ~30 dead or wounded
- British: 80–150 killed or wounded; **~1,700 captured** (including noncombatants, laborers, shipbuilders, women, and children with the garrison)

**Surrender terms:** Montcalm refused to grant the defeated army the full honours of war. Some British soldiers trying to escape during the capitulation were "tomahawked and killed" amid Native plundering.

**Captured materiel:** 121 British cannons; boats and supplies.

**French action after capture:** Destroyed all three forts and the captured boats/supplies before withdrawing. Returned to Quebec in triumph with 1,700 prisoners.

### Strategic Consequence

The fall of Fort Oswego gave France complete control of Lake Ontario. Britain had no presence on the lake; the route from Fort Frontenac to the Ohio Valley was secure. It also dramatically impressed neutral or wavering Native nations — the Oneida and Seneca, who had been leaning British, switched their allegiance toward France after seeing Oswego fall. Montcalm's victory was the high-water mark of French strategic momentum.

## 6. The Siege and "Massacre" of Fort William Henry (August 3–9, 1757)

**Sources:** Siege of Fort William Henry Wikipedia (raw), warfare history network article, Penn Museum expedition article, web searches including Historynet, ABT.

### Background

Fort William Henry had been built by Johnson after the Battle of Lake George (1755), at the southern tip of Lake George (in the Province of New York). It sat at the northern end of the strategic corridor to Albany. The French held Fort Carillon (Ticonderoga) at the foot of Lake Champlain, ~15 miles to the north.

In August 1757, Montcalm assembled the largest force ever fielded in North American warfare to that point and moved south to destroy Fort William Henry.

### The Siege

- **Dates:** August 3–9, 1757
- **Location:** Fort William Henry, south end of Lake George, Province of New York

**French force:** 6,200 regulars and militia + ~1,800 Native American warriors from approximately 33 nations
- Commander: Louis-Joseph de Montcalm (French)
- Other French commanders: François-Charles de Bourlamaque (French), Charles Michel de Langlade (French-Ojibwa)
- Native nations present: Odawa (Ottawa), Abenaki, Potawatomi, Nipissing, Ojibwa (Chippewa), Winnebago, Sauk and Fox, Iowa, Menominee, Caughnawaga (Catholic Mohawk), others — a coalition from across the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence, many of whom had traveled hundreds of miles for this campaign

**British garrison:** ~2,500 total
- Fort garrison: ~200 regular British soldiers + ~800 colonial militia (many ill with smallpox and other diseases)
- At Fort Edward, 17 miles south: ~1,500 militiamen + ~150 Mohawk under William Johnson — **who were never sent to help**
- Commander: Lieutenant Colonel George Monro (British), born c. 1700, Clonfin, County Longford, Ireland; career officer of the 35th Foot; the regiment arrived in Nova Scotia in 1756 and was deployed to the New York frontier

**French siege operations:**
- August 5: French opened approach trenches to the northwest of the fort; artillery bombardment began from ~2,000 yards
- August 7: Fort walls breached, most guns disabled
- Monro sent repeated urgent requests to General Daniel Webb (British), commanding officer at Fort Edward, for reinforcements

**General Daniel Webb's refusal:**
Webb had been deceived by a French prisoner into believing the French force numbered 11,000–12,000 men. He refused to send reinforcements and sent Monro a message advising him to "negotiate the best terms left in your power." ⚠️ This message was intercepted by the French and handed to Montcalm before it reached Monro. Webb was subsequently recalled from command. Sir William Johnson later wrote that Webb was "the only Englishman [I] ever knew who was a coward." [Source: web searches, warfare history network.]

**Surrender — August 9:**
Monro surrendered after six days of bombardment. Montcalm had warned him in writing beforehand: "perhaps there would not be time, more would it be in [his] power to restrain the cruelties of a mob of Indians." [Source: ABT Battlefield Trust article.] Despite the warning, Montcalm granted generous terms.

**Surrender terms:** The garrison could withdraw to Fort Edward with full honours of war — keeping colours, muskets, and one symbolic cannon (but no powder). They were paroled: not to fight against France for 18 months. French were to release British prisoners within three months.

### The "Massacre" — August 9

**What happened:** On the morning of August 9, before and during the withdrawal, warriors from the allied Native nations attacked the British garrison and camp followers.

The Native warriors — who had traveled hundreds of miles expecting prisoners (for ransom, adoption, or ritual) and scalps as war honours — felt betrayed by the surrender terms, which denied them the material spoils of war without compensation or alternative arrangement. Their chiefs held limited coercive authority over individual warriors' actions in this context.

Warriors killed and scalped wounded British prisoners, seized women and children, attacked the withdrawing column, and forced chaos.

**Montcalm's response:** He personally intervened, together with other French officers, recovering ~500 captives from Native custody — an act described as requiring physical courage. He could not prevent the initial outbreak but worked actively to limit it.

**Casualty debate — the central contested fact:**

| Estimate | Source | Notes |
|----------|--------|-------|
| ~1,500 | Contemporary British accounts | Widely inflated for propaganda |
| ~185 | Warfare History Network article; Penn Museum | Described as "probably greater" |
| 69–184 | Ian Steele, *Betrayals* (detailed reconstruction) | Steele: "at least 69, could not possibly have exceeded 184" — i.e., 2.8%–7.5% of surrendered forces |
| 50–174 | Other scholarly range | Consistent with Steele |
| ~200+ | Contemporary historians, higher estimate | Some place toll "over 200" |

⚠️ **The Steele range (69–184) is the most carefully sourced modern scholarly estimate.** Ian Steele's *Betrayals: Fort William Henry and the "Massacre"* (1990) is the standard academic reference. The consensus is: French sources undercounted (propaganda of minimization), British sources vastly overcounted (propaganda of outrage). The real toll was a genuine atrocity but far below the "1,500" figure popularized by Francis Parkman and James Fenimore Cooper. Approximately 200 additional captives were taken by departing Native warriors; by year's end ~300 remained missing, with at least 40 refusing repatriation (likely adopted into Native communities).

**Who killed whom:** The perpetrators were primarily warriors from the Great Lakes nations — Potawatomi, Ottawa, Menominee, others — not the Abenaki or Caughnawaga (who had longer relationships with the French and were more constrained). ⚠️ This distinction matters for Native agency analysis; the sources examined did not uniformly specify, so use carefully.

**Scholarly interpretation:**
- **Francis Parkman (19th c.):** Defended Montcalm as powerless; blamed Native "savagery." This framing is now considered Eurocentric and inadequate.
- **Francis Jennings:** Argued Montcalm "deliberately ignored" the impending violence as a strategic choice.
- **Ian Steele:** Argues French promises of war prizes, combined with surrender terms that denied those prizes, bred a specific resentment; Montcalm failed to arrange an alternative compensation system for his allies. The violence was not random — it followed a rational (if horrifying) logic of unmet expectations.
- **Recent scholarship (general):** Emphasizes Native agency — these warriors were not passive tools of French strategy but independent actors with their own objectives, who felt their agreements had been broken.

### The Last of the Mohicans Myth

James Fenimore Cooper's novel *The Last of the Mohicans* (1826) dramatized the Fort William Henry events — but with significant inaccuracies, inflating casualty estimates and presenting the violence through a lens of 19th-century racial hierarchy. Subsequent popularizers (Benson Lossing, Francis Parkman) amplified these inflated numbers. The "1,500" figure in popular culture traces almost entirely to this literary/historiographic chain rather than primary evidence. [Source: Siege of Fort William Henry Wikipedia raw.]

### The Smallpox Aftermath

Warriors who had exhumed bodies from the fort's military cemetery to collect scalps unknowingly carried smallpox back along the trails to the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence regions. A massive smallpox epidemic followed, devastating multiple nations — the Potawatomi population was nearly decimated. [Source: warfare history network article, Penn Museum expedition article.] This was one of the deadliest unintended consequences of the war for Native peoples — the side that "won" at Fort William Henry paid an enormous biological price.

### George Monro's Fate

Monro survived the massacre and the march to Albany. He died suddenly in Albany on November 3, 1757 — of apoplexy (stroke). Some historians suggest his death was brought on by rage at Webb's failure to reinforce him, and the trauma of watching the massacre of his men. [Source: web searches, George Monro Wikipedia.]

## 7. Britain Reeling: Why These Years Went So Badly

**Sources:** French and Indian War Wikipedia, Seven Years War Wikipedia, search results.

### The Structural Problems (1755–1757)

1. **No theater commander after Braddock died:** Command devolved to Governor William Shirley (British), who proved unable to coordinate the multiple colonial and regular British forces. His interprovincial rivalries with Johnson undermined the Niagara expedition.

2. **French military excellence in the theater:** Montcalm arrived in 1756 as a professional, experienced European general with trained regular troops. He immediately went on offense — Oswego (1756), Fort William Henry (1757) — while Britain struggled to mount any coherent counteroffensive.

3. **The "Diplomatic Revolution" and the Seven Years War formalization (1756):** Britain's alliance with Prussia in the Convention of Westminster (January 16, 1756) triggered France's alliance with Austria (First Treaty of Versailles, May 1, 1756). Britain formally declared war on France on **May 17, 1756**. The war was now global — Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India.

4. **Newcastle ministry failures:** The Duke of Newcastle, as Prime Minister, had believed diplomacy could prevent European conflict. The loss of Minorca (June 1756 — French captured it; Admiral John Byng failed to relieve it and was later court-martialed and executed, becoming a byword for British humiliation), combined with Oswego and Fort William Henry, destroyed public confidence in Newcastle's government.

5. **Native coalition firmly with France:** After Braddock's defeat (1755) demonstrated British vulnerability, the Ohio Valley nations (Delaware, Shawnee) — already alienated by Braddock's "No Savage Shall Inherit the Land" — joined the French alliance, unleashing devastating raids on the Pennsylvania and Virginia frontiers. The fall of Oswego (1756) further pulled the Oneida and Seneca (two Iroquois Confederacy nations that had been leaning British) toward France.

### The Pitt Solution (June 1757)

After Newcastle's government fell, William Pitt (British) emerged as the dominant political figure. Pitt declared when taking office (1756/1757): "My Lord, I am sure I can save this country, and no one else can." [Source: William Pitt Wikipedia raw — ⚠️ sourced but mark as ATTRIBUTED.]

By June 1757, Pitt formed a coalition with Newcastle — Newcastle handled parliamentary management while Pitt directed military and colonial strategy. This partnership lasted until 1761 and transformed the war's trajectory. Pitt's approach:
- Subsidized Prussia heavily to tie down French forces in Europe ("winning Canada on the banks of the Rhine")
- Launched simultaneous aggressive campaigns in North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, and India
- Appointed new, capable commanders (Amherst, Wolfe, Howe) replacing the failed ones
- Committed resources on a scale Britain had never previously attempted

The victories that would follow (1758–1760) were the product of this Pitt-directed strategy — but they were not yet visible at the end of 1757.

## 8. Native Nations as First-Class Actors

**Sources:** Multiple Wikipedia articles, web searches, Franco-Indian Alliance Wikipedia.

### Why Great Lakes / Western Nations Sided with France

1. **The fur trade:** The French empire in North America was built on the fur trade, which required Native partnerships. French traders "did business with local Indian tribes, and often married Indian women," creating deep commercial and kinship ties. The western nations — Wyandot/Huron, Odawa/Ottawa, Ojibwa/Chippewa, Potawatomi, Menominee, Winnebago, Mississauga, Abenaki, Illinois, Sioux — were France's primary trading partners and suppliers.

2. **Cultural respect and integration:** French officials, traders, and officers adopted Native customs, participated in councils, intermarried, and produced the Métis people. The 1627 French Ordonnance granted converted Native Catholics legal status as "natural Frenchmen." French commanders wore Native attire in councils. This was not universal or idealistic — it was strategic — but it worked where British transactionalism did not.

3. **Land security:** British settlement threatened displacement. French Canada's population was small (~75,000 in 1755 vs. ~1.5 million British colonists), and French farms did not push westward into Native hunting territory the way British settlement did. Allying with France preserved the status quo that Native peoples depended on.

4. **The war's objectives:** For the Great Lakes nations, the war was about maintaining their position as middle-men in the fur trade and preventing British settlers from crossing the Appalachians into the Ohio Valley and Great Lakes. France's objectives aligned with theirs. Britain's did not.

### Why the Iroquois (Mostly) Sided with Britain

The Six Nations of the Iroquois Confederacy (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, Tuscarora) occupied the critical corridor between the British colonies and New France. Their position was genuinely ambivalent and never monolithic.

- **Sir William Johnson** was the key factor. His decade-long residence in the Mohawk Valley, adoption of Mohawk customs, fluency in Mohawk, and later marriage to Molly Brant (Mohawk) gave him unique access and credibility. The Mohawk trusted him personally in ways they trusted no other British official.
- **Land security promises:** Britain promised the Iroquois their territory would be protected from settler encroachment — promises that would prove hollow after 1763 (Proclamation Line) and catastrophically broken thereafter.
- **Trade access:** British trade goods were often cheaper and more abundant than French equivalents.
- **The Mohawk-French split:** The Catholic Caughnawaga (Mohawk who had converted and moved to mission villages near Montreal) fought for France; the traditional Mohawk of the Mohawk Valley fought for Britain. This literal division within the Mohawk nation reflects the depth of colonial splitting of Indigenous communities.

### What Happened After Oswego (Iroquois Shift)

After Fort Oswego fell in 1756, the Oneida and Seneca (who had been leaning British) shifted toward the French alliance. This was a serious blow. The Iroquois "swing" between 1755 and 1758 illustrates that their alliance was always conditional — earned and lost based on military performance, treaty honoring, and the personal relationships of men like Johnson and Hendrick.

## 10. Contested / Uncertain Items

1. ⚠️ **Braddock French/Native casualties at Monongahela:** Two Wikipedia articles give conflicting numbers. Battle of the Monongahela article: 39 killed, 57 wounded (total). Braddock Expedition article: 8 French killed, 4 wounded; 15 Native killed, 12 wounded (total ~23 killed, ~16 wounded). The discrepancy likely reflects different accounting of who is included. Use the higher figures with a note that exact counts are uncertain.

2. ⚠️ **Theyanoguin's death method:** Most consistent account = bayoneted by French-allied Native warriors after his horse was shot from under him. Some variation in sources. Do not embellish beyond: "his horse was shot from under him; he was bayoneted as he fell."

3. ⚠️ **Battle of Lake George casualties:** Sources range widely. Steele's corrected figures (British: ~223 killed, ~108 wounded; French: 149 killed, 163 wounded, 27 captured) are the best scholarly reconstruction. The "500–800" French total in some sources appears to be an older, uncorrected estimate.

4. ⚠️ **Acadian total numbers:** 11,500 deported (Wikipedia raw), vs. 10,000–18,000 total displaced (wider scholarly range). The 5,000 deaths figure is itself approximate. The 53% death rate for the post-1758 wave (3,100 deported → 1,649 dead) is the most precisely sourced figure.

5. ⚠️ **Fort William Henry "massacre" toll:** The most defensible range is Ian Steele's 69–184 killed (2.8–7.5% of surrendered forces). Some sources say ~185 or "approximately 200." The "1,500" figure is a myth traceable to Cooper/Parkman. The ~200 additional captives taken by departing warriors are a separate category.

6. ⚠️ **Braddock's last words** ("Who would have thought?" / "we shall know better another time"): Attributed in Wikipedia; marked as "reportedly." Use as color with clear attribution hedge.

7. ⚠️ **Pitt's "I am sure I can save this country" quote:** Sourced in Wikipedia but the date context is slightly ambiguous (1756 vs. 1757). Mark as attributed.

8. ⚠️ **Washington's "charming sound" quote** (1754): This is from Jumonville Glen 1754, NOT from Monongahela 1755. Do not misplace it chronologically in the narrative.

9. ⚠️ **Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre identity at Lake George:** The same name appears as the French officer who met Washington at Fort Le Boeuf in 1753. If confirmed, this is a resonant detail (the man who first refused Washington's ultimatum, killed at Lake George). Verify carefully — there are multiple Legardeur family members in the French colonial service.

10. ⚠️ **Montcalm's decision not to press toward Fort Edward after Fort William Henry:** Montcalm cited poor roads; Vaudreuil argued he should have advanced. Some historians argue the smallpox in the fort and the dispersal of his Native allies made an advance impossible regardless. The "missed opportunity" narrative oversimplifies a genuinely constrained situation.

### A. Admiral John Byng — court-martial and execution

**Verified facts:**

- **Execution date:** 14 March 1757 — confirmed by Wikipedia (John Byng raw), Royal Museums Greenwich collection record, The Gazette (March in History), History Today.
- **Ship and location:** Shot on the quarterdeck of HMS *Monarch*, anchored in the Solent off Portsmouth Harbour, in the presence of all hands and men in boats from surrounding fleet vessels.
- **Charge:** Breach of the 12th Article of War — failing to "do his utmost" to engage the enemy and relieve the Minorca garrison. The court acquitted him of personal cowardice but found he had kept his fleet at too great a distance, opened fire at ineffective range, and chose to return to Gibraltar rather than proceed immediately to Minorca's relief.
- **Court-martial convened:** 28 December 1756, aboard HMS *St George*, Portsmouth Harbour. Verdict: 27 January 1757. King George II refused clemency despite the court's own mercy recommendation (the 12th Article carried a mandatory death sentence for the guilty finding; the court had unanimously recommended mercy but lacked authority to impose a lesser sentence).
- **Voltaire:** *Candide* (1759), Chapter 23. The exact French: *"Dans ce pays-ci, il est bon de tuer de temps en temps un amiral pour encourager les autres."* Standard English rendering: "In this country it is good to kill an admiral from time to time, to encourage the others." [Confirmed via Wikipedia John Byng article; the phrase is attributable to Voltaire in *Candide* — it is not a direct quote from Voltaire about Byng independently but is presented in the novel's satire of the execution.]

⚠️ **The "pour encourager les autres" phrase** is from a fictional narrator in *Candide*, not a personal letter or public statement by Voltaire. It is universally understood as Voltaire's satirical commentary on the Byng execution and is safe to use as such, with that framing.

**Use in narrative:** The Byng execution (March 14, 1757) falls within this chapter's scope and fits the "Britain reeling / political panic" argument. The Minorca loss (June 1756) triggered his arrest; his execution followed the Fort Oswego humiliation and preceded Fort William Henry — a drumbeat of disaster that destroyed the Newcastle ministry.

### B. Why Johnson did not advance on Crown Point after Lake George (September 1755)

**Verified — sourced to Fred Anderson, *Crucible of War*, Chapter 12 (fetched from erenow.org/ww/crucible-of-war-the-seven-years-war/12.php):**

Multiple converging factors halted the advance, all documented by Anderson:

1. **Johnson's own wound:** He "had taken a musket ball in one buttock and was in no shape to continue."
2. **Troop exhaustion and low morale:** His men were described as "greatly fatigued" and spent three days burying dead in hot weather — described in period accounts as "most meloncoly Peace of busaness."
3. **Disease:** Men "began to sicken in large numbers" in the days after the battle.
4. **Supply shortages:** "Provisions remained short enough to forestall a new offensive."
5. **Loss of Mohawk allies:** The Mohawks "went home to conduct their condolence rituals" — removing the allied fighters Johnson depended on.
6. **French fortifying Ticonderoga:** By late September, scouts "were returning with reports that the French had begun to fortify Ticonderoga" (Fort Carillon) — making an advance strategically inadvisable as a new French blocking position was being established.
7. **Advancing season:** The campaigning season was "fast slipping away."

**Outcome:** At a council of war on September 29, Johnson's officers voted to build Fort William Henry instead, establishing a defensive anchor at the south end of Lake George.

**Cite as:** Fred Anderson, *Crucible of War: The Seven Years' War and the Fate of Empire in British North America, 1754–1766* (Knopf, 2000), ch. 12 (Northern Campaigns 1755).

⚠️ The erenow.org source is a reproduction of the book text. Specific page numbers are not available from the web fetch; the chapter (ch. 12) is confirmed.

### C. Pays d'en haut / Great Lakes nations — strategic reasons for joining the 1757 Fort William Henry campaign

**Verified — sourced to Fred Anderson, *Crucible of War*, Chapter 21 (fetched from erenow.org/ww/crucible-of-war-the-seven-years-war/21.php) and Siege of Fort William Henry Wikipedia (raw):**

**Scale of participation:** Approximately 979 warriors from the pays d'en haut (upper Great Lakes and midwest) joined the campaign, traveling extraordinary distances — some as far as 1,500 miles. Named nations present: Odawa (Ottawa, 300+ from upper Lake Michigan), Ojibwa/Chippewa and Mississauga (~300 from Lake Superior shores), Menominee (~100, lower Michigan), Potawatomi (~100, lower Michigan), Winnebago (~50, Wisconsin), Sauk and Fox (farther west), Miami, Delaware (Ohio Country), and even ~10 Iowa warriors — "a nation never seen in Canada before" (Anderson). This was joined by ~820 Catholic mission Indians from eastern missions (Abenaki, Caughnawaga/Catholic Mohawk, etc.) — total Native coalition ~1,800.

**Why they came — verified motivations:**

1. **War honors, plunder, and captives:** Anderson states explicitly: warriors "had expected were plunder, trophies to prove their prowess in battle, and captives to adopt or sacrifice as replacements for dead warriors or perhaps hold for ransom." This was the primary stated motivation — these nations served without wages; they "asked only for their rations, ammunition, and what few gifts Montcalm had bestowed."
2. **The French recruiting drive and material alliance:** Governor-General Vaudreuil's recruiting campaign was "fueled by stories circulated by Indian participants in the capture of Oswego" — the 1756 victory had demonstrated French military power and generosity (brandy, prisoner ransoms, gifts). The Great Lakes nations' alliance with France was sustained by this ongoing material exchange.
3. **The trade sphere and supply relationship:** The French commercial alliance — detailed in the fact pack's existing Section 8 — underpinned their participation. Maintaining French dominance preserved their role as middlemen in the fur trade and kept supply lines open. Anderson's Ch. 21 focuses on immediate incentives (plunder/captives) rather than re-explaining the structural alliance already documented in Section 8.

⚠️ **Anderson does not use the phrase "defending their Great Lakes trade sphere" specifically in Ch. 21** — that framing is accurate per the broader argument in the book (and per Section 8 of this fact pack) but is synthesis, not a direct quote from that chapter. The plunder/captive/honor motivation and the Oswego-story recruitment dynamic are directly sourced.

**Betrayal dynamic:** When the surrender terms denied warriors the expected plunder (French took war provisions; British kept personal effects; captives were to be repatriated), "the French were conspiring with their enemies, the British, against their friends, the Indians, who were left without any of the promised war trophies" (Anderson, via Wikipedia Steele citation). This is the documented logic behind the massacre.

### D. Mi'kmaq stake in Acadie

**Verified — sourced to Wikipedia (Father Le Loutre's War raw), Canada.ca Parks Canada ("18th Century Mi'kmaw-French Alliance"), Canada.ca DND ("The Imperial Wars"), and secondary web sources:**

**The Mi'kmaq's own stake:**

- **Territory:** Mi'kmaq traditional territory included present-day Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, eastern New Brunswick, and the Gaspé Peninsula — the exact lands Britain was consolidating as "Nova Scotia." They regarded themselves as the original landholders.
- **Formal assertion of ownership:** On September 24, 1749 — within months of the founding of Halifax — Mi'kmaq leaders formally communicated to Governor Cornwallis through Father Maillard, "proclaiming their ownership of the land, and expressing their opposition to the British actions in settling at Halifax." (Wikipedia, Father Le Loutre's War.)
- **Prior and continuous armed resistance:** The Mi'kmaq were already at war with British colonizers before the French and Indian War proper. Father Le Loutre's War (1749–1755), also called the Mi'kmaq War and the Anglo-Mi'kmaq War, was a continuous armed conflict from Britain's founding of Halifax in 1749 through the outbreak of the Seven Years' War — fought by Mi'kmaq warriors using guerrilla tactics, raiding British settlements and supply lines. It was not a sideshow to the main war; it predated it by five years.
- **French alliance:** The Mi'kmaq–French alliance dated to the 17th century and was formalized through diplomatic councils by the 1720s. It was sustained by "religion and trade" — the Mi'kmaq were Catholic converts (through French missionaries) with long commercial ties to French traders and Acadian settlers. The partnership involved joint raids against British settlements from the 1670s onward. (Canada.ca Parks Canada source.)
- **Population context:** At the start of Father Le Loutre's War, approximately 2,500 Mi'kmaq inhabited the region alongside ~12,000 Acadians. The founding of Halifax in 1749 brought 3,229 settlers in the first wave alone — exceeding the total Mi'kmaq regional population almost immediately — which is why Halifax's establishment was experienced as an existential demographic threat.
- **The Acadian connection:** The Mi'kmaq and Acadians were not merely "neighbors" — they shared Catholic faith, intermarried in some communities, and had parallel interests in keeping British Protestant colonialism contained. The Acadian Expulsion (1755) directly removed the Mi'kmaq's most reliable allied settler population. "Without their French allies, the Mi'kmaq were unable to sustain their war against the British" after Louisbourg fell in 1758. (Canada.ca DND source.)

⚠️ **The Mi'kmaq–Acadian relationship as "allied neighbors" is well sourced; the specific extent of intermarriage is noted in sources as occurring in "some communities" but is not comprehensively documented in the sources consulted. Use with appropriate qualifier.**

**One-sentence verified use:** The Mi'kmaq — original landholders of Nova Scotia and longtime French allies, already at war with British colonizers since 1749 — had their own stake in Acadie: the Acadian Expulsion removed their closest allied settler community and accelerated the British consolidation of land the Mi'kmaq had formally proclaimed as their own.
