# Stones River — STORYTELLING critic gate (step 4)

Reviewed: `audits/war-pipeline/stones-river-draft.md` (5 sections).
Voice/standard reference: shipped Shiloh `section-narrative.tsx`.
Judged AS A STORY (the #1 product goal). Selective coverage is GOOD here — coverage is another gate's job. This gate flags hook/stakes, felt human cost, play-by-play tedium (the #1 failure mode), dual POV, voice, pacing.

---

## OVERALL GRADE: GOOD (strong, near-shippable; 0 REWRITE, 2 NEEDS WORK)

The draft is genuinely strong storytelling and reads close to the Shiloh standard. It leads with stakes (Fredericksburg desperation + the Proclamation taking effect mid-battle is a superb spine), it has real human anchors (Garesché decapitated at Rosecrans's side, the Orphan Brigade, the shared "Home! Sweet Home!" serenade), and it almost entirely avoids play-by-play tedium. The two weakest sections — `the-dawn-blow` and `the-new-years-pause` — are weak in opposite ways: the first slips into a roster/troop-movement recitation in places, the second is structurally thin (it's a "nothing happened" day and currently reads like one). Neither is broken; both are fixable with targeted moves.

The biggest cross-cutting gap vs. Shiloh: **the human cost is told more than felt.** Shiloh has Johnston's boot quietly filling with blood, Sherman with three horses shot from under him, the men shot down among their cook fires. Stones River names its horrors (Garesché, the Orphans, the Slaughter Pen) but several land as statistics rather than scenes. See SHOULD-IMPROVE.

---

## Section 1 — `the-armies-face-off` — STRONG

**Hook & stakes:** Excellent. "The war did not pause for Christmas" is a clean cold open, and it immediately stacks the real stakes — Fredericksburg, the North reeling, the Proclamation about to take effect mid-battle. This is exactly the Shiloh move ("the war in the West was going badly for the South, and it was about to get worse").

**The mirror-image-plans framing is the best structural choice in the draft.** "The strange thing about the plan each general made is that it was the same plan... It came down to the clock." That's a hook a reader remembers. Keep it untouched.

**Human dimension:** The "Home! Sweet Home!" serenade is a gift of a closing image — "thousands of soldiers who would be trying to kill each other at first light, singing the same song about home across the cold ground between them." This is the emotional anchor the section needed. Good.

**Voice:** House voice is on. "a devout, fast-talking engineer of a man," "a harsh disciplinarian whose own generals could barely stand him" — character in one line, Shiloh-grade.

**Minor — should-improve, not blocking:**
- Para 2 ("It is worth saying plainly, here at the start...") is the slavery-cause requirement and it's well done, but the meta-framing ("It is worth saying plainly, here at the start") is a touch academic/throat-clearing. Shiloh weaves the same point in without announcing it ("The Confederacy he was trying to split apart was a new country... for one overriding reason"). Consider folding the cause into the narrative motion rather than flagging it as An Important Aside.
- The parenthetical pile-up in para 1 — "(Civil War armies were usually named after rivers, not states)" AND "(48 km)" AND the river/railroad clause — makes the opening sentence work hard. This is faithful to Shiloh's inline-define style so it's fine, but watch the density.

## Section 2 — `the-dawn-blow` — NEEDS WORK

This is the section most at risk of the #1 failure mode. The bones of a great section are here (the jackknife image, Sheridan's stand, Garesché's death), but it's diluted by **roster recitation and "this brigade, that hour" movement-tracking** that the reader can't hold and doesn't feel.

**Play-by-play tedium — flag (the core issue):**
- Para 1: *"Lieutenant General William J. Hardee (South)... led the assault, and the division of Major General John P. McCown (South) spearheaded it — roughly ten thousand men coming on in a great wave, with the division of Major General Patrick Cleburne (South) filling in close behind. The Union soldiers on that flank were the men of Major General Alexander M. McCook (North)."* That's four named generals + two divisions in three sentences, and the reader hasn't been given a reason to care about any of them. Compare Shiloh, which introduces commanders only when they carry a *story* (Sherman "had the day of his life"; Prentiss "got outsized credit mostly because he survived captivity to tell his own version"). RECOMMEND: cut McCown and Cleburne by name (or demote to "ten thousand men under Hardee, the army's best tactician, came howling out of the cedars"); keep McCook because his unanchored flank is the story.
- Para 2: *"driven roughly three miles by mid-morning until it had been shoved all the way back to the Nashville Pike... McCook's broken wing fell back behind the Wilkinson Turnpike. By ten in the morning..."* — this is the "this line fell back to that road by that hour" cadence. The jackknife metaphor is doing the real work; the turnpike/clock bookkeeping around it is play-by-play. Tighten to the image + the outcome, drop the survey-marker geography.

**What's STRONG and must be protected:**
- The jackknife: *"like a jackknife closing — the line bent back on itself."* Vivid, memorable, exactly right. Keep.
- Sheridan's stand: *"all three of Sheridan's brigade commanders were killed, and more than a third of his division went down. But the four hours they bought were the four hours the rest of the army needed."* Strong — cost + meaning fused. Keep.
- Garesché: *"decapitated by a cannonball as the two rode together. Rosecrans, spattered with the blood of his own friend, rode on..."* This is the felt-cost high point of the whole draft. Keep verbatim.

**Human dimension:** Good where it counts (Garesché, Sheridan's bled-out division), but the Slaughter Pen — a place the soldiers named for the horror — is told at a distance ("a holding action in the cedars so brutal the soldiers named the place for it"). The naming is great; give the reader one concrete beat of WHY it earned the name, the way Shiloh gives the Hornet's Nest its sound ("out of the sound the bullets made tearing through the thickets").

**Dual POV:** Good. The Confederate friction (Breckinridge pulled away, Bragg's feud, "he believed he had won, and wired Richmond to say so") gives the gray side an interior, and mirrors Shiloh's Beauregard "complete victory" beat well.

**MUST-IMPROVE:** trim the commander roster and the turnpike/clock movement-tracking in paras 1–2 so the jackknife and Sheridan stand carry the section without play-by-play around them.

## Section 3 — `the-new-years-pause` — NEEDS WORK

The hardest section to write — a day where almost nothing happens — and right now it reads like a day where almost nothing happens. The Emancipation spine is the right anchor and it's well handled, but structurally the section is thin and a little flat in the middle.

**Hook & stakes:** The frame is correct — "the great event of the day was not a charge but a piece of paper read a thousand miles away." Good instinct. But it's then immediately RE-stated three times: para 2 ("Because this was the morning the Emancipation Proclamation took effect"), the OUTCOME box, and the Meanwhile sidebar all make the same Proclamation point. The repetition flattens what should be the draft's most powerful single idea. CONSOLIDATE: let the section body carry the Proclamation emotionally; let the sidebar carry the Washington scene (the hand-shaking + the pen — that detail is great and underused). Don't say "the meaning is the subject of the final section" twice (it's flagged in para 1 of §1, the close of §3 para 2, AND §3 OUTCOME) — that forward-pointer is now a tic.

**Play-by-play — minor flag:** Para 3 ("Rosecrans ordered a division to cross... seize a low hill... enfilade...") is functional but dry — it's the one genuinely tactical beat and it reads like a staff report. The payoff ("a hill across the river that Bragg did not like one bit") is good house voice; lean on that and compress the mechanics.

**Human dimension — the gap:** This section has almost no PEOPLE in it. The men "rested among their own dead" is the one human image and it's strong — but it's a throwaway line. A pause day is the *opportunity* for the human cost (the wounded still lying out, the burying parties, the soldiers who have no idea the war's meaning just changed under them). The draft even gestures at the last one — "None of them on the field knew it yet" — which is a beautiful, eerie idea (Shiloh-grade: cf. "The men holding the line... were, without knowing it, holding up the document too"). Build the section AROUND that dramatic irony instead of around the troop-movement bookkeeping. The soldiers freezing in the cedar brakes, unaware that what they're fighting for changed at dawn — that's the section.

**Voice:** "a hill across the river that Bragg did not like one bit" — good. Otherwise serviceable but not as sparky as §1/§4.

**MUST-IMPROVE:** restructure around the dramatic irony (men unaware the war's purpose just changed); de-duplicate the Proclamation statement (3x → 1 in-body + the Washington sidebar); add at least one concrete human beat to a section that currently has none.

## Section 4 — `breckinridges-charge` — STRONG

The best-told section in the draft. It has the cleanest dramatic shape (a general told to do a thing he knows is suicide, does it, is proven horribly right), real human cost, and almost no tedium.

**Hook & stakes:** "The hill across the river was the problem." Clean, concrete, immediate. Then the human stakes load fast: Breckinridge, a former Vice President of the United States now a Confederate general, ordered into a charge he calls a death trap. That's a premise a reader will follow anywhere.

**Human dimension — STRONG, the felt-cost peak of the draft:**
- *"More than 1,800 Confederates fell in roughly sixty minutes — better than a third of the five thousand men who made the charge."* — and crucially it doesn't stop at the number: the Orphan Brigade, "Kentuckians who could not go home because their state stayed in the Union," Hanson mortally wounded. The Orphan Brigade tragedy is the emotional engine and the Meanwhile sidebar lands it perfectly: *"orphaned twice over by the end of January 2."* That's a closing line worthy of the Shiloh set.

**Play-by-play:** Almost none — and where tactics appear (Mendenhall's ~50 massed guns) they're rendered as a TRAP SPRINGING, not a survey: *"As Breckinridge's men crowded into the open by the river, all of it opened at once."* This is how to do artillery as story (cf. Shiloh's Ruggles battery / Webster's wall of guns). Keep.

**Dual POV:** Strong, and unusually it's a section told primarily from the SOUTH's experience (the doomed attacker), which is a smart variation — it stops the draft from being "Union holds, South fails" four sections running.

**Pacing:** Excellent. "It was over in under an hour" / "The whole ferocious gamble had lasted less than an hour and accomplished nothing but the gutting of one of Bragg's best divisions." The speed of the prose mirrors the speed of the slaughter.

**Minor — should-improve:** The gun-count hedging ("somewhere around fifty cannon, wheel to wheel, sources putting it near 45 guns on the ridge with another dozen") is fact-pack honesty bleeding into the prose and momentarily breaks the spell. Shiloh states "roughly 62 cannons" / "roughly 50 cannons" cleanly and saves the sourcing nuance for the ledger. RECOMMEND: in-prose say "roughly fifty guns, wheel to wheel"; let the ledger carry the 45+12 split.

## Section 5 — `the-cost-and-the-meaning` — STRONG

Mirrors the Shiloh "Reckoning" closer beautifully and earns its ending. "Start with the number, because the number is the whole story" is borrowed almost verbatim from Shiloh's reckoning ("Start with the number, because the number is the whole story") — which is fine, it's the house template for a closer, and it works.

**Hook & stakes:** "The army that retreats off the field is the army that lost, and that army was Bragg's." Crisp verdict. Good.

**Human dimension:** Handled at the right altitude for a closer — the *rate* framing ("What makes that figure land is not the raw total but the rate") is the smart move, making an abstract number land as "a third of everyone on the field." Good.

**The Proclamation payoff lands:** *"Had Rosecrans retreated up the Nashville Pike, the story of emancipation would have opened on a defeat."* This is the whole draft's thesis delivered in one sentence, and the Lincoln letter ("a hard earned victory, which, had there been a defeat instead, the nation could scarcely have lived over") is a perfect, verified capstone — the §5 equivalent of Grant's "I gave up all idea of saving the Union except by complete conquest." Strong close.

**Voice:** "this small, half-forgotten battle in the Tennessee cedars was among the bloodiest the country ever fought" — house voice, earns its keep.

**Should-improve (minor):**
- The casualty-rate hedging runs long: "By one common reckoning it ran higher than Shiloh or Antietam; by another it stands second only to Gettysburg. The exact ranking depends on how you count, and honest sources put it different ways." This is the right honesty, but it's two full sentences of qualification mid-closer and slightly deflates the punch. Compress to one ("By some reckonings it ran bloodier than Shiloh or Antietam; by others, second only to Gettysburg — either way, among the worst of the war"). The "every way of counting agrees on the headline" sentence then becomes redundant.
- Slight thesis over-repetition: the Proclamation-timing point is now made in §3 (×3), here in the body, in the OUTCOME box, AND in the Meanwhile. By the fifth telling it's earned but at risk of feeling hammered. Trust the reader.
