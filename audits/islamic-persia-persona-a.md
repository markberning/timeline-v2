## Chapter 1 — The Empire That Vanished

1. **"a cosmic struggle between a god of light and truth, **Ahura Mazda**, and a principle of darkness and lies"** — the opposing dark principle is described but never named, while the light god is — feels lopsided; does the darkness have a name (the reader expects one if light gets one)?
2. **"fighting the Roman Empire to a standstill"** — Chapter says the Sasanians fought Rome, then immediately switches to "Byzantines" — does the reader know Rome and the eastern Roman Empire are the same thread here, or that this is the same war continued?
3. **"the chronicles describe a single ceremonial carpet"** — "the chronicles" is used as a source with no introduction — which chronicles? written by whom, when? the reader has no handle on who is telling this.
4. **"His son **Peroz III** (peh-ROZE) fled all the way to the court of **Tang China** in 661"** — Tang China is dropped in with no definition — what was Tang China, where, and why would a Persian prince expect troops from it?
5. **"kept the books in the old Sasanian way"** — what was "the old Sasanian way"? — the reader gets told there was a method but not one concrete thing about how it differed from anything else.
6. **"a special tax. That tax was the **jizya**"** — fine, but earlier the text says Zoroastrians were "allowed to" keep their faith "provided he accepted Muslim rule, kept his head down politically" — what does "kept his head down politically" mean concretely? what was forbidden?
7. **"there are Zoroastrians today, in Iran and especially among the **Parsis** of India"** — Parsis introduced as "communities that emigrated" but the *when* and *how* is vague — when did they leave, and over what stretch of time?
8. **"most lurid of all there was **al-Muqanna** ... claimed to be divine, and held out against the caliph's armies in a fortress in Central Asia"** — "claimed to be divine" with no mechanism — divine how? what did he actually claim or do that made people follow him?
9. **"he built it as a perfect circle, a "round city" ... a design that was itself an old Persian and Mesopotamian idea about kingship, the ruler at the hub of a cosmic wheel"** — asserts the round design was an "old Persian and Mesopotamian idea" but gives no prior example — was there an actual earlier round city, or is this a general claim?
10. **"There was **Sunpadh**, who rose partly to avenge Abu Muslim; there was **Ustadh Sis**"** — two named rebels listed with almost nothing attached — who were they beyond a name, and why list them if there's nothing to picture?

## Chapter 2 — A Language Refuses to Die

1. **"a difficult, ambiguous script descended ultimately from the alphabet of the ancient Aramaeans"** — "ambiguous script" asserted but not shown — what made it ambiguous/difficult? give one concrete thing (e.g., one letter standing for several sounds).
2. **"the ancient Aramaeans"** — dropped in undefined — who were the Aramaeans, and roughly when/where?
3. **"a milieu later romanticized as the **House of Wisdom**"** — the House of Wisdom is named almost dismissively — what actually was it (a library? an academy? a translation bureau?)?
4. **"They held the office of **vizier** (vih-ZEER), the chief minister"** — vizier is defined here in Ch 2, but the pronunciation-gloss + definition is the first full one; fine — but later in Ch 2 the Barmakids "effectively *ran the empire*" then "held the office of vizier" — the reader wonders: was the vizier above or below the caliph in practice?
5. **"the hereditary guardians of a famous Buddhist temple there"** — a "famous Buddhist temple" in Afghanistan is dropped with no name or detail — which temple, and why does it matter that they were Buddhist guardians?
6. **"the caliph of the *Thousand and One Nights*"** — assumes the reader knows what the *Thousand and One Nights* is and that Harun al-Rashid features in it — what is this work and why is the caliph in it?
7. **"The word comes from a Quranic verse that speaks of God creating humanity in "peoples and tribes""** — a quoted phrase presented as Quranic — is "peoples and tribes" an actual quotation, and does the reader need to trust it as one?
8. **"In 821 the caliph rewarded a loyal Persian general, **Tahir ibn Husayn**"** — which caliph? — the named caliphs were al-Mansur (Ch 1) and Harun al-Rashid; the reader can't tell who is on the throne in 821.
9. **"mentioning his name in the Friday prayers"** — "the Friday prayers" used as if the significance is obvious — what are the Friday prayers and why does mentioning the caliph's name there signal loyalty?
10. **"fighting bandits and heretics"** — "heretics" from whose point of view, and what kind? — the reader has no idea what counted as a heretic here.

## Chapter 3 — The Samanid Renaissance

1. **"the lands beyond the Amu Darya river that the Arabs called **Transoxiana**"** — Transoxiana is defined as "the land beyond the Oxus," then "the Oxus being the old name for the Amu Darya" — but the reader doesn't know where the Amu Darya is — which direction from Persia, and what's on the other side?
2. **"buried in the hoards of **Viking** traders"** — Vikings defined as "the Norse seafarers and merchants of the far north," fine, but **"Rus' traders"** appears in the next sentence undefined — who were the Rus'?
3. **"the young Avicenna would later describe as a wonder"** — Avicenna is named here before his full introduction later in the chapter — the reader meets the name with no idea who he is yet (he's defined ~a page later).
4. **"blind (by tradition, though the sources are unsure whether from birth)"** — "the sources" again unintroduced — which sources tell us about Rudaki?
5. **"several times the length of the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* combined"** — assumes the reader knows the length of the Iliad and Odyssey — this only lands if the reader has a sense of those; is the comparison meaningful to a newcomer?
6. **"the Persian Hercules and Achilles rolled into one"** — Hercules is safe, but Achilles assumes the reader knows Achilles specifically — does the reader know what Achilles represents (and why pairing him with Hercules)?
7. **"the tragedy of **Sohrab**, in which Rustam, not knowing he has a son, meets that son in single combat and kills him"** — clear, but the names **Rustam / Sohrab / Zahhak / Kaveh** arrive in a rush — confusingly similar enough that a newcomer juggling them could lose track; minor disambiguation would help.
8. **"the Turkic sultan **Mahmud of Ghazni** (Chapter's end below)"** — "(Chapter's end below)" is an odd forward reference inside the same chapter — does the payoff actually arrive, and is this phrasing meant to point a few paragraphs down?
9. **"a clever method involving the angle of the horizon from a mountaintop"** — process by result — how did measuring an angle from a mountaintop yield Earth's radius? the reader thinks "but how does that work?"
10. **"the first to clearly distinguish smallpox from measles by their symptoms"** — fine as a fact, but the reader may wonder what was thought before — were they considered the same disease?
11. **"working in Baghdad in the ninth century, the Persian mathematician **al-Khwarizmi**"** — al-Khwarizmi is placed "in Baghdad in the ninth century" inside a chapter whose frame is the Samanids of the tenth century and Transoxiana — the reader is confused why he's in this chapter and this place.
12. **"some of these soldier-slaves seized power for themselves. That is what the Ghaznavids did"** — "Turkish military slaves" / "soldier-slaves" — how does a slave seize power? what was the mechanism that let an enslaved soldier become a king?

## Chapter 4 — The Persian Intermezzo

1. **"They came from **Daylam** ... the rugged, rain-soaked highlands south of the Caspian Sea"** — clear, but **"the Caspian Sea"** is used repeatedly across chapters as a fixed landmark — for a US reader who can't place it, one early "the great inland sea of northern Iran" would anchor it (first heavy use is here).
2. **"the Buyid ruler took the title **amir al-umara**, "commander of commanders""** — defined, good — but the reader wonders how this differs in power from "sultan" and "vizier," titles that pile up across chapters; some sense of the hierarchy would help.
3. **"several caliphs of this period were blinded (a traditional way of disqualifying a man from rule without quite killing him)"** — why does blinding disqualify a man from rule? — the reader wants the reason (a ruler had to be physically whole?).
4. **"descending through his cousin and son-in-law **Ali**"** — Ali is introduced here cleanly, but the reader meets "the party of Ali" and the Sunni/Shia split for the first time in Ch 4 — given how central this becomes, is one early sentence on *why* succession was disputed (Muhammad left no son?) enough? (It does say "without clearly naming a successor" — adequate, flagging only the density of new terms here.)
5. **"the canonical collections of Shia **hadith** ... were compiled in this period"** — hadith is defined, good — but "canonical collections ... were compiled" is process-by-result: who compiled them, and how does an oral saying become a "canonical collection"?
6. **"He built hospitals (including a famous one in Baghdad), repaired the irrigation works and dams"** — "a famous one" — famous for what? if it's worth naming as famous, the reader wants the one concrete thing about it.
7. **"the public mourning rituals for the martyred imams that are still central to Shia devotion today"** — "the martyred imams" assumes the reader knows imams were martyred — which imams, martyred how, and why is mourning them central? this is the first the reader hears of martyrdom.
8. **"Daylamite infantry increasingly had to share the army with Turkish cavalry whose interests pulled the other way"** — "whose interests pulled the other way" — pulled which way, and why? the reader gets told there's tension but not what the two sides actually wanted.

## Chapter 5 — Turks Who Ruled in Persian

1. **"as distant from Persian as Hungarian is from Italian"** — the analogy assumes the reader knows Hungarian and Italian are unrelated — does a US newcomer reliably know that? (Many would assume all European languages are related.)
2. **"the composite bow — the steppe's signature weapon, a short, powerful recurved bow a rider could shoot at full gallop"** — good, defined — no flag, noted as a model of the concrete handle the persona wants.
3. **"a Turkish dynasty called the **Ghaznavids** (GHAZ-nuh-vidz), founded by slave-soldiers who had risen to power ... The most famous Ghaznavid, **Mahmud of Ghazni**"** — the Ghaznavids and Mahmud were already fully introduced in Chapter 3 (including the seventeen raids and the Ferdowsi presentation), and here they are re-defined at nearly the same length — identity re-definition; later mentions should tag, not re-explain.
4. **"the seventeen plundering raids" / "roughly seventeen plundering raids into the temple-rich plains of northern India"** — the "seventeen raids into India" appears in Ch 3 and again here — the reader feels the repeat without new information.
5. **"had himself proclaimed **sultan** ... a title meaning, roughly, "holder of power""** — sultan defined here in Ch 5, but the word **"sultan"** was already used in Ch 3 ("the Turkic sultan Mahmud of Ghazni") undefined — the definition arrives two chapters after first use.
6. **"think of it as a prime minister with the powers of a chief of staff and a treasurer rolled together"** — vizier is being re-defined again (it was defined in Ch 2 and Ch 4) — identity re-definition of a known office.
7. **"a "mirror for princes" (a book that holds up the image of the ideal ruler for an actual ruler to study)"** — clear and good; no flag.
8. **"the rival Shia caliphate in Egypt"** — dropped in as a known thing — which caliphate? (This is the Fatimids, but they aren't named until Ch 6; the reader meets "Shia caliphate in Egypt" with no handle here.)
9. **"captured the Byzantine emperor himself, Romanos IV"** — Romanos IV named once with no other detail — fine as a fact, but the reader has no sense of who he was beyond "the emperor."
10. **"the **Gregorian calendar** that Europe would adopt five centuries later and that the world uses now"** — fine, but the claim that the Jalali calendar was "more accurately than the Gregorian" is a "how do we know / how much more accurate?" moment — by what measure was it more accurate?
11. **"that honeycomb of stacked niches called **muqarnas**"** — defined, good — but it's hard to picture from words alone; the reader thinks "what does a honeycomb of stacked niches actually look like?" (borderline — flagging because it recurs across later chapters assuming this image stuck).
12. **"systematized a practice called the **iqta** — a grant by which a soldier or officer was assigned the tax revenue of a piece of land, in place of a salary"** — clear, good; the comparison to "feudal Europe" assumes the reader knows how European feudalism worked — does the newcomer have that?

## Chapter 6 — The Castles in the Mountains

1. **"an esoteric inner doctrine — a belief that scripture and law carry hidden spiritual meanings accessible only through the teaching of the rightful, living Imam"** — "esoteric inner doctrine" is then defined, good — but the reader wonders what one of these "hidden meanings" actually was; an example would make it concrete rather than abstract.
2. **"the **Fatimids** ... a Shia dynasty ruling from Egypt, who were the great religious and political rivals of the Sunni Abbasids and the Seljuks"** — finally defines the "Shia caliphate in Egypt" referenced back in Ch 5 — the reader who flagged it in Ch 5 had to wait a chapter; noting the delayed payoff.
3. **"a remote mountain fortress called **Alamut** ... perched on a high crag in the Elburz mountains"** — "the Elburz mountains" dropped in undefined — where are the Elburz mountains relative to everything else (the text earlier leaned on the Zagros)? a newcomer can't place a second mountain range.
4. **"struck down by a Nizari killer disguised as a Sufi in 1092"** — clear, but "disguised as a Sufi" assumes the reader still holds what a Sufi is — Sufism isn't fully defined until Chapter 7, which comes *after* this; the term is used here before its definition.
5. **"the medieval equivalent of calling someone a deranged drug fiend"** — fine; no flag.
6. **"author of the planetary models that some scholars believe influenced Copernicus two centuries later"** — Copernicus is named with a one-clause gloss only later (Ch 10: "the man who put the sun at the center"); here in Ch 6 Copernicus is dropped with no gloss at all — who was Copernicus and why does influencing him matter?
7. **"now styled the Aga Khan"** — the Aga Khan dropped at the very end with no explanation — who/what is the Aga Khan, and would a reader know this is a real present-day title?

## Chapter 7 — The Inner Path

1. **"Where the religious law (the *sharia*)"** — sharia introduced parenthetically as "the religious law" — adequate, but the reader may want one concrete thing it governs; borderline.
2. **"reached conclusions that al-Ghazali judged incompatible with Islamic doctrine (such as the eternity of the world, or limits on God's knowledge of particular things)"** — "the eternity of the world" and "limits on God's knowledge of particular things" are stated as the contested conclusions but not explained — what does "the eternity of the world" mean as a philosophical claim, and why is it a problem for the faith?
3. **"a famous rebuttal a century later from the Spanish-Arab philosopher Averroes, titled *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*"** — Averroes named with a one-line tag ("Spanish-Arab philosopher"), fine — but "carried into Latin Europe, would help shape Western thought" is vague — shaped it how?
4. **"the **masnavi** ... a poem in rhyming couplets that could run to enormous length"** — clear; no flag.
5. **"a mythical bird called the Simurgh"** — Simurgh introduced — fine, but the pun "(*si murgh* means "thirty birds")" depends on the reader trusting an untranslated Persian phrase; acceptable, noting it.
6. **"founded a whole school of mystical philosophy he called the "Philosophy of Illumination" — an attempt to fuse Avicenna's logic with Platonic and ancient Persian imagery of light"** — "Platonic" dropped in undefined — does the newcomer know what "Platonic" refers to (Plato / Greek philosophy)?
7. **"the place-name "Rum" (Rome) gave Rumi his name"** — clear, but the reader met "Sultanate of Rum" briefly in Ch 5; this is a nice payoff — no flag, noted as working.
8. **"so revered that it is sometimes called "the Quran in the Persian tongue""** — fine; no flag.
9. **"the meditative spinning dance called the *sama*"** — clear; no flag.
10. **Rumi placement** — Rumi's full story is told here in Ch 7, but Ch 9 (which the reader reaches *later*) also tells the Rumi-flees-Balkh story and points "back" to Ch 7 — within Ch 7 itself the forward reference "the storm that is Chapter 9" is fine; just noting Rumi is covered in two chapters and a reader may feel the overlap.

## Chapter 8 — The Brittle Colossus

1. **"centered on the delta of the **Amu Darya** ... where it empties into the Aral Sea"** — "the Aral Sea" dropped in undefined — where is the Aral Sea? a newcomer has no mental map for it.
2. **"these governors did what governors all over the crumbling empire were doing: they made themselves independent"** — process by result — *how* does a governor "make himself independent"? stop sending tribute? raise his own army? the mechanism is asserted, not shown (it was shown more concretely for the Tahirids in Ch 2, but not recapped here).
3. **"Its army was a heterogeneous mass of recently-recruited Turkic nomad warriors whose loyalty ran to their own tribal commanders rather than to the throne"** — clear; no flag.
4. **"the subject, in full, of this project's Mongol Empire timeline"** — a cross-reference to another timeline in the project — a first-time reader of *this* document may not know what "this project's Mongol Empire timeline" is or how to reach it; mild dangling reference.
5. **"by the practice of the steppe held the persons of envoys and the security of trade to be sacred"** — asserts a steppe custom — how do we know this was a steppe practice, and was it written law or just custom? (borderline — the chapter does explain the logic.)

## Chapter 9 — The End of the World

1. **"a confederation of Turkic and Mongolic nomad tribes from the grasslands north of China"** — clear; no flag.
2. **"sometimes a hundred miles (160 km) in a day"** — miles-first, good; no flag.
3. **"the word "horde" comes from the Mongol *ordu*, a royal camp"** — fine; no flag.
4. **"Juvayni's famous line about Bukhara — that the Mongols "came, they uprooted, they burned, they slew, they carried off, they departed""** — a direct quote attributed to Juvayni — the reader who doesn't know Juvayni well wonders: is this a real translated line? (Juvayni is introduced two paragraphs earlier, so the *who* is covered; flagging the quote itself only as something a reader registers.)
5. **"the heads of the dead were stacked in pyramids, men, women, and children sorted into separate heaps"** — vivid, but the text elsewhere warns these chronicle images are "memory rather than measurement" — here the reader can't tell if this specific image is fact or the same kind of literary horror; minor inconsistency in signposting.
6. **"reportedly by a Kurdish bandit who had no idea who he was"** — "a Kurdish bandit" — Kurds not introduced anywhere; who are the Kurds and where? (one of the few ethnic groups dropped without a gloss).
7. **Rumi recap** — **"The family's young son would grow up to be **Rumi** ... We will come to his poetry in its own place (Chapter 7 covers the Sufi flowering that produced it)"** — this is a backward pointer to Ch 7, but the reader has *already read* Ch 7; the same flight-from-Balkh story is told in both chapters — the reader feels the retelling rather than a terse callback.
8. **"an Egyptian army of **Mamluks** ... a regime of military slave-soldiers, mostly of Turkic and Caucasian origin, who had seized power in Egypt"** — "Caucasian origin" — the reader may read "Caucasian" as the modern racial term rather than "from the Caucasus mountains"; a one-word clarification would prevent the misread.
9. **"the **Great Khan** in Mongolia (then Hulagu's brother)"** — "the Great Khan" used as a defined office here, but the relationship gets clearer only in Ch 10 — within Ch 9 the reader meets "Great Khan" assuming it's understood; mild.

## Chapter 10 — The Conquerors Convert

1. **"Hulagu himself leaned toward Buddhism and had a Christian wife"** — fine; no flag.
2. **"a mathematical device now called the **Tusi couple** — a clever way of producing straight-line motion from two rotating circles"** — defined, but the reader struggles to picture *why* this matters for astronomy — what problem did producing straight-line motion from circles solve? (process gestured at, not shown).
3. **"the man who put the sun at the center of the cosmos"** — this is the gloss on Copernicus that was *missing* back in Ch 6 where he was first dropped; here it finally arrives — noting the definition is two chapters late.
4. **"the seventh Ilkhan, **Ghazan**"** — "the seventh Ilkhan" implies a counted line the reader hasn't been given — the reader has met Hulagu and now Ghazan; "seventh" makes them wonder about the six in between.
5. **"born Jewish and converted to Islam"** (Rashid al-Din) — fine; no flag.
6. **"the *Pax Mongolica* (the "Mongol peace," the period when a single Mongol-dominated political order made the overland routes ... unusually safe)"** — clear; no flag.
7. **"out of that fusion of Chinese style, older Persian and Mesopotamian traditions, and a touch of Byzantine influence from the west"** — "Mesopotamian traditions" of *painting* is asserted — the book established Mesopotamia geographically (Ch 1) but never any Mesopotamian visual-art tradition; the reader has nothing to attach this to.
8. **"the **Great Mongol Shahnameh** (also called the Demotte Shahnameh after a later dealer who, infamously, broke it up to sell the pages separately)"** — the parenthetical is ~20 words and packs in a name + an action + "infamously" — borderline long aside that briefly breaks the sentence.
9. **"one of the largest brick domes ever built anywhere up to that time"** — "up to that time" — the reader wants a sense of scale or comparison; largest compared to what (the Pantheon? Hagia Sophia)? especially since Hagia Sophia/Byzantium are in the book's world.

## Chapter 11 — Skulls and Tilework

1. **"His name is a title — it means one who has memorized the entire Qur'an by heart"** (Hafez) — clear; no flag.
2. **"a practice called *fal-e Hafez*"** — defined in context ("consult it as an oracle ... reading the first lines"), good; no flag.
3. **"Goethe, the towering figure of German literature"** — Goethe given a one-line tag, fine — but "wrote a whole collection of poems in homage" with no title or date is vague; minor.
4. **"a corruption of *Timur-e Lang*, "Timur the Lame," after a leg wound"** — clear; no flag.
5. **"He was a Turco-Mongol — a man from the Turkic-speaking, partly Mongol-descended military aristocracy"** — clear; no flag.
6. **"because by the rigid steppe law of legitimacy only a direct male descendant of Genghis could be a khan"** — this "steppe law of legitimacy" is asserted as a fixed rule — how firm a rule was it, and who enforced it? the reader takes it on faith.
7. **"the architectural grandfather, through his descendants the Mughals (the dynasty Timur's line would later found in India), of the Taj Mahal itself"** — the parenthetical defining the Mughals is fine, but the chain "Gur-e-Amir → Mughals → Taj Mahal" is compressed; the reader may not know the Taj Mahal is a tomb (the load-bearing parallel) unless they already do.
8. **"an enormous stone sextant — a curved measuring instrument set into a trench in the ground, tens of feet across"** — clear; no flag.
9. **"Bihzad is to Persian painting roughly what his rough contemporaries Leonardo and Botticelli were to Italian painting"** — assumes the reader knows who Leonardo and Botticelli were and what they represent — Leonardo is safe; Botticelli is less universally known to a newcomer.
10. **"steeped though he was in Persian culture, he argued passionately that his own native tongue, **Chagatai Turkish** ... was a worthy vehicle for high literature"** — clear; no flag.

## Chapter 12 — The Red-Heads and the Boy King

1. **"the populist, partly Shia **Sarbadars** ... whose name means roughly "the gallows-headed," from a defiant boast"** — clear; no flag.
2. **"the **Kara Koyunlu** ... the "Black Sheep" Turkmen, and the **Aq Qoyunlu** ... the "White Sheep" Turkmen"** — the two names are near-mirror images (Kara/Aq Koyunlu, Black/White Sheep) and recur many times — confusingly similar; a newcomer will struggle to keep straight which is which without re-checking (text disambiguates by color once, but the foreign names dominate afterward).
3. **"ambitious enough to negotiate an alliance with the merchant republic of Venice"** — Venice given a tag ("the merchant republic of Venice"), fine — but *why* Venice cared about Persia against the Ottomans is left implicit; the reader wonders what Venice's stake was.
4. **"importing Shia scholars to staff a clergy he largely had to build from scratch"** — "build a clergy from scratch" is process-by-result — how do you import scholars and build a clergy? from where did the Shia scholars come (the text doesn't say — Lebanon/Bahrain in reality)?
5. **"ordering the public cursing of the first caliphs revered by Sunnis"** — "the first caliphs revered by Sunnis" assumes the reader knows who those caliphs were and why cursing them is the sharpest possible anti-Sunni act — which caliphs, and why is this the insult that defines the Sunni/Shia rupture?
6. **"a near-divine figure, the rightful lord, a manifestation of the holy, worth dying for"** — "a manifestation of the holy" is abstract — what did the Qizilbash actually believe Ismail *was* (a reincarnation? a prophet? God himself)? the claim is striking but stays vague.
7. **"the twelve hereditary leaders that this branch of Shiism, called **Twelver Shiism**, recognized"** — Twelver Shiism was already defined in Ch 4 ("twelve rightful imams ... the last of whom did not die but went into a miraculous hidden state") and is re-defined here — identity re-definition of a concept fully explained earlier.
