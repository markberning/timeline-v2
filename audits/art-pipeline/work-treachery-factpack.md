## 2. THE STORY

By 1929 René Magritte had settled into the deadpan style that defines his Surrealism: ordinary objects rendered with the cool clarity of a commercial illustration, then placed into a situation that short-circuits the eye. *The Treachery of Images* is the purest example. It shows a single tobacco pipe — plainly, even blandly painted, the kind of picture you'd find in a smoker's catalogue — floating on an empty light-brown ground. Beneath it, in a tidy cursive hand, runs the line "Ceci n'est pas une pipe": *This is not a pipe.*

The contradiction is the whole point, and it is not a trick. The painted statement is literally true. What hangs on the museum wall is not a pipe — you cannot fill it, light it, or smoke it. It is an *image* of a pipe: pigment arranged on canvas. Magritte is forcing apart three things our minds normally fuse without noticing — the **thing** (a real pipe), the **picture** of the thing, and the **word** for the thing. None of them is the others. A drawing of a pipe is not a pipe; the word "pipe" is not a pipe; and so the picture, captioned, tells the truth precisely by denying what it appears to claim. The painting belongs to a run of "word-image" works Magritte made in the late 1920s probing exactly this gap — most explicitly his 1929 diagram-essay *Les mots et les images* ("Words and Images"), published in the Surrealist journal *La Révolution surréaliste*, which lays out propositions like "an object never performs the same function as its name or its image."

The picture went on to a second life as a philosophical touchstone. The French philosopher Michel Foucault was so taken with it that he wrote a short essay on it titled *Ceci n'est pas une pipe* (first published in the journal *Les Cahiers du chemin* in 1968, then expanded into a small book in 1973; translated into English by James Harkness as *This Is Not a Pipe*). Foucault used Magritte's pipe to pick apart the old contract between resemblance and affirmation in Western art — the assumption that a picture both *resembles* its subject and silently *asserts* "this is that." Magritte severs the two. Partly through Foucault, partly through endless reproduction and parody, the captioned pipe became one of the most recognizable images in modern art and a stock emblem of the idea that representations are not the realities they stand for.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK *(required)*

**Before:** For centuries a realistic picture made an implicit, unspoken claim — it resembled a thing and therefore *asserted* that thing ("here is a pipe"). Words, when they appeared in a painting at all (titles, inscriptions, mottos), reinforced the image. Resemblance and affirmation traveled together; the viewer was meant to look *through* the painted surface to the object it named.

**After:** Magritte turns a painting into a **philosophical proposition about representation itself**. He puts word and image on the *same surface* and sets them at war: the picture says "pipe," the sentence says "not a pipe," and both are correct, because the painted pipe was never a pipe to begin with. The break is twofold — (1) the canvas stops being a window onto a thing and becomes an argument about the gap between sign and referent; (2) text is no longer the image's servant but its adversary, a caption that contradicts rather than captions. It is the moment representation is caught looking at its own machinery — the conceptual seed later movements (Conceptual art, Pop's sign-play, Pictures-generation appropriation) would harvest directly.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT

**The painted caption (verbatim).** Exact French:
> Ceci n'est pas une pipe.

English:
> This is not a pipe.

(Lowercase opening per Magritte's cursive line; standard transcription closes with a period. The apostrophe is in "n'est." Source: LACMA title, painting itself.)

**Magritte's own gloss (sourced).** Verbatim, as cited to Harry Torczyner, *Magritte: Ideas and Images* (Harry N. Abrams, 1977), p. 71:
> "The famous pipe. How people reproached me for it! And yet, could you stuff my pipe? No, it's just a representation, is it not? So if I had written on my picture 'This is a pipe,' I'd have been lying!"

- **Source / flag:** This is the most widely cited English wording, traced to Torczyner (1977). The quotation is a translation from the French and **circulates in minor variants** — punctuation around the inner quote ("This is a pipe" vs 'This is a pipe'), and "it's just a representation" vs "it's just a representation, is it not?" Treat the Torczyner wording above as the canonical English. The original context/interlocutor is not fixed by Wikipedia; attribute to Torczyner, *Magritte: Ideas and Images*, p. 71. **Do not over-specify when/where he said it.**

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The pipe itself.** A single brown briar tobacco pipe, bowl to the right, stem to the left, sitting in the middle of the canvas with no shadow anchoring it to anything — it floats on the ground rather than resting on a surface.
2. **The plainness of the rendering.** It is painted with deliberate, unglamorous clarity — smooth modeling, even light, no painterly flourish. It looks less like a "painting" of a pipe than like an illustration *of* a pipe, the kind in a product catalogue or a teaching plate.
3. **The empty ground.** Behind the pipe is a flat, neutral tan-brown field — no room, no table, no horizon, no context. The blankness strips away everything that would tell you this is a scene; it leaves only the sign.
4. **The cursive caption below.** Under the pipe, centered, runs the handwritten-looking line "Ceci n'est pas une pipe" in a tidy schoolroom script — the lettering deliberately recalls a copybook or a labeled diagram, the visual grammar of a *lesson*.
5. **The lesson-plate / blackboard format.** Object on top, label underneath: the whole layout mimics an educational chart ("this is a ___"). Magritte borrows that format precisely so he can break its promise — the label refuses to confirm the object.
6. **The deadpan.** Nothing in the handling signals a joke or a flourish; the contradiction is delivered with a completely straight face. The flatness *is* the argument — it asks you to notice that calm illustration and outright denial occupy one untroubled surface.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (corrected with sources)

1. **"Which pipe painting is THE one?" — multiple versions trap.** Magritte returned to the captioned-pipe motif repeatedly across his career (e.g., later canvases and the 1948/1952 *Ceci n'est pas une pipe (L'air et la chanson)* "The Tune and Also the Words"; the pipe also recurs inside *Les deux mystères*, 1966, which shows the captioned pipe-painting on an easel). **The canonical, museum-anchored, art-historically "first" work is the 1929 oil at LACMA, accession 78.7** — that is the one meant by id `treachery`. The wired image `ART_IMG.magrittePipe` is the standard Wikipedia file of exactly that 1929 LACMA canvas; **confirmed match.** Do not conflate it with the 1948/1952 variant or the 1966 *Les deux mystères*.

2. **Dimensions trap.** A loose figure of **63.5 × 93.98 cm (~25 × 37 in)** circulates online and surfaced in early searching. It is **wrong / stale.** LACMA measures the canvas at **23 3/4 × 31 15/16 in = 60.33 × 81.12 cm**. **Use 60.3 × 81.1 cm.**

3. **Caption punctuation/case trap.** It is **"Ceci n'est pas une pipe"** — lowercase opening "Ceci"? (Magritte's painted line capitalizes "Ceci" as a sentence start); the apostrophe is in **n'est**; transcriptions close with a period. Avoid the common misrendering "Ceci nest pas une pipe" (missing apostrophe) or capitalizing mid-sentence words.

4. **Foucault essay date trap.** Foucault's text **first appeared in 1968** (journal *Les Cahiers du chemin*; one source says 1967 — **flag as 1968, with a 1967 minority claim**), and the **expanded book is 1973**; the **English translation by James Harkness is 1982/1983** (University of California Press). Do **not** cite "1973" as the essay's only date, and do not cite the 2008 reprint as the original. Safe statement: *"Foucault's essay (1968; expanded as a book, 1973)."*

5. **Title-soup trap.** The work travels under several names — *The Treachery of Images*, *This Is Not a Pipe*, *La trahison des images*, and the variant *The Wind and the Song* / *L'air et la chanson* (the last properly belongs to the **later** version, not the 1929 LACMA painting). Lead with **The Treachery of Images (La trahison des images)** and gloss the painted caption separately.

6. **"It's a drawing/lithograph" trap.** It is an **oil on canvas**, not a print or drawing, despite its illustrational look. (LACMA: oil on canvas.)
