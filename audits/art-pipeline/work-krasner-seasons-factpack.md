## 2. THE STORY

Jackson Pollock — Krasner's husband and, by 1956, the most famous painter in America — died in a car crash near their home in Springs, on the east end of Long Island, on **August 11, 1956**. Krasner was in Europe at the time and returned to bury him. **[S]**

In the months after his death, Krasner moved her own work into the **barn behind the house that had been Pollock's studio** — a far larger room than the small bedroom she had been painting in. For the first time she had the floor space and wall to work at **mural scale**, and she used it. *The Seasons* (1957) was the largest canvas she had ever attempted: nearly **17 feet wide and more than 7 feet high.** **[V/S — Whitney, Smarthistory/secondary]**

The canvas is a wide, rolling, allover field of **swelling pink and rose organic forms**, off-white bulbous shapes, and passages of **lush green and ochre**, tied together and bounded by **sweeping black contour lines.** The forms read at once as bodies, buds, fruit, leaves, and — in several readings — as sexual/plant organs. Against the fact of Pollock's death and her own grief, the painting is overwhelmingly a **fertility / regeneration image** — growth, ripeness, the turning of the seasons toward renewal. **[V/S]**

*The Seasons* is a central work of Krasner's **"Earth Green" series** (commonly dated **1956–59**, sometimes 1957–59), the body of large, biomorphic, vegetal, brightly colored canvases she made in the barn studio in these years (related works: *Sun Woman I*, *Earth Green*, etc.). **[S]**

Throughout her career Krasner fought to be seen as a painter **in her own right** rather than as "Mrs. Jackson Pollock." She had been a serious abstract painter *before* she met Pollock and kept working through and after the marriage, but reviews and the art world repeatedly folded her into his shadow (a 1949 review billed her as "Lee Krasner (Mrs. Jackson Pollock)"). Critics even attacked the Earth Green works as "derivative of Pollock's and too decorative" — *decorative* functioning as a coded put-down for *feminine*. Independent recognition came later: a 1965 Whitechapel Gallery retrospective (London), a 1973 Whitney show *Lee Krasner: Large Paintings*, and full reappraisal in the 1980s. **[S]**

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK  *(REQUIRED — before / after)*

**Before:** Abstract Expressionism's heroic, mural-scale, full-arm "action" canvas was coded male and was, in practice, made almost entirely by men — Pollock, de Kooning, Kline, Rothko, Newman. Women in the first generation (Krasner among the very few) showed and were taken seriously, but the *big* gestural statement — the wall-sized, ambitious, museum-scale painting — was treated as men's territory. Krasner herself had been working smaller, partly for lack of a large studio, and partly inside Pollock's shadow.

**After / the break:**
1. **A woman painting AbEx at full mural scale and ambition.** *The Seasons* is a ~17-foot canvas made by a woman, on her own terms, in the very studio where Pollock had made his largest drip paintings — claiming the scale and the wall, not a reduced version of them.
2. **Biomorphic, life-affirming abstraction.** Where the dominant AbEx mode trended toward the brooding, the sublime, the anxious, or the void, Krasner answered grief with a **green, swelling, fertile** image — abstraction as growth and renewal rather than dread.
3. **The body/nature rhythm.** The painting builds its allover field out of bud-, fruit-, and body-suggesting forms in steady rhythmic repetition — a way of organizing a huge surface that is gestural and abstract yet rooted in living, organic form, distinct from Pollock's poured skeins or the color-field stain.

So the "break" is not a new technique so much as **who is allowed the big AbEx statement, and what that statement is allowed to be about** — a woman, at mural scale, making a life-affirming, body/nature abstraction out of loss.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS  *(~6 — prose pointers, no coordinates)*

1. **The swelling pink/rose forms.** The dominant note is a field of full, bulbous, ripened pink-and-rose shapes — they read as bodies, breasts, buds about to open, fruit about to fall. This is the painting's emotional engine: ripeness, not grief.
2. **The green and the ochre.** Threaded through and around the pinks are passages of lush green and earthy ochre — the "earth green" that names the series, grounding the bright bodies in foliage and soil.
3. **The rhythmic, allover repetition.** No single focal point: the forms recur across the whole width in a steady, rolling beat, so the eye travels the full canvas left to right the way it would scan a field or a frieze. It is "allover" composition (Pollock's term) bent toward organic rhythm.
4. **Body / bud / fruit suggestions.** The shapes hover deliberately between abstraction and the figurative — the same form can be read as a torso, a seed-pod, a piece of fruit, or a sexual organ. Krasner keeps them un-pinned-down on purpose.
5. **The heavy black contour lines.** Sweeping black brush-lines wrap and separate the colored forms, drawing the bodies and giving the whole surface its swing and structure — the drawing inside the painting.
6. **The mural width.** Step back: the thing is ~17 feet across. It is built to be walked, not glanced at — a wall-sized statement that asks for the same physical scale as the big male AbEx canvases of its moment.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS  *(correct, keep proportionate)*

- **"Just a grieving widow" framing — AVOID.** The barn-studio / post-Pollock context is **factual and central** (he died Aug 1956; she moved into his studio; *The Seasons* and the Earth Green series followed). State it. But do **not** reduce the painting to a widow's catharsis: Krasner was a committed abstract painter **before** Pollock and **for nearly 30 years after** his death (d. 1984), and *The Seasons* is an act of ambition and recognition, not only mourning. Keep the death as context, not as the whole explanation. **[S]**
- **"Earth Green" series — get the dating right.** The series is usually dated **1956–59** (some sources 1957–59). *The Seasons* belongs to it. Don't conflate it with the later **Night Journey / "Umber" / night paintings** (her insomnia-driven, dark-palette series, c. 1959–62) — those are a *different*, darker series, not the green ones. **[S]**
- **Date = 1957.** Firmly. The barn move and series began 1956; *this* canvas is 1957 per the Whitney. Don't backdate it to 1956 just because Pollock died that year. **[V]**
- **Dimensions traps.** Confirmed **235.6 × 517.8 cm = 92 3/4 × 203 7/8 in**. Beware the loose phrasing: "nearly 17 feet wide" actually rounds *down* — width is a true **~17 ft 0 in**. Height is **7 ft 8 3/4 in**, i.e. "more than 7 feet," not "about 7 feet." Some listings flip the order to "518 × 236 cm" (width-first) — same object. **[V]**
- **Medium trap.** It is **oil AND house paint** on canvas — not oil alone. Krasner, like Pollock, used commercial house paint. Don't drop the "house paint." **[V]**
- **Acquisition-year trap.** Do not print "1987" as a hard fact unless confirmed; it is **inferred** from accession 87.7. The Whitney page shows the credit line but not an explicit year. **[?]**
- **Quote-attribution trap.** The "*this was my answer*" and the "*damn good painter*" quotes circulate widely with slightly varying punctuation/ellipses and are attributed to oral-history / interview sources I could not open directly (AAA 403). **Verify each to its primary transcript before printing**, and flag any that can't be pinned.
