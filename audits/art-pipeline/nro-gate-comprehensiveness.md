## Independent must-cover checklist

A fair survey of the Neoclassical & Romantic era (c.1750–1850) would have to include, at the appropriate altitude:

**ERA ALTITUDE (the argument)**
- The Rococo foil (Fragonard, the Swing) as the thing both sides rejected
- Winckelmann 1755 — the founding theorist; "noble simplicity and quiet grandeur"
- Pompeii and Herculaneum as the archaeological catalyst (not just theory)
- The Enlightenment as the intellectual engine of Neoclassicism
- David as the movement's body and the Revolution's image-maker (Oath of the Horatii, Death of Marat)
- Ingres as the line-defender, David's heir, the Salon rival
- Edmund Burke 1757 and the theory of the sublime
- The political earthquake spine (Enlightenment → Revolution → Napoleon → Restoration → 1830)
- Goya as the Romantic proto-type and David's near-contemporary — their overlap
- Géricault (Raft of the Medusa) — the Romantic scale shift
- Delacroix (Liberty) — color vs line made explicit
- Turner and Friedrich for the landscape/natural sublime
- Constable as the quieter British pole
- The argument that they are NOT a sequence (David/Goya; Ingres/Delacroix contemporaneous)
- Courbet/Realism as the handoff — the third position that rejected both

**NEOCLASSICISM MOVEMENT ALTITUDE**
All of the above for the era level, plus deeper treatment of:
- Mengs as the first programmatic Neoclassical painter (doctrine turned to paint, 1761)
- The Rococo break made concrete (Fragonard → David, specific visual swaps)
- David's documented political role (regicide, festival-designer, Napoleon's painter) — precise
- Death of Socrates (1787) — the movement's ideal in a body
- Napoleon Crossing the Alps (1801–05) — heroic myth, not reportage
- Ingres: Grande Odalisque, Napoleon on Imperial Throne, Apotheosis of Homer
- Canova, Kauffman, West — the international spread
- Antoine-Jean Gros and/or Girodet — the second-generation Davidians who bridged toward Romanticism
- Neoclassicism becoming the academy — the rules everyone later broke
- "Neoclassicism" as a later, originally pejorative label

**ROMANTICISM MOVEMENT ALTITUDE**
- Burke's sublime as the philosophical license for the movement
- Goya's Spain: Los Caprichos (sleep/dream of reason), Third of May, Black Paintings
- Goya's Disasters of War as the anti-war graphic series
- Géricault: research method (morgue, survivors, model raft), the Raft as public scandal
- Delacroix: Sardanapalus (1827) — the first provocation; Liberty (1830) — the rival in the open; the 1824 Massacre at Chios as the crystallizing moment in the Ingres/Delacroix rivalry
- Turner: the cosmic sublime — Fighting Temeraire, Slave Ship (Zong/abolition context), Rain Steam Speed, Snow Storm
- Constable: the intimate/observed English weather; Hay Wain; influence on Delacroix; seed of Impressionism
- Friedrich: the Rückenfigur, Monk by the Sea, Wanderer
- The manifesto absence + literary surrogates (Wordsworth, Friedrich's aphorisms, Delacroix's Journal, Baudelaire)
- **Blake and Fuseli — the dark/visionary/supernatural strand** (The Nightmare, 1781; Blake's prophetic imagery) — the nightmare and dream dimension the movement constantly claims but never delivers a visual anchor for
- Romantic nationalism/medievalism as a strand (German in particular)

**MISSING: Gros and the transitional Davidians** — Antoine-Jean Gros (1771–1835) is the most important second-generation Davidian, and his Napoleonic paintings (*Napoleon at the Pesthouse at Jaffa*, 1804; *Napoleon at Eylau*, 1808) are the bridge between Neoclassicism and Romanticism. They take David's format (history painting at grand scale, morally serious) but fill it with visible, Romantic-style emotion: plague victims, the horror of the battlefield. David himself reportedly praised Gros but worried he was heading the wrong direction. Girodet (1767–1824) similarly pushed Neoclassicism's edges. Without this bridge, the movement read jumps from David/Ingres to Romanticism as if the movements were hermetically sealed — which both fact pack and drafts explicitly argue against. The narrative says Ingres and Delacroix "ran side by side for decades" but doesn't explain how the Neoclassical tradition itself evolved internally to produce painters who anticipated Romanticism. Gros in particular is the missing link.

**MISSING: Blake and Fuseli — the dark/supernatural/visionary strand**

The Romanticism narrative repeatedly promises its darker dimension. Chapter 1 explicitly lists "dream and nightmare" as core themes. But the actual content delivers only: a firing squad, a shipwreck, a barricade, storms and landscapes. Not one work depicting dream, nightmare, the supernatural, or the visionary inner life. This is a structural gap, because the chapter claims the movement was about "the dream" and then never shows it.

Henry Fuseli (1741–1825, Swiss-British) and William Blake (1757–1827, British) are the painters of that strand. Fuseli's *The Nightmare* (1781, Detroit Institute of Arts / Städel Museum, Frankfurt) is the era's most famous nightmare image: a woman draped unconscious, an incubus crouching on her chest, a wild-eyed horse peering through a curtain. It predates Goya's Caprichos by nearly two decades and is direct evidence that the dark/visionary impulse was not a late arrival. Blake's prophetic imagery (*The Ancient of Days*, 1794; illustrations to Dante and Milton) represents an entirely different mode — the sublime as divine vision, not just as weather or warfare.

Their absence isn't compensated by the MeanwhileSheet that mentions "Byron and Goethe," because those are literary references, not visual ones. The movement read has no image or sustained discussion of the nightmare, the supernatural, or the visionary.

This is MUST-ADD for the movement read, though at era altitude it is not blocking.

## MUST-ADD items (blocking — with sourced facts for the fact-checker)

### 1. Antoine-Jean Gros — the bridge figure (Neoclassicism movement read)

Gros is a David pupil who went where David was unwilling to go: Napoleonic battle scenes with genuine Romantic emotional charge. He represents the internal evolution of the Neoclassical tradition that explains how Romanticism grew out of it rather than simply arriving from outside.

**Verified facts:**
- **Antoine-Jean Gros** (1771–1835), French painter, trained under David.
- ***Napoleon Visiting the Pesthouse at Jaffa*** (also called *Napoleon Bonaparte Visiting the Plague-Stricken in Jaffa*), **1804**, Musée du Louvre. 523 × 715 cm (~17 ft 2 in × 23 ft 5 in). Shows Napoleon touching the plague sores of sick soldiers — a propaganda piece in the grand Davidian scale, but with a theatricality, orientalist setting, and raw physical intensity that anticipates Romanticism.
  - Commons file: `File:Antoine-Jean Gros - Bonaparte visitant les pestiférés de Jaffa.jpg` — PD worldwide (Gros d.1835).
- ***The Battle of Eylau*** (*Napoléon sur le champ de bataille d'Eylau*), **1808**, Musée du Louvre. 521 × 784 cm (~17 ft 1 in × 25 ft 8 in). Shows Napoleon on horseback above a battlefield strewn with dead and wounded soldiers, in extreme cold, the aftermath of the 1807 battle. The horror of the dead, the emotional weight, and the bleak winter light are Romantic in all but name.
  - Commons file: `File:Antoine-Jean Gros- Napoléon Bonaparte visitant les pestiférés de Jaffa.jpg` — confirm correct work by eye at gate 6 (multiple Gros files on Commons).
- David reportedly found Gros's direction troubling and said he was "straying from the true path." Gros eventually committed suicide in 1835, partly from professional despair as the Romantic movement made his in-between position untenable. [Sources: Wikipedia "Antoine-Jean Gros"; Britannica "Antoine-Jean Gros"; Met Museum TOAH "Romanticism" essay.]
- Girodet (Anne-Louis Girodet de Roussy-Trioson, 1767–1824) is the other second-generation Davidian worth naming, particularly his *The Sleep of Endymion* (1791, Louvre) which takes a classical subject and dissolves it in dreamlike silvery moonlight — already Romantic in effect. [Sources: Wikipedia "Anne-Louis Girodet de Roussy-Trioson"; Louvre collections.]

**Placement:** Fits in chapter 3 ("The official style of the Revolution") or chapter 4 ("Ingres and the cult of line") of the Neoclassicism movement read — as the transitional note explaining that the Romantic impulse grew from within the Neoclassical tradition, not just against it.

### 2. Blake and Fuseli — the dark/visionary strand (Romanticism movement read)

The Romanticism narrative claims "dream and nightmare" as core themes but shows no painting of either. Blake and Fuseli are the visual evidence for that strand.

**Verified facts:**

**Henry Fuseli (Johann Heinrich Füssli)** (1741–1825, Swiss-born, worked in Britain)
- ***The Nightmare***, **1781**, Detroit Institute of Arts (version 1; a second version, 1790–91, is at the Städel Museum, Frankfurt). 101.6 × 127 cm (~3 ft 4 in × 4 ft 2 in). Oil on canvas.
  - Shows a woman draped unconscious on a bed, an incubus (goblin/demon) squatting on her chest, a wild-eyed horse peering through parted red curtains behind. The image is the era's most famous visualization of the nightmare, the supernatural, and the irrational — everything Enlightenment reason denied. Exhibited at the Royal Academy, London, 1782; caused an immediate sensation and was widely engraved.
  - Commons file: `File:John Henry Fuseli - The Nightmare.JPG` — PD worldwide (Fuseli d.1825). ✅
  - Dimensions: Detroit version 101.6 × 127 cm = ~3 ft 4 in × 4 ft 2 in. [Source: Detroit Institute of Arts collection page; Wikipedia "The Nightmare (Fuseli)".]
- Fuseli also translated Winckelmann's 1765 essay into English ("sedate grandeur" — already in the Neoclassicism fact pack). He was therefore literally the pivot between the two tendencies.
- Fuseli was made Keeper and later Professor at the Royal Academy; the institution accepted him as a mainstream figure, showing that the visionary/dark strand was not marginal.

**William Blake** (1757–1827, British)
- ***The Ancient of Days***, **1794** (frontispiece to *Europe: A Prophecy*). Relief etching with watercolor, multiple impressions made during Blake's lifetime. The Whitworth, Manchester holds a version; the British Museum and other institutions hold others. 23.3 × 16.8 cm (the Whitworth version).
  - Shows the creator-figure Urizen kneeling in the void, reaching down with a golden compass to measure the world — at once a critique of Enlightenment rationalism and one of the era's most recognizable images. Blake saw the Enlightenment's reduction of the world to measurement as a spiritual disaster: "May God us keep / From Single vision and Newton's sleep."
  - Commons file: `File:Europe a Prophecy copy D 1794 British Museum frontispiece.jpg` — PD worldwide (Blake d.1827). ✅
  - [Sources: Wikipedia "The Ancient of Days"; Tate Blake collection; British Museum collection.]
- Blake has no single "most important" painting in the conventional sense (he worked primarily as an engraver/illuminated poet), but The Ancient of Days is his most reproduced visual image and the one most readers will recognize.

**For the writer:** Blake and Fuseli represent the strand Romanticism always invokes — "dream and nightmare, the irrational, the inner life" — but that no other figure in the read actually embodies in paint. They belong in the "RISE" chapter or as a dedicated brief section showing that the dark/visionary current was active in Britain from the 1780s, predating Goya's turn. This also provides the British complement to Goya's nightmares — the same impulse surfacing in Spain and Britain at almost the same moment.

**Note on Fuseli's dual position:** Because Fuseli translated Winckelmann and spent time in Rome (Neoclassicism's school), his pivot to The Nightmare is itself a small illustration of how the two currents overlap — fitting the era's overall argument.

## THIN areas (not blocking but worth addressing)

### Goya's Disasters of War (Romanticism movement read — thin)
The fact pack names the Disasters of War in the RISE section but the narrative text skips them. The series (c.1810–1823, published posthumously 1863) is Goya's most sustained record of Napoleonic atrocity — 82 etchings of executions, famine, mutilation, rape, and aftermath — arguably the most complete anti-war visual document before the twentieth century. The Third of May is the more famous single image, but the Disasters are the more comprehensive statement. A sentence naming them, even without an inline figure, would address the thin.

**Verified fact:** *The Disasters of War* (*Los desastres de la guerra*), a series of 82 etchings, created c.1810–1820, not published until 1863 (by the Royal Academy of Fine Arts of San Fernando, Madrid), 35 years after Goya's death. The plates were kept by Goya's son Xavier. First edition: 1863. [Wikipedia "The Disasters of War"; Museo del Prado entry.]

### Delacroix's Massacre at Chios (Romanticism and Neoclassicism movement reads — thin)
The *Massacre at Chios* (*Scène des massacres de Scio*, 1824, Louvre, 417 × 354 cm) is the work Delacroix showed at the 1824 Salon alongside Ingres's *Vow of Louis XIII* — the two pictures made the rivalry visible to the Paris public. Ingres allegedly called it "the massacre of painting." The movement reads describe the Ingres/Delacroix rivalry as central but don't name the specific 1824 paintings that made it explicit. The narrative covers Sardanapalus (1827) and Liberty (1830) but skips the earlier, equally significant confrontation. SHOULD-CONSIDER.

**Verified fact:** *Massacre at Chios*, exhibited Salon of 1824; depicts Greek civilians massacred by Ottoman forces during the Greek War of Independence (1821–1829); Louvre INV 3823; 417 × 354 cm. [Wikipedia "Massacre at Chios"; Louvre collections.]

### Romantic nationalism/medievalism (Romanticism movement read — thin)
The narrative covers the national spread of the temperament (Spain, France, Britain, Germany) through its major landscape and history painters, but never names nationalism explicitly as a theme, and the medievalism strand (the German Romantics' interest in Gothic cathedrals, Arthurian legend, folk tales) is absent. Friedrich's nationalism is implied but not named. This is minor given the scope, but since the era-level fact pack specifically lists "the individual and the nation" as Romantic themes and the MeanwhileSheet mentions "the nation," this could be briefly addressed in the afterlife chapter.

### Géricault's portraits of the insane (Romanticism movement read — absent, SHOULD-CONSIDER)
Géricault's late series of portraits of patients at the Salpêtrière asylum (*Monomaniacs*, c.1819–1822) — ten painted clinical portraits of individuals with different forms of mental illness — represent an important Romantic interest in the individual psyche, the irrational, and the marginal. They are not essential for a movement survey that already covers Goya's madness theme and the Black Paintings, but they show Géricault's range beyond the Raft. SHOULD-CONSIDER for a future work-read on Géricault.

## Fact-pack blind spots

2. **Romanticism fact pack** — does not mention Blake or Fuseli anywhere. The "rise" section covers only Burke (theory) and Goya (Spain), then jumps to the French peak (Géricault, Delacroix). The visionary/supernatural British strand is unrepresented.

4. **Romanticism fact pack** — the Disasters of War series is named in the movement story (RISE) but has no canon entry, no verifiable image data, and is not quoted in the draft. Its posthumous publication date (1863) is also not flagged, which is important for framing Goya's reception.

**MUST-ADD 2 — Blake and Fuseli, Romanticism movement read**
The dark/visionary/supernatural strand. Fuseli's *The Nightmare* (1781, Detroit Institute of Arts; PD worldwide, Commons `File:John Henry Fuseli - The Nightmare.JPG`) is the era's most famous image of the irrational and predates Goya's dark turn. The Romanticism narrative promises "dream and nightmare" as core themes in chapter 1 and then never delivers a visual anchor for them. Without Blake and Fuseli, the movement read presents Romanticism as if it were only about landscape, historical catastrophe, and current events — missing the dimension of inner vision, the supernatural, and the unconscious that was a distinct and significant thread.
