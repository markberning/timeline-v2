### MUST-ADD findings

**M2-A — MUST-ADD: The *Westminster Gazette* review is attributed to the wrong person**

The draft attributes "a study in degradation" to "the critic for the *Westminster Gazette*" without naming the critic. Web research (L'Absinthe Wikipedia; New Criterion article; Mental Floss) confirms the review that used that phrase was written by **Walter Crane**, the prominent English Arts and Crafts designer and illustrator, writing in the *Westminster Gazette*. The draft also implies the review was only a newspaper dismissal; in fact, Crane's prominence made it a more pointed cultural flashpoint than the prose suggests — this was not an anonymous hack piece, it was a notable figure weighing in. Naming Crane would take one clause. His identity matters because it underscores how broadly the picture spread beyond mere art criticism into general moral discourse.

**Where it goes:** `show` chapter, the paragraph about the *Westminster Gazette* review. Suggested: "The review that landed hardest came from **Walter Crane** (1845–1915) — the prominent Arts and Crafts designer whose illustration work was as famous as his opinions — writing in the *Westminster Gazette*. Crane called it a 'study in degradation.'"

**Verified:** Wikipedia's L'Absinthe article; the New Criterion "Absinthe minded" essay; multiple sources give Crane as the author of the "study in degradation" line.

**M2-B — MUST-ADD: Fénéon interview venue is stated incorrectly**

The draft says Andrée gave her 1921 interview "at the **Théâtre Édouard VII** in Paris." The fact pack itself says this, citing Mental Floss. However, the Fénéon interview is typically cited as appearing in a publication — Fénéon was an art critic and editor, not a theatre manager. This detail deserves a flag: the "Théâtre Édouard VII" location is plausible as the venue where the interview took place (Andrée was an actress; she may have been working there), but the primary source is Fénéon's published interview, and what the reader needs confirmed is that it was a documented recorded interview, not an oral legend. The current prose presents the location as a verified fact; the fact pack tags it DOCUMENTED but its source is Mental Floss, not the primary source. The prose should either lightly hedge the venue ("at what Fénéon recorded as the Théâtre Édouard VII") or drop the venue name and simply say "in a 1921 interview with the critic Félix Fénéon." The incorrect or unverifiable specificity creates a fact-check risk.

**Where it goes:** `show` chapter, the Andrée-corrects-the-record paragraph. Easy edit: remove "at the Théâtre Édouard VII" or hedge it.

**Rating: 2 MUST-ADD (M2-A = Crane attribution; M2-B = Fénéon venue hedge). Both are one-clause fixes.**

### SHOULD-CONSIDER

**SC2-A — SHOULD-CONSIDER: The 1876 Brighton Royal Pavilion loan is mentioned in the fact pack but absent from the draft**

The fact pack documents a quiet first English showing at the Brighton Royal Pavilion Gallery in 1876, when Hill lent the picture shortly after buying it — and which generated no scandal whatsoever. ### MUST-ADD findings

**M3-A — MUST-ADD: Duncan Phillips "twelve years of pursuit" — the draft says ten years, web research says twelve**

The draft says Phillips "chased it for roughly a decade" and "pursued the picture for roughly a decade." The Phillips Collection's own website and web sources (including the Artnet article) consistently state he pursued it for approximately **twelve years** — he first saw it in Europe in **1911** and bought it in **1923**. **M3-B — MUST-ADD: Durand-Ruel sale price to Renoir — 6,000 francs — is absent**

The draft says Durand-Ruel "bought the picture from Renoir directly in February 1881" and paid "a fair price." The actual documented price Durand-Ruel paid was **6,000 francs** (Wikipedia cites this; the February 14, 1881 date and 6,000 fr price appear in multiple sources). This is a concrete, interesting number — it contextualizes the $125,000 Phillips paid 42 years later as a stunning appreciation. The draft names the Phillips purchase price ($125,000) but not the Durand-Ruel price, so the reader can't make the comparison. The 6,000 fr figure is not in the fact pack, which says only "bought directly from Renoir" — this is a gap in the fact pack the draft propagated.

**Where it goes:** `show` chapter, the Durand-Ruel acquisition paragraph. One sentence: "Durand-Ruel paid Renoir **6,000 francs** for it in February 1881 — a sum he would more than twenty times over when Phillips paid $125,000 in 1923." **Verified:** Wikipedia, *Luncheon of the Boating Party* provenance table; Wikiart; multiple sourcing.

**M3-C — MUST-ADD: The Maison Fournaise near-demolition and current status are mentioned but the 1970s date is unverified**

This is flagged in the draft's own fact ledger as an open item to confirm. Web research (France With Véro; Sortiraparis.com) confirms the restaurant + museum status is current, but the specific 1970s rescue date is not confirmed in the sources found. The Maison Fournaise museum officially reopened in **1990** per the Sortiraparis article and related sources; the restoration campaign spans the 1980s. The "1970s" framing may be slightly early. Either confirm the rescue decade to the 1980s or soften "1970s" to "several decades ago" to avoid a wrong decade.

**Where it goes:** `afterlife` chapter, Maison Fournaise footnote. This is the lowest priority of the three MUST-ADDs — it is one date in a footnote — but because the draft itself flagged it as unconfirmed, it should not ship as "1970s."

### MUST-ADD findings

**M4-A — MUST-ADD: Caillebotte bequest numbers are understated and the rejected paintings are not mentioned**

The draft describes the Caillebotte bequest fight and says the French State "accepted *part* of the bequest" in 1896. The prose says "the Saint-Lazare canvas made the cut" and notes "(One of the rejected paintings was a Cézanne.)" This is correct but thin on the specifics that make the story interesting.

The documented facts (Orsay exhibition page; Gustave Caillebotte Wikipedia; Art Institute of Chicago):
- Caillebotte's will left **approximately 67 paintings** to the French State.
- After negotiation, the State accepted **38 of those 67** in 1896 (some sources say 40, but the range is 38–40 depending on counting method; Wikipedia gives 38, the Orsay gives 40).
- The remaining **27–29 rejected canvases** were offered back to the French government in 1904 and again in 1908 — both times refused. They remained with the family.
- The full bequest included works by Monet, Renoir, Pissarro, Sisley, Degas, Manet, Cézanne, and Caillebotte's own paintings.

The draft says only "accepted part of the bequest including this Monet" and notes a rejected Cézanne. The numbers (67 offered / 38 accepted) are the ones that make the story land — that France rejected more than half of the foundational Impressionist collection offered to it. Without the numbers, the "fierce fight" described reads as abstract. The correct numbers should appear in `afterlife`.

**Where it goes:** `afterlife` chapter, the Caillebotte bequest paragraph. Suggested addition: "Of the **sixty-seven paintings** Caillebotte had left to the French State, the compromise accepted **thirty-eight**. The other twenty-nine — including a Cézanne — were offered back to the government twice more, in 1904 and 1908; France refused both times. Those pictures stayed with the family."

**Verified:** Wikipedia, "Gustave Caillebotte" article (bequest section); Musée d'Orsay exhibition page on the Caillebotte bequest; Art Institute of Chicago.

**M4-B — MUST-ADD: The Hoschedé provenance step is completely absent**

The draft's provenance in `afterlife` runs: "Monet sold *La Gare Saint-Lazare* (this Orsay version) directly to... **Gustave Caillebotte**. The price was **685 francs**." This is correct. But the fact pack's own provenance table (step 1 under the Orsay version) reads: "Atelier Monet → **Ernest Hoschedé**, Paris, by 1877–78. (Hoschedé was the department-store-magnate patron whose bankruptcy auction the next year scattered the great early Impressionist collection.)" The draft skips entirely to the Caillebotte transaction. The fact pack notes that the Hoschedé connection applies to *some* versions of the series — the Chicago *Arrival of the Normandy Train* was bought by Hoschedé in March 1877, while the Orsay version was sold to Caillebotte on 10 March 1878. Web research confirms the Orsay canvas went directly to Caillebotte; the Hoschedé step in the fact pack appears to apply to a different canvas in the series.

However: the fact pack lists Hoschedé as step 1 for the Orsay version specifically. This is a provenance discrepancy between the fact pack and the Wikipedia/Orsay sources that the draft resolved by skipping Hoschedé entirely. **The draft should either (a) explicitly note the Hoschedé → Drouot bankruptcy sale of June 1878 as context for why Caillebotte was acquiring that month, even if Caillebotte bought from Monet directly; or (b) note that the fact pack's Hoschedé step may refer to a different canvas and flag this for the fact-checker.** The current prose is incomplete on this point, and the Hoschedé bankruptcy sale is colorful, important context: it is the reason the early Impressionist collection was scattered, and it provides the backdrop for Caillebotte's acquisitions.

**Where it goes:** `afterlife`, between the Monet-studio sentence and the Caillebotte paragraph. One sentence: "This was also the month of the **Hoschedé bankruptcy sale** at the Hôtel Drouot — the auction that scattered the great early Impressionist collection accumulated by department-store magnate **Ernest Hoschedé**, who had gone bankrupt and whose Impressionist pictures now went for fractions of their value. Caillebotte was buying in that same distressed market, though he acquired this Monet directly from the artist." This does not require resolving the ambiguity in the fact pack; it contextualizes the purchase moment correctly.

**Verified:** Wikipedia, "Ernest Hoschedé"; Wikipedia, "Gare Saint-Lazare (Monet series)"; Art Institute of Chicago, Cat. 16 (confirms Hoschedé bought the Chicago version in March 1877; the Orsay went to Caillebotte from Monet on 10 March 1878).

**Rating: 2 MUST-ADD (M4-A = Caillebotte bequest 67/38 numbers; M4-B = Hoschedé bankruptcy context).**

## Top 5 MUST-ADD items (ranked by importance to the reader)

**#1 — M4-A: Caillebotte bequest numbers (Gare Saint-Lazare)**
67 paintings offered, 38 accepted; 29 rejected and refused twice more. Without the numbers the "fierce fight" is abstract. This is the founding story of what is now the Musée d'Orsay's Impressionist collection; the reader deserves the specific stakes.

**#2 — M3-B: Durand-Ruel paid Renoir 6,000 francs in February 1881 (Boating Party)**
The draft gives the $125,000 Phillips price but not the 6,000 fr Durand-Ruel price; without the baseline, the forty-two-year appreciation story loses its punch. One number, one clause.

**#3 — M2-A: Walter Crane is the author of "a study in degradation" (L'Absinthe)**
Attributing the most famous review of the 1893 scandal to "the critic for the *Westminster Gazette*" is anonymizing what was in fact a pointed intervention by a prominent named figure. Naming Crane takes five words and gives the reader a real person to locate in context.

**#4 — M4-B: Hoschedé bankruptcy sale context (Gare Saint-Lazare)**
The June 1878 Hoschedé sale is the moment the early Impressionist market cracked open, and Caillebotte was buying into it. One sentence of context makes the Caillebotte purchase intelligible as an act in a specific art-market moment, not just "a friend bought it."

**#5 — M3-A: Duncan Phillips pursued the painting for twelve years, not ten (Boating Party)**
The Phillips Collection's own website uses "twelve years" and a 1911 first-sighting; the draft says "roughly a decade." A small correction with a larger payoff: the anchor-painting-pursuit is the whole point of the Phillips story, and the right number is in the Phillips Collection's own text.
