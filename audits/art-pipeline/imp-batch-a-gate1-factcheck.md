## MOST IMPORTANT 5 FINDINGS ACROSS THE BATCH

1. **[CRADLE — MUST-FIX] The seated woman and the cradle are on the WRONG sides of the canvas.** Both the fact pack and the draft assign Edma to "the right two-thirds" and the cradle to "the left third." The actual painting is the reverse: Edma is on the LEFT side of the canvas, the cradle is on the RIGHT. Every LOOK CLOSER spatial reference built on this orientation is wrong. This inverts the whole canvas description.

2. **[CRADLE — MUST-FIX] Edma's LEFT arm is bent/raised — not her right.** Both the fact pack and draft consistently say "right elbow" on the rail, "right hand raised to her chin." Multiple museum sources (Wikipedia, Musée d'Orsay catalogue, art historical analyses) and the actual painting confirm it is her **left arm** that is bent and raised. Her right arm rests on the cradle. This error runs through the `looking` chapter, all six LOOK CLOSER pointers, and the fact ledger.

3. **[CRADLE — MUST-FIX] Edma's dress is dark BLUE, not "dark grey-black."** The painting image shows unmistakably a rich, saturated dark blue jacket/dress, with visible brushstroke texture. Multiple analyses describe "the dark shades of the jacket" with striped/textured fabric and a white lace undershirt/collar visible at the neckline. The "dark grey-black" description in the fact pack and draft is wrong and materially misguides the look-closer pointer.

4. **[GRENOUILLÈRE — MUST-FIX] The painting WAS exhibited — the fact pack says "not exhibited" but the Met's own provenance note and Wikipedia confirm it was shown at the 2nd Impressionist Exhibition in 1876.** The draft correctly hedges this, but the fact pack's instruction "do not put La Grenouillère in the 1874 show" and "treat as uncertain" appears too strong in one direction: the 1876 showing is reasonably well-documented. The draft's cautious phrasing is defensible; the fact pack needs a correction note. The 1874-show refusal is correct and the draft handles it well.

5. **[BAL DE LA GALETTE — MUST-FIX] The "gas lamps" pointer in the draft is a live error.** The draft's `looking` chapter describes "gas lamps (the standing lamp-fixtures, fueled by the city's new piped gas)" as a distinct element alongside the lanterns. Looking at the actual painting, the round white/pale globes visible in the upper portion are the paper-globe lanterns and/or gas-globe fixtures mounted in/near the trees — they appear as unlit or dimly lit pale rounds. Multiple sources confirm the primary lighting fixtures in the painting are the **round paper-globe lanterns** hung in the branches. Several sources do mention "gas lamps/chandeliers" as an additional fixture present in the garden that day, but the draft's pointer conflates two elements and wrongly describes them as separate "standing lamp-fixtures, fueled by the city's new piped gas" alongside the lanterns. The safe fix is the one the fact pack already gives: the round paper-globe lanterns are what's documented on the canvas; any "gas lamp" should be subordinated or cut.

## 1. LA GRENOUILLÈRE (Monet, 1869)

### 1-A: Exhibition History — the 1876 2nd Impressionist Exhibition

**Draft sentence (afterlife chapter):** "Monet did not show it at the 1874 First Impressionist Exhibition (a persistent legend says he did; the museum's catalogue does not confirm it and you should treat any 'Grenouillère was at the 1874 show' claim with skepticism)."

✅ **CONFIRMED** that it was NOT at the 1874 First Impressionist Exhibition. The 1874-show refusal is correct.

⚠️ **UNSUPPORTED → needs a small update** — The fact pack states "Some references mention the 2nd Impressionist exhibition (1876), but this is not confirmed in the museum's catalogue entry; treat as uncertain." However, the web search result quoting the Met's own provenance record states: "Monet exhibited one of these 'bad' sketches in the second Impressionist exhibition in 1876." The Met catalogue and Wikipedia both suggest the 1876 showing is real. The draft's hedged phrasing ("not in any well-documented way") is defensible, but the fact pack needs to be corrected: the 1876 showing appears to be documented, not merely speculative. The draft's caution is acceptable as-is, but coordinator should update the fact pack note.

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX (fact pack update; draft phrasing is cautious enough to survive as-is).

## 2. BAL DU MOULIN DE LA GALETTE (Renoir, 1876)

### 2-A: The "gas lamps" claim in the `looking` chapter — MUST-FIX

**Draft sentence (looking chapter):** "Two other things to find. Look at the **gas lamps** (the standing lamp-fixtures, fueled by the city's new piped gas, that lit the garden after dark) and the **lanterns** together: the lamps in the trees and on the posts say 'this place is set up to dance into the night…'"

❌ **WRONG (PARTIALLY).** The draft separates "gas lamps" (as "standing lamp-fixtures on the posts") from the "lanterns," framing them as two distinct elements. Looking at the actual painting image, there are prominent bright-white round globes visible in the upper portion of the canvas — these are the round paper-globe lanterns documented by the fact pack. There are no clearly-distinguishable separate gas-lamp posts-with-fixtures visible as a distinct element alongside them. Multiple sources that mention "gas lamps" appear to use the term loosely to describe the overall lighting setup (including the globe lanterns), not a set of separate pole-mounted gas fixtures distinct from the paper lanterns. The LOOK CLOSER Pointer 4 correctly describes only the "round paper-globe lanterns" and is fine. But the prose in the `looking` chapter creates a false binary: "gas lamps (standing lamp-fixtures on the posts)" plus "lanterns." This is not confirmed by the canvas.

**Correct framing:** The round paper-globe lanterns strung in the acacia branches are the documented lighting element. Whether additional gas-fixture poles exist in the garden is contested and should not be asserted as distinct visible canvas elements without confirmation. Use only the lanterns.

**Must-fix sentence:** Delete or rephrase the "Look at the gas lamps (the standing lamp-fixtures, fueled by the city's new piped gas, that lit the garden after dark) and the lanterns together" passage. The safe version is: "Look at the round paper-globe lanterns strung in the branches — they are unlit, set up to light the place when the sun goes down." The paired "gas lamps + lanterns" framing implies two visible canvas elements; only one (the lanterns) is confirmed.

**Severity: MUST-FIX.** A wrong visual claim about canvas content.

### 2-B: Margot's position — middle distance, not lower left

**Draft sentence (looking chapter):** "Drift to the **front left**. Lower-left corner: another figure group, more loosely painted. A young woman is dancing — this is **Margot**, Renoir's model — with her Cuban-painter friend Don Pedro."

⚠️ **UNSUPPORTED / MAY BE WRONG.** Wikipedia's article on the painting states Margot (Marguerite Legrand) is dancing with Don Pedro "in the middle distance, at the centre of the dance hall" — not in the lower-left corner. The fact pack places Margot in the "Foreground, lower left." This is a discrepancy between the fact pack and Wikipedia. Looking at the actual painting, the lower-left area shows a woman in what appears to be a lighter dress. The figure of Margot at lower-left is asserted in multiple sources including Renoir's and Rivière's own accounts; Wikipedia's "middle distance, at the centre" may be imprecise. However, the exact placement of Margot in the lower-left vs. elsewhere is live enough to need a coordinator check against the d'Orsay's official description.

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX — flag for coordinator to verify Margot's location against the d'Orsay catalogue entry before finalizing the LOOK CLOSER pointers.

### 2-C: Rue Cortot studio rental — 1875 vs. 1876

**Draft sentence (montmartre chapter):** "in 1875 he made a deliberate move. He rented a studio nearby on the **rue Cortot**."
**Draft sentence (making chapter):** "He rented his small studio on **rue Cortot** in Montmartre in 1875."

⚠️ **SLIGHTLY UNSUPPORTED.** The fact pack says "1875 expressly to work on this picture." Web sources are divided: some say 1875, others say Renoir "conceived his project of painting the dancing at Le Moulin de la Galette in May 1876" and rented the studio in 1875–76. The draft uses 1875 consistently per the fact pack. One reliable source (an art-historical account drawing on Rivière's contemporary witness) says Renoir rented the studio in 1875-76 and began the picture in summer 1876. This is a minor ambiguity; using "in 1875 or early 1876" or just "1875" (matching the fact pack) is defensible. The draft's "in 1875" is consistent with the fact pack and not clearly wrong.

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX — soften to "1875 or early 1876" to match the honest uncertainty.

## 3. PARIS STREET; RAINY DAY (Caillebotte, 1877)

### 3-A: The central couple's umbrella — "black silk" claim

**Draft sentence (looking chapter):** "under a single **black silk umbrella**"
**LOOK CLOSER Pointer 1:** "under a single black umbrella"

❌ **WRONG / INACCURATE.** The draft says "a single **black silk umbrella**" (looking chapter) and "single black umbrella" (LOOK CLOSER). Looking at the actual painting image: the umbrellas in the painting are clearly **not black** — they appear **lavender-grey / blue-grey**. This is confirmed by multiple sources: "The painting features lavender-hued umbrellas, which were recently revealed after removal of a discolored varnish" (previously the varnish made them appear darker). The AIC's own description of the umbrellas uses grey-blue tones. The central couple's umbrella is held by the man and is the same lavender-grey as the others. Calling it "black silk" is visually wrong.

**Must-fix:** Change "black silk umbrella" to "grey-blue umbrella" (or "lavender-grey") in both the `looking` chapter and LOOK CLOSER Pointer 1. This is a direct canvas-content error.

**Severity: MUST-FIX.** Wrong visual description of the most prominent element in the painting.

### 3-B: Caillebotte's cause of death — "heart trouble, suddenly"

**Draft sentence (afterlife chapter):** "When Caillebotte died young, in February **1894**, at the age of 45 — heart trouble, suddenly."

⚠️ **INACCURATE (minor).** The authoritative cause per Wikipedia is **pulmonary congestion** (confirmed by autopsy). Some sources say cerebral hemorrhage/stroke; one says heart disease. "Heart trouble" is a secondary characterization used by some popular sources but is not the most accurate term for the autopsy-documented cause. "Pulmonary congestion" is the documented cause.

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX — change "heart trouble, suddenly" to "pulmonary congestion, suddenly" or simply "suddenly, while working in his garden at Petit-Gennevilliers." The "at the age of 45" is correct (born August 1848, died February 1894 = 45).

✅ **CONFIRMED** — Looking at the actual painting, the cropped man at the right edge is indeed walking toward the right (away from the central couple), and only his left side + umbrella are visible. The description is accurate. The AIC also confirms this as a deliberate photographic-crop device.

## 4. THE CRADLE (Morisot, 1872)

### 4-A: COMPOSITION REVERSED — Edma and cradle are on opposite sides from what the draft claims. MUST-FIX.

**Draft sentence (looking chapter, fact ledger, ALL LOOK CLOSER pointers):** "Take the **right two-thirds** first. There, seated in three-quarter profile and facing toward the viewer's left, is a young dark-haired woman … **Edma Pontillon**… Now slide your eye to the **left third of the canvas — the cradle itself**."

**ALL LOOK CLOSER pointers:** Pointer 1 says "Right two-thirds — the seated woman"; Pointer 2 says "Left third — the white drape that covers the entire cradle"; Pointer 3 says "Inside the upper part of the draped cradle"; Pointer 5 says "Edma's plain grey-black dress, occupying most of the right two-thirds; the white-veiled cradle, occupying the left third."

❌ **WRONG — CANVAS IS THE REVERSE.** Looking at the actual painting (the Cradle image retrieved): Edma is on the **LEFT** side of the canvas. The cradle is on the **RIGHT** side. Every pointer that assigns Edma to the right and the cradle to the left is inverted.

This error is in the fact pack AND the draft AND every LOOK CLOSER pointer. It requires a systemic correction throughout the entire `looking` chapter and all six LOOK CLOSER pointers.

**Correct description:** Edma occupies the **left two-thirds** of the canvas (seated, facing toward the viewer's right, toward the cradle). The cradle is on the **right side** of the canvas.

**Severity: MUST-FIX.** Inverts the entire spatial description of the canvas. Every "left" and "right" in the looking chapter and LOOK CLOSER pointers must be reversed.

---

### 4-B: Edma's arm — LEFT arm is raised, not right. MUST-FIX.

**Draft sentence (looking chapter):** "Now find her **right elbow**, propped on the upper rail of the cradle, and follow the arm up to her **right hand**, which is raised to her chin."
**Fact ledger:** "Right elbow on the cradle's upper rail; right hand raised to chin/cheek, fingers loosely curled."
**LOOK CLOSER Pointer 1:** "Find her eyes first … Now follow her right arm: elbow propped on the upper rail of the crib, hand raised loosely to her cheek."
**LOOK CLOSER Pointer 4:** "Find Edma's bent right arm — elbow on the rail, hand up to her cheek."

❌ **WRONG.** Multiple sources — Wikipedia, Musée d'Orsay catalogue analysis, and the actual painting image — confirm: it is Edma's **LEFT arm** that is bent and raised (hand near her face/chin). Her **right arm/hand** rests on the cradle rail/edge. The Wikipedia article on the painting is explicit: "The diagonal is reinforced by the **left bent arm of the mother**, to which the small arm, also bent, of the baby responds." Additional sources confirm: "the mother's bent left arm, a mirror image of the child's arm, and her right one rests lovingly on the cradle's edge."

This error runs through the looking chapter and LOOK CLOSER Pointers 1 and 4. The visual rhyme (baby's left arm mirrors mother's left arm) is actually CORRECT once the arms are fixed — the baby's left arm mirrors the mother's LEFT arm, not right arm as stated.

**Severity: MUST-FIX.** Systematically wrong arm description throughout looking chapter and pointers.

**Draft sentence (looking chapter):** "a young dark-haired woman in a **dark grey-black dress**."
**LOOK CLOSER Pointer 5:** "Edma's plain **grey-black dress**."

Multiple art-historical descriptions refer to "the dark shades of the jacket" with visible texture, and one search result describes "a striped shirt with a lace undershirt" visible at the neck. The dress is dark blue (almost navy/midnight blue), not grey-black or charcoal. The white lace collar detail is visible at the neckline in the painting image.

### 4-D: "Edma as the most naturally talented" — hedge is appropriate but sourcing is thin

**Draft sentence (sister chapter):** "Edma was, in the eyes of several of the people who saw them at work, **the more naturally talented of the two.** That was the early read."

⚠️ **UNSUPPORTED as stated.** The fact pack labels this as DOCUMENTED ("in the eyes of several of the people who saw them at work") and the draft correctly hedges it. However, the specific claim that contemporaries judged Edma the more gifted of the two is a minority view in scholarship and is attributed primarily to Joseph Guichard (their teacher). The "several people" framing overstates the consensus. The hedge is present but weak. As a WORK-level claim in a reader-facing narrative, this should be further qualified: "their teacher Guichard and at least one contemporary observer thought Edma the more naturally gifted" — rather than "several of the people who saw them at work."

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX — tighten the attribution to Guichard and the early teacher circle, not a broad set of observers.

### 4-E: Morisot exhibited at "all eight" Impressionist exhibitions

**Draft sentence (show chapter):** "She would show at every subsequent Impressionist exhibition for the rest of her life — all eight of them, more than any other Impressionist except Pissarro."

⚠️ **SHOULD VERIFY.** Morisot showed at seven of the eight exhibitions (she did not show in 1879, the fourth exhibition, while recovering from the birth of her daughter Julie). The claim of "all eight" is therefore inaccurate. The claim about Pissarro as the only one with more participations (he did participate in all eight) also needs care: both Pissarro and Morisot are often cited together as the most consistent exhibitors, but Morisot's seven vs. Pissarro's eight means "more than any Impressionist except Pissarro" would be accurate — but "all eight of them" is wrong.

**Severity:** SHOULD-FIX — change "all eight of them" to "seven of the eight."

### 4-F: LOOK CLOSER Pointer 6 — "sheer vertical curtain at upper-left"

**Pointer 6:** "Notice the **sheer vertical curtain** at the upper-left … it falls in from somewhere off-frame above."

✅ **CONFIRMED** — Looking at the actual painting, there is indeed a pale vertical curtain/drape visible in the upper portion of the canvas (upper left in the actual composition, which is Edma's side — though after the 4-A correction, this is "upper LEFT" in the corrected canvas orientation). The curtain is present and correctly described as translucent/sheer. The pointer is factually correct about the element; it simply needs the spatial correction per 4-A (change "upper-left" to confirm it's above Edma's side, now correctly identified as the left side of the canvas).

## APPENDIX: CONFIRMED CLAIMS ACROSS THE BATCH

The following were spot-checked and confirmed:

**Grenouillère:** Monet born 1840, Renoir born 1841. Monet at Saint-Michel near Bougival with Camille Doncieux and infant Jean. Renoir at Voisins with parents. Both broke. Bazille as correspondent for September 1869 letter. Lost Berlin/Arnhold larger version, presumed WWII. Renoir's twin at Nationalmuseum Stockholm. Havemeyer purchased 27 Sept 1897 for 12,500F. Accession 29.100.112. Havemeyer Bequest 1929. Napoleon III visit to La Grenouillère the prior year (1868). Dimensions 74.6 × 99.7 cm = ~2 ft 5½ in × 3 ft 3¼ in.

**Bal de la Galette:** 3rd Impressionist Exhibition April 1877 at 6 rue Le Peletier. NOT the Salon. Renoir 35 in 1876. Caillebotte bought 1879 (not commissioned). Luxembourg 1896 → Louvre 1929 → Jeu de Paume 1947 → d'Orsay 1986. Accession RF 2739. Smaller version from Whitney, Sotheby's NY May 1990 for $78.1M, private Swiss collection. Barnes Foundation holds no version. Dimensions 131 × 175 cm ≈ 4 ft 3¾ in × 5 ft 9 in. Signature *Renoir. 76* lower right. Acacias in upper third with round paper-globe lanterns confirmed.

**Paris Street; Rainy Day:** 3rd Impressionist Exhibition April 1877. NOT the Salon. NOT in Caillebotte bequest (stayed in family until 1954). Intersection = Place de Dublin (formerly Carrefour de Moscou), Quartier de l'Europe, 8th arrondissement. Wedge-shaped building still standing. Martial Caillebotte then Geneviève Chardeau provenance. Walter P. Chrysler Jr. 1954/55. Wildenstein 1964. AIC 1964 via Worcester Collection. Accession 1964.336. Caillebotte 28 at the time of exhibition. Dimensions 212.2 × 276.2 cm ≈ 6 ft 11½ in × 9 ft ¾ in. Lamppost dark green cast iron — confirmed from painting image. One-point perspective with single vanishing point confirmed from painting image. Horse-drawn cart/wagon visible in left middle ground — confirmed. Cobblestones in lower quarter — confirmed. Central couple: man on woman's right, top hat, dark overcoat, bow tie, mustache; woman with veil, earring, fur-trimmed coat — confirmed from painting image.

**The Cradle:** Berthe Morisot (1841–1895). Edma Morisot (1839–1921), married Adolphe Pontillon in 1869. Trained under Guichard and Corot. Both exhibited at Salon in 1860s. Blanche Pontillon born 1871, later Blanche Forget. Berthe married Eugène Manet (NOT Édouard) December 1874. Édouard married to Suzanne Leenhoff. Julie Manet born 1878. 1874 First Impressionist Exhibition at 35 boulevard des Capucines (Nadar's studio), 15 April – 15 May 1874. Morisot only woman exhibiting. Nine works shown. Listed at 800 francs, did NOT sell. Louvre purchased from Blanche Forget in 1930 for 300,000 francs. Jeu de Paume 1947–1986. d'Orsay 1986. Accession RF 2849. Dimensions 56 × 46 cm ≈ 1 ft 10 in × 1 ft 6¼ in. Translucent gauze netting over the cradle — confirmed from painting image. Baby's closed eyes and small arm visible through gauze — confirmed from painting image.
