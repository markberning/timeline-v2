## 2. THE STORY

A founding image of **British Pop art** — widely called "the first work of pop art to achieve iconic status" (Wikipedia). Hamilton built it in 1956 for the *This Is Tomorrow* exhibition catalogue, working entirely from images cut out of **American magazines**.

The picture is a **modern living room turned into a catalogue of consumer desire**. Inside it:
- A **bodybuilder** posed on the carpet, holding an **oversized Tootsie Pop lollipop** whose wrapper reads **"POP"** — he is the male ideal as a sales image. (The figure is the real bodybuilder **Irvin "Zabo" Koszewski**, photographed in *Tomorrow's Man* magazine, Sept. 1954.) (Wikipedia)
- A **burlesque pin-up** seated/reclining on the sofa — the female body likewise sold as product. (Wikipedia)
- A **television set** (a Stromberg-Carlson model in the source ad). (Wikipedia)
- A **tape recorder** (Boosey & Hawkes) on the floor. (Wikipedia)
- A **Hoover vacuum-cleaner ad**, its hose stretched absurdly up the staircase ("Ordinary cleaners reach only this far"). (Wikipedia)
- A **comic-book cover** framed on the wall like a painting — a **Jack Kirby *Young Romance* cover**. (Wikipedia)
- A **tinned ham** sitting on the coffee table as if it were a centerpiece. (commonly catalogued)
- The **Moon / planet Earth** filling the ceiling — the cosmos pulled indoors. (Wikipedia notes "planet Earth" as the ceiling element.)
- A **Ford emblem** and assorted brand marks scattered through the room.

The word **"POP"** on the lollipop is frequently cited as a likely source of the movement's name (see §8 for scope — this is a popular claim, not settled fact).

**Authorship / sourcing:** Hamilton **assembled and composed the collage himself**, but **John McHale** (a fellow Independent Group member, then in the US) had **supplied a stock of American magazines / a rough layout** that Hamilton drew the images from. Wikipedia: McHale "provided him with a rough layout for six pages for the *This Is Tomorrow* exhibition catalogue," of which Hamilton used two; **Magda Cordell and McHale are usually credited with feeding Hamilton the American source material.** Credit McHale fairly as image-supplier, not co-author (see §8).

A year later, Hamilton wrote his famous one-paragraph **definition of Pop** (§4), which reads almost as a caption to this very picture.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required — before / after)

**BEFORE:** Serious art still drew its imagery from "high" sources — the figure, landscape, myth, abstraction, the school of Paris. Even when modern life entered art, it was filtered through the artist's hand (Cubist still lifes, Léger's machine forms). Advertising and mass-media imagery were "low," beneath fine art — material for commercial designers, not gallery walls.

**AFTER:** Hamilton makes a finished art object **assembled entirely out of consumer and advertising imagery** — magazine ads, a comic-book cover, brand logos, a pin-up, a body-builder, a tinned-ham photo. Nothing is "drawn"; the artist's job becomes **selection and arrangement of the mass-produced image**. The domestic interior — the aspirational living room — is reframed as a **catalogue of desire**, every surface a product. This is the break Pop runs with: that the imagery of advertising and mass culture is now legitimate, even primary, art subject matter, and that **quotation/collage of found commercial images is itself the work**. Hamilton's 1957 list (§4) is the manifesto-in-miniature of that shift.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (gated — quote exactly)

Hamilton's definition of Pop, from his **letter to Alison and Peter Smithson, dated 16 January 1957** (verbatim, with his parentheticals):

> Pop Art is:
> Popular (designed for a mass audience)
> Transient (short-term solution)
> Expendable (easily-forgotten)
> Low cost
> Mass produced
> Young (aimed at youth)
> Witty
> Sexy
> Gimmicky
> Glamorous
> Big Business

— Richard Hamilton, letter to Peter and Alison Smithson, 16 January 1957 (source: WarholStars transcription; the list is reproduced widely, e.g. Tate/Foster).

**Variant flags (state plainly, do not silently merge):**
- The **date** is given as **16 January 1957** (WarholStars). Some sources say only "1957."
- **Punctuation/format varies** by source: some run it as a single inline sentence ("popular, transient, expendable, low-cost, mass-produced, young, witty, sexy, gimmicky, glamorous, and Big Business"); some drop the parentheticals; some add a closing line. The **stacked list with parentheticals above is the form Hamilton wrote in the letter** — prefer it, and note the inline form exists.
- "Expendable (easily-forgotten)" appears both hyphenated and as "easily forgotten." Minor.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6 — prose pointers, NO coordinates)

1. **The POP lollipop.** The bodybuilder grips a giant Tootsie-Pop lollipop the size of a tennis racket; its wrapper spells **POP** in block letters. It reads at once as a sweet, a phallic gag, and — pointed at the viewer — the **word that may have named the whole movement**.
2. **The muscleman.** The posed body-builder (real-life Mr. L.A. winner Zabo Koszewski, lifted from a physique magazine) stands in for the *male* body sold as an image — the masculine ideal as advertisement.
3. **The pin-up on the couch.** The burlesque/pin-up figure lounging on the sofa is the *female* counterpart — the body as product, mirroring the muscleman across the room.
4. **The TV and the tape recorder.** A boxy television and a reel-to-reel tape recorder sit in the room as the new domestic gods — entertainment and recording technology installed as furniture.
5. **The comic on the wall.** Where a family portrait or a landscape would hang, Hamilton frames a **romance-comic cover** (a Jack Kirby *Young Romance*) — pulp pinned up like a Rembrandt, the gallery hierarchy turned upside down.
6. **The Ford emblem, the Hoover ad, and the Moon ceiling.** Brand marks (a Ford badge) dot the room; a vacuum-cleaner ad runs its hose impossibly up the stairs; and overhead, the **ceiling is the Moon / planet Earth** — the cosmos itself pulled indoors and made part of the décor.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

1. **"The POP lollipop named the movement."** SCOPE IT — likely-but-not-proven. The term "Pop art" is variously credited to critic **Lawrence Alloway** (he used "pop art" in print in 1958, popularizing it) and to the **Smithsons/Independent Group**, and the **first artwork to contain the word "POP" was Eduardo Paolozzi's collage *I Was a Rich Man's Plaything* (1947)**, not Hamilton's. So: the "POP" on Hamilton's lollipop is a *celebrated visual coincidence often pointed to as a source of the name*, but the naming is **not definitively traceable to this collage**. Phrase as "often cited as a likely source," never as fact. (Wikipedia, "Pop art".)
2. **John McHale's contribution.** Credit fairly: McHale (with Magda Cordell) **supplied Hamilton the American magazines / source material and a rough catalogue layout**; **Hamilton selected, cut, and composed the collage.** Don't call McHale a co-author, but don't erase him either — the image bank was largely his. (Wikipedia.)
3. **Multiple later versions.** Hamilton **reworked the subject repeatedly.** The wired image must be the **1956 ORIGINAL collage** (Kunsthalle Tübingen), NOT: the **1992** digital reworking *Just what is it that makes today's homes so different?* (made with Quantel Paintbox, featuring female body-builder **Bernie Price**; in Tate, P11358), nor the **2004** *Just what was it that made yesterday's homes so different, so appealing? (upgrade)*. Verify the file is the b&w-era 1956 paper collage, not a later color/digital version. (Wikipedia; Tate.)
4. **The title's punctuation.** It is a **question** ending in a question mark, with the comma: *"…so different, so appealing?"* Lowercase interior words. Don't drop the comma or the "?" and don't capitalize it as a normal title.
5. **Dimensions.** Small — **~26 × 24.8 cm (10¼ × 9¾ in)**. Sources round inconsistently (260 mm vs 248 mm sides) but agree on ~26 × ~25 cm. Do NOT inflate to a poster-sized work; the *poster/catalogue* reproductions were larger, but the **original collage is letter-paper sized.**
6. **The bodybuilder is not literally "Charles Atlas."** The brief's "Charles Atlas type" is a TYPE descriptor; the actual man is **Irvin "Zabo" Koszewski** from *Tomorrow's Man* (Sept. 1954). If naming him, name Koszewski; "a Charles-Atlas-style muscleman" is fine as a description but is not his identity.
