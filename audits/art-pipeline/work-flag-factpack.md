## 1. CORE FACTS

- **Title:** *Flag*
- **Artist:** Jasper Johns (American, born Augusta, Georgia, 1930 — **still living** as of 2026; do NOT write a death date).
- **Date:** **1954–55** (MoMA's title line reads "1954-55"; the canvas is **dated 1954 on the reverse**). [MoMA]
- **Medium (MoMA's exact line):** **"Encaustic, oil, and collage on fabric mounted on plywood, three panels."** [MoMA]
  - *Encaustic* = pigment suspended in **hot/melted wax**. [MoMA audio; Smarthistory]
  - The collage element is **strips of newspaper / newsprint** (and bits of cloth) dipped in the hot wax and affixed to the fabric, then worked over in encaustic — so the newsprint is visible *through* the wax. The support fabric is widely described as a **cut bedsheet**. [MoMA; Wikipedia]
- **Dimensions:** **42¼ × 60⅝ in** = **107.3 × 153.8 cm**. [MoMA]
  - Ft/in for the reader: **~3 ft 6¼ in × 5 ft ⅝ in** (≈ 3.5 ft tall × 5.05 ft wide). Convert as **"about 3½ by 5 feet"** in prose if a round phrase is wanted.
- **Current location:** **The Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York.**
- **Credit line:** **"Gift of Philip Johnson in honor of Alfred H. Barr, Jr."** — acquired **1973**. [MoMA; multiple corroborating sources]
- **Object number:** MoMA **78805** (collection record URL).

⚑ Note the three-panel construction is part of the official medium line — it is a verifiable looking-point, not a guess.

## 2. THE STORY

Johns said the painting began in a **dream**: *"One night I dreamed that I painted a large American flag, and the next morning I got up and I went out and bought the materials to begin it."* (See §4 for the quote and its sourcing caveat.) He was about 24. In **1954 he destroyed essentially all the work he then owned** and started over with motifs he called *"things the mind already knows"* — flags, targets, numbers, letters, maps: ready-made, common images he didn't invent. [MoMA course; Smarthistory; Art History Unstuffed]

Technically the picture is built, not just painted. Johns penciled the flag's outline onto fabric (a cut bedsheet) mounted on plywood, then laid down **strips of newspaper dipped in molten wax** and worked colored **encaustic** over them. Because wax cools fast, each stroke stays put instead of blending, so the surface is **lumpy, dripped, and crusted**, and you can read **fragments of newsprint** under the red, white, and blue. He reportedly steered clear of headlines or political stories, using inconsequential clippings and ads. [MoMA audio; Wikipedia]

The work poses its famous question: **is it a flag, or is it a painting OF a flag?** The image is flat, frontal, and exactly coextensive with the canvas — there's no scene, no horizon, no figure standing in front of a flag. The thing depicted and the object you're looking at occupy the same rectangle. That collapse is the whole point. [Smarthistory; The Broad]

It functioned as a **bridge OUT of Abstract Expressionism**. Where the AbEx generation (Pollock, de Kooning) made the canvas a field of personal, gestural emotion, Johns took an impersonal, public sign and handled it with what The Broad calls an **"analytic, cool, and unsentimental"** touch. Critics read the painting as pointing forward to **Pop, Minimalism, and Conceptual art**. [The Broad; TheArtStory; Whitney]

The acquisition story is part of the lore. **Alfred H. Barr, Jr.**, MoMA's founding director, wanted *Flag* (and other works) out of Johns's **first solo show at the Leo Castelli Gallery, 1958**. But MoMA's committee/trustees worried the work might be read as **"unpatriotic"** (a flag, painted, in the Cold War). Barr's workaround: he got architect **Philip Johnson** to buy *Flag* privately, with the understanding it would come to the museum later. **In 1973, as Barr was retiring, Johnson gave it to MoMA in his honor** — which is exactly what the credit line records. [Sothebys; MoMA; mentalfloss]

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (before / after — REQUIRED)

**Before:** the dominant American mode was **Abstract Expressionism** — large, gestural, non-representational canvases meant to register the artist's inner state and the act of painting itself (Pollock's drips, de Kooning, Rothko's fields). The painting pointed *inward* to feeling and *away* from recognizable subjects.

**The break:** Johns made the **whole subject a common, flat, already-known SIGN** — the U.S. flag — rendered at full size, frontal, edge-to-edge. There is no composition to invent (the flag's design is given), no expressive scene, no background. The result is that **the painting IS its own image**: depiction and object collapse into one rectangle. And the handling is **cool, detached, deadpan** — the opposite of AbEx angst — even though the wax surface is intensely, visibly worked by hand.

**After:** by treating a mass-culture image as fair subject matter, *Flag* opened the door to **Pop Art** (Warhol, Lichtenstein, taking soup cans and comics straight from the culture) and, via its literalness and seriality, to **Minimalism and Conceptual art**. It is routinely cited as one of the hinge works of postwar art. [Smarthistory; TheArtStory; Whitney]

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced)

**Primary key passage — the dream account:**

> "One night I dreamed that I painted a large American flag, and the next morning I got up and I went out and bought the materials to begin it."
> — Jasper Johns

- ⚑ **Sourcing caveat (use in the fact-check ledger, not the reader):** This is the canonical wording, now enshrined in **MoMA's own text** for the work, and quoted by virtually every museum (The Broad reproduces it identically). It is Johns's *own account*, but the exact phrasing has **varied across his retellings** — in a 1966 NET interview he gave the looser "one night I dreamed I painted a flag and I got up and I painted a flag," and even teased the interviewer with "Which story? Which one do you know?" Attribute it as **"Johns has said"** / per MoMA, and do not over-specify the date or interviewer. [MoMA wall text via greg.org; The Broad; greg.org analysis]

**Secondary key statement — his working motto (1964 sketchbook):**

> "Take an object. Do something to it. Do something else to it."
> — Jasper Johns, sketchbook note, **1964**

- This is a slightly *later* statement of method, not a statement made *about Flag* in 1954. Use it as a window on his procedure, dated honestly to 1964. [Duke continuing-studies course note; widely anthologized]

**The "ready-made image" idea:**

> Johns painted "things the mind already knows" — images "seen but not looked at, not examined."
> [paraphrase of Johns, per MoMA / Art History Unstuffed — the exact phrase "things the mind already knows" is the quotable fragment; the longer gloss is curatorial.]

**Flag variants (for context, not this work):** Johns returned to the motif many times — e.g. *White Flag* (1955, Met), *Three Flags* (1958, Whitney), *Flag* (1960s versions, incl. The Broad's 1967 panel), and the *Flags* prints. The MoMA *Flag* (1954–55) is the **first**. [Met; Whitney; The Broad]

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6 — prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **The 48-star field fills the canvas.** The blue canton sits where it always does, top-left, but the flag isn't *in* a picture — it *is* the picture, running clean to all four edges with no border, sky, or pole. (Count the stars: **48**, not 50 — see §8.)
2. **The encaustic / wax texture.** Look at how thick and uneven the colored surface is — drips, ridges, crusts of pigmented wax. Because the wax set as he worked, each stroke froze in place instead of smoothing out.
3. **Newspaper showing through.** Under the stripes and the blue, you can read scraps of **newsprint** — actual collaged paper dipped in wax. The mass-produced word-fabric of the everyday literally underlies the national symbol.
4. **A handmade surface under a machine-made design.** The flag's design is rigid and official; Johns's touch is anything but. The tension between the **regular, known pattern** and the **brushy, irregular, obviously-by-hand** surface is the painting's engine.
5. **The three joined panels.** It's built on **three separate panels** mounted on plywood — a seam-and-construction fact, not one continuous sheet. The flag is fabricated, like a flag is.
6. **No background, no depth, no scene.** Nothing recedes; nothing sits in front of anything. The absence of any setting is what forces the "is it a flag or a painting?" question.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (corrected, sourced)

- **It's a 48-STAR flag — and that's CORRECT for 1954.** The U.S. flag had 48 stars from 1912 until 1959 (Alaska and Hawaii were admitted 1959–60). A reader expecting 50 stars may "catch" an error that isn't one — the **48-star canton is historically right** for the date and is itself a looking-point. Do **not** write "50 stars." [Wikipedia; mentalfloss]
- **Medium = encaustic over collaged NEWSPAPER, on fabric/plywood — not "oil on canvas."** Repeating the lazy "oil on canvas" loses the whole point of the surface. Use MoMA's line: **encaustic, oil, and collage on fabric mounted on plywood, three panels.** [MoMA]
- **The dream origin is Johns's OWN account, with caveats.** It is genuinely his story (and MoMA's text), but the **exact wording varies across his retellings**, and the *original* documented source is not cleanly pinned to one interview. Attribute as "Johns has said" / per MoMA; don't fabricate a precise interview/date. [greg.org]
- **Is Johns "Pop"? Scope it.** Johns is the file's Pop-Art work-read, but art-historically he is usually classed as **Neo-Dada / proto-Pop**, not a card-carrying Pop artist like Warhol or Lichtenstein. *Flag* (1954–55) **predates** Pop Art's emergence and helped make it possible. Safe framing: **"a bridge out of Abstract Expressionism toward Pop"** / **"proto-Pop / Neo-Dada."** Don't flatly call him "a Pop artist" without that qualifier. [TheArtStory; Whitney; Art History Unstuffed]
- **Dimensions.** Use **107.3 × 153.8 cm = 42¼ × 60⅝ in ≈ 3½ × 5 ft.** Do **not** borrow numbers from a *different* Johns flag — e.g. The Broad's 1967 *Flag* is a smaller, different object (33½ × 56¼ in); the Met's *White Flag* (1955) is larger. Keep this work's numbers to the MoMA 1954–55 painting. [MoMA; The Broad; Met]
- **"Cut bedsheet" support.** Commonly stated and consistent with MoMA's "fabric" — fine to mention as the fabric, but lead with MoMA's own word **"fabric."** [Wikipedia; MoMA]

## 9. STATS (3 suggested)

1. **48** — stars in the canton (correct U.S. flag, 1912–1959; reads "wrong" to a modern eye, which is part of the catch).
