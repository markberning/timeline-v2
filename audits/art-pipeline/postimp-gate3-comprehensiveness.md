# Gate 3 — COMPREHENSIVENESS AUDIT — Post-Impressionism

Auditor: Gate 3 (Comprehensiveness) of the art content pipeline.
Source: `audits/art-pipeline/postimp-draft.md` against
`audits/art-pipeline/postimp-factpack.md`.

Brief: a zero-knowledge reader walks through the page once. What does the
prose leave them not knowing that the fact pack supplied?

For each chapter: **MUST-ADD** = reader trips on a missing fact / unglossed
term. **SHOULD-ADD** = richer context the pack supplied and the prose
skipped. **NICE-TO-HAVE** = a beat that would land harder.

---

## Overall verdict

This is a strong draft. It uses the fact pack with discipline, hedges
correctly (Wheatfield-with-Crows-as-last; Naifeh-Smith; pycnodysostosis;
Seurat's cause of death; the legend-ledger items generally), it foregrounds
the "five answers, not one creed" framing the pack asked for, and it faces
the Gauguin colonial frame head-on. The headline gaps are concentrated in
**Chapter 1** (Chevreul / Rood / optical mixing introduced as a label without
the mechanism), **Chapter 7** (the wider Nabi cast is too thin: Roussel /
Ranson / Maillol / Rippl-Rónai land as a name-list with no functions, and
Bonnard's *japonard* nickname is dropped without saying why he earned it),
and **Chapter 8** (the Fry catalogue's most-quoted line — "aimed not at
illusion but at reality" — never appears, so the surrogate manifesto has no
quotable face). Several first-use gloss misses recur (Symbolism, Cubism,
Fauvism, *succès de scandale*, La Goulue's nickname, ukiyo-e). The Cassatt
1890 print show year is wrong (draft says 1890 but appends "over 700
works"; the pack does not source that figure — verify or drop).

## Chapter 1 — "After the moment, the structure" — **GAPS**

The chapter sets the rue-Laffitte room well, but it introduces three of the
four "answers" with their *labels* and skips the mechanisms the pack supplied.
A zero-knowledge reader who has never heard of pointillism / optical mixing /
*synthetism* still doesn't know how any of these techniques actually work
by the time the chapter ends.

### MUST-ADD

- **Optical mixing — the mechanism, not just the label.** The chapter says
  Seurat's *Grande Jatte* is "built out of millions of tiny separate dots of
  pure color, laid down like a mosaic. Not strokes. *Dots.* Step back four
  feet and it shimmers; step back ten and it locks into place." That's the
  visual; the *why* — **the eye does the mixing, not the palette, and the
  result is more luminous than any premixed pigment** — never lands. The
  pack §3 and §9 supply Chevreul (1839) and Rood (1879) as the named premise.
  At minimum, one sentence: "The premise was that small adjacent dots of
  pure pigment would combine in the eye into a more luminous tone than any
  color premixed on the palette — color theory walked out of Chevreul's
  Gobelins tapestry shop and into the studio." The draft *does* get to
  Chevreul/Rood in the Seurat one-paragraph at Ch 2 and again in Ch 6, but
  Chapter 1 introduces the technique and the chapter ends without it.
- **"Pointillism" is named but never defined here.** The word never appears
  in Chapter 1 — only "dots." A zero-knowledge reader does not know that the
  technique they are looking at has a name, and so cannot connect the rue-
  Laffitte room to the word the rest of the page will use.
- **Gauguin's Synthetism is one-clause-glossed but not explained.** The
  bullet says: "A Breton peasant kneeling in church doesn't *see* a Bible
  story; she *imagines* one. How do you paint imagination, not vision?"
  Lovely, but the *technique* (flat color areas + dark contours +
  cloisonnisme analogy) is held until Chapter 5. A one-clause technical
  gloss at first introduction — "flat zones of saturated color separated by
  dark contour lines, like stained glass" — would do it.
- **The reader has not been told what "Impressionism" actually was in
  technical terms.** The chapter says "the previous level of this read tells
  that story in full," which is fine *if* the integrator confirms the reader
  walking onto this page has already read the Impressionism page. If they
  haven't, the four-part "objection" bullets land on air. Either confirm the
  page is gated behind Impressionism in the reader's navigation, or add one
  pre-bullet sentence: "Impressionism, at the high-water mark, had been the
  art of catching the moment: broken color, complementary shadows, the
  flicker of light on a poppy field at four in the afternoon."

### SHOULD-ADD

- **Pissarro's role in getting Seurat into the 1886 show.** The chapter
  mentions Pissarro defended Seurat and "got called a deserter by his own
  friends," but doesn't say *why* this mattered: Pissarro was the only major
  Impressionist who took Seurat seriously enough to briefly try Divisionism
  himself in the late 1880s before reverting. That's the cross-civ /
  cross-movement parallel: a senior member of one movement crossing the
  floor to the next.
- **Why 1886 was "the last."** The chapter says it was the eighth and last
  group show, but a one-line *why* — the cooperative had been splintering
  for years; Monet had defected back to the Salon; the 1886 split between
  Pissarro-Seurat-Signac on one side and the rest on the other broke the
  group for good — would land the "tipping over" framing harder.
- **The 1874 "Impressionist" insult origin.** The chapter calls 1874 "the
  year a hostile critic gave them the name as a joke," in passing. The
  critic was **Louis Leroy**, the joke was on Monet's *Impression, soleil
  levant*. Even half-a-sentence ("a critic named Louis Leroy mocked Monet's
  *Impression, Sunrise*") would make the parallel to Fry-in-1910 (another
  critic, another improvised label) explicit.

### NICE-TO-HAVE

- A one-sentence reminder of where Seurat physically was *as he painted* the
  *Grande Jatte* — the long series of small studies he did on the actual
  island. The fact pack mentions two summers and a winter, and that
  preparatory discipline is signature Seurat.

## Chapter 2 — "The five who weren't a group" — COMPLETE

This chapter does the survey job. Each anchor gets a paragraph; the move,
the city, and the chapter-handoff are all clear. The "five answers, not one
creed" framing locks in. A few minor catches:

### MUST-ADD

- None.

### SHOULD-ADD

- **The Cézanne–Gauguin antagonism gets a one-line drop ("Cézanne thought
  Gauguin was a 'Chinese painter' and a thief of his ideas") that the
  zero-knowledge reader can't parse.** "Chinese painter" reads as racist
  shorthand without the period context (Cézanne meant decorative / flat /
  un-Western — a 19th-century French insult dismissing flat-color work as
  borrowed from the Far East). Either gloss it or drop the line.
- **The Vauxcelles / Fauves origin** (Salon d'Automne 1905, "les fauves" =
  "the wild beasts," named by critic Louis Vauxcelles) is mentioned only
  as a label — "the wild-color movement that had been named in 1905." The
  pack §2 supplies the full naming story. Three more words ("named by
  critic Louis Vauxcelles") would make Fauvism's naming the explicit
  parallel to Post-Impressionism's naming five years later, which is the
  page's spine.

### NICE-TO-HAVE

- The Lautrec–Van Gogh Paris 1886 meeting at Cormon's studio is mentioned
  in two chapters without a date for the first one; consistent dating
  helps a reader thread their actual encounter.

## Chapter 3 — "Cézanne — the cylinder, the sphere, the cone" — COMPLETE

This is the strongest chapter. Inheritance, Jas de Bouffan, the three
signature series, the letter, the rainstorm, the 1907 retrospective and
its effect on Picasso/Braque — all there, all linked, all properly hedged.

### MUST-ADD

- None.

### SHOULD-ADD

- **The 1904 / 1905 / 1906 Salon d'Automne memorial rooms — *while
  Cézanne was still alive*.** The chapter has this, with the exact work
  counts ("about 30 of his works"; "10 works"; "10 works again"), which
  is good. But the *significance* of memorial rooms for a living painter
  — that the Salon d'Automne was *already* canonizing him while he was
  still in Aix — is the cross-civ-style insight the page can deliver.
  One sentence: "Memorial rooms for a living painter were almost unheard
  of in the French exhibition system; the Salon d'Automne was
  effectively canonizing Cézanne in his last three years of life." That
  re-frames the 1907 retrospective as the apex of a four-year priming,
  not a standalone event.
- **Vollard's 1895 show — "about 150 works"** is in the chapter. The pack
  also notes that the show "made him visible again — and even then mostly
  to other painters" — a sharp framing the draft drops. The
  painters-recognized-him-before-the-public-did beat is worth keeping.

### NICE-TO-HAVE

- Picasso's *"Cézanne was my one and only master"* lands clean; pairing it
  with **Matisse's** equivalent (Matisse called Cézanne "the father of
  us all") would distribute the patrimony across both founders of the
  next generation, not just Picasso.

## Chapter 4 — "Van Gogh — the yellow house, the wheat, the gun" — COMPLETE

This is the second-strongest chapter. The late start, Theo, Paris, Arles,
the ear, Saint-Rémy, Auvers, the gunshot, the *Tristesse* line, Theo's
death, Jo's posthumous work, the *Red Vineyard* hedge, the Naifeh-Smith
hedge — all there.

### MUST-ADD

- **The Kröller-Müller collection.** The fact pack §5 supplies it (Helene
  Kröller-Müller, ~1908 onward, ~90 Van Goghs, second-largest holding
  after the Van Gogh Museum, Otterlo, opened 1938). The draft mentions
  it only as the location of *Café Terrace at Night* in passing in the
  Ch 6/8 wider drift, never explains who she was. For a reader, this
  matters: when the page lists Van Gogh paintings at Kröller-Müller
  (*Café Terrace*, the Redon *Cyclops*), they need to know what
  Kröller-Müller is. One sentence in Ch 4: "From around 1908 onward
  Helene Kröller-Müller, the wife of a Dutch industrialist, began
  buying Van Goghs systematically; her collection of about 90 paintings
  became the Kröller-Müller Museum in Otterlo (1938), the second-largest
  Van Gogh holding after Amsterdam." This is the *collecting* arc that
  matters as much as the painting arc.

### SHOULD-ADD

- **The *Portrait of Dr Gachet* $82.5 million record.** The chapter has it
  ("$82.5 million to the Japanese paper-industry tycoon Ryoei Saito"),
  good. But the pack supplies a sharper market beat: *that price held the
  auction record for Impressionist / Post-Impressionist work for years*.
  The "Van Gogh sold one painting alive, became the auction record dead"
  arithmetic is the punch the chapter is reaching for and never quite
  closes. One line in the closing-section paragraph would close it.
- **The Arles–Gauguin handshake at the chapter's seam.** The chapter
  carries the ear-cutting from Vincent's side. Across to Gauguin's chapter
  it gets retold from Gauguin's side, which is fine. But the seam between
  the two chapters could use a single explicit pointer: "Gauguin's side of
  this is the next chapter."

### NICE-TO-HAVE

- The brief Van Gogh / Toulouse-Lautrec friendship gets one mention in
  Ch 4 and one in Ch 6, both in passing. The fact pack notes Lautrec
  defended Van Gogh's work publicly at one of the Indépendants. A single
  cross-reference between the two would knit the two short-lived painters
  closer.

## Chapter 5 — "Gauguin — Pont-Aven, Tahiti, the colonial question" — COMPLETE

The colonial frame is faced honestly. The Teha'amana / Pau'ura / Vaeoho
ledgers are named with their ages, the "do not project 21st-century legal
categories backward" / "do not use that context to disappear what these
relationships actually were" framing is exactly the calibration the pack
required. The Flora-Tristan-grandmother detail is in. The 1882 crash is in.
Mette and the abandoned five children are in. The *Vision after the Sermon*
mechanism is one of the best-written passages in the draft.

### MUST-ADD

- None.

### SHOULD-ADD

- **The Vollard 1898 show — the moment Western critical interest in
  Gauguin starts.** The fact pack §5 supplies it (Galerie Vollard, Paris,
  November 1898, ~9 Tahitian paintings + 1 sculpture). The draft skips
  it, jumping from his sail-for-Tahiti to his death without the
  intervening market beat that is the parallel to Cézanne's 1895
  Vollard show. Both painters became visible to Paris through the same
  dealer within three years of each other. That's a structural pattern
  the page can deliver.
- **The 1903 Tahiti estate sale.** The chapter says "His pictures and
  effects were auctioned cheap on Tahiti the following autumn. Within
  fifteen years they would be unaffordable in any market on earth."
  The pack supplies the same beat but warns: "Specific prices: hedge,
  don't cite figures without re-verification." The draft hedges
  correctly — fine. But the dispersal *path* (most pictures left the
  Pacific via French colonial buyers and were resold in Paris over the
  next decade) is the mechanism behind "unaffordable in fifteen years."
  Half a sentence on the dispersal would close the loop.
- **"École de Pont-Aven" is glossed in Ch 2 and re-glossed in Ch 5.** The
  Ch 5 gloss is fine; consider trimming the Ch 2 one to avoid the
  repeat.
- **The pamphlets attacking the colonial administration.** The chapter
  has them ("which is to his credit") — good. The pack §5 doesn't
  elaborate; if a verified specific (the late-1902 letters to the
  governor over the Marquesan church school) is available, it lands
  the credit harder than a generic mention.

### NICE-TO-HAVE

- The Cézanne "Chinese painter" insult dropped in Ch 2 connects directly
  to Gauguin's *flat color + Japanese print* practice, which is what
  Cézanne was reaching for with that insult. Glossing it there and
  cross-linking it here would carry the argument.

## Chapter 6 — "Seurat's dots, Lautrec's posters" — **GAPS**

Mechanically strong. The Société des Artistes Indépendants founding is
clearly told; the Seurat life-and-method paragraph is clean; the Lautrec
biography is well-handled (height, parents, pycnodysostosis as inference,
the brothel pictures, absinthe + syphilis, the Albi museum). But three
specific gaps for a zero-knowledge reader.

### MUST-ADD

- **"Pointillism" / "Divisionism" — the actual mechanism, in one
  technical sentence.** The chapter walks the reader through the
  *origins* of the technique (Chevreul, Rood, Blanc, optical mixing)
  but the operative sentence that says "do scientifically, by formula,
  what the Impressionists had been doing by gut instinct" presupposes
  the reader already knows what optical mixing IS. The Ch 1 gap
  repeats here. The chapter has all the ingredients — just say it
  plainly once: "The viewer's eye, at the right distance, blends two
  adjacent pure-color dots into a third color, brighter than the same
  color premixed on the palette." Until that mechanism is on the page,
  every later reference to pointillism / divisionism / neo-impressionism
  is a label without a referent.
- **"La Goulue" / Louise Weber — name glossed but not the role.** The
  chapter says "the Moulin Rouge's star dancer **Louise Weber** ('La
  Goulue' — the *Glutton*)." Good gloss on the name. But *why* La Goulue
  was famous (she was the inventor of the can-can-adjacent
  *chahut*-style high-kick the Moulin Rouge sold tickets on) is missing.
  A reader who doesn't know who La Goulue is gets a name, not a person.
- **"Succès de scandale"** is used in Ch 8 (about Duchamp's *Nude
  Descending*) without a gloss. It is the only French phrase in the
  whole draft that goes unglossed. One quick: "(*succès de scandale* —
  a hit because of the outrage)" would close it.

### SHOULD-ADD

- **The Fénéon link.** Critic Félix Fénéon is named in Ch 6 as the
  coiner of "Néo-Impressionnisme" in 1886; he reappears in Ch 7 as the
  subject of Signac's grand portrait. The chapter never connects them.
  A single sentence in Ch 6 — "Fénéon was the small, anarchist,
  bowler-hatted Paris art critic Signac would later paint in a
  spectacular pointillist portrait now at MoMA" — would carry him
  through and make the Ch 7 reappearance pay off.
- **The Lautrec posters — 31 posters / ~370 lithographs.** The pack
  supplies the production numbers (he made ~31 posters and ~370
  lithographs over a ~10-year career). The chapter has the
  *significance* (the modern poster industry descends from him) but
  not the *productivity* (one major poster every four months,
  alongside the paintings, alongside the brothel work). Half a
  sentence on the scale would land the "he made it a medium" point.
- **The Japanese-print debt — *ukiyo-e*.** The chapter says "Japanese
  prints" three times without naming the genre. *Ukiyo-e* ("pictures of
  the floating world") is glossed nowhere on the page. The pack §9
  uses it. A one-clause gloss the first time it appears (Ch 4 in Paris,
  ideally) — "the **ukiyo-e** woodblock prints of Hokusai, Hiroshige
  and Utamaro flooding into Paris from the 1860s" — would let the rest
  of the page lean on the term.
- **Henri Lehmann — pupil of Ingres.** The chapter glosses Lehmann
  ("had been a pupil of the great Neoclassical painter Ingres"). Good,
  but a reader who has not done the previous level may not know Ingres.
  Half a sentence: "Ingres, the great early-19th-century classicist who
  built the École's drawing curriculum and was every Impressionist's
  notional enemy" — or drop the lineage altogether if the Ingres reach
  is too far.

### NICE-TO-HAVE

- The *Bathers at Asnières* "rejected from the Salon, hung at the
  Indépendants instead, in a beer hall" beat is good — the pack also
  notes it hung *in the exhibition's beer hall*, which is a sharper
  detail than the chapter gives. Push that further.
- The Lautrec / Van Gogh / Bernard / Anquetin meeting at **Cormon's
  studio** in 1886 is in the chapter as one clause. The fact that
  Cormon was a Salon academic who somehow taught the four most
  anti-academic painters of the next generation is one of the great
  ironies of the period and could carry more weight.

## Chapter 7 — "Bonnard, Vuillard, Denis + cast" — **GAPS**

The chapter rests its weight on Denis's 1890 line (carried beautifully) and
Bonnard/Vuillard/Signac/Redon/Rousseau/Cassatt one-paragraph treatments. The
*Talisman* origin scene is one of the better passages on the page. But the
*wider* cast — Roussel, Ranson, Maillol, Rippl-Rónai, Vallotton, the rest
the brief asked for — gets the most cursory treatment in the draft. The
auditor brief explicitly flagged this risk: "does the wider cast get enough
texture to register, or is it a name-rattle?" Currently — name-rattle.

### MUST-ADD

- **The wider Nabis are named but have no function.** The Nabi list reads:
  "The core members were Sérusier himself, Pierre Bonnard, Édouard Vuillard,
  Maurice Denis, Ker-Xavier Roussel, Paul Ranson, the sculptor Aristide
  Maillol, and the Hungarian József Rippl-Rónai." Four of these eight
  (Roussel, Ranson, Maillol, Rippl-Rónai) never appear again, with no
  role attached. For each, one clause:
  - **Ker-Xavier Roussel** — Vuillard's brother-in-law and lifelong studio
    partner; mythological pastoral landscapes.
  - **Paul Ranson** — host of the Temple (the chapter says "Ranson's
    studio, which they jokingly called the Temple" — good — but Ranson
    himself never gets a function); founded the Académie Ranson 1908
    where Bonnard, Vuillard and Denis later taught.
  - **Aristide Maillol** — *the sculptor*, named; the major Post-
    Impressionist sculptor full stop. Glossing "sculptor" is not glossing
    Maillol. One clause: "Maillol turned from Nabi paintings to massive
    classicizing female nudes in bronze and stone after about 1900 and
    became the major Post-Impressionist sculptor."
  - **József Rippl-Rónai** — Hungarian; carried the Nabi instinct back to
    Budapest and is the founding modernist of Hungarian painting.
  - **Vallotton** — named in Ch 8 (Fry's 1910 list) without ever having
    been introduced. Should appear in Ch 7 as the **Swiss-born Nabi
    woodcut master** whose hard black-and-white interiors of married
    couples in cramped Paris apartments are the dark sibling to Vuillard's
    patterned ones.

  Without these, a reader who finishes Ch 7 cannot tell you what any of
  these names did. The brief explicitly asked for this.
- **Bonnard's *japonard* nickname is dropped without saying why.** The
  chapter has: "Bonnard was *le nabi très japonard*, the 'very Japanese-
  like' one." But why he earned that nickname — his early posters and
  decorative panels were the most overtly Japanese-print-derived of any
  Nabi — is left to the reader to infer. One clause closes it.
- **Cassatt's 1890 Japanese-print show — work count cited but not in
  pack.** The chapter says the Beaux-Arts mounted "a major exhibition
  of **Japanese woodblock prints** — over 700 works, the most ambitious
  show of *ukiyo-e* ever held in Europe up to that date." The fact
  pack §6 confirms the exhibition happened and the year but does
  **not** supply the "over 700 works" figure or the "most ambitious in
  Europe" superlative. **Either verify these against an independent
  source and cite, or drop them.** The Gate 1 fact-checker will likely
  flag this; flag it from Gate 3 too as an unsourced numeric claim.

### SHOULD-ADD

- **The Natanson / *La Revue blanche* milieu — texture.** The chapter
  gives Vuillard's patron relationship with the Natansons and mentions
  Mallarmé and Proust, fine. But *La Revue blanche* was the central
  avant-garde Paris cultural journal of the 1890s and the meeting-
  point of Nabis + Symbolist writers + Toulouse-Lautrec (who designed
  posters for it) + Signac (who serialized *D'Eugène Delacroix au
  néo-impressionnisme* in it). One more sentence stitching that milieu
  together — "the Nabis, the Néo-Impressionnistes, and Toulouse-
  Lautrec all converged at *La Revue blanche*, the Natanson brothers'
  cultural review (Paris, 1889–1903), which was the central periodical
  of the French avant-garde in the 1890s" — would re-tighten the room.
- **The "very Japanese-like" Nabi nicknames as a feature.** The fact
  that the Nabis gave each other nicknames is in the draft, but the
  list is funny and revealing — Denis was *le nabi aux belles icônes*
  ("the Nabi of beautiful icons"), Vuillard was *le nabi zouave*
  (the soldier-Nabi, for his red beard), Sérusier was *le nabi à la
  barbe rutilante* (the radiant-bearded one). One more nickname or
  two would make the Nabis feel like a group of people, not a list.
  (Verify against Wikipedia before citing.)
- **Signac's 1899 *D'Eugène Delacroix au néo-impressionnisme*** — the
  chapter has it. The pack notes it was published by *Revue blanche*
  editions — connecting it to the Natanson milieu would tie the
  pointillists to the Nabis at the publishing layer.
- **Pissarro's brief pointillist period.** The chapter mentions in its
  closing sweep "the late Pissarro who experimented briefly with
  pointillism before going back to his own way." The dates (~1886–88)
  and the social cost (Pissarro lost dealer support during the
  pointillist years and recanted in 1888) are worth one sentence.

### NICE-TO-HAVE

- The *Talisman* opening would land harder with the panel's physical
  appearance described concretely — the pack supplies "a few flat
  patches of orange, green, blue and red that *read* as a landscape
  only if you already know it's a landscape," which the chapter does
  carry. Good. Consider adding the dimensions cleanly (10½ × 8½ in,
  cigar-box lid) right next to the panel — it currently floats a
  paragraph later.

## Chapter 8 — "How a category got its name" — **GAPS**

The chapter does the Fry story well: the firing from the Met, the gallery,
the deadline, the catalogue title, the 25,000 visitors, the £4,600 receipts,
the Bloomsbury defense, the Woolf "On or about December 1910" epitaph, the
1912 sequel, the 1913 Armory. But on a chapter explicitly framed as "the
manifesto surrogate" (the page's manifesto block lives here), the central
*quotable line* from the Fry catalogue is missing.

### MUST-ADD

- **The Fry catalogue's most-quoted sentence — the surrogate manifesto's
  actual face.** The fact pack §1 supplies it as a "safe-to-quote" passage,
  reproduced in Fry's *Vision and Design* (1920):
  > "The pictures collected together in the present Exhibition are the
  > work of a group of artists who cannot be defined by any single term.
  > … They have aimed not at illusion but at reality."
  Without that line, the chapter says Fry wrote a catalogue without ever
  showing the reader what was in it. This is the chapter's *one*
  required quote and it isn't there. (The Cézanne cylinder/sphere/cone
  passage *is* quoted in Ch 3 — the other surrogate. Quote Fry too.)
- **Roger Fry's introduction was actually drafted by Desmond MacCarthy.**
  The chapter says MacCarthy "helped draft the catalogue introduction"
  — softened. The fact pack §4 is sharper: "the unsigned 'Editorial' is
  by Desmond MacCarthy, drafted with Fry's input — the authorship has
  been parsed by Berkowitz 2017." The historical record is that the
  *introduction itself was MacCarthy's prose*; Fry signed off and added
  paragraphs. The chapter softens this; a more accurate phrasing matches
  the pack's hedge.
- **The 1907 *interview Fry gave to The Nation*** where he said he picked
  the name on the fly — the chapter has this. Good.
- **The Vision-and-Design / Significant Form scaffolding is named only
  through Clive Bell.** The chapter says Bell "would in 1914 publish
  *Art* and the concept of 'significant form.'" It doesn't name Fry's
  *Vision and Design* (1920), the book in which Fry's own catalogue
  intro and *An Essay in Aesthetics* (1909) appear. The pack supplies
  both. A reader who wants to know where to *read* Fry on Post-
  Impressionism currently doesn't get the title. One sentence: "Fry
  collected his Post-Impressionist writings in *Vision and Design*
  (Chatto & Windus, London, 1920), which is where the catalogue
  introduction now lives" — would close it.

### SHOULD-ADD

- **Salon de la Section d'Or / the European cross-traffic.** The chapter
  jumps from the 1912 Grafton sequel directly to the 1913 New York
  Armory Show without noting that the *same* year (October 1912) saw
  the Section d'Or Cubist exhibition in Paris and a major Sonderbund
  show of Post-Impressionism + Expressionism in Cologne. The cross-
  Atlantic Post-Impressionist canonization happened in three cities
  almost simultaneously (London, Cologne, Paris) in autumn 1912 and
  one city (New York) the next February. Even one sentence — "By the
  autumn of 1912 the same Post-Impressionist + modernist canon was
  being hung in London, Cologne and Paris within weeks of each other" —
  would land the *internationalization* the chapter is otherwise
  reaching for.
- **The 1912 sequel's specific cooling of the press.** The chapter says

## Two structural cross-chapter omissions

These are not chapter-local — they are gaps in the page as a whole.

1. **Optical mixing, the mechanism.** Three chapters (1, 2, 6) introduce
   pointillism / divisionism / neo-impressionism as labels. Two
   (Chevreul, Rood, Blanc) are named correctly. *Nowhere* on the page does
   the prose plainly say what optical mixing is, the way the prose plainly
   says what Synthetism is (Ch 5: "flat saturated red"). Pick a chapter —
   Ch 1, when the *Grande Jatte* is first standing in front of the reader,
   is the right one — and say it.

2. **The five-was-named, the five-was-made gap.** The page is correctly
   structured as "five answers (Chs 3–6) framed by a naming chapter
   (Ch 8)" — that is exactly what the pack asked for. But the page never
   tells the reader the *interval* explicitly: the youngest of the five
   pictures the room canonized (Cézanne's last Mont Sainte-Victoires)
   was painted four years before Fry hung the show; the oldest
   (Seurat's *Bathers at Asnières*) was painted 26 years before. The
   *fact pack* makes this point in its tone note ("a critic in London in
   1910 needed a name for the painters who came after Impressionism but
   were not yet Fauves or Cubists, and the name stuck"). The Ch 8 closer
   (the "all dead" list) gets most of the way there. One sentence in
   Ch 8 explicitly saying "the youngest picture in the show was painted
   four years before the catalogue, the oldest twenty-six years before"
   would close the loop perfectly.
