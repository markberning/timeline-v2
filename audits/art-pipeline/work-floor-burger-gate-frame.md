### [FIX] C-1 — The Happenings / Ray Gun Theater origin of the sewn technique is missing
The draft presents the sewing-and-stuffing method as if it arrived for The Store / the floor pieces. The actual genesis (well-documented, and the more interesting story) is that soft, sewn forms came out of Oldenburg's **Happenings** — the oversize costumes and props for the **Ray Gun Theater** (1962), which Patty Mucha sewed. The floor food sculptures were, in Oldenburg's own framing, "a short step" from those colossal stage props. This both deepens the making-story and strengthens the Mucha credit (it shows her sewing predated and seeded the sculptures, not just executed them).
**Suggested add** — in `FlrMaking`, after the Singer-machine sentence:
> The sewing was not new to them. Patty Mucha had been stitching giant soft props and costumes for Oldenburg's *Happenings* — the staged, semi-improvised performance pieces he was making around the same time — and the floor food grew straight out of that stage work: an eighteen-foot airplane and a fifteen-foot man in cloth were only a short step from a seven-foot burger.

*(Sourced: Artforum / Barbara Rose; Pulitzer Arts Foundation, "The Ordinary Must Not Be Dull." Verify the "18-ft airplane / 15-ft man" prop figures against a primary source before printing them as hard numbers; if unverified at final, drop the specific dimensions and keep "giant soft props and costumes.")*

### [NICE] C-2 — "first wave Pop stayed flat on the wall" is slightly under-supported
FlrBreak ¶1 and the factpack both say Warhol and Lichtenstein "had stayed flat on the wall." True as a generalization, but worth one half-clause acknowledging Oldenburg's own painted-plaster Store objects already had relief/mass — so the burger is the move into *full* three dimensions and *soft* mass, not the first 3-D Pop object. The draft mostly handles this via The Store framing; optional to make the wall/relief/full-3D ladder explicit. Not required.

### [NICE] C-3 — No mention that the food/consumer subject is itself the Pop thesis
The draft treats the hamburger mostly as "least heroic object" (anti-monument axis). It under-plays that choosing a *branded fast-food commodity* is the Pop-Art consumer-culture thesis in three dimensions — the same impulse as Warhol's soup cans (which is the adjacent chain work). One sentence tying the burger to American consumer abundance would tighten the chain. Optional.

## 2. FRAMING / FAIRNESS

### 2.1 Patty Mucha credit — strong, with two tightenings

The draft credits Mucha well: she is named, called "the artist Patty Mucha," and credited with sewing AND stuffing in the const, provenance, FlrMaking, and a looking-annotation. This clears the fairness bar. Two refinements:

#### [FIX] F-1 — The Singer machine was *Mucha's own*, bought for her own clothes — not a generic "household machine"
The draft says "an ordinary household Singer sewing machine" (FlrMaking) and "a home Singer machine" (elsewhere). The sourced detail is more specific and more to Mucha's credit: it was **her own** portable Singer, which **she had bought to make her own clothes**, and she was a trained seamstress (taught by her mother). "Household machine" subtly de-personalizes her contribution into mere domestic equipment. Restore the agency.
**Suggested edit** — FlrMaking:
> …on **her own portable Singer sewing machine**, one she had bought to make her own clothes.

*(Sourced: AGO history; Smithsonian AAA / Pulitzer. The factpack §2 already carries "a small Singer machine bought by Oldenburg's wife.")*

#### [FIX] F-2 — Promote Mucha from "wife who sewed" to "collaborator on the soft sculptures"
The draft's verbs are accurate but uniformly subordinate: she "sewed and stuffed," he "designed and painted." Multiple sources now frame her as the **collaborator on all the early sewn sculptures**, not a helper — her sewing technique (out of the Happenings) is part of how soft sculpture was *invented*, not just fabricated. Recommend one phrase upgrading the relationship without overclaiming joint authorship (the works are Oldenburg's; the conception and the public authorship are his).
**Suggested edit** — FlrMaking, the "Oldenburg designed the shapes and painted them; she sewed and stuffed them" sentence, append:
> …she sewed and stuffed them. Her sewing was not a service to the project but part of how this kind of object came to exist at all: the soft, sewn form was a technique she brought from the costumes she made for his performances.

*(Keeps authorship with Oldenburg — correct — while refusing the "skilled-seamstress-wife who merely executed" framing that older accounts used. Sourced: Smithsonian AAA oral-history framing "collaborator for all of his early sewn sculptures"; Pulitzer.)*

### 2.2 Soft-sculpture scope — correctly handled, hold the line

#### [PASS, with one [FIX] guardrail] F-3
The draft never says "first sculpture ever to sag" / "the first soft sculpture." It explicitly scopes: "one of a set rather than the lone first example," "Oldenburg helped invent," "the soft sculpture Oldenburg helped invent." This is exactly right and clears the framing gate.

**One [FIX] guardrail:** the const `hook` and the FlrStore line "a material no sculptor had used this way before" / "these 1962 floor pieces are where the soft sculpture became a serious, room-scale thing" are defensible, but note the factpack and web both record that Oldenburg himself dates a soft predecessor to **1957** (a stuffed stocking) and that the technique grew from the 1962 Happenings props — i.e. the *floor food* is where it went room-scale and public, not where softness began. The draft's "where the soft sculpture became a serious, room-scale thing" already threads this correctly. Just make sure "a material no sculptor had used this way before" keeps the qualifier **this way** (it does) — that clause is true of the floor-scale stuffed-canvas object and is NOT a claim that no one ever stuffed cloth before. No edit required; flagged so a later trim doesn't drop "this way" and create an overclaim.

### 2.3 Genius-myth / first-only overclaim / proportion

#### [FIX] F-4 — "one of the most influential single objects in postwar sculpture" (FlrAfterlife close)
This is a sweeping superlative asserted in the app's own voice. It's *plausible* and defensible for Pop / soft sculpture specifically, but "in postwar sculpture" full stop is a big, unattributed claim that the factpack does not source. Either narrow the domain or attribute it.
**Suggested edit:**
> …turned out to be one of the defining objects of Pop Art's move into three dimensions, and of the soft-sculpture idea it helped launch.
*(Narrows to the claim the sources actually support; drops the unsourced "postwar sculpture" magnitude.)*

#### [NICE] F-5 — "the most quoted thing he ever said" (FlrStore)
Minor superlative about the "I am for an art…" line. Likely true but unsourced as a *most*. Soften to "one of the most quoted things he ever wrote" or "the line of it that became famous." Optional.

#### [PASS] Proportion / genius-myth otherwise
The draft is well-proportioned: the controversy (ketchup-bottle protest), the conservation (Sherry Phillips, named and credited), the dealer chain, and the $2,000 are all kept as token/handshake framing rather than market drama, and the const comment block explicitly forbids asserting a market price. No lone-genius myth: Mucha, Bellamy, Janis, Phillips, and (in "after") van Bruggen are all named. Good.

### 2.4 [NICE] F-6 — Coosje van Bruggen credit is thin
FlrBreak "after" names "his partner Coosje van Bruggen" for the later public monuments. Van Bruggen was a full **co-author** of those works (they are credited jointly), not just a partner Oldenburg "made [them] with." Since those works are outside this read's scope it's not a blocker, but if the line stays, "with his collaborator Coosje van Bruggen, with whom he co-created them" is fairer than "his partner." Optional.
