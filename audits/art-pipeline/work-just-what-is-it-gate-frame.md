## 2. FRAMING / FAIRNESS

### [BLOCKER-1] The term-origin scope contains a factual error: Alloway's 1958 essay did NOT use the term "Pop art."
This is the one to fix before ship — it's wrong, and it's wrong inside the very sentence whose job is to *scope a claim carefully*.

Two places assert it:
- ch. `afterlife`: *"…the British critic Lawrence Alloway, who used it in print around 1958 and did the most to popularize it…"*
- art-content.ts annotation (`The lollipop that reads POP`): *"the term 'Pop art' is usually credited to the critic Lawrence Alloway and the Independent Group"* (the "around 1958" implication carries from the afterlife chapter).

The record (Wikipedia "Pop art" + "Lawrence Alloway", verified this pass): Alloway's Feb 1958 *Architectural Design* essay **"The Arts and the Mass Media"** used **"mass popular art" / "popular mass culture"** — it did **NOT contain the term "Pop art."** This is a documented, oft-repeated misconception, and Alloway *himself* later clarified he used the term for the *products of mass media, not for works of art*. The **earliest print appearance of "pop art"** is the Smithsons' **"But Today We Collect Ads," *Ark*, 1956** — which also moves the first-print date *earlier than the collage's reception, not after it*. The draft's own "three years before 'Pop art' became common usage" (ch. `looking`) compounds the error by implying a ~1959 currency.

Suggested replacement (ch. `afterlife`, the credit sentence):
> "The term 'Pop art' grew up inside the Independent Group's own conversation in the mid-1950s, and the critic Lawrence Alloway did the most to carry it into wider use. (Alloway is often said to have coined it in a 1958 essay, but that piece actually used 'mass popular art'; the words 'pop art' had already appeared in print in 1956, in an article by Alison and Peter Smithson.) Either way, the naming was a group affair, not a christening by this one lollipop."

And drop/soften the "three years before 'Pop art' became common usage" line in ch. `looking` — the dating is unsupported. Suggested: *"…the single word that would come to name a whole movement — though, as we'll see, it didn't name it from here."*

This does NOT change the draft's correct headline conclusion (the lollipop did not name the movement; Paolozzi's 1947 *I Was a Rich Man's Plaything* carried the word "POP" first — both verified this pass). It fixes the supporting attribution.

### [FIX-1] The McHale split is asserted cleanly; the record is DISPUTED. The draft must hedge the dispute, not just the credit.
This is the specific fairness call the gate was asked to confirm — and the draft does **not** currently clear it. The draft (ch. `cutting`) writes:
> *"McHale is not a co-author and Hamilton is not erasing him; the source pile was largely McHale's, the arrangement is entirely Hamilton's."*

and the art-content.ts header asserts *"John McHale supplied the American source images / a rough layout; Hamilton selected, cut, and composed it. Both credited fairly."*

This presents one settled version of a **two-sided, openly contested** account:
- **McHale's side (his son John McHale Jr., publicly 2006):** McHale provided a **detailed, measured design mock-up** specifying where each piece went; the source magazines were his (sent from Yale); Hamilton's role was essentially **"mechanical" cutting-and-pasting to McHale's design**. The family claimed McHale was effectively the **author**.
- **Hamilton's side:** called that "absurd"; said he made the collage in a morning at the Cordells' (where McHale lodged), assisted by **Terry O'Reilly and Magda Cordell as picture researchers**; that his authorship went uncontested by McHale Sr. in his lifetime.

So "the arrangement is **entirely** Hamilton's" is precisely the assertion the McHale family disputes — the draft states Hamilton's case as fact. The gate's instruction is to **hedge rather than assert a clean split.** Note also a small internal inconsistency: ch. `cutting` says McHale supplied "a rough page layout," which is *Hamilton's* characterization of exactly the thing McHale's family says was a detailed measured design — the draft has silently adopted one party's word.

Suggested replacement (ch. `cutting`, replace the "McHale is not a co-author…" sentence):
> "How to split that credit is genuinely contested. Hamilton always called the collage his own — made in a single morning, with Magda Cordell and Terry O'Reilly hunting the photographs. McHale's family later told it differently: that McHale supplied not just the magazines but a worked-out design for where everything should go, and that Hamilton's hand was closer to assembly than authorship. Hamilton called that version absurd. What isn't in dispute is that the American image-bank was largely McHale's and the finished object came out of Hamilton's room; where exactly the authorship sits between them is a question the two camps never settled."

And change the art-content.ts header note from the asserted split to a hedge, e.g.: *"John McHale supplied the American source images (and, his family later argued, much of the layout); Hamilton assembled the collage. Authorship between them is DISPUTED — hedge, never assert a clean split."*

Also adjust the `figures` role labels, which currently read as a settled split ("Hamilton — Composed the collage" / "McHale — Supplied the American images"). Suggested: McHale role → *"Supplied the images (and, his family said, more)"* or similar, so the card doesn't quietly take Hamilton's side.

- **Genius-myth:** The draft actively works *against* it — "a method anyone with scissors could copy," "the talent has moved from the wrist to the eye," and (once FIX-1 lands) shared credit. No lone-genius inflation. GOOD.
- **First/only overclaim:** Handled with care. "Often called the first iconic work of Pop" / "claimed for decades as the first iconic work" is correctly hedged as a *reception* claim, not an ontological one (matches Wikipedia's "first work of pop art to achieve iconic status"). The naming claim is debunked, not asserted. The Paolozzi 1947 priority is given. GOOD — provided BLOCKER-1's Alloway error is fixed.

The "most-pointed-at object in twentieth-century collage" (ch. `looking`) is a mild rhetorical flourish but defensible and self-aware. NICE-only: could soften to "the most-discussed" if a reviewer wants zero hyperbole — not required.
