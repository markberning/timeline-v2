## 2. THE STORY

By 1950 de Kooning was a central figure of the New York School, and the orthodoxy of the moment — set by Pollock's drip canvases and the color-field of Rothko/Newman — was **pure abstraction**. De Kooning broke ranks by going back to **the figure**: a large, frontal, seated woman. To his peers and many critics this looked almost like a betrayal of abstraction's hard-won purity. (Smarthistory; MoMA acquisition context.)

The making of the picture became legendary for its **violence and duration**. De Kooning worked the canvas from 1950 into the summer of 1952 — roughly **two years** — painting, scraping, effacing and repainting the figure over and over (MoMA's text describes the canvas being painted, effaced and repainted on the order of **fifty times**; *Life*/ARTnews documented him working from a stack of preparatory studies). Well into the second year he **pulled it off the stretcher and gave it up for lost.** The painting was rescued by a single studio visit: the art historian **Meyer Schapiro** came by, looked at the abandoned canvas, and encouraged de Kooning to keep going — a visit the critic Peter Schjeldahl later called **"history's luckiest studio visit."** De Kooning went back to it and finished it.

The result is a frontal, larger-than-life-size **grinning woman** with huge staring eyes, massive breasts, and a bared, toothy smile — at once **ancient idol** (de Kooning compared the grin to Mesopotamian idols "looking to the sky with this smile"), **commercial pin-up / billboard advertisement** (the broad toothy mouth in the preparatory studies was literally **collaged from a cigarette ad** — a "T-Zone" Camel smile clipped from a magazine), and an explosion of slashing paint. The brushwork is overtly aggressive: thick smears, drips, scraped passages, the body dissolving in and out of its background.

When the six *Women* debuted at the **Sidney Janis Gallery in 1953**, they detonated the New York art world. MoMA bought *Woman I* that same year; the acquisition committee approved it only grudgingly, recording that it **"found the picture quite frightening, but felt that it had intense vitality and liked the quality of the color."** Ever since, the painting has carried a **controversy**: later readings (especially feminist criticism) saw misogyny in the snarling, devouring female; de Kooning himself was ambivalent, insisting the grin and the figure came partly out of humor and out of the whole image-history of "the idol, the Venus, the nude," not out of hatred. (Sources: MoMA; Smarthistory; Phaidon "The strange story behind Woman I"; Tate "The T-Zone"; Sylvester/*Location* interview.)

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (before / after — REQUIRED)

**Before:** By 1950 the leading edge of Abstract Expressionism had pushed *toward total abstraction* — Pollock's all-over drip, the color-field fields of Rothko and Newman. The recognizable human figure was treated as the thing modern painting had finally outgrown; serious avant-garde painting was non-objective.

**The break:** De Kooning **refused to give up the figure**, and instead **fused figuration with full gestural abstraction.** The woman is not drawn *then* painted; she is built and destroyed in the same violent brush-marks as the abstraction around her, so that figure and ground, image and paint-action, are the same event. The female body becomes a **site of paint-violence** — scraped, repainted, smeared, half-dissolved — rather than a depicted subject sitting calmly inside an illusionistic space.

**After:** This proved that gestural abstraction did **not** require abandoning the human image — you could keep the figure *and* keep the radical, all-over, action-painting surface. It reopened the figure for a postwar avant-garde that had declared it dead, and fed directly into later figuration (and, via the collaged magazine mouth, prefigured Pop's appropriation of advertising imagery). It also made de Kooning the great counter-argument to the idea that abstraction's only honest endpoint was non-objectivity.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — with variant flags)

**Primary, best-sourced quote (use this one).** From the David Sylvester interview (recorded March 1960, BBC; edited and published as **"Content is a Glimpse," *Location* 1, no. 1, Spring 1963**; reprinted in Sylvester, *Interviews with American Artists*, Yale, 2001):

> "Maybe the grin. It's rather like the Mesopotamian idols, you know. They always stand up straight looking to the sky with this smile, like they were just astonished about the forces of nature you feel, not about problems they had with one another. That I was very conscious of."

*(Source: Willem de Kooning Foundation, dekooning.org/documentation/words/content-is-a-glimpse; same interview text. The exact run-on after "with this smile" varies slightly by printing; the "Mesopotamian idols … with this smile" core is stable and is the quote to anchor to.)*

Also from the same interview, on the flesh-colored body — useful for the looking points:

> "At one time, it was very daring to make a figure red or blue — I think now it is just as daring to make it flesh-colored."

**The famous "flesh" line — FLAG AS A VARIANT / weakly sourced.** The widely-circulated one-liner **"Flesh was the reason oil paint was invented"** is genuinely attributed to de Kooning and is repeated by reputable institutions (e.g. the Hirshhorn). **But** the citations that quote it (Hirshhorn's own post included) give **no original source or date** — it appears to be a famous remark passed down without a fixed primary citation. **Do not present it as a documented, dated quotation.** If used at all, attribute it cautiously ("a remark often attributed to de Kooning") and lead with the *Location* "Mesopotamian idols / grin" quote, which IS primary-sourced and is directly about *Woman I*.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6; prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **The bared teeth / grin.** The single most arresting feature: a wide toothy smile that reads as a billboard's "American smile" — and which, in the preparatory studies, was a mouth literally **clipped from a cigarette ad** in a magazine and pasted on. It hovers between cheerful advertisement and a snarl.
2. **The huge eyes.** Enormous, fixed, frontal, staring straight out at the viewer — the "ferocious," "frightening" stare the MoMA committee recoiled from.
3. **The slashing brushstrokes.** Look at how the marks attack the canvas: fast, thick, drippy, scraped — the same gestural violence as pure abstraction, here building a body. Paint is action, not description.
4. **The body dissolving into the background.** There is almost no stable edge between the figure and the space around her; arms, shoulders and ground bleed into one another, so the woman keeps coming together and falling apart.
5. **The pink / ochre flesh tones.** Smeared pinks, ochres and reds make the body insistently fleshly — de Kooning's own point that painting a figure "flesh-colored" was now the daring choice.
6. **The collaged, scraped, worked-to-death surface.** Years of painting-over leave the surface a record of its own history: thin drippy passages next to thick matte slabs, ghosts of effaced earlier states, scraped-down areas — the painting carries the marks of being made and unmade roughly fifty times.

## 6. PROVENANCE

- Painted by de Kooning, New York, **1950–1952.**
- Exhibited in de Kooning's *Women* show at the **Sidney Janis Gallery, New York, 1953** (the show that launched the series publicly).
- **Acquired by The Museum of Modern Art, New York, in 1953** by **purchase** (credit line: **"Purchase"**; accession no. **478.1953**). The acquisition was contested among trustees, who approved it only reluctantly given the image's ferocity. *(Sources: MoMA object record; Phaidon.)*
- Remains in MoMA's collection (Painting and Sculpture).

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (corrected, with sources)

- **The Schapiro rescue anecdote — true in outline, but treat as "the standard account," not gospel.** De Kooning did abandon the canvas in 1952 and resumed after a studio visit from **Meyer Schapiro**; this is widely documented and Schjeldahl dubbed it "history's luckiest studio visit." **However**, sources note the *exact* story has been questioned over the years (degree of Schapiro's influence, what was actually said). **Present it as the well-attested account, not as a verbatim-verified scene.** (Source: Phaidon; SINGULART; TheArtStory on Schapiro.)
- **"Two years" — solid.** Painted 1950 → summer 1952 ≈ two years. Safe to state.
- **The "scraped/repainted fifty times" figure — usable, attribute it.** MoMA-derived text gives "painted, effaced and repainted some fifty times." Frame it as a documented characterization ("roughly fifty times"), not an exact count.
- **"200 studies" — DO NOT assert that number.** Sources confirm de Kooning made **numerous** preparatory studies and worked from a stack of them, and that he "started over several times." I found **no reliable source for a specific "200 studies" figure.** Say "many / numerous preliminary studies," not a count.
- **The grin from a cigarette ad — true and specific.** The toothy mouth in the *studies* was collaged from a **"T-Zone" Camel cigarette advertisement** clipped from a magazine (Tate, "The T-Zone"). Safe to state for the studies; in the *finished* painting the mouth is painted, echoing that collaged source — phrase it that way.
- **Medium specifics (see §1).** Correct line = **oil and metallic paint on canvas** (MoMA). Don't add "enamel/charcoal" to the medium; charcoal was drawing/process, not the catalog medium. Plain "oil on canvas" is incomplete (drops the metallic paint).
- **Dimensions.** Correct = **192.7 × 147.3 cm (6 ft 3⅞ in × 58 in)**. Reject Wikipedia's "150 cm" width; 58 in = 147.3 cm.
- **The misogyny reading — keep it a debate, not a verdict.** Later (esp. feminist) critics read the *Women* as hostile/misogynist; de Kooning was ambivalent and tied the image to humor and to the long art-history of "the idol, the Venus, the nude." **Present both, do not adjudicate.**
- **"Flesh was the reason oil paint was invented" — see §4: famous but un-sourced; don't present as a dated documented quote.**
