## 3. HOOK (one line, house voice — author may rewrite)

A street that Carrà had been swept into himself — an anarchist's coffin pitching on the
mourners' shoulders while mounted police charged — repainted years later as a red-and-black
explosion, the funeral turned into a battle the viewer can't stand outside of.

## 4. NARRATIVE BEATS (~5 — the spine; author weights the *looking*)

**Beat 1 — The real event: Milan, May 1906. [DOCUMENTED]**
A general strike convulsed Milan in spring 1906. **Angelo Galli**, an anarchist and a
strike organizer, was **stabbed to death on 10 May 1906** by security guards of the
**Macchi and Pessoni factory** as he and fellow anarchists (Enrico Recalcati, Carlo
Gelosa) approached a picket line. The strike followed Royal Guard violence against
protestors. (*Source: Wikipedia, Smarthistory.* The often-repeated "Royal Guard fired,
killing one and injuring eight" detail appears on Wikipedia but the precise casualty
figure could not be second-sourced here — **author should attribute it loosely
("the strike had begun after the authorities fired on the crowd") rather than cite the
1-killed/8-injured count as carved fact.** [DISPUTED — single-source detail].)

**Beat 2 — The funeral, the police line, the riot Carrà witnessed. [DOCUMENTED]**
Galli's funeral, **13 May 1906**, drew hundreds of anarchists, watched by **police on
horseback**. The authorities **refused to let the anarchist procession enter the
cemetery**. When the mourners pushed back, the police charged; a violent melee broke out
around the coffin. **Carrà was there** — living in Milan, moving in the city's anarchist
circles — and was caught in the crush. (*Wikipedia, Smarthistory.*) [DOCUMENTED]

**Beat 3 — Carrà's own account (the verbatim source for the painting). [DOCUMENTED — as autobiography]**
In his 1945 memoir ***La mia vita*** Carrà recalled:
> "I found myself unwillingly in the centre of it; before me I saw the coffin, covered in
> red carnations, sway dangerously on the shoulders of the pallbearers; I saw horses go
> mad, sticks and lances clash; it seemed to me that the corpse could have fallen to the
> ground at any moment and the horses would have trampled it."
He went home and **made a drawing immediately.** (*La mia vita*, 1945, quoted in
Wikipedia + Smarthistory.) **FRAME IT as his own much-later recollection** — it is a 1945
memoir of a 1906 event, vivid but retrospective; "by his own later account" / "as he
remembered it." [DOCUMENTED as a quote; treat its details as memoir, not transcript.]

**Beat 4 — Carrà's anarchist sympathies, and the 1904/1906 confusion. [DOCUMENTED + LEGEND catch]**
Carrà was genuinely tied to Milanese anarchism in these years — this is not retro-fitted
politics. **BUT his memory of the date is wrong:** in *La mia vita* he attributes Galli's
death to the **general strike of 1904**, not 1906. *Corriere della Sera* first flagged
the slip; **some have suggested Carrà conflated it with the larger, more famous 1904
general strike to attach himself to a bigger event** — that motive is speculation, the
date-error itself is documented. (*Wikipedia.*) **The painting is about the 1906 Galli
funeral; flag the 1904 date as Carrà's own error, do NOT let the prose repeat it as
fact.** [DOCUMENTED error / DISPUTED motive — verified per the brief's "1904 vs 1906" ask.]

**Beat 5 — Divisionism → Futurism: a remembered street fight becomes lines of force. [DOCUMENTED]**
Carrà's **1910 study** still used traditional, near one-point perspective. He was a
signatory of the **Manifesto of the Futurist Painters (1910)** and the **Technical
Manifesto of Futurist Painting (1910)** alongside **Boccioni, Russolo, Balla, Severini**;
he had come up through **Divisionism** (the Italian post-Pointillist technique of separated
strokes of pure color). After the Futurists' **1911 visit to Paris** exposed them to
**Picasso's Cubism**, Carrà rebuilt the canvas — fracturing the figures and driving
diagonal **"lines of force"** (*linee-forza*) through the scene so red light and black mass
collide. The **Technical Manifesto** held that such force-lines must "**encircle and involve
the spectator so that he will… be forced to struggle himself with the persons in the
picture.**" The funeral is the test case: chaos made structural. (*Wikipedia,
Smarthistory; Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting, 1910.*) [DOCUMENTED]
- **First exhibited:** **Paris, Galerie Bernheim-Jeune, Feb 1912**, the Futurists'
  landmark group show (organized with Félix Fénéon). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Classical scaffold under the chaos:** MoMA's **Alfred H. Barr Jr.** called it "**as
  classically organized as a fifteenth-century battle piece by Paolo Uccello**," likening
  it to Uccello's ***Battle of San Romano***. Use as a comparison (attribute to Barr);
  Rosalind McKever has argued Carrà likely knew the Uccello. [DOCUMENTED as Barr's reading]

## 5. "LOOK CLOSER" ANNOTATIONS (6 — `{label, where, detail}`)

Pointers describe what is **literally on the canvas**; `where` is a prose location, no
coordinates (per the locked WORK rule). Verify each against the painting image at gate-1.

1. **`label:` The red coffin at the heart of it**
   `where:` Upper center, raised on the mourners' shoulders, the one blazing red mass.
   `detail:` The single brightest, reddest passage in the picture is Galli's coffin,
   draped and (in Carrà's memory) heaped with red carnations, hoisted on the pallbearers'
   shoulders. Carrà makes it a literal source of light — red flares out from it across the
   black figures. It is the still pivot the whole brawl turns around, and the one thing the
   eye finds first. [DOCUMENTED — coffin/red carnations from *La mia vita* + canvas]

2. **`label:` Black mourners, mounted police**
   `where:` Massed across the lower and central canvas; the cavalry pressing in from the left.
   `detail:` Almost everyone wears anarchist black, so the figures read as a single dark
   churning mass rather than individuals. Pushing against them from the left are the police
   on horseback — the line that wouldn't let the coffin reach the cemetery. The whole
   picture is built as that collision: black crowd versus red coffin versus charging horse.
   [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia/Smarthistory composition]

3. **`label:` The rearing clash**
   `where:` Left and center, where horse and figures buckle into each other.
   `detail:` Carrà remembered "horses go mad"; on the canvas a horse rears into the crowd
   and bodies bend back from it. This is the literal flashpoint of the riot — the moment
   the funeral became a fight — and it sits just left of the coffin so the eye reads
   violence and stillness in the same glance. [DOCUMENTED — *La mia vita* + canvas]

4. **`label:` The lances, poles and banners overhead**
   `where:` The upper third, a thicket of dark diagonals stabbing up into the light.
   `detail:` Across the top, hard black lines shoot skyward: the police lances and the
   anarchists' flag-poles and banner-staffs, with industrial cranes of the factory district
   behind. They are the "sticks and lances clash" of Carrà's account, abstracted into a
   bristle of diagonals — half real objects, half pure direction. [DOCUMENTED — *La mia
   vita* "sticks and lances"; cranes per Wikipedia/Smarthistory]

5. **`label:` The sunburst of force-lines**
   `where:` Radiating out from the coffin and the sky across the whole upper canvas.
   `detail:` Light doesn't fall on this scene from one place; it explodes outward in
   sheaves of diagonal rays — the Futurist *linee-forza*, "lines of force." They fracture
   every figure they cross and are meant, per the Futurists' own manifesto, to drag the
   viewer bodily into the fight rather than let you watch from outside. This is the single
   clearest mark of the Cubist-charged 1911 rework over the calmer 1910 study.
   [DOCUMENTED — Technical Manifesto + Wikipedia]

6. **`label:` The chaos of bodies**
   `where:` Throughout the central and lower zone, where limbs and torsos fuse and overlap.
   `detail:` Step in close and the figures stop being separate people — arms, backs and
   heads splinter and interpenetrate, the way Cubism let one form pass through another.
   Carrà fractures the crowd so the surge reads as one continuous motion, not a row of
   portraits. It's why the funeral feels like a single body convulsing rather than a
   gathering of mourners. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia/Smarthistory on the Cubist fracturing]

*(Gate-1 note: all six are verifiable against the canvas. The red coffin, black crowd,
mounted police, overhead poles/cranes, radiating force-lines and fractured bodies are all
present and described consistently across Wikipedia + Smarthistory; confirm left/right
placement of the rearing horse against the image before locking annotation 3.)*

## 8. LEGEND / DISPUTE LEDGER (the gate-1 watchlist)

- **[DOCUMENTED-ERROR] Galli's death = 1906, NOT 1904.** Carrà himself misdated it to the
  1904 general strike in *La mia vita*; *Corriere della Sera* corrected it. The painting is
  the **1906** Galli funeral. Prose must use 1906 and may note Carrà's own slip; never
  repeat 1904 as fact. (Brief's named trap — verified.)
- **[DISPUTED] "Carrà conflated it with 1904 to attach himself to a bigger event"** — a
  proposed *motive* for the date error, not established fact. Attribute as a suggestion if
  used at all.
- **[DOCUMENTED-as-memoir, FRAME IT] The Carrà quote** ("I found myself unwillingly in the
  centre of it…"). Real and sourced (*La mia vita*, 1945), but a recollection written ~39
  years after the event — frame as "by his own later account," not as live reportage.
- **[DISPUTED] "Royal Guard fired, killing one and injuring eight"** — on Wikipedia but not
  second-sourced here; soften to a general "after the authorities fired on the crowd."
- **[UNSUPPORTED] Any active role for Marinetti in this canvas** — he founded the movement;
  he did not make or co-make this picture. Don't imply otherwise.
- **[DOCUMENTED, attribute] The Uccello / *Battle of San Romano* comparison** — it is
  Alfred Barr's framing (and later McKever's), a reading, not the artist's stated intent;
  attribute it.
