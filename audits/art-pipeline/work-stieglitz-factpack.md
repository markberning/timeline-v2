So: pen/ink + graphite + cut-and-pasted printed paper. ## 2. THE STORY

Around 1915 New York had its own proto-Dada cell orbiting **Alfred Stieglitz** (1864–1946), the photographer-impresario whose Fifth Avenue gallery **"291"** (the Little Galleries of the Photo-Secession, at 291 Fifth Avenue) and its companion **magazine *291*** were the command post for advanced modern art in America. Picabia, in New York after fleeing wartime France, fell in with this circle (Stieglitz, Marius de Zayas, Paul Haviland, Agnes Meyer). Out of it came what is now called **New York machine Dada** — and Picabia's signature invention, the **"object-portrait" / mechanomorphic portrait**: a person rendered not as a face but as a **machine diagram**, deadpan, drawn in the flat style of a commercial catalogue or patent illustration.

For *291*'s double summer issue, Picabia made a suite of these mechanomorphs of the people who ran the magazine. The cover one is Stieglitz. Its visual source is a **magazine advertisement for the popular Vest Pocket Kodak** — a **folding-bellows plate camera**. Picabia draws Stieglitz **as that camera**, and crucially **as a broken one**: the bellows are **extended but limp / disengaged** (the lens hanging at "half-mast"), the camera straining **upward toward the word "IDEAL"** lettered (in a heavy **Gothic / blackletter script**) on what reads as a gearshift plate — but **it never reaches it**, because an **automobile gearshift is set in neutral/park** and a **hand-brake is engaged**. The inscription **"FOI ET AMOUR" ("faith and love")** sits with the title.

The reading the Met and most scholars give: this is a portrait of **idealism that has stalled.** Within the *291* inner circle there was real worry that Stieglitz — for years the tireless champion of the new — was **losing his drive to keep fighting for modern art.** So the broken camera "striving toward but not reaching the Ideal," gears in neutral, brake on, says, gently, that **Stieglitz is going nowhere.** Yet it is built from the language of *faith and love* — so it lands as **homage and satire at once**: affectionate ribbing of a hero's idealism, not a takedown. (See §8 — both readings are legitimate and should be presented as such.)

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (before / after — REQUIRED)

**Before:** A portrait meant a likeness — a face, a body, a psychology rendered in paint or charcoal. Even the most radical Cubist portrait of 1912–14 still fractured *a depicted human figure*. Identity lived in the features.

**After (the break this work makes):** Picabia throws the human figure out entirely and substitutes a **machine**. The sitter becomes a **labelled mechanical diagram** — appropriated from a commercial advertisement, drawn in a cool, impersonal, **deadpan engineering style**, with **printed words doing the characterization** ("IDEAL," "FOI ET AMOUR"). The person's character is encoded in how the machine *fails* (limp bellows, brake on). This is a genuine rupture: portraiture as **ready-made-and-caption** rather than depiction; the artist's "hand" and "soul" replaced by catalogue line-work and lettering.

**Why it matters / influence:** These 1915 *291* mechanomorphs are foundational documents of **New York Dada** and feed straight into the movement's core moves — appropriation of mass-produced imagery, the machine as metaphor for the human (and the erotic), text-as-image, and the cool anti-expressive surface. They run parallel to Duchamp's readymades of exactly these years and helped set the template Dada and, later, Pop and conceptual portraiture would mine.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (sourced verbatim Picabia quote)

The famous statement of Picabia's man-as-machine credo comes from his **1915 New York interview**:

> "The machine has become more than a mere adjunct of life. It is really a part of human life… perhaps the very soul."

And, from the same interview, on his program:

> "Almost immediately upon coming to America it flashed on me that the genius of the modern world is in machinery, and that through machinery art ought to find a most vivid expression. … I have enlisted the machinery of the modern world, and introduced it into my studio. … [I mean to] work on and on until I attain the pinnacle of mechanical symbolism."

**Source:** Francis Picabia, interviewed in **"French Artists Spur on an American Art," *New-York Tribune*, Sunday, October 24, 1915** (section 4, p. 2). Widely reprinted (Wikiquote; picabia.com biography; standard Picabia scholarship, e.g. Pepe Karmel, "Francis Picabia, 1915: The Sex of a New Machine").

**Variant flags:**
- The article title appears both as **"French Artists Spur on an American Art"** and shortened to **"French Artists Spur on American Art."** Use the longer, with "an."
- The "soul" line is sometimes printed with ellipses tightened or expanded; the wording **"more than a mere adjunct of life … really a part of human life … perhaps the very soul"** is stable across sources.
- The second quote is sometimes chained together across two non-adjacent sentences in the original; flag if quoting as one continuous sentence. Best to present the "machinery is the genius of the modern world" and "pinnacle of mechanical symbolism" clauses as distinct.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The bellows plate camera.** The whole "body" of Stieglitz is a **folding Vest Pocket Kodak** — the accordion-pleated bellows and lens board are the figure. Recognizing it as a real, advertised consumer camera is half the joke.
2. **The lens / bellows at half-mast.** The bellows are **extended but slack and disengaged** — the lens droops rather than aiming sharply. A camera that can't quite focus; read by scholars as limp, "broken," even sexually deflated.
3. **The word "IDEAL," in Gothic/blackletter script.** Lettered toward the top, on the gearshift/lever plate — the thing the camera **strains toward but never reaches.**
4. **"FOI ET AMOUR" ("faith and love").** The inscribed words that give the work its tone — the vocabulary of devotion, attached to a stalled machine. The full inscription also carries the title phrase *Ici, c'est ici Stieglitz.*
5. **The automobile gearshift, in neutral/park.** A car's shift lever lodged out of gear — the mechanism that should drive the climb to "IDEAL" is **disengaged.**
6. **The engaged hand-brake.** A set brake — the second "you're going nowhere" cue. Together with the neutral gear it's the deadpan punchline: all this faith and love, and the thing is parked.

(Visible-only: also note the **cut-and-pasted strips of printed text and visible pencil/graphite corrections** — the surface admits it is a working magazine layout, not a finished "picture.")

## 6. PROVENANCE

The drawing entered the Met as part of the **Alfred Stieglitz Collection** — i.e. from the holdings of the very man it portrays — **gift/bequest realized in 1949** (Stieglitz died 1946; his collection was distributed to several museums by Georgia O'Keeffe and the estate, the Met receiving its share in 1949). **Credit line: "Alfred Stieglitz Collection, 1949"; accession no. 49.70.14.** Source: Met collection record. *(Minor uncertainty: the precise estate-distribution mechanism — gift vs. bequest — is not spelled out on the object page; the "Alfred Stieglitz Collection, 1949" credit line is the citable fact.)*

## 7. RIGHTS

**Public domain in the US — published 1915.** The work was **published as the cover of *291* in 1915**, putting it in the US public domain (anything published before 1929 is PD-US). Picabia died in **1953**, so in some life+70 jurisdictions the underlying work cleared copyright only in **2024** — but the **US status turns on the 1915 publication, not the death date**, so it is solidly **PD-US**. The wired Wikimedia file is tagged accordingly. State plainly: **public domain (US), first published 1915.**

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

- **TRAP — "It's a painting."** No. The Met object is a **DRAWING/collage**: *"Ink, graphite, and pasted and printed papers on paper."* Never call it a painting. (And the Met object is **not a print** either — that's the published cover / the NPG & Orsay relief-print copies; keep the drawing distinct.) *Source: Met record.*
- **TRAP — "Cover of *291* No. 1."** The Wikimedia **filename literally says "No1"** and is **wrong**. It is **nos. 5–6, July–August 1915.** (No. 1 was March 1915.) *Source: Wikipedia 291 run; Met ("July–August 1915 issue").*
- **TRAP — title spelling.** Correct French is ***"foi et amour."*** Avoid the corrupted **"Foit et Amour"** (seen on Artsy). The Met's own short title is **"Here, This Is Stieglitz Here."**
- **CAMERA-MODEL reading.** The camera is identified as a **Vest Pocket Kodak** (a folding-bellows camera), taken from a **period advertisement**. This is the standard scholarly reading (Met curatorial text), but it is an *identification of the source image*, not an inscription — present it as "based on / appropriated from an ad for the Vest Pocket Kodak," not as a label inside the picture.
- **SATIRE vs. HOMAGE — present both, fairly.** Two readings coexist and **both are defensible**:
  - *Gentle satire:* the dominant curatorial reading — a stalled, broken camera (limp bellows, neutral gear, brake on) that can't reach the "IDEAL," voicing the *291* circle's worry that Stieglitz had **lost his fighting drive.** *(Met.)*
  - *Homage:* it is built from **"faith and love"** and made *for* Stieglitz's own magazine and circle, by a friend/ally; the affection is real. Many accounts frame it as **affectionate caricature among collaborators**, not mockery — and it is telling that **Stieglitz himself owned it** (it came to the Met *from his collection*).
  - The honest line: **homage and satire at once** — fond ribbing of a hero's idealism. Do not assert one to the exclusion of the other.
- **Quote sourcing.** The "machine… the very soul" line is real and from the **NY Tribune, Oct. 24, 1915** — not a later memoir or apocryphal Dada manifesto. Cite the interview, not "Picabia once said."
