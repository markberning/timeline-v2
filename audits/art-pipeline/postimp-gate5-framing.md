# Gate 5 — Framing / Ethics Critique
## Post-Impressionism: Movement-Level Narrative (8 Chapters)

**Critic role:** Editorial conscience. Hunt frames that would land wrong with a
thoughtful 21st-century reader. Hunt canonization. Hunt sanded-down ethics.
Hunt the lone-genius myth and the unexamined class privilege under it. Hunt
romanticization of colonial structure. Distinguish *work* from *legend.*

**Not scope:** factual accuracy (Gate 1), storytelling (Gate 2),
comprehensiveness (Gate 3), clarity (Gate 4). Those are separate gates.

**Overall verdict:** SUBSTANTIALLY FAIR with three real fix needs. The draft is
much braver than the typical Post-Impressionist popular history — it names the
Gauguin / Polynesian-girl problem directly in Chapter 5, hedges Lautrec's
pycnodysostosis as *inferred*, frames the Naifeh-Smith Van Gogh hypothesis as a
*hypothesis*, and refuses the "starving artist" myth at least in Cézanne's case.
But the canon stays five-Frenchmen-and-one-Dutchman until Chapter 7 — and
Chapter 7 is where the wider cast lives, including Cassatt and the not-yet-named
women. The five-anchors structure is itself defensible (it's Fry's umbrella,
not the draft's invention), but the *framing of why those five* needs one
honest sentence at the top, not just a Chapter 7 catch. And the Fry/colonial-
metropole frame in Chapter 8 is the chapter's most under-developed beat —
1910 London is the imperial center of a globe-spanning empire and the
Tahitian pictures are the most charged objects in the room. The draft says
nothing about it.

Per-chapter grades below. No EVASIVE grades. Two SOFT (Chs 2 & 8); five FAIR;
one STRONG (Ch 5).

---

## Chapter 1 — `why` (Paris 1886 / Aix 1886)

**Grade: FAIR**

### What it gets right

- The 8th Impressionist Exhibition is rendered with the older painters' actual
  resistance named (Monet refusing to show with Seurat; Pissarro getting called
  a deserter). Not "Impressionism heroically welcomed its successor"; the
  succession is contested in the room.
- Cézanne's banker father is named *in this chapter* — "his father ran a small
  bank, and where he now lives off the inheritance that bank produced." That is
  the class fact the rest of the popular Cézanne literature skirts. Honest.
- The "Cézanne in 1886 is 47 and has been ridiculed for twenty years" framing
  acknowledges that the inheritance bought him *time to be ridiculed*, which is
  the real privilege.
- The four-objections quartet (weight / feeling / meaning / science) attributes
  each anchor a specific aesthetic position; it's not "five geniuses" but
  "five answers to a real artistic problem."

### Issues found

- **SHOULD-FIX — the four objections become a four-Frenchmen-and-one-Dutchman
  list before the chapter has earned the reduction.** The bullet quartet
  (Cézanne / Van Gogh / Gauguin / Seurat, plus Lautrec mentioned in the next
  paragraph) lands as *the* shape of Post-Impressionism in the chapter that's
  supposed to be setting it up. Chapter 7 will widen the cast, but a reader
  could leave Chapter 1 thinking the next-gen answer-to-Impressionism was made
  by exactly these five men in five French/Provençal cities. One sentence after
  the bullets — "(Other painters were making other answers — Cassatt was
  reinventing her practice off the 1890 Japanese-print show, Pissarro was
  pointillizing then unpointillizing, Bonnard and Vuillard were already orbiting
  Gauguin in their twenties, and a wider cast we'll meet in Chapter 7 was
  building around all of them.)" — would close the gap.

### Nice-to-have

- The "Cézanne 47, son of a banker" pairing is right next to "his friends in
  Paris burning themselves out trying to make rent." Could spell out, in one
  beat, that Cézanne's slowness was *purchased*. The chapter already says it;
  could say it a hair more sharply.

## Chapter 2 — `five` (1886–1905, the five who weren't a group)

**Grade: SOFT**

This is the chapter that most embodies the structural problem of the draft:
*it tells the canon as the canon, with the wider-cast caveat held back to
Chapter 7.* The chapter is, structurally, exactly what Fry did in 1910 — five
French men under one umbrella — and the chapter does not enough name that
this is a Fry-shape, not a fact.

### What it gets right

- The chapter explicitly says the label is **retrospective and applied later**:
  *"They got grouped because, in 1910, an English critic… needed a name."* That
  is the right move and the right frame for the whole movement.
- The chapter shows the five painters *did not get along* (Cézanne calling
  Gauguin "a Chinese painter," the Arles disaster), which refuses the implicit
  "five friends, one movement" myth.
- Gauguin's Tahiti is flagged in this chapter as "complicated in ways we'll
  have to deal with honestly (Chapter 5)" — promissory but right.
- Lautrec's pycnodysostosis is hedged as *inferred* and as a "20th-century
  guess from his case history." Two thumbs up.
- Lautrec's class — "born into the old French aristocracy" — is named, and
  Gauguin's ex-stockbroker bourgeoisie is named in the chapter that introduces
  him. The class fact is at least not hidden.

### Issues found

- **MUST-FIX — the chapter writes the five-anchors list as if it were the
  actual shape of the field, then promises Chapter 7 will widen.** This is the
  classic canon-then-footnote move. The fix is small but real: the section
  "The shape of the room — Five answers, no creed" already lists the French
  smaller categories (Néo-Impressionnistes / École de Pont-Aven / Nabis /
  Symbolistes) and *that's exactly the right move*, but then says "What
  French art history did not have was an umbrella big enough" — and pivots
  immediately to "The five we now call the anchors." It needs a beat in
  between: *"This is Fry's list, not the field's. The field at 1900 had at
  least forty painters answering Impressionism — Cassatt and Morisot were
  still working at high level out of the prior generation, Bonnard and
  Vuillard were a decade into the Nabi project, Signac was the surviving
  Divisionist running the Salon des Indépendants, Redon was making Symbolist
  pastels, and a customs-officer named Rousseau was painting his weekend
  jungles. The reason we tell the next four chapters as five painters is that
  Fry's umbrella came late and the umbrella shaped the canon. We'll widen the
  cast in Chapter 7 — but the widening is the truer picture."* Without
  something like that, the chapter ratifies the canon it claims to be
  describing.

- **SHOULD-FIX — Cassatt and Morisot are entirely absent from Chapter 2.**
  Morisot died in 1895 — she was working through the early Post-Impressionist
  decade and she is in no version of "the five." Cassatt is in Chapter 7,
  briefly. The structural reason the five are five men is that Fry picked
  five men. The chapter that names Fry's picking should also name that the
  picking was a male picking. One sentence in Chapter 2: "Morisot was still
  showing in the 1880s when this generation was tipping over; Cassatt reinvented
  her practice off the 1890 Japanese-print show in Paris. Neither was in
  Fry's 1910 room. Neither would be on any list of the five. That is a fact
  about Fry's room, not about the painters."

- **SHOULD-FIX — class is named (Cézanne banker, Lautrec aristocracy, Gauguin
  ex-stockbroker, Van Gogh's pastor-family bourgeoisie) but never assembled
  into the actual observation: Post-Impressionism's "starving artist" mythos
  was largely the choice of comfortable men.** Van Gogh is the exception (and
  even he had Theo, who was a working art dealer at a major firm — a safety
  net most working-class would-be painters didn't have). The chapter would be
  stronger if one paragraph synthesized: *"None of the five was born poor.
  Cézanne lived on his banker father's inheritance. Lautrec lived on the
  Toulouse-Lautrec estate. Gauguin had been a Paris stockbroker before the
  1882 crash and chose to leave a wife and five children rather than take
  another desk job. Van Gogh's brother Theo bankrolled him for ten years.
  Seurat's father was a Paris property speculator. The Post-Impressionist
  myth is the starving artist; the Post-Impressionist fact is mostly
  comfortable men with private incomes choosing not to sell."*

### Nice-to-have

- The phrase "the five we now call the anchors" is fine but quietly absorbs
  the canon. Could become "the five Fry-and-after named the anchors" — putting
  the naming in someone's hand.

## Chapter 3 — `cezanne` (Aix, 1870s–1906)

**Grade: FAIR**

### What it gets right

- **The class frame is named in the chapter's own structural beat: "Start with
  the father, because in Cézanne's case the father is what makes the painter
  possible."** That's the right sentence. It then names the inheritance as
  "the rarest thing a French painter could have… the freedom not to sell."
  That is the version of the Cézanne story that doesn't mythologize his
  slowness as virtue divorced from privilege.
- The *cylinder, sphere, cone* letter is correctly framed as one sentence in
  a private letter to a young painter, **not as a manifesto**: *"It is one
  sentence in a long, informal letter… Cézanne was *not* prescribing Cubism
  in advance."* This refuses the canonization-of-the-quote that has
  recurred in every Cubism textbook for a century.
- The 1907 retrospective is hedged: *"It is hard to say one show alone caused
  all of that — the memorial rooms of 1904–06 had been priming the ground for
  three years."* Refuses the single-trigger-event myth.

### Issues found

- **NICE-TO-HAVE — Hortense Fiquet, Cézanne's longtime partner and mother of
  his son, is named once in passing ("his longtime partner Hortense Fiquet,
  mostly to legitimize their son") and then disappears from the chapter
  entirely.** She sat for him many times; she is the person whose face most
  appears in his portraits after the Bibémus quarries. The chapter's quiet
  habit of giving her one phrase and moving on is the standard art-history
  treatment. It would be a small improvement to name her presence in his
  picture-making at least once more. (Not a fairness flag at the SHOULD level,
  because the chapter is in compressed-anchor mode; in the dedicated work-level
  read of the Card Players or the Bathers, Hortense should be more present.)

- **NICE-TO-HAVE — the "Picasso later admitted" line about the Demoiselles and
  Cézanne's Bathers attributing the lineage to Cézanne is, in context, a
  *Picasso-canon* sentence inside a *Cézanne-canon* chapter.** It works,
  but ratifies the lone-genius transmission line. The chapter is, in the
  next-to-last paragraph, already nuancing the 1907-show-as-cause; it could
  also nuance the Bathers-to-Demoiselles direct-influence claim by saying
  "Picasso later said so; he also said many things later about influences"
  — which would be fair.

### Nice-to-have

- The chapter's only female presence is Hortense in one phrase. The
  Bathers are mentioned but only as "blocky, gendered only in outline,
  with faces like masks" — not the chapter's job to humanize them, but the
  art-historical canon's treatment of the female nude in Cézanne is itself a
  framing question (eroticism refused into architecture). Not a flag, just an
  observation.

## Chapter 4 — `van-gogh` (Arles 1888, Auvers 1890)

**Grade: FAIR**

The chapter is **disciplined against the madness-genius myth** in a way that
the popular Van Gogh literature mostly is not. It works hard to distinguish
the work from the legend.

### What it gets right

- The chapter's first move: *"The hardest fact about Van Gogh, the one the
  romance always blurs, is how *late* he started."* This is a counter-myth
  sentence and a good one. It refuses the prodigy frame.
- The ear-cutting is corrected for the reader: **not the whole ear, the lower
  lobe**. ("This is the standard correction.") That is the corrected fact, and
  the chapter knows the myth needs explicit unwinding.
- The Naifeh-Smith 2011 theory is framed exactly as Gate 5 asks: *"the
  biographers Steven Naifeh and Gregory White Smith published a hypothesis…
  It is **a hypothesis**, not the consensus; the Van Gogh Museum continues to
  treat the death as a suicide. Mention the Naifeh-Smith theory as the
  most-discussed alternative; do not write it as fact."* That parenthetical
  hedge is the right hedge.
- *Wheatfield with Crows* as "last picture" is refused (*Tree Roots* is the
  current Van Gogh Museum view). The chapter says: *"Treat *Wheatfield with
  Crows* as one of his last, not the last."*
- The "sold only one painting" myth is named, qualified, and reframed: the
  *Red Vineyard* sale is detailed (Anna Boch, 400 francs, March/April 1890,
  with Van Gogh alive); the Van Gogh Museum's textured correction is noted
  ("the only painting he is known to have sold to a public buyer at full
  price"). Pristine handling.
- **The suffering-as-source-of-art question is not the chapter's frame.** The
  chapter is about the *career compression* — ten years, ~900 canvases,
  late start, brother as patron, death at 37. Mental illness is named where
  it happened (Saint-Rémy, the asylum admission), but the chapter does not
  argue that the asylum produced the art. *The Starry Night* is noted as
  "painted from memory and the view out his barred east-facing window." That
  is enough. The chapter is restrained where the popular literature is not.

### Issues found

- **SHOULD-FIX — the chapter doesn't quite name *that* it is refusing the
  madness-genius myth.** It works against the myth structurally — by framing
  the career as a career, by hedging the death, by correcting the "one
  painting" line — but never says to the reader, *"the myth of Van Gogh is
  that suffering produced the work. The work was produced by a man who
  picked up a brush at 28, worked at top speed for ten years, was supported
  by his brother throughout, and who experienced episodes of mental illness
  that interrupted the work as often as they coincided with it. Distinguish
  the work from the legend."* A reader who has the legend in the back of
  the head needs one direct sentence pushing against it, not just structural
  refusal. Otherwise the chapter is read as *biography* by a reader who
  already knows the legend and the legend wins by default.

- **SHOULD-FIX — Theo's wife Jo van Gogh-Bonger is named once ("Jo, Theo's
  wife, was left with…") and her role in building Vincent's posthumous
  reputation is named in one sentence: *"She spent the rest of her life
  building Vincent's posthumous reputation; she is the reason the Van Gogh
  Museum in Amsterdam exists."*** That sentence is excellent but it is the
  whole appearance she gets. She edited and translated the brothers'
  correspondence and was the curator of Vincent's posthumous market for
  three decades. One additional clause — "and she edited the letters that
  made him knowable as a person rather than a legend" — would credit her
  as the second person, after Theo, who is the reason the painter Van Gogh
  exists in the public mind. The chapter is one woman-named-and-moved-past
  short of fair. (This is a lone-genius-myth flag in a different key: the
  myth survives partly *because* the women who built the posthumous
  reputation get one phrase.)

### Nice-to-have

- The chapter says Vincent "died face-down in a field" in the last sentence
  of the sales-in-life section. This is rhetorical and gestural; the actual
  death was *in his rented room at the Auberge Ravoux, two days after a
  gunshot in a field somewhere outside Auvers, with Theo at his side*. The
  chapter knows this — it just said it five paragraphs earlier. The
  closing image conflates the legend with the fact. Could be dialed back to
  "He died at 37, in his brother's arms, having sold one painting for money
  he could spend."

## Chapter 5 — `gauguin` (Pont-Aven 1886, Tahiti 1891–1903)

**Grade: STRONG**

This is the chapter the brief is most worried about, and it is the chapter the
draft handles most courageously. The Gauguin chapter does the thing the
Gauguin popular history has been pretending it doesn't have to do for a
century: it names what the relationships were, names the colonial structure
around them, and refuses to disappear either fact into "the pictures matter."

### What it gets right

- **The wife and five children are named and the abandonment is named.** *"He
  was, by any honest accounting, an absent father."* The bracketed paragraph
  is the chapter's most important framing move: *"There is no charitable way
  to tell this part of the story. Gauguin was not poor, in 1885, in the way
  Van Gogh was poor. He was a man with a wife and five small children whom he
  chose to leave behind so that he could be a painter. The art does not exist
  without that decision. The decision is part of the art's price."* That
  paragraph is the model for how the ethical accounting should sound across
  every art-pipeline chapter. Lock it.

- **The colonial structure is named explicitly, by date, with the names of
  the institutions: "Tahiti had been a French protectorate since 1842 and a
  formal colony since 1880. Papeete had cathedrals, gendarmes, a port, French
  administrators, French missionaries, French gunboats."** This is the
  sentence the popular Gauguin literature mostly does not write. It is the
  difference between "exotic" and "colonial." The draft writes it.

- **The "primitif" fantasy is named as a fantasy he carried in his luggage,
  *not* as a place he found.** *"Gauguin's *primitive* Tahiti was a fantasy
  he carried with him in his luggage; the real Tahiti was a French outpost."*
  The Romantic frame is dismantled and the colonial one substituted.

- **Teha'amana, Pau'ura, and Vaeoho are named, their approximate ages are
  given, and their ages are qualified.** *"The figure is uncertain but she
  was certainly a child."* The "approximate / uncertain / qualified" model
  is exactly the Gate 5 ask.

- **The 19th-century-French-legal-context defense is named and then refused
  as a sufficient defense.** *"It is important not to project 21st-century
  legal categories backward without thinking — adolescent marriage was legal
  and common in late-19th-century France and across French colonial
  territory — and it is also important not to use that context to disappear
  what these relationships actually were. Gauguin was a European man in his
  40s using colonial power and money to set up sexual partnerships with
  Polynesian girls who could not, by any honest reading of the situation,
  have refused him on equal terms."* This is the right two-step. It blocks
  the most common "but it was legal then" deflection without flattening
  history.

- **The contradiction is held, not resolved:** *"He kept writing pamphlets
  attacking the colonial authorities for their treatment of the Polynesians,
  which is to his credit, while continuing his personal exploitation of
  Polynesian girls, which is not."* The chapter refuses to make Gauguin
  either a one-note villain or a misunderstood radical. It holds both.

### Issues found

- **SHOULD-FIX — the chapter names the relationships and the pictures
  but doesn't quite make the connection that some of the canonical
  Tahitian pictures *are pictures of these specific girls*.** It says
  *"Several of his greatest paintings — including *Manaò tupapaú* — depict
  these girls."* That is the right sentence, but it lands without the
  reader registering that the *Spirit of the Dead Watching* nude on the
  bed is **Teha'amana**, that the painting Gauguin presented as a Tahitian
  mythological subject was a portrait of a girl he had taken into his
  household whose age estimates begin at 13. The chapter has the facts;
  it could line them up more directly so a reader cannot leave the
  paragraph not knowing what the picture is of and who it is of.

### Nice-to-have

- The arsenic-suicide-attempt in 1897 and the *Where Do We Come From* mural
  as testament before the attempt are tied together correctly. The
  *which-was-painted-first* question is handled. This is fine, possibly
  STRONG.

- The line "His pictures and effects were auctioned cheap on Tahiti the
  following autumn. Within fifteen years they would be unaffordable in any
  market on earth." closes the chapter on the *value* arc, not the *ethics*
  arc. Could be read as ironic — the rising price as cosmic punchline — but
  some readers will read it as the chapter falling back into the
  market-rewarded-the-art frame the chapter has been refusing. Could
  consider a different closing beat — something that doesn't end with the
  market vindication. (Minor.)

## Chapter 6 — `seurat-lautrec` (Paris 1884–1901)

**Grade: FAIR**

### What it gets right

- **Lautrec's disability is framed as context, not engine.** The chapter
  *describes* the medical facts (the breaks, the inherited disorder, the
  inferred diagnosis, "no autopsy was done, no DNA exists; the diagnosis
  is a 20th-century inference from his medical history. Treat it as a
  guess") and then *does not* say that the disability produced the art.
  The chapter says Lautrec painted Montmartre because he moved there
  in 1884 and lived there for the rest of his life; the choice of subject
  is presented as a choice, not a consequence of the disability. This is
  the correct framing.
- **The brothel pictures are framed without the leer:** *"In the Salon at
  the Rue des Moulins (1894, Musée Toulouse-Lautrec, Albi) shows the
  brothel waiting room — three women on red plush sofas, dressed in long
  camisoles, bored, waiting. There is no leer in the painting. The women
  are people, off the clock, and Lautrec looks at them with the same
  matter-of-fact gaze he turns on the dancers and the drinkers."* This
  is well done. It names the subject as workers off shift, refuses both
  the prurient and the canonization frames.
- **Class is named and assembled:** *"Direct descendants of the medieval
  Counts of Toulouse… His parents were first cousins."* The chapter does
  the thing Chapter 2 should be doing — names the privilege that bought
  the freedom to paint in Montmartre as choice, not necessity.
- Seurat's death is hedged with the actual confusion: *"a sudden infection
  — possibly diphtheria, possibly meningitis; the record is unclear."*
  Refuses the "tragic genius cut down" frame by sitting with the
  evidentiary mess.

### Issues found

- **SHOULD-FIX — the chapter pairs Seurat and Lautrec as "two opposite
  ways out of Impressionism: science on the one hand, the modern Parisian
  night on the other. Neither was at peace." The "neither was at peace"
  closing line drifts toward the romantic-tragic-genius register the rest
  of the chapter has been resisting.** Seurat died of an unidentified
  infection at 31; Lautrec drank himself to death at 36. These are not
  the same kind of "not at peace." Seurat's death was not the consequence
  of any inner torment we have evidence for — he was working hard, had
  a partner, had a baby, was running a movement. Lautrec's death *was* the
  consequence of self-destructive behavior. The chapter ends as if they
  were both versions of the tortured-artist myth. Could be reframed: "Both
  died young, in cities where they had been working hard. Seurat at the
  height of his powers, of an infection no one understood; Lautrec at the
  end of a decade of heavy drinking and untreated illness. Different
  deaths, paired only by the calendar." Something that breaks the
  symmetry.

- **NICE-TO-HAVE — La Goulue, the dancer at the center of the *Moulin
  Rouge* poster, is named (Louise Weber) but appears only as the subject
  of Lautrec's gaze.** She had her own arc — she was the highest-paid
  performer at the Moulin Rouge, was famous in her own right, lost the
  job, ended in poverty as a sideshow attraction, died in 1929. Not the
  chapter's job to tell her life, but the existing one-phrase treatment
  is the standard art-history one-phrase treatment of the model. Could
  add one clause: "Louise Weber ('La Goulue' — the *Glutton*), the
  highest-paid dancer at the Moulin Rouge in 1891."

### Nice-to-have

- The chapter opens at the **1884 Salon des Indépendants** — a jury-free
  exhibiting society — and names women painters in the wider art world but
  not in this scene. The Indépendants accepted women from the start (it was
  the door the Beaux-Arts didn't open). One sentence — "Among the founding
  Indépendants were women painters too; the no-jury rule was the first thing
  in French exhibiting that didn't have a gender ceiling on it" — would
  ground a claim the chapter already implies.

## Chapter 7 — `nabis` (Paris 1888–1905, the wider field)

**Grade: FAIR**

The chapter is doing real ethical work — it widens the cast from five to
forty — but the widening is the *job* of Chapter 7 in the structure as
designed, which means the rest of the draft has been able to treat the
canon as canon because Chapter 7 catches it. The framing question is whether
the catch is enough.

### What it gets right

- **Cassatt is here, named, with the 1890 Japanese-print show as the
  inflection point, and her work positioned as *Post*-Impressionist on
  its late side.** *"Cassatt sits half in Impressionism (her early work)
  and half in Post-Impressionism (her later work), and the dividing line
  is sharp."* This is correct and is the kind of placement that contradicts
  the implicit five-Frenchmen frame of the preceding chapters.
- The Nabi cast is given in full, with their actual studio social fabric
  (Ranson's "Temple," the weekly meetings, the nicknames, the women — Marthe
  in Bonnard's life named with her real birth name Maria Boursin and the
  fact that she "claimed a noble background she did not have").
- **Marthe is named with her real biography**, not as Bonnard's muse. *"She
  changed her name to Marthe de Méligny when they met, claiming a noble
  background she did not have."* That single phrase reframes a woman who
  in most art-history treatment is a body in a bathtub.
- **Maurice Denis's catechism sentence is *contextualized*** — "He was 19
  years old" — and is *not* over-canonized; the chapter notes that the
  Taine 1865 precedent existed. Refuses the "Denis invented modernism in
  one sentence" frame.
- The Natanson brothers' role as patrons is named, including that they were
  "Polish-French Jewish founders of *La Revue blanche*." That is a fact
  most surveys omit; it is the kind of identification that matters because
  the Dreyfus-era French art world had its own ethnic-political fault lines
  and the Nabis were on a particular side of them. Good.
- Rousseau is hedged in real time: *"Rousseau is **adjacent** to Post-
  Impressionism, not central. He is better called an outsider whom the
  avant-garde elected as a forerunner."* Refuses canonization-by-association.

### Issues found

- **MUST-FIX — Berthe Morisot is not in this chapter, and the absence
  is the most significant single woman-shaped omission in the draft.**
  Morisot died in 1895; she was the *only* woman among the founding
  Impressionists; she was still working at high level through the early
  Post-Impressionist decade; her late work increasingly moves toward
  looser, more freely-colored handling that puts her on the bridge the
  chapter is describing. If Cassatt is in this chapter as a half-
  Impressionist half-Post-Impressionist, Morisot should be too — and
  Morisot's exclusion from "the five" is even sharper because she was
  *of* the prior generation, not just contemporary with it. One paragraph
  on Morisot is the smallest acceptable fix. The chapter's claim "By
  1900 there are not five Post-Impressionists, there are forty or fifty
  painters" needs at least the most obvious of the missing names to
  show up by name.

- **SHOULD-FIX — Suzanne Valadon is missing from the wider cast.** A
  professional model in Montmartre in the 1880s who became one of the
  most interesting painters of the next generation, who sat for Renoir
  and Lautrec and Degas, who learned to draw watching them and was
  encouraged by Lautrec specifically. She is the bridge between the
  Montmartre Lautrec painted *and* the next-generation painter who came
  out of that world rather than just modeling for it. Not central, but
  a movement-level chapter on Post-Impressionism that names the
  Natansons and Rippl-Rónai and not Valadon is leaving a pretty large
  woman out of the room.

- **NICE-TO-HAVE — Camille Claudel is absent.** Sculptor, not painter,
  but the chapter is already crowded with adjacent figures (Maillol,
  Maillol-the-sculptor is in the Nabi list). The Claudel-Rodin
  relationship is contemporary with the Pont-Aven and Nabi period and
  is the most famous ethical disaster in late-19th-century French art
  after Gauguin's. Probably out of scope for this chapter at this level
  of resolution, but flagging it because the absence is the same shape
  as the Morisot one.

### Nice-to-have

- The chapter ends with the sentence "All they need is somebody to walk
  into the room and put a name on the door." Lovely line, but the chapter
  has just spent four pages establishing that the room had forty painters
  and that Fry's "Post-Impressionism" reduced them to five. The closing
  line, in its lovely-line way, ratifies the umbrella that the chapter
  has been complicating. Could keep the sentence and add: "He would
  pick five. The other thirty-five would orbit the name afterward."

## Chapter 8 — `fry` (London, 8 November 1910)

**Grade: SOFT**

The chapter is competent on the Bloomsbury-as-defenders and the press-freakout
beats. It is **under-developed on the imperial-metropole frame**, which is
the chapter's central ethical exposure.

### What it gets right

- Fry is rendered as a person — recently fired from the Met, looking for work,
  on a printer's deadline. Refuses the "great critic discovers the future" frame.
- The naming-was-late-and-improvised story is told plainly: *"He had picked
  the name almost on the fly… **Post-Impressionists** was elastic enough to
  fit them all."* Good. The category is named as expedient, not insight.
- The press freakout is given in actual quoted contemporary language ("an
  extremely bad joke or a swindle," "the work of madmen," "the rejection of
  all that civilization has done"), with the visitor-book entries and the
  attendance and gate-receipt figures. This refuses the after-the-fact
  smoothing.
- Virginia Woolf's "on or about December 1910 human character changed" is
  named *and contextualized:* *"Woolf was talking about a whole shift in
  modernist sensibility, not just the Grafton show."* Refuses the line as
  a one-show-changes-everything pull quote.
- The "they were all dead" closing is *the* right closing — it makes the
  retrospective character of the umbrella unforgettable.

### Issues found

- **MUST-FIX — the chapter does not face that 1910 London is the imperial
  metropole and the Gauguin Tahitian pictures in the Grafton Galleries are
  trophies of empire as much as they are paintings.** This is the chapter's
  single largest framing gap. London in 1910 is the capital of the British
  Empire at the moment of its maximum extent — King Edward VII has just died
  in May, George V is on the throne, the empire covers roughly a quarter of
  the planet. The pictures Fry has hung include Gauguin's Tahitian nudes —
  pictures *of* colonized Polynesian women — being shown in the imperial
  capital, to a Mayfair audience, in the autumn after the Conservative
  *Daily Mail* has spent years selling the empire as civilizing mission.
  The chapter says nothing about it. The chapter spends three paragraphs on
  Roger Fry's printer's deadline and zero on the political-economic
  context of *whose pictures of whose bodies* are circulating to *whose
  galleries.* Chapter 5 names the colonial structure when the pictures are
  made; Chapter 8 has to name the colonial structure when the same pictures
  are *exhibited.* One paragraph: "The Grafton Galleries in November 1910
  was eight blocks from Buckingham Palace, two blocks from the Royal
  Academy, in the imperial capital at the moment of its widest extent. The
  Tahitian pictures on the walls — Gauguin's nudes of Polynesian girls in
  a French Pacific colony — were being looked at in the heart of British
  empire. The press freakout was about the painting style. The pictures
  themselves were trophies of one European empire being displayed in the
  capital of another. Nobody in the press noticed."

- **SHOULD-FIX — the Bloomsbury defense of the show is rendered as the
  birth of British modernism, which it was, but the Bloomsbury Group's own
  imperial and colonial entanglements are left out.** The chapter names
  Lytton Strachey, Keynes, Virginia Woolf, Vanessa Bell, Duncan Grant. It
  doesn't say Keynes was working at the India Office until 1908. It doesn't
  say Leonard Woolf had served seven years as a colonial administrator in
  Ceylon before resigning in 1911 (i.e., the year of the show's close).
  These are not random facts; they are the actual class-and-empire
  positions of the people in the room. The chapter doesn't have to make a
  whole argument about it; it has to put one beat in. "The Bloomsbury
  Group defending Fry in 1910 were the daughters and sons of the imperial
  professional class — Keynes was on his way out of the India Office,
  Leonard Woolf was about to leave seven years of colonial service in
  Ceylon. They were not outsiders to empire. They were the children of
  empire defending modern French painting in empire's capital."

- **SHOULD-FIX — the Armory Show (1913) is named as "where American
  audiences encountered the entire Post-Impressionist and early-modernist
  range at once" and "American collectors went home and started buying."
  That last clause is the chapter's only acknowledgment that one of the
  things Post-Impressionism *was*, by 1913, was a market.** And not a
  small one. The pictures that had been auctioned cheap on Tahiti in
  1903 were now being sold in New York for the kind of prices that started
  the modern art market. The chapter has named the £4,600 in 1910 gate
  receipts and the $82.5 million 1990 *Gachet* sale and the $250–320
  million 2011 *Card Players.* The arc is in the draft but not assembled.
  One closing beat in Chapter 8 should: the picture that was made by a
  comfortably-inherited Provençal at his easel in front of a mountain was
  by 1990 the most expensive object an American auction house had ever
  rung up. The market is part of the story. The chapter is one paragraph
  short of saying so.

### Nice-to-have

- "Manet and the Post-Impressionists" — Manet as the elder father-figure is
  named but it isn't quite said what borrowing Manet was rhetorically doing:
  Manet was already canonical and respectable by 1910, and putting his name
  at the front of the title was Fry's way of buying credibility for the
  unknown French painters behind him. The chapter has the facts; could spell
  out the rhetorical move.

## Cross-chapter synthesis

### What works at the movement level

- **The Gauguin chapter is the model.** It is the chapter the brief was
  most worried about and it does the job: names the wife and children,
  names the abandonment, names the colonial structure with dates, names
  the girls with qualified ages, refuses the "but it was legal then"
  defense as sufficient, holds the contradiction (anti-colonial pamphlets +
  personal exploitation), and refuses to make the pictures pay for the
  ethics. Lock that chapter as the template.

- **The class fact is named in three places** — Cézanne in Ch 1 and Ch 3,
  Lautrec in Ch 2 and Ch 6, Gauguin in Ch 5. It is not assembled into the
  larger observation (Post-Impressionism's starving-artist myth was
  largely a choice made by comfortable men) but the facts are there.

- **Two recurring myths are explicitly hedged:** Lautrec's pycnodysostosis
  as *inferred*, the Naifeh-Smith Van Gogh hypothesis as *hypothesis.* Both
  hedges are done correctly. The draft has the discipline to leave a fact
  uncertain when the fact is uncertain.

### What the draft does *not* do enough

- **The five-anchors structure is itself a framing decision** that the
  draft inherits from Fry without naming as a decision until Chapter 8 (and
  even there it's named structurally — "the umbrella was retrospective" —
  not in the terms "this is the men's umbrella, this is why no women are
  in it, this is why this isn't the only true shape of the field."). The
  fix is small (one paragraph in Ch 2, one mention of Morisot in Ch 7,
  Cassatt named earlier than Ch 7) but real.

- **The Bloomsbury-as-imperial-class fact, and the Gauguin-Tahitian-
  pictures-as-imperial-trophies fact, are missing from Chapter 8.** The
  draft does the colonial ethics in Chapter 5 and then drops the thread
  when it returns to London in Chapter 8. The colonial frame should
  follow the pictures across the Channel.

- **Women appear mostly in single-phrase appearances** — Hortense Fiquet,
  Marthe Boursin, Jo van Gogh-Bonger, La Goulue. The phrases are
  individually fine; the cumulative effect is the standard one-phrase-
  and-on we want to push against. Marthe is the best of these (her real
  name and her claimed-noble-background are named); Jo is the most
  under-credited (a single clause for the person who built Vincent's
  posthumous reputation); Hortense is the most invisible.

### Verdict

Ship-blocking flags: none. None of the grades are EVASIVE. The MUST-FIXes
are all editable in single paragraphs and do not require restructuring.

The two SOFT chapters (Ch 2, Ch 8) need their MUST-FIX additions before
ship. The remaining FAIR chapters need their SHOULD-FIXes worked through
before ship; the SHOULD-FIXes are improvements, not corrections.

Once those are in, this is the cleanest popular treatment of Post-
Impressionism I have read on these specific ethical questions. The
Gauguin chapter alone is the strongest single chapter on a colonial-era
European painter in the pipeline so far.
