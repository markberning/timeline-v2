# PART A — Movement metadata

## hook (one sentence)

Where Abstract Expressionism flung paint from the soul, Pop hung a soup can on the wall and refused to say whether it was a joke.

## hookLong (2-4 sentences)

Pop Art took the cheapest, most public images anybody saw every day, soup labels, comic strips, advertising, movie stars, supermarket packaging, and made them the content of serious art, done cold. It was the cool, flat, mechanical answer to Abstract Expressionism's hot, gestural, soul-baring painting (the movement Pop dethroned), and it started in Britain first, not America: an informal London discussion club of young artists, critics, and architects called the Independent Group got obsessed with glossy American mass culture in grey, rationed postwar Britain years before Warhol picked up a silkscreen, the printing trick that let him repeat an image mechanically. Then America made it enormous, Warhol's grids of soup cans and silkscreened Marilyns, Lichtenstein's giant comic-strip panels, Oldenburg's saggy soft hamburgers, and ran it as a deliberate machine, with Warhol saying flatly "I want to be a machine." The deepest question Pop poses it never answers on purpose: is this a love letter to consumer abundance or a deadpan burial under it, and the same deadpan that makes that question unanswerable also hid, for decades, the women it wrote out (Britain's Pauline Boty above all), the near-total whiteness of its canon, and the uncredited people whose real designs and labor the machine ran on.

## artists row (~7)

1. **Richard Hamilton** (1922–2011): the father of British Pop. Made the tiny 1956 collage *Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing?* and the next year wrote the letter to the Smithsons that defined Pop like an ad slogan; the most deliberate theorist of the movement. (Portrait: in copyright, use the gradient fallback.)
2. **Eduardo Paolozzi** (1924–2005): the proto-Pop collagist. Scottish-Italian; glued American magazine and advertising cuttings into the *BUNK!* collages from 1947, putting mass imagery into art a decade before anyone called it Pop, including the 1947 collage with a toy gun firing the word "POP!" (Portrait: gradient.)
3. **Andy Warhol** (1928–1987): the icon and the machine. Soup cans, silkscreened Marilyns, Brillo boxes, the Factory, "I want to be a machine"; turned celebrity, repetition, and appropriation into the center of art. (Portrait: gradient, in copyright.)
4. **Roy Lichtenstein** (1923–1997): the comic-strip painter. Blew up war and romance comic panels in primary colors with hand-painted Ben-Day dots (*Whaam!*, *Drowning Girl*). (Portrait: gradient.)
5. **Claes Oldenburg** (1929–2022): the maker of giant soft things. *The Store* (1961), floppy vinyl hamburgers, monumental everyday objects. (Portrait: gradient.)
6. **James Rosenquist** (1933–2017): the billboard painter. Brought commercial sign-painting's scale and splice into fine art (*F-111*, room-length). (Portrait: gradient.)
7. **Pauline Boty** (1938–1966): the woman British Pop nearly erased. The only prominent woman of British Pop; painted female desire and the male gaze head-on; died at 28 and was written out of the story for decades. (Portrait: gradient.)

(Optional 8th if room: **Jasper Johns**, b.1930, the bridge, *Flag*; **Robert Rauschenberg**, 1925–2008, the combines, the bridge; **Marisol** (Escobar), 1930–2016, the carved-wood pop totems Warhol called "the first girl artist with glamour"; **Tom Wesselmann**, 1931–2004, the *Great American Nude*; **Ed Ruscha**, b.1937, the West Coast gas stations and word-paintings.)

## parallels (2-3 "meanwhile")

1. **1950s–1960s · USA / UK**: *The postwar consumer boom and the advertising age.* Pop is inseparable from the supermarket, the suburb, the TV set, and Madison Avenue. Postwar prosperity flooded America (and, later and hungrier, Britain) with branded goods, appliances, and ads, and Pop is the art that took that flood as its subject. The British fascination was sharper precisely because Britain was still grey and rationed while American abundance glittered from across the Atlantic.
2. **1947–1960s · New York**: *Abstract Expressionism's reign and fall.* While Pop was being born, AbEx (Pollock, Rothko, de Kooning) was the establishment avant-garde, championed by the critic Clement Greenberg as the heroic, soul-baring high point of painting. Pop's rise is the story of AbEx being dethroned: the hot lone genius replaced by the cool machine, two New York art worlds at once, one baring its soul, one silkscreening a soup can.
3. **1950s–1960s · the West**: *Mass media and celebrity culture exploding.* Television, glossy magazines, and Hollywood's star machine all peaked in exactly these years, manufacturing fame as a product. Warhol's Marilyns (made the month she died) and his celebrity portraits ride that wave directly: fame, repetition, and image-as-merchandise.

## lineage

**Parents (gave):**
- **Abstract Expressionism** (New York, 1940s–50s): gave: the foil. The parent-by-reaction. Pop is the cool, flat, mechanical, outward-looking answer to AbEx's hot, gestural, inward art; it took AbEx's scale and the New York gallery world and inverted everything else. (Reference the AbEx contrast, do not re-tell it.)
- **Neo-Dada / Johns and Rauschenberg** (mid-1950s): gave: the door. The direct bridge. By dragging the everyday object and image back into art, they cracked AbEx open and opened the way to Pop.
- **Dada and the readymade** (1916–24): gave: collage, appropriation, the readymade (Duchamp's signed urinal). Paolozzi's and Hamilton's collages descend straight from Dada collage. (Reference the Dada read, do not re-explain it.)
- **Mass and commercial culture itself** (advertising, comics, packaging, Hollywood, the supermarket): gave: the literal raw material and the subject.

**Children (took):**
- **Conceptual art** (1960s on): took: the idea that the thought, not the handmade object, can be the art (the Brillo box question pushed to its limit).
- **The Pictures Generation** (late 1970s–80s: Sherrie Levine, Richard Prince, Cindy Sherman): took: appropriation, now wielded as a critical strategy, straight from Warhol.
- **Neo-Pop / Jeff Koons** (1980s on): took: consumer kitsch and branding as fine art.
- **Street art, graphic design, advertising**: took: the dissolved high/low wall, so the museum and the market now share a visual language.

## influenceSummary (one italic sentence)

*Pop took Abstract Expressionism's scale and Dada's readymade, turned art outward to the cheapest, most public mass imagery and rendered it cool and mechanical, and handed appropriation and mechanical reproduction forward as legitimate art, dissolving the wall between fine art and the commercial image so completely that the museum and the supermarket now speak the same visual language.*

**prose (3 paragraphs):**

1. In the New York of the 1950s, one kind of painting sat at the top of the heap. Abstract Expressionism (the first art movement America could call its own, huge canvases of pure abstract gesture and raw feeling) had dethroned Paris and ruled the serious art world. The picture was made of the artist's inner life and nothing else: Jackson Pollock poured and flung skeins of house paint across canvas laid on the floor; Mark Rothko stacked vast soft rectangles of glowing color meant to swallow you whole. It was hot, gestural, heroic, abstract, and deadly serious, the lone genius baring his soul, and even its rebels agreed on the one thing underneath it all: art comes from inside the artist. (The Abstract Expressionism read tells that story in full; here it is only the wall Pop pushed against.)

2. Pop turned the whole thing inside out. It threw the camera away from the artist's soul and pointed it at the most public, most disposable images everybody already saw every day: soup labels, comic panels, movie stars, billboards, supermarket packaging. And it treated them dead straight, with no irony you could pin down and no feeling on display. Where AbEx was hot, Pop went cool. Where AbEx was a storm of personal brushwork, Pop went flat, deadpan, mechanical, and impersonal, the artist's hand deliberately erased by commercial techniques: the silkscreen, the printed Ben-Day dot. Andy Warhol said it as plainly as it can be said. "I want to be a machine."

3. That is the break, and it is why Pop counts as a new movement and not just a new subject. The thing art was made of had changed. For centuries the art world had drawn a hard line between "high" culture (the museum, serious painting, the things worth taking seriously) and "low" or commercial culture (advertising, comics, packaging, the throwaway images of daily life), and that line was treated as natural and important: real art was partly defined by not being the other thing. Pop walked straight across it. A Campbell's soup can now hung where a Rothko had hung, painted not to express a feeling but to sit there, cool and blank, exactly as a soup can sits on a shelf. The subject of art was no longer the artist's private inner world; it was the shared outer world of advertising and the store. And Pop refused, on purpose, to tell you how to feel about that. Whether the soup can is a celebration of plenty or a deadpan indictment of it is left unanswered, which is itself the most modern thing about it.

**prose for the block (tell it straight):**

Pop never got a manifesto. It got a letter. Most movements announce themselves with a public declaration, a sheet of demands nailed to the door of the art world. Pop had no such thing, no founding document and no signatures, which fits a movement that was deadpan and noncommittal on principle. What it had instead was a private letter. In 1957 Richard Hamilton wrote to his friends Alison and Peter Smithson, two architects in the Independent Group, and listed what "pop art" should be. The list is the closest thing the movement ever had to a creed, and it reads exactly like the advertising it was about: "Popular, transient, expendable, low cost, mass produced, young, witty, sexy, gimmicky, glamorous, big business." That is not a call to arms. It is a product description. Which is the whole point. (Andy Warhol gave the movement its other famous lines, but they are color, not a creed: "I want to be a machine," from a 1963 interview, and a line about fifteen minutes of fame that got printed in 1968 and that he may never actually have said, two facts worth keeping straight.)

## Chapter 1 — Britain saw it first

### London · 1947–1956 · Grey island, glossy dream

Start with the thing almost everybody gets wrong. Pop Art did not start in America with Andy Warhol. It started in Britain, years earlier, and it started for a very British reason: envy. In the late 1940s and early 1950s Britain was still digging out of the Second World War. Food was rationed (some rationing did not end until 1954), cities were bombed flat, money was tight, and everything was, by every account, grey. And across the Atlantic sat America, fat and bright and undamaged, pumping out a flood of glossy mass culture that washed up on British shores in magazines and movies: Hollywood stars, enormous chrome Detroit cars, full-color Madison Avenue advertising, science-fiction pulp, comic books, and supermarket packaging in colors Britain had not seen on its own shelves in years. To a young British artist, all that American abundance was not just stuff. It was a fantasy, half-loved and half-suspicious, beamed in from a richer planet. That mixture, hunger plus a cool, analytical distance, is where Pop was born.

The people who first turned it into art were a loose circle of young artists, critics, and architects who met at the Institute of Contemporary Arts in London. They called themselves the **Independent Group** (an informal discussion club, not a school or a gallery), and they first convened in the winter of 1952 to 1953, with a second run in 1953 to 1954. Two of them carry this chapter: the sculptor and collagist **Eduardo Paolozzi**, who had the eye, and the painter **Richard Hamilton**, who supplied the theory. Around them sat a handful of photographers, designers, and critics, including **Lawrence Alloway**, the critic usually credited with first attaching the phrase "Pop art" to all this, and the architects **Alison and Peter Smithson**, to whom Hamilton would later write the letter that defined the movement. What bound the group was not a style. It was a refusal to sneer. The respectable view was that American mass culture was junk, beneath the notice of serious art. The Independent Group looked at the junk and got fascinated. They studied car styling and ad layouts and sci-fi covers with the seriousness other people gave to old masters, and asked the question that became Pop: why is this not art?

### Paolozzi · A toy gun that fires the word "POP"

The first artist to act on that question was Eduardo Paolozzi (1924 to 2005), a Scottish-Italian sculptor who had been hoarding American magazines for years. As early as **1947**, a full decade before anyone used the term "Pop art," Paolozzi was cutting up that hoard and gluing the pieces into **collage** (an image built by pasting found scraps of printed paper onto a surface, a technique he inherited from Dada and Surrealist collage, covered in the earlier reads). He showed a slide-lecture of them at the ICA in 1952, and they were later collected under the title *BUNK!*

The one to picture is *I was a Rich Man's Plaything*, made in 1947 and now at the Tate. It is a small thing, a sheet of pasted-down cuttings, but it is dense with the whole future of Pop. There is the cover of a pulp romance magazine, with a glamorous woman's face; a Coca-Cola advertisement; a cherry-pie ad; a pin-up; and a pointing hand from an Army recruiting poster. And in the top corner, cut from the packaging of a toy gun, a pistol fires so that a little puff of smoke balloons out of the barrel with a single word printed inside it: "POP!" That puff is often called the first appearance of the very word in this art, a comic-book gunshot that happens to spell out the name of a movement that did not yet exist. The picture is in copyright and cannot be shown here, so hold it in your mind: the cheapest disposable American imagery, glued together by a man on a grey island who could not have it, with the word "POP" going off like a cap gun in the corner.

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Paolozzi, *I was a Rich Man's Plaything*, 1947, Tate (T01462), and the *BUNK!* series; in copyright, permission via DACS. No figure.]

### Hamilton · The whole consumer dream in one room

The picture that usually gets called the first iconic Pop work came nine years later, from Richard Hamilton (1922 to 2011), the most deliberate thinker in the group. In the summer of 1956 the Whitechapel Art Gallery in London staged an exhibition called **"This Is Tomorrow,"** in which twelve interdisciplinary teams (38 participants in all, the core drawn from the Independent Group) each built an environment about modern living. For the catalogue, Hamilton made a tiny collage, about ten inches square, with a title as long as a sentence: *Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing?*

It is a single modern living room, pasted together from magazine cuttings, and stuffed with every shiny promise of 1950s consumer life at once. A bodybuilder stands flexing in the center, naked except for his trunks, holding up a giant Tootsie Pop lollipop the size of a tennis racket, with the word "POP" running up its wrapper (there is the word again). A pin-up lounges on the sofa wearing a lampshade hat. There is a television, a reel-to-reel tape recorder, a vacuum cleaner with a hose snaking up the stairs, a tinned ham sitting on the coffee table like a centerpiece, a framed comic-book cover hung on the wall as if it were a painting, and a lampshade stamped with the Ford logo. Through the window you can see a movie marquee. It is the entire dream of postwar abundance, appliances, celebrity, packaged food, advertising, all crammed, deadpan, into one room. Hamilton was not painting any of it from life; he was assembling it from the ads, which is exactly the point. The picture is itself a piece of consumer culture, made of consumer culture. Like everything in this read, it is in copyright and lives here in description only.

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Hamilton, *Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing?*, 1956, Kunsthalle Tübingen. No figure.]

### The letter that read like an ad

The year after that, Hamilton wrote the document that became the nearest thing Pop ever had to a creed, and it was not a public manifesto at all. In 1957 he wrote a private letter to Alison and Peter Smithson, the two architects from the group, and in it he listed the "characteristics of pop art." (A **manifesto**, in the sense the earlier reads use it, is a public declaration a movement nails up to announce what it stands for. This was no such thing. It was a letter between friends. But it got reprinted so often that it ended up doing a manifesto's job.) The list is the movement in a nutshell, and the striking thing is that it reads exactly like the advertising it describes: "Pop Art is: Popular, transient, expendable, low cost, mass produced, young, witty, sexy, gimmicky, glamorous, big business." Notice what is not in it. There is nothing about the soul, nothing about beauty, nothing about genius. It is a product spec sheet for a movement, and that flatness is the message. (The manifesto block above carries the full list and its source.)

British Pop kept going after Hamilton. **Peter Blake** folded badges and magazines and pop-culture faces into his paintings and would later design the cover of the Beatles' *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band* (1967), the most famous Pop image most people own without knowing it. A younger British generation followed, **David Hockney**, **Allen Jones**, **R. B. Kitaj**, **Derek Boshier**, and one prominent woman, **Pauline Boty**, whom Chapter 5 will not let the story forget. But by the late 1950s the center of gravity was already shifting west. The British had the idea first. The Americans were about to make it loud.

[MEANWHILE: 1950s, USA and UK. The whole movement is inseparable from the postwar consumer boom. American prosperity flooded the suburbs with branded goods, appliances, supermarkets, and television, and Madison Avenue turned advertising into a high-gloss art of its own. Britain got the same flood later and hungrier, still grey and rationed while American abundance glittered in imported magazines. That gap is exactly why the British saw the strangeness of consumer culture so clearly: they were looking at it from outside, with their noses against the glass.]

## Chapter 2 — Killing the lone genius

### New York · the 1950s · The painting on top of the heap

To see why American Pop hit like a thrown brick, you have to know what it was thrown at. In the New York of the 1950s, the most serious painting in the world was **Abstract Expressionism** (the first art movement America could call its own, and the one that finally pulled the center of the art world from Paris to New York). It was huge, abstract, and made entirely of feeling. Jackson Pollock laid canvas on the floor and poured, dripped, and flung house paint across it in great looping skeins, the picture a record of his own body moving. Mark Rothko stacked enormous soft rectangles of glowing color meant to swallow the viewer whole and reduce some of them to tears. Willem de Kooning slashed at the canvas with a loaded brush until a woman's face half-emerged from the violence. The work was hot, gestural, heroic, and deadly serious, and underneath all of it ran one shared faith: that real art comes from inside the artist, poured out raw, and that the painter is a kind of lone genius wrestling his own soul onto the wall. (The Abstract Expressionism read tells that story properly; here it is only the giant Pop had to knock over.)

That faith had hardened into an orthodoxy, and orthodoxies invite revolt. The first cracks came not from the Pop artists themselves but from two slightly older New Yorkers who loved the everyday world too much to leave it out. They are usually filed under a different label, **Neo-Dada** (literally "new Dada," art that revived the old Dada trick of dragging real objects and found images back into fine art), and the experts are careful not to call them Pop artists proper. They are the bridge: the door from Abstract Expressionism that Pop would later walk through.

### Johns · A flag that is only a flag

The quieter of the two was **Jasper Johns** (born 1930, and still alive), and the picture that broke the spell was a flag. Between 1954 and 1955 Johns painted *Flag*, the Stars and Stripes, life-size, filling the whole canvas with nothing else. There is no sky behind it, no pole, no scene, no story; it is just the flag, edge to edge. And here is the move. Abstract Expressionism insisted a painting be a window into the artist's inner life. Johns picked the single most familiar, most public, flattest image he could think of, an image that is already a flat design before anybody paints it, and painted that. A flag is not a feeling. It is a thing everybody already recognizes without thinking. By making his subject something so common it is almost invisible, Johns drained the personal soul right out of painting. He even painted it in **encaustic** (an old technique of mixing pigment into hot wax), laid over scraps of newspaper, so that the surface up close is thick, textured, and lovingly handmade, full of lumps and stuck-down print, while the image it adds up to stays as flat and impersonal as the flag on a post-office wall. Is it a painting of a flag, or is it just a flag? Johns will not say, and that refusal is the seed of everything Pop. The work is in copyright and lives here in words.

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Johns, *Flag*, 1954–55, MoMA (encaustic, oil, and collage on fabric on plywood). Johns b.1930, alive. No figure.]

### Rauschenberg · A bed on the wall

The louder one was **Robert Rauschenberg** (1925 to 2008), Johns's close friend and downstairs studio neighbor, who attacked the same orthodoxy from the opposite direction. Where Johns made a flat public image, Rauschenberg dragged actual three-dimensional junk straight into the picture. He coined a word for the result: the **combine** (his own term for a work that combines painting with real found objects bolted, glued, or hung onto it, half-painting and half-sculpture). The one to picture is *Bed*, from 1955. Rauschenberg took his own bedding, a quilt, a sheet, and a pillow, hung it upright on the wall like a canvas, and then splashed and dribbled paint down it in a way that looks, deliberately, exactly like a Pollock. It is a brilliant joke and a serious argument at once. He keeps Abstract Expressionism's gestural, poured paint, but he pours it onto a real bed, the most intimate, ordinary object in any human life. The heroic gesture lands on a household object, and the spell breaks. Real life is back in the painting, literally stitched into it.

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Rauschenberg, *Bed*, 1955, MoMA (the first "combine," oil and pencil on pillow, quilt, and sheet on wood). Rauschenberg d.2008. No figure.]

### The turn from inside to outside

None of this went down quietly. There was an establishment on the other side of it, and it had defenders. The critic Clement Greenberg, the most powerful voice in American art, had spent years arguing that Abstract Expressionism was the high point of all painting, the place the whole art of the West had been heading, and to him and the people who had crowned the movement, dragging a flag or a bed back into a picture looked like a betrayal, a step backward into the ordinary world serious painting had finally escaped. The older painters, who had fought their way up from poverty and obscurity to make abstraction matter, did not relish watching two younger men make jokes with their hard-won gestures. The turn outward was not just an idea; it was a fight over who got to say what serious art was, and it cost the people making it the approval of the men who then ran the room.

Still, hold Johns and Rauschenberg side by side and the hinge is clear. Abstract Expressionism looked inward, at the artist's soul, and made art out of pure gesture and feeling. These two turned the painting back outward, toward the shared, ordinary, public world: a flag, a bed, a newspaper. They did not yet treat that world as coolly and mechanically as Pop would; their surfaces are still rich and handmade, still in love with paint. But they had done the essential thing. They had reopened the door between art and the everyday object that Abstract Expressionism had slammed shut, and they had done it without sentiment or a sermon. Through that door, in 1962, walked Andy Warhol, still paying his bills drawing shoe advertisements, about to push the cool, the flat, and the mechanical further than either of them had dared.

[MEANWHILE: New York, 1947 to the early 1960s. Even as Abstract Expressionism reigned, championed by the powerful critic Clement Greenberg as the high point of all painting, its dominance was the thing that made the revolt necessary. For about fifteen years two New York art worlds overlapped: the older one baring its soul in poured paint, the younger one about to silkscreen a soup can. Pop's whole rise is the story of the second world dethroning the first.]

## Chapter 3 — The supermarket walks into the museum

### Andy Warhol · The man who wanted to be a machine

The center of the American wave, and of the whole movement in most people's memory, is **Andy Warhol** (1928 to 1987). Before he was a fine artist he was a hugely successful commercial illustrator, drawing shoe ads in New York, and he never pretended to outgrow that. The commercial world was not something Warhol slummed in on his way to art. It was his subject and his method, and he flattened the wall between the two on purpose. In 1962 he made the work that is shorthand for the whole movement, and it could hardly be more ordinary: thirty-two paintings of soup cans.

*Campbell's Soup Cans* (1962) is thirty-two separate canvases, each a little under two feet tall, each showing a single Campbell's soup can, painted close to mechanically, one canvas for each variety the company then sold (Tomato, Chicken Noodle, and on down the list). When they were first shown, at the Ferus Gallery in Los Angeles in July 1962, they were propped on a thin shelf running around the wall, exactly the way cans sit in a grocery aisle. That is the entire gesture, and it is enormous: the supermarket walks into the museum and hangs where a Rothko hung. There is no feeling on display, no brushwork to admire, no story. Just the can, and then the can again, and again, the dull repetition of the shelf treated as a subject worthy of a wall. The soup cans are in copyright and cannot be shown; the closest the page can offer is a period photograph of the actual aisle they came from, captioned for what it is.

Warhol himself was as deadpan as the work, and the blankness was a performance he never dropped. In interviews he answered questions with "yes," "no," "gee," and "I don't know," insisting there was nothing behind the surface and nothing to find, that if you wanted to know Andy Warhol you should just look at the surface of his paintings and there he was, with nothing behind it. The pale skin, the silver wig, the dark glasses worn indoors were a costume of vacancy, and whether it hid a sphinx or hid nothing at all was exactly the question he wanted you stuck on. He turned not-answering into a kind of genius.

Warhol's next move industrialized the art itself. He switched to **silkscreen** (a printing technique that pushes ink through a stencil mesh to lay down an image fast and identically, over and over, the same process used to print commercial posters and T-shirts). Silkscreen let him reproduce an image as many times as he liked, mechanically, with almost no trace of his own hand, which was the whole appeal. He set up a studio he called **the Factory** (a real loft, famously lined with silver foil, and the name is the point: a place where art is manufactured, not agonized over), filled it with assistants, and turned out work in editions like a print shop. When Marilyn Monroe died in August 1962, Warhol made *Marilyn Diptych*, a grid of fifty silkscreened Marilyns taken from a single publicity still, the left half in hot, garish color, the right half printed in fading, smeared grays until she nearly disappears. It is celebrity, mass reproduction, and death all at once: the star reduced to a repeatable product, copied until she wears out. And when the critic Gene Swenson asked Warhol why he worked this way, in a 1963 magazine interview, he gave the line that is the closest thing the movement has to a motto, at least in the form it was printed at the time: "I want to be a machine."

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Warhol, *Marilyn Diptych*, 1962, Tate (silkscreen). Warhol d.1987. No figure.]

The most provocative thing Warhol ever made was a box. In 1964, at the Stable Gallery in New York, he showed the **Brillo Boxes**: stacks of plywood boxes, silkscreened so carefully that they looked exactly like the cardboard shipping cartons of Brillo soap pads you would find stacked in any stockroom. Not a painting of a box. A box, indistinguishable from the real thing, presented as sculpture. The question it forced is the one Pop kept asking and never answered: if a work of art is identical to a grocery carton, what makes one art and the other trash? Is it the gallery, the signature, the price, the intention? The Brillo box put a philosophical bomb under the whole idea of art, and it had a sharp twist at its center that Chapter 4 will return to: the design Warhol copied was not Brillo's anonymous packaging. It was the work of a real, named commercial artist.

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Warhol, *Brillo Boxes*, 1964, Stable Gallery debut (silkscreen and house paint on plywood). Warhol d.1987. No figure.]

### Lichtenstein · The comic strip blown up huge

If Warhol made art out of the supermarket, **Roy Lichtenstein** (1923 to 1997) made it out of the comic book. He took single panels from cheap printed comics, war comics and romance comics, and blew them up to enormous size, painted in flat primary colors with bold black outlines. The signature detail is the dots. Cheap color comics were printed using **Ben-Day dots** (a commercial printing process, named for the printer Benjamin Day, that builds up shades and tones out of tiny evenly spaced colored dots; look closely at any old comic and you will see them). Lichtenstein kept the dots, and then painted them by hand, one by one, through a stencil, faking the look of cheap mechanical printing with slow, deliberate craft.

The icon is *Whaam!*, from 1963, a painting thirteen feet wide in two panels. On the left, a fighter plane fires a rocket; on the right, the enemy plane erupts in a yellow-and-red explosion with the word "WHAAM!" lettered across it in jagged comic-book caps. He took it from a real panel in a DC war comic, *All-American Men of War*, and recomposed and simplified it. Just as deadpan is *Drowning Girl* (1963), a single comic panel of a weeping woman sinking under a stylized wave, a thought bubble over her head reading "I don't care! I'd rather sink than call Brad for help!" Lichtenstein froze a throwaway moment of soap-opera melodrama and monumentalized it, dots and all, so that the cheapest, most disposable storytelling in America hung in a museum at the scale of history painting. Both works are in copyright and described here only.

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Lichtenstein, *Whaam!*, 1963, Tate (diptych), and *Drowning Girl*, 1963, MoMA. Lichtenstein d.1997. No figure.]

### Oldenburg and Rosenquist · Soft giants and billboard scale

Two more Americans round out the big wave. **Claes Oldenburg** (1929 to 2022) attacked the everyday object from yet another angle: he made it huge and soft. In 1961 he rented a storefront on the Lower East Side and ran it as *The Store*, an actual shop where he sold lumpy, brightly painted plaster hamburgers, slices of pie, and other goods, the gallery turned literally into the store it was about. Then came his **soft sculptures**: giant versions of hard everyday objects, a hamburger, a typewriter, an electric fan, remade in floppy stuffed vinyl and canvas so they sag and droop. *Floor Burger* (1962) is a hamburger the size of a sofa, sitting on the floor, and the wrongness of it is physical before it is funny. A burger is supposed to be small enough to hold and firm enough to bite; this one is big enough to lie down on and soft enough that the bun sinks under its own weight, the whole thing slumping sideways like something melting in slow motion. You stand next to an object that your hands and your stomach both recognize, scaled and softened until it stops being food and starts being a body, the most familiar thing in the world made monstrous and absurd.

**James Rosenquist** (1933 to 2017) brought the one thing the others did not have, the literal scale and technique of advertising, because he had painted actual billboards for a living. His masterwork is *F-111* (1964–65), a painting that wraps around an entire room, about eighty-six feet of interlocking panels. Stretched the length of it is a US fighter-bomber, the F-111, and spliced across and through it, in the abrupt cut-up style of overlapping billboards, are a tangle of spaghetti, a car tire, a beach umbrella over a mushroom cloud, and a smiling little girl under a salon hair-dryer shaped like a missile nose cone. Consumer abundance and the war machine are jammed into one continuous surface, at the overwhelming scale of the ads on the highway. Both Oldenburg's and Rosenquist's works are in copyright and live in words.

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Oldenburg, *The Store*, 1961, and *Floor Burger*, 1962, Art Gallery of Ontario; Rosenquist, *F-111*, 1964–65, MoMA. Oldenburg d.2022, Rosenquist d.2017. No figure.]

So the manner is now complete, and it is the exact opposite of Abstract Expressionism on every axis. Cool, not hot. Flat, not gestural. Mechanical, not handmade (or, in Lichtenstein's case, handmade to look mechanical). Impersonal, not soul-baring. Outward, at the shared world of the store and the screen, not inward at the artist. The lone genius wrestling his soul onto the canvas has been replaced by a man who says he wants to be a machine, and means it as a compliment.

[MEANWHILE: the West, the 1950s and 1960s. Mass media and celebrity culture were exploding in exactly these years. Television saturated the home, glossy magazines manufactured taste, and Hollywood's star machine turned human beings into reproducible images. Warhol's Marilyns and his celebrity portraits ride that wave directly: fame itself becomes a product to be printed, repeated, and sold, which is half of what his silkscreens are about.]

## Chapter 4 — Celebration or indictment?

### A designer walks into a gallery

In April 1964, a commercial artist named James Harvey walked into the Stable Gallery in New York and found his own work hanging on the wall as art, with another man's name on it. Three years earlier Harvey had designed the cardboard shipping carton for Brillo soap pads, the red, white, and blue box you would find stacked in any supermarket stockroom. He did package design for a living and painted abstract canvases at night, and the Brillo box was just a job. Now here it was, rebuilt in plywood, silkscreened to look exactly like his carton, stacked in a gallery, and selling as a Warhol. None of the money, and none of the credit, was coming to him. Hold that scene, because it is the whole argument of this chapter in one room: a piece of throwaway commercial design, lifted without credit, turned into expensive fine art, and nobody quite able to say whether that is theft, homage, a brilliant idea, or all three at once. Pop is built on exactly that uneasiness, and it never resolves it.

### The question Pop will not answer

Start with the bigger uncertainty, the one the Harvey scene sits inside. Here is the strangest thing about Pop Art, and the reason it stays interesting long after the soup can stopped being shocking: nobody can agree on what it thinks. Stand in front of Warhol's soup cans or Lichtenstein's explosion and ask the obvious question, is this for consumer culture or against it, and the work simply stares back. There are two completely opposite readings, both serious, and the art refuses to settle between them. This is not a gap in the scholarship that someone will eventually fill in. It is the central, deliberate fact of the movement, and the honest thing is to lay out both readings and leave them standing.

The first reading is **celebration**. On this view Pop is a genuine love letter to the modern world: democratic, generous, optimistic, finally honest about what people actually look at and enjoy. The supermarket, the comic, the movie star, the can of soup are the real shared culture of ordinary life, far more than any Old Master, and Pop has the nerve and the affection to put them on the wall without sneering. Warhol said he loved Coca-Cola precisely because the president and the poorest person both drink the same Coke, the same product for everyone, and meant it warmly. From this angle Pop is the most democratic art ever made, art that takes the things everybody already has and calls them beautiful.

The second reading is **critique**. On this view Pop is a cold, deadpan indictment of exactly the same world. The endless repetition (fifty Marilyns, thirty-two identical cans) is not a celebration of plenty but a portrait of a culture grinding everything, even a dead movie star, into interchangeable, mass-produced product. The blankness is the point: this is what consumer capitalism does to images and to people, flattens them, copies them, drains them, and Pop holds up a mirror to that emptiness without comment. The same Marilyns that look like a tribute look, tilted the other way, like an autopsy.

The trap is to pick one. The artists themselves mostly refused to, and refused on purpose. Warhol in particular built a public persona out of saying nothing meaningful, answering interviewers in flat non-answers, insisting there was nothing behind the surface, that he wanted to be a machine and machines do not have opinions. Whether that blankness was a profound statement or a brilliant evasion is, once again, exactly the question Pop leaves open. So hold both readings at once. A soup can can be a hymn and a diagnosis in the same instant, and the deadpan that makes it impossible to tell which is the most modern thing about it.

### The appropriation question · The names the machine left off

There is a second debate folded inside the first, and it has a sharper moral edge, because here Pop did do something concrete that you can argue about: it took. The engine of the whole movement is **appropriation** (copying or reusing an image or design somebody else made, and re-presenting it as your own art). It descends from a Dada idea called the **readymade**, Marcel Duchamp's notorious gesture of taking a mass-produced object, a urinal, signing it, and declaring it sculpture (the Dada read tells that story). The two are cousins but not the same: in a readymade the found object itself is the artwork, presented as-is; appropriation goes a step further and remakes or reuses someone else's image inside a new work, which is exactly what Warhol did when he repainted a soup label and what Lichtenstein did when he repainted a comic panel. Pop runs on both ideas at scale. And once you ask where all that appropriated material actually came from, real people start appearing whose names got left off.

The clearest case is the Brillo box this chapter opened with. Warhol's "machine" did not invent that box; it lifted **James Harvey**'s real, paid commercial design, silkscreened it onto plywood, and sold it for many times what Harvey ever earned for it, and the deadpan brilliance of the gesture sits uneasily on top of an uncredited man's labor. The box is so perfect a test case precisely because it makes the question impossible to dodge: if the art is identical to the thing in the stockroom, then whatever turned it into art (the gallery, the signature, the price) was added by Warhol, while the actual image was made by Harvey, who got neither.

The same shadow falls across Lichtenstein. His comic paintings were taken from real, published comic panels drawn by working comic-book artists. *Whaam!* came from a war comic drawn by **Irv Novick**, with an element borrowed from a panel by **Russ Heath**; the weeping woman of *Drowning Girl* came from a romance comic drawn by **Tony Abruzzo**. Lichtenstein cropped, recolored, simplified, and recomposed his sources, so it was never simple copying. But the original artists were not credited and not paid, while the paintings based on their panels sold for fortunes and hung in museums. None of this is a reason to throw Pop out. It is a reason to tell the whole truth about it: a movement built on appropriating commercial imagery was, by the same logic, built on other people's uncredited work, and the names of those people belong in the story, not in a footnote.

[MEANWHILE: throughout the 1960s. The celebration-or-critique argument was live among critics from the start, and it never closed. Some hailed Pop as a liberation, art finally rejoining ordinary life; others, including defenders of Abstract Expressionism, attacked it as cynical, empty, and complicit, art that had given up on meaning and sold out to the very commercial culture it pretended to examine. Both camps were reading the same blank surfaces. That the surfaces support both readings equally well is the achievement, and the evasion, at once.]

## Chapter 5 — Who got left out

### The boys' club, and the women it wrote out

Pop is remembered as a boys' club, and the memory is mostly accurate, but the reason it is so clean a boys' club is that the women were there and got written out of the story afterward. The standard Pop story is short and tidy, and it is tidy partly because someone tidied the women out of it. Putting them back is not a takedown of the movement; it is just telling the rest of what happened.

Start with the women, because the erasure there is the most documented. British Pop's one prominent woman was **Pauline Boty** (1938 to 1966), a founding figure of the British movement who painted female desire and the male gaze head-on, from a woman's side, at a time when Pop's women were mostly painted by men as objects. In *It's a Man's World*, she turned the tables and assembled the icons of male culture, public men and the imagery aimed at them, into a Pop collage seen plainly from a woman looking back at it, the gaze running the other way for once. She was glamorous and well known in her brief life, a real presence on the scene, and then she died of cancer at twenty-eight, and within a generation she had been so thoroughly dropped from the history that her paintings were literally lost for decades, some of them rediscovered in a barn. She is the sharpest single case of the erasure: not a minor figure overlooked, but a central one deleted.

The American side had its own roster, every one a serious artist, every one marginalized. **Marisol** (Marisol Escobar, 1930 to 2016) made boxy, carved-wood figures, part folk totem and part Pop portrait, and was famous in the 1960s; Warhol called her, in a line that says everything about how the era saw its women, "the first girl artist with glamour." **Rosalyn Drexler** (born 1926) made vivid paintings built over collaged film and pulp images. **Idelle Weber** (1932 to 2020) painted flat, silhouetted businessmen in elevators and on escalators, the gray-flannel world seen as pattern. **Marjorie Strider** (1931 to 2014) made relief "build-out" paintings where parts of the figure pushed physically out of the canvas into the room. **Evelyne Axell** (1935 to 1972), a Belgian, painted female pleasure frankly and from the woman's point of view. And back in Britain, **Jann Haworth** (born 1942) made soft sculpture in the same years, the same idea as Oldenburg's slumping objects, worked out independently by a woman the histories rarely list beside him. Name them as artists, not as wives, muses, or footnotes, because that is what they were and what the record long refused to say.

### The objectified nude, and Pop's whiteness

There is a related problem inside the art itself, not just around it. A great deal of Pop, when it did put women in the picture, reduced them to flat, faceless objects, interchangeable with the products around them. The clearest example is **Tom Wesselmann**'s long *Great American Nude* series, begun in 1961: bright, flat female nudes, often faceless, arranged among branded packaging, patriotic colors, and household goods, the woman presented as one more consumer object on the shelf. You can read it, generously, as a deadpan diagnosis of how advertising already treated women's bodies. You can also read it as Pop simply doing the same thing advertising did, with a museum's blessing. Both readings are fair, and the gender politics of Pop sit right in that uncomfortable overlap.

And then there is who is almost entirely absent. The Pop canon, British and American alike, is very nearly all white. A movement that claimed to hold up a mirror to American mass culture, the supermarket, the billboard, the TV, the magazine, somehow reflected a culture with almost no Black faces in it, at the exact moment the civil rights movement was reshaping the country. Later writers have looked for a "Black Pop" and reread artists working with mass imagery and race in these years, but the honest statement is the plain one: the famous Pop canon is near-totally white, and that gap is part of the truth about it, not a distraction from it.

### The uncredited machine

Finally, the machine itself. Warhol's whole persona was the artist as factory, and the Factory was a real place full of real people doing the actual labor: assistants like Gerard Malanga pulling the silkscreens, a rotating cast of performers and collaborators making the films and keeping the place running. "I want to be a machine" sounds like a statement about style, and it is, but a machine needs operators, and Warhol's machine ran on many hands besides his own, most of them not famous and not credited the way he was. This connects straight back to the Brillo box and James Harvey from the last chapter: the cool, mechanical, hand-erased Pop surface was, underneath, made by a lot of uncredited human hands. Saying so does not diminish Warhol's genuine reinvention of what an artist could be. It just refuses to let the "machine" pose hide the people inside it.

[MEANWHILE: the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. The reckoning came late. It took decades, and a wave of feminist art history and rediscovery shows, before Boty, Marisol, Drexler, Weber, Strider, Axell, and the rest were widely shown and written about as the serious artists they always were, and before their prices and museum walls began to catch up with the men they had been standing next to all along. The story you are reading now is part of that correction.]

## Chapter 6 — What Pop left us

### The wall that never went back up

Pop Art did not last long as a named movement; by around 1970 the moment had passed, the artists had scattered into their separate later careers, and the shock had worn off. But almost nothing else in twentieth-century art changed the basic rules as permanently as Pop did, because the thing it broke never got fixed back. Before Pop there was a wall, taken for granted, between fine art on one side and commercial, mass, low culture on the other: the museum and the supermarket spoke different visual languages, and serious art was defined partly by not being the other thing. Pop knocked that wall down so completely that it never went back up. After Pop, a soup label, a comic panel, a brand logo, and a movie star are all legitimate raw material for serious art, permanently, without anyone needing to argue the point again. That is the single biggest thing it left us, and we now live so far inside it that it is hard to feel how radical it was.

The other permanent legacy is a method: **appropriation** and mechanical reproduction made fully respectable. After Warhol, an artist could take an existing image, copy it, repeat it, silkscreen it, and the taking itself could be the art. That idea ran straight forward into the next generations. **Conceptual art** (where the idea behind a work matters more than the handmade object, the Brillo-box question pushed all the way) took Pop's challenge to its logical end. **The Pictures Generation** of the late 1970s and 1980s, artists like Sherrie Levine, Richard Prince, and Cindy Sherman, turned appropriation into a sharp critical tool, re-photographing and re-staging mass images to expose how they work. And **Neo-Pop**, above all Jeff Koons from the 1980s on, took consumer kitsch and branding and made them fine art again, sometimes celebrating, sometimes skewering, always trading on the high/low collapse Pop set off. The line runs unbroken from Warhol's shelf of soup cans to the present, where advertising, street art, graphic design, and the gallery all draw from the same image bank.

### Both halves of the truth

So hold both halves of Pop at the end, because both are true and the movement is more interesting if you refuse to flatten it. On one side, Pop was a genuine, permanent rethinking of what art can be made of. It dragged the most public, most despised everyday imagery into the museum, killed the lone-genius mystique with a single deadpan gesture, dissolved a wall that had stood for centuries, and asked a question (what makes a thing art rather than a grocery carton?) that art is still answering. On the other side, it was a movement with real blind spots: it wrote its women out of its own history, near-totally white in a country it claimed to mirror, deadpan about an objectified female nude, and built, for all its "machine" talk, on a great deal of uncredited human labor and a few uncredited commercial designers. The cool surface that made Pop possible is the same cool surface that let it look past all of that without blinking.

And the question it was built around it still has not answered, on purpose. Is the soup can a love letter to consumer abundance or a deadpan burial under it? Pop hangs the can on the wall, refuses to tell you, and lets you keep arguing. Sixty years later, in a world where the museum and the supermarket genuinely do speak the same visual language, that refusal looks less like an evasion and more like the most honest thing the movement ever did.

[MEANWHILE: today. We now live inside Pop's outcome so completely it is invisible. The image-saturated world of brands, screens, memes, and endless reproduction, where the line between an advertisement, a museum piece, and a thing on your phone has all but vanished, is the world Pop saw coming and made art out of first. Whether that world is something to celebrate or something to mourn is, fittingly, the exact question Pop left open.]
