### [FIX] The Factory / serial production is a missing payoff, not just a tag.

This is the most substantive gap. The draft's whole spine is "made by hand to *look*
machine-made," and it repeatedly promises the reader that the real machine came "slightly
later." But it never cashes that in. The reader is told silkscreens "came slightly later in
1962" three times and never told what that *became*: Warhol moved the work into the
**Factory** (his studio, opened 1963), where assistants — Gerard Malanga the primary
silkscreen assistant — physically pulled the prints, and where Warhol openly said the point
was that "one of my assistants or anyone else... can reproduce the design as well as I
could" (corroborated via MoMA *Inside/Out* and Guy Hepner / Factory sources). That single
fact is the *proof* of the authorship argument the `break` section makes in the abstract.
The soup cans imitate a machine by hand; the Factory *is* the machine, and a collaborative
one. Leaving it out means the "after" of a break section is thinner than the "before."

Suggested addition — a short beat at the end of `SupAfterlife` OR folded into the close of
`SupBreak` (place it wherever the coordinator prefers; text is the same either way):

> Within a year of the soup cans, Warhol made good on the threat the paintings only mimed.
> He gave up the brush for the silkscreen and moved the work into a studio he called the
> Factory, where assistants like Gerard Malanga pulled the prints alongside him. Warhol said
> the quiet part out loud: he liked the screen because "one of my assistants, or anyone else
> for that matter, can reproduce the design as well as I could." The soup cans had imitated a
> machine by hand; the Factory simply became one, and a shared one. The question they raised
> about the artist's unique touch, Warhol now answered with a workshop.

(Use the verbatim Warhol quote only if the coordinator re-confirms the exact wording against
a primary source; if not confirmed at ship time, paraphrase it as reported and drop the
quotation marks. The fact-checker gate owns that verification — flagging here for fairness,
not asserting the quote as gated.)

### [FIX] Influence is named but never shown.

## 2. FRAMING / FAIRNESS

### Authorship / originality / commercial-appropriation as genuine debate — PASS.

The `break` section handles this well. It poses the authorship question as a question
("openly question whether it matters at all"; "put authorship and originality themselves on
the table"; "propose that the artist *might be* a deadpan reproducer") rather than ruling on
it. It does not sneer "anyone could do it," and it does not crown Warhol a genius for it.
This is the right register. Keep it.

### [FIX] One missing voice unbalances the fairness: the contemporaries who thought it was a con.

The draft does not include a single hostile contemporary reaction, and that absence is itself
a framing choice — it makes the soup cans read as instantly, self-evidently important and the
afterlife as a frictionless rise to MoMA. The historical record is the opposite: the 1962
Ferus show was widely mocked. A critic called Warhol "either a soft-headed fool or a
hard-headed charlatan"; the *Los Angeles Times* ran a lampooning cartoon; and a rival dealer
down the street stacked real Campbell's cans in his window with a sign reading "Do Not Be
Misled. Get the Original. Our Low Price — Two for 33 Cents" (corroborated via History.com and
Wikipedia). Including the derision is not anti-Warhol editorializing — it is the *fair*
treatment, because the "anyone could do it / it's a con" reaction is a real and serious side
of the debate the `break` section says it wants to keep open. Right now the draft states that
debate in the abstract while quietly stacking the deck by reporting only the eventual
canonization.

Suggested addition — a short beat in `SupAfterlife`, right after the "Almost nothing sold"
sentence:

> The early verdict was not kind. One critic dismissed Warhol as "either a soft-headed fool
> or a hard-headed charlatan," a Los Angeles newspaper ran a cartoon mocking the show, and a
> rival dealer down the block stacked actual Campbell's cans in his window under a sign:
> "Do Not Be Misled. Get the Original. Our Low Price — Two for 33 Cents." The "anyone could
> do this" reaction was there from the first day, and it is still the honest objection the
> picture has to answer.

(Quotes are widely reported but were authored here from secondary sources — the fact-checker
gate must confirm the exact wording of the critic line and the shop sign before ship. Flagged
for fairness; not gated by this gate.)

### Genius-myth / persona / Factory-as-collaborative — PASS, with a watch-item.

The draft is restrained on the genius myth. It does not mythologize Warhol; if anything it
deflates him (the deadpan, the "transcribed a catalog," "imitating a machine"). Good. The
one place to keep proportionate is the persona dimension: the draft presents Warhol's deadpan
as sincere autobiography ("He ate the same soup every day for years, and so he painted it...
in a real way, the picture simply *is* Campbell's"). That is a defensible reading, but the
"twenty years"/"liking things" deadpan is also widely read as a *constructed persona* — Warhol
performing flatness, not confessing it. The draft already nods at this ("a performance of the
impersonal") in `SupMaking`, so it is not a violation; just ensure the `lunch` section's
straight-faced "the picture simply *is* Campbell's" is not read as the only available reading.
[NICE] One clause — "(or so the deadpan went)" or similar — would keep the persona
proportionate without over-claiming that it was all a put-on.

### First / only / proportion — PASS.

No "first Pop artwork," no "the painting that invented Pop," no "only." The draft correctly
says the cans "open the door for" Pop, not that they *were* the start of it. The chain framing
("Works of Pop Art, 4 of 9") implicitly and correctly places it as one node, not the origin.
The "most successful illustrator" claim in the `lunch` header is hedged in the prose ("one of
the most sought-after") — keep the hedged form, not the bare-superlative header phrasing, if
the header ever gets tightened. No action required.
