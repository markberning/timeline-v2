## STORYTELLING

### What works (keep)
- **Persona is alive.** "ex-cyclist who would not be taught," "self-made man with no patience for the academy," "the way to attack good taste was to out-colour it," "Squeeze, daub, swirl," "He was trying to hit you with it," "one of modernism's loudest scolds." This is exactly the absinthe register and it carries the anti-academic self-taught identity the brief asked for.
- **Scene-set before paint** (matches the absinthe "hold the date" / "that's the room Degas walked into" move): ChaTown closes with "Hold the place in your mind before we get to the paint… That is the room Vlaminck walked into," and the Renoir/*Luncheon of the Boating Party* setup ("he did not paint it gently") is a clean, witty comparison anchor.
- **Legends handled in-voice, not bolted on:** "take it as the boast it was rather than a verbatim manifesto" and "read it as the kind of thing he said" are graceful and on-brand.
- **No meta-narration of structure.** Good — no "in this chapter we will." It tells the story.

### [FIX] FS-1 — One image is overworked across all five chapters ("loud / top of its voice / at full volume")
The draft leans on a single metaphor cluster so many times it goes from punchy to a tic. Instances:
- ChaTown hook (PART A): "one of the loudest landscapes of the Fauve year."
- ChaLooking title: "A landscape painted at full volume."
- ChaLooking: "painted at the absolute top of its voice."
- ChaLooking: "feels loud all over, edge to edge."
- annotations (PART A): "a quiet river town painted at the top of its voice."
- ChaAfterlife: "The short, loud peak" / "the loud Chatou canvases" / "the loudness drained out" / "the wild beast."

**Why it's weak:** by the third "top of its voice" the reader stops feeling it. The absinthe reference earns its repeated motif ("the room with the life drained out") by *varying the wording* each time. Here the wording barely varies.
**Fix:** keep ONE "top of its voice" (it's the strongest), and vary the rest — e.g. "the highest-pitched landscape of the Fauve year," "a river town turned up past distortion," "the brightness drained out of his work" (afterlife already half-does this with "loudness drained out" — good, keep that one). Aim for at most 2 uses of the literal "loud" cluster in any single chapter.

### [FIX] FS-2 — ChaAfterlife final two paragraphs restate the same beat twice
The provenance paragraph ends "which is how a Fauve riverbank painted outside Paris in 1906 ended up on a wall on Fifth Avenue." The very next (closing) paragraph ends "The Chatou riverbank now hangs a few rooms from its own ancestor, in a city its painter never needed to see, still squeezed straight from the tube, still burning." Two consecutive "ended-up-in-New-York, far from home" sign-offs. The van-Gogh-symmetry close is the better of the two and is genuinely good; the Fifth-Avenue line one paragraph earlier softens it.
**Fix:** trim the first sign-off — end the provenance paragraph on the plain fact ("…came to the Met in 1998, accession 1999.363.84") and let the van-Gogh-symmetry paragraph be the only emotional landing.

### [NICE] FS-3 — "gloriously simple" is the one place the narrator editorializes a hair too warmly
ChaTube: "So here is the method, and it is gloriously simple." Minor; the absinthe voice does allow this register ("the most important room in modern painting"). Leave it unless a later pass wants more restraint. Not a blocker.

### [NICE] FS-4 — School-of-Chatou train accident hedged twice in one breath
ChaSchool: "by the standard account, in 1900, after a train accident: their train derailed (accounts place it near Chatou)." Two hedges ("by the standard account" + "accounts place it near Chatou") stack a little. Fine for accuracy; could drop one for flow. Optional.

## LOOKING

This is the strongest gate. The ChaLooking chapter does real guided looking and consistently tells the reader *where* to look and *what they will see* before naming the concept — the absinthe "now let your eye land on it" method.

### What works (keep)
- **Reddish-orange trunks:** "the colour of hot embers, not of bark… orange against the blues and greens around it *burns*… a tree painted the wrong colour *on purpose*." Concrete, sensory, and ties the seen thing to the Fauve idea. Exactly the target.
- **Impasto / broken strokes:** "thick, separate daubs and swirls… standing ridges, little furrows of colour you could almost read with your fingertips. Each stroke stays itself. The orange does not melt into the green beside it." This is the SEE-it delivery the gate wants — texture made tactile.
- **Broken-color water (not a mirror):** "bands and dashes of blue and green and pale light, not a glassy reflection. The reflections do not mirror; they vibrate." Strong, and it does the comparison-to-the-expected move ("A timid painter would have made the Seine a smooth pane").
- **The boats:** tugboat/sailboats + the blue-red-white background boat as "a little knot where the painting's entire palette is concentrated" — gives the reader a specific small target and a reason to care.
- **High-key sky:** "does not soften into a hazy pale blue the way a traditional landscape sky recedes into air. It stays bright, stays charged" — concrete contrast with what a normal sky does.
- **Tube intensity:** "the pigment goes on at nearly the strength it had in the tube" (ChaTube) and "pushed to full strength, so the white blazes" (ChaLooking). The reader feels the saturation.
- **Pull-back close:** "no soothing passage anywhere in it, no smooth water, no soft sky, no true colour to rest on" — mirrors the absinthe "pull back one more time" synthesis. Good.

### [FIX] FL-1 — The reader is never told the COMPOSITIONAL layout (where the bank sits, which way the river runs)
ChaLooking sets size and lists elements ("a riverbank running along one side… the broad surface of the Seine filling the middle… a few boats… a bright sky above") but "one side" is vague. The absinthe looking chapter is precise about geography (lower-left zig-zag, upper-right figures) and that precision is what lets the reader's eye actually move. Here the spatial map is fuzzier than the texture description.
**Fix:** in the opening ChaLooking paragraph, pin the layout concretely — e.g. which side the bank/trees rise on, that the river fills the lower/center, boats mid-water, sky as the top band. Even one added clause ("the bank and its trees stacked up the left, the river opening out to the right") gives the eye a route. (Note: confirm the actual left/right against the image before asserting — the fact pack/Met text should govern; do not invent a side.)

### [NICE] FL-2 — "milky river" promised in the section blurb, not delivered in prose
PART A `looking` blurb title is "Reddish-orange trees and a milky river," and the FS hook/section calls the river "milky." But the ChaLooking water paragraph describes the river as broken color/dashes, never as *milky* or pale-clouded. Minor mismatch between the card label and the prose. Either drop "milky" from the card or add the milky/pale quality to the water paragraph so the promised word pays off. Low priority.

### [NICE] FL-3 — No `onZoom` invitation in the looking prose
The components all receive `onZoom` but the prose never says "look closely" in a way tied to zooming the ridges (the absinthe draft also doesn't push zoom in prose, so this is consistent — flagging only because impasto ridges are the one thing that *rewards* a zoom). Optional: a single "zoom in and you can see the ridges stand up off the surface." Not required.

## CLARITY (zero-knowledge reader)

All four primary target terms are glossed inline on first use. Checked:

- **impasto** — GLOSSED (ChaLooking): "This is **impasto**, paint heaped so high the brush leaves standing ridges, little furrows of colour you could almost read with your fingertips." Also glossed again in PART A annotations consistently. ✓
- **Fauvism / fauves** — GLOSSED (ChaSchool): "called these bright-colour painters **'fauves,'** French for **'wild beasts.'**… Fauvism takes its name from that one critic's jab." ✓ Clear etymology + origin story.
- **high key** — GLOSSED (ChaLooking): "the Fauve idea of **high key**, which means keeping colours near their full intensity rather than darkening or greying them down to fake distance and atmosphere." ✓
- **tube color / straight from the tube** — GLOSSED (ChaTube): "He took the **colour straight from the tube** and put it on the canvas… No careful local colour, no atmospheric softening… Squeeze, daub, swirl." ✓ The concept is explained mechanically, not just named.

Other terms checked and OK:

- **École des Beaux-Arts** — glossed ("the official state art school in Paris, the gatekeeper of respectable French painting"). ✓
- **School of Chatou / l'École de Chatou** — glossed ("an art-historian's tidy label for what was really just two friends with two easels"). ✓

### [FIX] FC-1 — "local colour" used twice, glossed only obliquely
"local colour" appears in ChaTube ("No careful local colour") and ChaLooking ("ordinary, expected local colour, white for a house, green for a leaf"). The ChaLooking instance is *effectively* a gloss via the examples ("white for a house, green for a leaf"), but it appears AFTER the unglossed ChaTube use, and a zero-knowledge reader hits the bare term first. "Local colour" is a real art-jargon term (the actual color of an object under neutral light) that a lay reader will not know.
**Fix:** on first use in ChaTube, add a three-word gloss — e.g. "No careful local colour (the true, observed colour of a thing)…" — or cut the term from ChaTube and let ChaLooking's example-gloss carry it.

### [FIX] FC-2 — "primaries" assumed, not explained
ChaLooking (boats) and ChaTube both lean on "the primaries of blue and red" / "the three primaries that run the whole canvas" / "blue and red and white, are the primaries." A zero-knowledge reader may not know what a primary color is, and the draft also (loosely) calls white a primary, which is technically off (white is not a primary). The Met phrasing is "combined the primaries of blue and red with white," which the draft mostly preserves — but the ChaLooking boat line ("blue, red, and white… those three… are the primaries the whole canvas runs on") flattens white INTO the primaries, which a careful reader could read as a factual slip.
**Fix (two birds):** (a) on first use add a micro-gloss — "the primary colours (blue, red, and yellow, the three you can't mix from others)" or simpler "the basic colours"; and (b) fix the boat line so white is not called a primary — e.g. "blue, red, and white, the same charged colours that run the whole canvas" rather than "the primaries the whole canvas runs on." This keeps it accurate to the Met's "primaries of blue and red WITH white."

### [NICE] FC-3 — "expressive brushwork" / "intuitive application of paint" quoted from the Met land slightly jargony
ChaTube: "Vlaminck emulated the undisguised brushwork and the intuitive application of paint of van Gogh's late, expressive style." "Undisguised brushwork" is decently self-explaining in context (the surrounding prose explains visible strokes), and "intuitive application" is soft. Acceptable as-is because the mechanics are spelled out right before it ("Squeeze, daub, swirl… the brush leaves its ridges"). No change required; flagged only for awareness.
