Gate: STORYTELLING + LOOKING + CLARITY/VOICE. Reviewer = storytelling/looking/clarity persona.
Draft reviewed: `audits/art-pipeline/work-its-a-mans-world-draft.md`
Factpack: `audits/art-pipeline/work-its-a-mans-world-factpack.md`

## VERDICT: PASS (with FIX-level cleanups; no BLOCKERS)

The read is genuinely strong. The narrative spine — the only woman of British Pop turning Pop's own
appropriated-image toolkit back on the system, the *I*/*II* question-and-answer, the erasure-then-rediscovery —
lands across all five sections and does NOT collapse her into a tragic-early-death footnote. The "sole-sister"
section opens on her exclusion-as-a-painter (not her death); the death is held to the *afterlife* section, framed
as the thing the world did to the work, not as the headline. The draft explicitly guards against the martyr-flattening
(§sole-sister ¶3, factpack §8.6) — that self-awareness is content, well handled. The looking section is concrete and
prose-pointered with hedged figure IDs. No meta-narration walls, no reader-command pileups, no honesty-label tics.

Findings below are mostly FIX-tier polish plus a few NICE. Nothing blocks.

## 1. STORYTELLING (primary)

**Overall: strong, compelling, well-sequenced.** The five sections build: exclusion → the pair's structure →
the field of men (looking) → why it's a break → the afterlife/recovery. The argument ("men get to be people,
women get to be looked at") is stated cleanly in *the-pair* and earns its weight by the time *afterlife* closes on
"the recovery did not just return some good paintings to the wall; it proved the argument." That closing is the
single best move in the read — the erasure becomes evidence, not pathos.

**The death does NOT swamp her.** Per the brief's central worry: the death is sequestered in *afterlife*, introduced
after the whole feminist-Pop argument has landed, and framed around her refusal (carrying Katy to term, declining
radiotherapy) rather than as a glamorous-genius-cut-short cliché. The martyr-flattening is named and resisted in
*sole-sister*. This is the right proportion.

### [FIX] *the-pair* ¶3 — *II* is described in more vivid concrete detail than *I* gets in its own looking section
Location: `ManThePair` ¶3 (lines 137–139), describing *II* (Stourhead Pantheon, headless central figure, the
high-nude / low-pin-up flattening). It's excellent — but *II* is the companion, and *I* is the lead. The risk is the
reader comes away with a sharper mental picture of *II* than of *I*. Not a defect in *the-pair* itself (the contrast
needs *II* fleshed out), but watch that the *looking* section (which is *I*-only) stays at least as concrete. As
drafted it does, so this is a soft flag, not a cut.
Suggested action: none required; keep an eye on it if *looking* gets trimmed later.

### [NICE] *break* — slight redundancy with *the-pair* on the core thesis
Location: `ManBreak` "after" ¶ (lines 187–188) restates "who gets to be a person and who gets to be a body," which
*the-pair* ¶4 already delivered as "The men get to be people; the women get to be looked at." The line is load-bearing
so some repetition is fine (break sections are allowed to re-state the thesis as the hinge), but the second pass adds
little new framing. Consider giving the *break* "after" paragraph a fresh angle (e.g. lean harder on "from *inside*
Pop," the insider-betrayal angle) rather than re-deriving the same sentence.

### [NICE] *afterlife* ¶4 — "almost too neatly" is a nice honest beat but lands twice
Location: `ManAfterlife` ¶4 (line 224) "became part of the work's meaning, almost too neatly." Good instinct to flag
the tidiness. It slightly undercuts the genuinely moving close that follows. Optional: drop "almost too neatly" or the
hedge, not both — keeping one preserves the self-awareness without softening the payoff.

## 2. LOOKING (Canvas I made concrete)

**Overall: passes cleanly.** The *looking* section (`ManLooking`, lines 147–175) makes Canvas I concrete via prose
pointers, no coordinates, with the field-of-male-faces density established first ("the crowd *is* the subject"), then
walked register by register: thinkers/stars → power/violence/jet → rose → facades → tentative faces. Every required
element from factpack §5 is present:
- field of famous male faces — yes (¶1, "packed with male faces... dozens of them")
- cut-from-media sources — yes (annotations + *the-pair*; "clipped or re-painted scrap of media")
- rose / flower motif — yes (¶4, "recurring painted rose, Boty's signature soft, sensual motif")
- architectural facade — yes (¶4, "grand architectural facades... inherited power")
- the jet — yes (¶3, B-52 + X-15)
- Zapruder / JFK frame — yes (¶3, correctly attributed to the Zapruder home movie, 1963 Dallas)

**Tentative IDs correctly hedged.** The matador (El Cordobés "probably"), jazz musician (Thelonious Monk "possibly,
though other names have been proposed"), and Plains Indian are all flagged uncertain and the read explicitly says
"read them by type... leave the exact names open" (lines 170–172). Matches factpack §5.6 / §8.4. Good.

**Ali = Cassius Clay handled correctly** as one man, named once, with the 1964 name-change noted (line 163,
"one man, not two"). Matches the §8.4 trap.

### [FIX] *looking* §3 says the B-52 flies over "a grand European estate"; the factpack/annotations are more specific
Location: `ManLooking` ¶3 (line 163) "set above a grand European estate." Factpack §5.3 names it the **Palazzina
di caccia di Stupinigi** (and the PART-A annotation at line 83 says "a grand Italian estate"). Within *looking* the
estate is downgraded to "European," which is vaguer than both the factpack and the read's own annotation block. Not
wrong, but inconsistent with the annotations and loses a verifiable specific. Either name Stupinigi once (matches
factpack) or at minimum keep it "Italian estate" to agree with the annotation. Recommend: "set above a grand Italian
estate" for internal consistency; naming Stupinigi is optional flavor.

### [NICE] The X-15 is mentioned but its relevance isn't landed
Location: `ManLooking` ¶3 and annotation (lines 83, 163). "with an X-15 aircraft nearby" — for a reader with zero
prior knowledge, "X-15" is an unglossed proper noun doing no narrative work (it's a rocket plane, not a bomber). The
B-52 carries the Cold-War-hardware point on its own. Consider cutting the X-15 or giving it one clause of gloss; as
drafted it's a bare unexplained term. (See also Clarity finding below.)

## 3. CLARITY + VOICE CONTRACT

Swept every section for: meta-narration, reader-commands, honesty-labels, condescending glosses, jargon-before-define,
walls of text, literal "—" in rendered strings, and `&mdash;` misused inside plain TS string fields.

**No honesty-labels.** Clean — no "to be honest / the truth is / let me be clear" tics.
**No meta-narration of the chapter-about-itself kind.** The read tells the story; it does not narrate "in this section
we will look at..." The one borderline ("the canvas this read leads on," *the-pair* line 135) is a structural pointer
to the *I*/*II* lead, which the factpack explicitly asks for; acceptable, not a violation.
**Inline-defines hold.** Pop art (line 114), Independent Group (114), diptych (132), provenance (216), Zapruder film

**No em-dash policy violation in rendered strings** — see the literal-character sweep below; the draft uses commas
where house style forbids em-dashes, consistent with the war/art em-dash doctrine.

### [FIX] Reader-command openers — two imperative "Stand in front of / Pick out / Now" beats
Location:
- `ManLooking` ¶1 (line 152): "**Stand in front of** *It's a Man's World I* and the first fact is sheer density."
- `ManLooking` ¶2 (line 157): "**Pick out** the faces."
- `ManLooking` ¶3 (line 162): "**Now** the harder register."
These are mild reader-commands / stage directions. The looking-section convention does tolerate gentle "look for"
pointers (the gate brief calls for prose pointers), and "Stand in front of" is a standard art-writing move — but
"Pick out the faces" and the bare "Now the harder register" are the imperative register the voice contract trims.
Suggested rewrites:
- "Pick out the faces." → "Among the crowd are the faces she knew by name." (declarative)
- "Now the harder register." → "Then the harder register." or fold into the sentence: "The harder register sits lower:
  a Boeing B-52..."
- "Stand in front of..." is the softest of the three; keep if you cut the other two, since one scene-set is fine.

### [FIX] Unglossed jargon: "X-15"
Location: `ManLooking` ¶3 (line 163) and annotation (line 83). For a zero-prior-knowledge reader "X-15" is an

### [NICE] Condescending-gloss watch: "one man, not two" / "the same man under two names"

### [PASS] Literal-character / `&mdash;` sweep (no findings)
- No literal "—" (em-dash) appears inside any rendered prose string or PART-A blurb/annotation field. The draft uses
  commas in the house-comma style where an em-dash would be tempting (e.g. "genius, glamour, power, and violence" /
  "blonde, glamorous, and photogenic"). Consistent with the art/war em-dash doctrine.
- `&ndash;` (en-dash) is used correctly and only in date/year ranges and life-spans inside JSX/HTML prose where an
  HTML entity is appropriate: "1938&ndash;1966" (line 117), "1962&ndash;64" (183), "1964&ndash;65" (138), "1996&ndash;97"
  (221). These are en-dashes for ranges, the correct glyph, and they're in `.tsx` JSX text (not plain TS string
  fields), so the entity renders. No `&mdash;` anywhere — correct.
- `&ldquo;/&rdquo;` and `&rsquo;` entities are used in JSX prose (correct context). The PART-A const strings use
  literal curly quotes (' " ") in plain TS string values (e.g. line 39 hook, line 51 blurb) — that's the right call for
  a plain TS string (entities would NOT render there; curly literals do). No mismatch found: entities live only in
  `.tsx`, literals live only in plain string fields. Clean separation.
