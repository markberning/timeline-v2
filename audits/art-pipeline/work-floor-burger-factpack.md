## 1. CORE FACTS

- **Title(s):** *Floor Burger* — the title now used by the Art Gallery of Ontario (AGO) and by virtually all current literature. Originally exhibited and frequently catalogued as ***Giant Hamburger***. Both are correct; *Giant Hamburger* is the original title, *Floor Burger* is the standing AGO title. [AGO record; AGO history article; Globe & Mail]
- **Artist:** **Claes Oldenburg** — Swedish-American. Born Stockholm, **1929**; died **2022**. (AGO records his nationality as "American, 1929–2022"; he was born in Sweden and is conventionally described as Swedish-American.) [AGO collection record]
- **Date:** **1962**. [AGO]
- **Medium (AGO's exact line):** **"Canvas filled with foam rubber and cardboard boxes, painted with acrylic paint."** This is a **soft sculpture**. (Construction in plain terms: sewn cotton-duck/canvas forms, hand-painted, stuffed with upholstery foam rubber and empty cardboard cartons.) [AGO collection record, art.ago.ca/objects/47906]
- **Dimensions (AGO catalog):** **132.1 × 226.1 cm (52 × 89 in.)** — i.e. **~4 ft 4 in tall × ~7 ft 5 in across (≈7 ft / ~2.13 m in diameter).** AGO's own history article rounds this to "4 feet, 4 inches tall, by 7 feet in diameter." Weight commonly cited at **~700 lb (~318 kg)**. [AGO record (132.1 × 226.1 cm); AGO history; ARTnews]
- **Current location:** **Art Gallery of Ontario, Toronto** — permanent collection. **Accession no. 66/29; credit line "Purchase, 1967."** Bought from the **Sidney Janis Gallery, New York, on 27 January 1967, for US $2,000.** [AGO record; AGO history; Globe & Mail]

## 2. THE STORY

In December 1961 Oldenburg rented a storefront on East Second Street in New York and filled it with handmade, brightly painted plaster objects resembling the goods sold in the neighborhood's shops — food, clothing, signage. He called the multi-year project **The Store**. For the 1962 "uptown" version of The Store, staged at Richard Bellamy's **Green Gallery** in midtown Manhattan in the fall of 1962, Oldenburg made a set of room-scale **soft sculptures** of food: **Floor Burger (Giant Hamburger), Floor Cake, and Floor Cone.** [AGO; MoMA; The Art Story; artland]

*Floor Burger* takes the cheapest, most disposable object imaginable — a fast-food hamburger — and blows it up to roughly **seven feet across**, then makes it **soft**. The forms were sewn (on a small Singer machine bought by Oldenburg's wife, the artist **Patty Mucha**, who sewed and stuffed the pieces; Oldenburg then painted them) from **cotton-duck canvas**, painted in **acrylic**, and stuffed — the pieces were so large that ordinary stuffing wasn't enough, so the interiors were packed with **upholstery foam and empty cardboard cartons** (ice-cream cartons) inserted through a **zipper sewn into the underside** of each form. The result is a monument that **slumps, sags, and bulges** on the floor under its own weight, topped with a sliced **pickle**. [AGO history; Globe & Mail; MoMA]

The work is a hinge in Pop Art's move into **three dimensions**. Where painting (Warhol, Lichtenstein) flattened the consumer image, Oldenburg gave the everyday object **mass, scale, and gravity** — and then undercut all three by making it soft, so the "monument" can't hold a heroic pose. The comedy is the content: the absurd scale, the sagging softness, the seriousness of a museum bestowed on a hamburger.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (before / after)

**Before:** A monument is hard, heroic, and vertical — bronze or marble, a hero on a pedestal — built to last and to ennoble its subject. Sculpture stands up. Pop's first wave (Warhol, Lichtenstein) lived on the flat wall.

**The break:** Oldenburg takes the era's least heroic object — a fast-food burger — and makes it **colossal** (≈7 ft across) **AND soft**, sewn from painted canvas and stuffed so it **sags on the floor** instead of standing on a pedestal. Three things flip at once:
1. **The everyday consumer object becomes the monument** — the subject of "important sculpture" is now a hamburger.
2. **The sculpture sags** — softness makes it **anti-monumental**: it can't strike a pose, can't last unchanged, droops under gravity. The medium contradicts the genre.
3. **Humor is the content, not a garnish** — the scale and the slump are the point, not decoration on a serious idea.

**After:** Sculpture is freed to be soft, funny, and made of "low" stuff; Oldenburg's soft objects (Soft Toilet, soft typewriters, soft drainpipes) and later the giant public Pop monuments (with Coosje van Bruggen) follow directly. The everyday object as colossal, deflatable monument becomes a permanent Pop register.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced)

> **"I am for an art that is political-erotical-mystical, that does something other than sit on its ass in a museum."**

— Claes Oldenburg, from his statement **"I am for an art…"**, written **1961** for the catalogue of the exhibition *Environments, Situations, Spaces* at the **Martha Jackson Gallery**, New York. [Walker Art Center, *I Am for an Art: Claes Oldenburg on His 1961 "Ode to Possibilities"*]

**Variant flag — "other than" vs "more than":** The Walker Art Center (which holds Oldenburg's archive) gives **"does something other than sit on its ass in a museum."** Many secondary sources (e.g. quote aggregators, Quotefancy) print **"does something more than."** Use the Walker's **"other than."** Note also Oldenburg later resisted calling the text a manifesto — in a 1974 conversation with Walker curators he called it a "literary effort … not a sort of cry," much of it "said for the sound of them." [Walker Art Center]

*(The full statement continues "…that grows up not knowing it is art at all, an art given the chance of having a starting point of zero…" — a long anaphoric "I am for…" litany. The single line above is the canonical pull-quote.)*

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The giant sagging bun.** The top bun is a swollen, hand-painted dome that doesn't sit crisply — it bulges and settles, the soft canvas pulling into folds where the stuffing shifts. The "freshness" of a burger is replaced by slump.
2. **The patty.** A thick dark disc wedged between the buns; conservators noted the patty and lower bun held their shape best — it reads as the dense, heavy core the rest of the burger droops around.
3. **The slump and soft folds.** The whole object spreads on the floor under its own weight (~700 lb). Look for the creases and sags where gravity, not the artist, finished the shaping — the anti-monument.
4. **The hand-painted canvas surface.** This is acrylic brushed onto cotton-duck canvas, not a printed or molded surface — visible brushwork and a slightly grimy, worked-by-hand finish, not a slick product render.
5. **The scale — you stand over it.** At ~7 ft across and only ~4 ft high, it sits on the floor and you look *down* at it. The viewer towers over a hamburger; the everyday object dwarfs you sideways while staying beneath you.
6. **The seams and stitching (and the pickle).** It's *sewn* — look for the seams of the sewn-and-stuffed forms (made on a home Singer machine by Patty Mucha) and the underside zippers used to pack in foam and cartons. The sliced **pickle** perched on top is the comic punctuation (the current pickle is a replacement).

## 6. PROVENANCE

- Made **1962** for The Store's uptown showing at the **Green Gallery**, New York (dealer Richard Bellamy), fall 1962. [MoMA; The Art Story]
- Handled by the **Sidney Janis Gallery, New York**.
- **Purchased by the Art Gallery of Ontario from the Sidney Janis Gallery on 27 January 1967 for US $2,000.** [AGO history; Globe & Mail]
- **AGO accession no. 66/29; credit line "Purchase, 1967."** Remains in the AGO permanent collection. [AGO collection record]
- *Note:* the AGO history dates the purchase to **early 1967** (27 Jan 1967); the accession number prefix "66/29" reflects the 1966 fiscal/announcement cycle (the acquisition was announced in 1966 and finalized Jan 1967). The credit line and all AGO public statements give **1967** as the acquisition year. **Use 1967.**
- Conserved/restored **2012** by AGO conservator Sherry Phillips (foam and cartons repositioned, internal Ethafoam-filled cotton bags added, surface cleaned, loose paint re-adhered). [Globe & Mail; ARTnews]

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (corrected, sourced)

- **Title confusion.** Both *Floor Burger* and *Giant Hamburger* are legitimate. *Giant Hamburger* is the **original** title; *Floor Burger* is the **AGO's current** title and today's standard. Don't present either as "wrong." [AGO]
- **Exact materials — AGO is authoritative.** Use AGO's line: **"canvas filled with foam rubber and cardboard boxes, painted with acrylic paint."** Avoid loose phrasings like "latex" or "vinyl" — the surface paint is **acrylic** on **canvas (cotton duck)**; the fill is **foam rubber + cardboard cartons**. (Some general descriptions say "latex"; the AGO record says acrylic — defer to AGO.) [AGO record]
- **Dimensions — defer to AGO catalog: 132.1 × 226.1 cm (52 × 89 in).** Popular sources round inconsistently: Globe & Mail wrote "213 cm by 132 cm"; AGO's own history says "4 ft 4 in tall by 7 ft in diameter." 226.1 cm ≈ 89 in ≈ ~7 ft 5 in (diameter); 132.1 cm ≈ 52 in ≈ 4 ft 4 in (height). Give the AGO figure and you can describe it informally as "about 7 feet across." Don't invent a single diameter beyond AGO's numbers. [AGO; Globe & Mail; AGO history]
- **Soft-sculpture innovation — scope it.** *Floor Burger* is a landmark of the **soft sculpture** idea but is one of a set (made alongside **Floor Cake** and **Floor Cone** for the 1962 Store). Don't call it "the first soft sculpture" full stop — call it one of Oldenburg's defining early floor-scale soft food sculptures. [MoMA; AGO]
- **Acquisition year — use 1967.** The accession number is "66/29" and the deal was announced in 1966, but the purchase finalized **27 January 1967** and AGO publicly states **1967**. Don't write "1966." [AGO history; Globe & Mail; AGO record]
- **The pickle.** The current sliced pickle is a **replacement** Oldenburg himself delivered from New York to Toronto (a colorful anecdote — verify before using as hard fact beyond "the pickle has been replaced"). [Globe & Mail]
- **Who sewed it.** Sewn (and stuffed) by **Patty Mucha** (then Patty Oldenburg), the artist's wife, on a home Singer machine; **Oldenburg painted** them. Credit her if construction is discussed. [AGO history; MoMA]
- **The "I am for an art" quote variant** — "**other than**" (Walker, authoritative), not "more than" (aggregators). See §4. [Walker]
