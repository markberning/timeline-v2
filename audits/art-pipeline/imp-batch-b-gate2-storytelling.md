# Gate 2 — Storytelling & "Looking" Critic
## Impressionism Batch B · Four Works

Critic pass, art content pipeline. Voice benchmark: `sunrise-work-final.md`.
Judge: hook & stakes; pacing; the art axis (does it make me SEE the canvas?).
Enforce: dry wit dialed up; comparisons/analogies as the #1 comprehension tool;
no buried point in muted styling; Look-Closer pointers = finding task, not chapter restatement.

Grades: STRONG / GOOD / NEEDS WORK / REWRITE per section.

## WORK 1 — The Dance Class (Degas)

### Per-Section Grades

| Section | Grade |
|---|---|
| rehearsal | STRONG |
| making | STRONG |
| looking | GOOD |
| show | GOOD |
| afterlife | STRONG |

### Findings

---

**MUST-FIX**

**[DC-M1] `looking` — The tipped floor is named but not shown first.**

Current sentence: *"The floor is tipped — wildly tipped, as if the room is on a hinge."*

That opener is great. But the paragraph then digresses into dimensions and medium
before it ever puts a body on that floor. The reader is told the floor tips up,
then yanked into an inventory of canvas size and oil-on-canvas before they get to
feel the tilt. The sunrise benchmark opens on the canvas and *stays* on the canvas
until the perception is lodged. This one breaks away.

**Fix:** After "leaning out over the floorboards from a balcony, half-falling in.
That is the first move" — go immediately to the figure consequences of the tilt
(the scattered dancers, the column looming forward, the cropped edge). Push the
medium/signature information into a parenthetical or a later beat. Hold the falling
sensation for at least two more sentences before releasing it.

---

**[DC-M2] `looking` — Japonisme paragraph length vs. payoff.**

Current: The Japonisme explanation runs roughly 200 words in one block, ending with
"The polite version of this painting would have been twelve dancers in a row."

The explanation is accurate and the final synthesis line is good. But the block is
doing essay work where it should be doing picture work. By the time "The room is a
record of attention, not arrangement" arrives, the reader has traveled through ukiyo-e,
Japonisme pronunciation, the floored-perspective history of Western painting, and
the column-slice — all correct, all a little textbook. The benchmark uses comparisons
as a *shortcut* ("the way a real reflection breaks up on moving water") not as a
lecture.

**Fix:** Cut the Japonisme block to its functional core: tipped floor = Japanese
woodblock move; column-slice = Japanese woodblock move; cropped figure = Japanese
woodblock move. Three bullet-beats, quickly. Then get to "The room is a record of
attention, not arrangement" faster. The current prose earns the summary but makes
the reader wait too long for it. Aim to trim this block by ~80 words.

---

**SHOULD-FIX**

**[DC-S1] `rehearsal` — The abonnés are introduced strong, then dropped.**

Current: *"Some of those arrangements became long-term liaisons. Some became marriages.
Many were closer to what we would today call exploitation..."*

The abonnés entry is one of the chapter's best moments — the reveal that the stage
mothers weren't just chaperones but economic defenders. But the paragraph ends softly
on "watching the men watching her daughter," which is a great image, and then the
subject is never quite returned to with the same sharpness in `looking`, where the
mother-in-black pointer softens it further ("the painting's quiet record of the rougher
economy underneath the glamour"). The voice benchmark never buries a substantive
finding in muted styling. The abonnés are the picture's dark undertow — name that more
boldly in `looking` when the benched mothers appear.

**Fix:** In the `looking` Look-Closer pointer #6 ("The mother in black at left"),
replace the current final sentence with something closer to: "Degas refuses to name
or describe them individually — they are a row of guardians, not portraits — but they
are the visible edge of the Opéra economy: working-class women in the room precisely
because wealthy men were allowed to be in the same room." Let the darker charge of
the economic arrangement land in the pointer, not just in chapter 1.

---

**[DC-S2] `show` — The Duranty pamphlet note is undersold.**

Current: *"The pamphlet is, in effect, The Dance Class's manifesto, written by the
painter's friend the same season the picture went on the wall."*

That is a genuinely interesting claim — the painting and its printed program appear
in the same room in the same season — and the sentence buries it. What did La Nouvelle
Peinture *argue*, in one clause? The reader who hasn't read it doesn't know, and the
claim "manifesto for this picture" lands without evidence. The benchmark doesn't just
call something interesting — it shows the interesting thing.

**Fix:** Before "The pamphlet is, in effect," add one clause: Duranty's argument was
that art's future lay in figures caught *in* their actual lives, not posed against
neutral backgrounds — in the rehearsal room and the laundry, not the studio. Then the
"manifesto for this painting" claim has content behind it.

---

**[DC-S3] `afterlife` — The mass-culture bargain sentence is strong; close with it.**

Current closing: *"The work, not the gala. It was the painter's whole argument from
the beginning, and it survives the painting's afterlife if you go to the room."*

The setup — "what gets remembered is the wrong half" — is the chapter's best line.
But the payoff sentence ("The work, not the gala") repeats the opening hook almost
verbatim. The benchmark's closing ("making a sun glow without ever being bright") is
a *new* formulation that earns the journey. This one returns to the starting point
rather than advancing beyond it.

**Fix:** Close instead on the watering can. Something like: *"The Degas dancer on the
tote bag is the show. The watering can on the floor of Gallery 815 is the argument.
Go to the room and you'll know which one lasts."* The watering can has been built up
across three chapters as the picture's emblem of labor — it can carry the ending.

---

### Look-Closer Assessment

**#1 Jules Perrot** — STRONG. The X-ray detail and the "still point" framing are
genuine finding tasks.

**#2 The dancer scratching** — GOOD. "Look for the *opposite* — the small unguarded
gestures" is a real task. Could sharpen by naming the specific arm-raised gesture
("one girl has reached one arm up to scratch the back of her neck — find her in the
middle group") rather than describing it twice.

**#3 The floor that tips** — NEEDS WORK. The pointer describes the trick (tipped
floor, Japanese woodblock) rather than giving a task. Fix: "Stand back and look at
the wooden floor closest to you — it should feel like it's coming at your feet, not
receding away from them. That is not an accident."

**#4 Cropped dancer** — STRONG. "The canvas stops in the middle of a person" is
exactly the finding task.

**#5 Watering can** — STRONG. "Degas paints it with the seriousness of a Dutch still
life" is a good comparison with a task attached.

**#6 The mother in black** — NEEDS WORK per DC-S1 above.

**#7 Guillaume Tell poster** — STRONG. Specific enough to be findable; context earns
the search.

## WORK 2 — L'Absinthe (Degas)

### Per-Section Grades

| Section | Grade |
|---|---|
| cafe | STRONG |
| making | STRONG |
| looking | STRONG |
| show | GOOD |
| afterlife | GOOD |

### Findings

This is the batch's best draft. The compositional emptiness as argument — the central
claim — is present and earned across both `making` and `looking`. The absinthe-louche
ritual, the mazagran reveal, the title-as-weapon beat, and the Camondo tragedy all
land cleanly. The benchmark voice is closely matched.

---

**MUST-FIX**

**[AB-M1] `looking` — The palette paragraph arrives late and underplays the key move.**

Current: The drained-palette description appears as the closing movement of `looking`,
after Andrée's expression, Desboutin's jacket, and the back-wall mirrors. The benchmark
establishes the compositional *logic* (what the drained palette is *doing*) before
working through the figures, because the palette is the frame everything else is read
inside.

Current key sentence: *"Stand back six feet and the whole picture looks like a wash
of dust and smoke, with two human figures faintly distinguishable in the upper right."*

That is good. But it arrives after the individual descriptions rather than before,
which means the reader has been trying to read Andrée's face in isolation before they
understand that the face is *deliberately drained of contrast against its background*.
The picture's argument — that these two people are the same color as the wall they sit
against — is the lens through which every detail should be seen.

**Fix:** Move the drained-palette paragraph to immediately after the opening diagonal-
tables beat. Put it before the description of Andrée, not after. The milky-green glass
passage can stay in its current position (it works as the *exception* to the drained
rule), but the rule itself should be established first.

---

**[AB-M2] `looking` — Andrée's middle-distance stare needs to SEE the glass it doesn't look at.**

Current: *"She is not looking at her drink."*

The stare-that-doesn't-look-at-the-glass is the picture's central irony — the painting
named for a drink, featuring a woman who refuses to look at it. That deserves more
than a clause. The look-at-nothing reads in isolation; it doesn't get its full charge
from the deliberate *not-looking-at-the-glass-right-in-front-of-her* until that is
made explicit.

**Fix:** Add: *"The glass is directly in front of her — the picture's one charged note
of color, the reason for the title — and she is looking somewhere else entirely.
That refusal to acknowledge the thing that defines her, to a Victorian eye, is the
picture's small, quiet trap."*

---

**SHOULD-FIX**

**[AB-S1] `show` — The "Same picture. Same paint." beat is the section's best moment — protect it.**

Current: *"Same picture. Same paint. Different title. Different country. Different decade.
Different scandal."*

That is the section's best line. But it lands in the second-to-last paragraph, after
a long explanation of the Grafton Gallery and the temperance movement, and because it
arrives late, readers who were skimming might have already moved on. The section's
structural lesson (a title does all the moral work; the paint is unchanged) should be
telegraphed higher — perhaps at the open of the show chapter — and then paid off here.

**Fix:** Open `show` with a version of the payoff: something like "The story of *L'Absinthe*
in public has almost nothing to do with the painting. It is a story about what happens
when you rename a picture." Then the Paris → London → scandal structure reads as the
extended proof of that claim.

---

**[AB-S2] `cafe` — The Nouveau-Athènes setup is strong; one vague beat to sharpen.**

Current: *"By 1875 this is the most important room in modern painting in France, and
almost nobody outside it knows that yet."*

That is an interesting claim but it earns the reader's skepticism. *Why* was it the
most important room? The next paragraph explains (Impressionists migrated here, brought
the writers), but the "most important room" line arrives before the evidence and so
reads as a promotional blurb rather than a fact the reader is about to understand.

**Fix:** Either move the claim to after the migration explanation, or replace with a
more concrete image: *"By 1875 the painters and the writers who were about to change
French art had all moved to the same few marble-topped tables."* Stakes first, claim second.

---

**[AB-S3] `afterlife` — The Camondo tragedy is handled with appropriate weight, but the grace note at the end competes with itself.**

Current final paragraph: *"The temperance frame is gone. The scandal is forgotten.
The picture, finally, has been allowed to be itself again..."*

The ending is warm and well-paced. The problem is the Camondo family tragedy (Nissim
killed flying; Béatrice and family killed at Auschwitz; the family ended) arrives in
the paragraph before, and it is genuinely moving. The subsequent paragraph about the
picture "finally being allowed to be itself" after the scandal feels like it's pushing
past something heavy without acknowledging the heaviness. The painting's survival and
the Camondo family's destruction sit side by side here, and the ending needs to
acknowledge that. Or it should restructure so the Camondo tragedy is not the immediate
setup for the "the painting is fine now" resolution.

**Fix:** Either move the Camondo paragraph earlier in `afterlife` (so the provenance
chain has space before the grace note) or add a beat that holds the contrast: the
collection survived; the people who left it to France did not.

---

### Look-Closer Assessment

**#1 Two figures shoved to corner** — STRONG. "These two are not the center of anything.
They're an afterthought in their own room" — that is a finding task with a payoff.

**#2 The milky-green glass** — STRONG. The desaturation logic ("the painting builds
a desaturated room so the green pulls") makes the reader understand what to notice and
why. This is the benchmark level.

**#3 The zig-zag of empty tables** — STRONG. "The emptiness isn't background — it
*is* the painting." Clear instruction + claim.

**#4 Andrée's lowered eyes** — GOOD. The "eighteenth pose" reading is good dry wit.
Could be sharper per AB-M2: name what she's not looking at.

**#5 Desboutin's jacket and pipe** — STRONG. "That's two people at the same table,
twice as alone" is the best closer in the batch's Look-Closer section.

**#6 The drained palette** — GOOD. "They're not just in the empty room. They're of it."
Strong close. The instruction to "stand back" is a genuine task.

## WORK 3 — Luncheon of the Boating Party (Renoir)

### Per-Section Grades

| Section | Grade |
|---|---|
| chatou | STRONG |
| making | STRONG |
| looking | GOOD |
| show | GOOD |
| afterlife | GOOD |

### Findings

The Veronese setup, the assembled-not-spontaneous lunch reveal, and the tube-paint
chemistry section are all excellent. The voice is consistently warm and the stakes
are clear. Weaknesses cluster in `looking`, where the crowd-scene reading is thorough
but the canvas is sometimes described rather than *seen*, and in `show`, where the
Degas comparison is strong but the show chapter itself risks becoming a secondary
story about Durand-Ruel rather than staying with the picture's public argument.

---

**MUST-FIX**

**[BP-M1] `looking` — The cluster structure describes faces, not the canvas.**

The looking chapter is organized as a cast list (left cluster, center cluster, right
cluster). This is factually complete and useful, but it functions as a visitor's guide
rather than as art writing. When does the *canvas* assert itself? Where is the light?
Where is the awning's color doing work? The benchmark on *Impression, Sunrise* opens
on the painting as a *visual experience* ("your eye does when it lands on it") before
moving to components. This chapter reverses that order — you get the cast tour, and
then at the end the striped awning and the river-behind-the-railings are mentioned as
structural devices.

Specific weak beat — current: *"The whole arrangement is held together by a few quiet
visual decisions."* This is an essay segue, not art writing. "Held together by quiet
decisions" tells the reader the picture is well-composed without showing them the
composing.

**Fix:** Open `looking` with the first impression the canvas makes at full size —
what does the eye do *before* it picks out individual faces? The warmth? The awning-
light dapple that covers everyone? The sense of a single shared space? Then run the
cast list. Close by returning to that first impression: what the whole picture feels
like once you know everyone in it.

---

**[BP-M2] `looking` — The still life on the table is buried in `making`, not in `looking`.**

The bottle-as-jewel passage is one of the draft's best pieces of looking — *"the light
passing through dark red into the white tablecloth below"* — but it appears in `making`
(the chemistry/pigment section) rather than in `looking`. The `looking` chapter mentions
the still life only briefly: *"the painting's quiet evidence that this is real food and
real wine."* The reader is given the *claim* of the still life but not the experience
of it.

**Fix:** In `looking`, when the eye reaches the foreground table, show the wine-bottle
glass thing properly: the light going through dark red into white cloth, the grapes
spilled across the napkin, the half-emptied glasses catching what little direct sun
gets under the awning. The reader should SEE the table. The current Look-Closer pointer
#6 handles this, which means the detail is there, but it should also be in the `looking`
prose.

---

**SHOULD-FIX**

**[BP-S1] `chatou` — The Veronese comparison payoff arrives late and could be placed earlier.**

Current: The Veronese comparison appears at the end of `chatou`, as Renoir's explicit
ambition. This works structurally. But the chapter opens with a warm scene-setting
passage ("Picture a long, thin island") that is strong, and the Veronese reveal would
land harder if it were placed as a closing punch rather than as the last paragraph
that runs into "He just needed his cast."

This is a minor structural issue; the comparison is placed correctly, it just ends
with a deflating setup line ("He just needed his cast. And the cast, conveniently,
was already in the room") that is a little too neat. A modern Sunday lunch on the
scale of a Venetian banquet deserves a stronger handoff.

**Fix:** Replace *"He just needed his cast. And the cast, conveniently, was already
in the room"* with something that holds the ambition: *"All he needed was fourteen
people willing to sit still on a balcony for a summer. And as it turned out, they
were already at the next table."* (Or similar — land the scale of what he was
attempting before the chapter hands off.)

---

**[BP-S2] `show` — The comparison to L'Absinthe is strong; the Durand-Ruel beat is too long.**

Current: The Durand-Ruel section ("held it for forty-two years") runs through most
of the second half of `show`. The dealer holding the picture for four decades is
interesting, but it reads as early `afterlife` content that has wandered into `show`.
The chapter should end on the picture's reception and public argument; the Durand-Ruel
story belongs in `afterlife` (or as a bridge sentence at the open of `afterlife`).

**Fix:** Cut the `show` chapter to end at *"He had moved his method one important
step forward."* (from the `gare-saint-lazare` draft — borrow that cadence.) Move the
Durand-Ruel 1881 purchase and forty-two-year hold to the opening of `afterlife`, where
it belongs chronologically and dramatically (the pursuit of the picture starts with
a dealer who won't let it go).

---

**[BP-S3] `looking` — Alphonse Fournaise Jr. and Caillebotte are easy to confuse.**

Current: Both Alphonse Jr. and Caillebotte are described as wearing white sleeveless
singlets and straw boater hats. The draft itself flags this in the fact ledger as
a potential confusion risk. In the `looking` prose it isn't flagged at all — the
reader who has been told to look for a man in a white singlet and straw boater at
the left, and then told to find another man in a white singlet and straw boater at
the right, has no help distinguishing them except their spatial position.

**Fix:** In the passage introducing Caillebotte, add a distinguishing feature that is
actually on the canvas — posture, position, or the backwards chair. Something like:
*"Caillebotte — white singlet and straw boater, same kit as Alphonse Jr. on the left,
but flipped around in his seat so the chair-back faces the table and he faces it
backwards."* The backwards chair is already mentioned; make it the distinguishing
signal, not just a pose note.

---

### Look-Closer Assessment

**#1 Aline and the dog** — STRONG. The "nine years" detail is the right kind of
dry wit; the Brussels griffon type is specific.

**#2 Caillebotte backwards** — STRONG. "Quiet thank-you" framing is warm and
earns the reader's attention.

**#3 Ellen Andrée with glass** — STRONG. The *L'Absinthe* callback makes this
a genuine cross-reading task.

**#4 Ephrussi in top hat** — GOOD. "He's overdressed for a lunch on a balcony"
is exactly the right observation. The Hare with Amber Eyes line is useful if
verified.

**#5 The striped awning** — STRONG. Veronese's curtain reference earns the look.

**#6 The wine-and-fruit still life** — STRONG. "Nobody wants to leave" is a real

**#7 The boats through the railings** — STRONG. "Almost the joke" is the right
register for this; it tells the reader where to look and why to look.

## WORK 4 — La Gare Saint-Lazare (Monet)

### Per-Section Grades

| Section | Grade |
|---|---|
| station | STRONG |
| making | STRONG |
| looking | GOOD |
| show | STRONG |
| afterlife | STRONG |

### Findings

The strongest draft of the four in structural ambition. The three-layers reading
(iron / steam / engines) is clear and genuinely teaches the eye. The series-invention
framing is well-placed in `making`. The afterlife closer — painting of a railway
station now hanging inside a railway station — is the best ending in the batch. Two
focus corrections.

---

**MUST-FIX**

**[GS-M1] `looking` — The steam is named as the subject; the canvas description doesn't fully deliver on it.**

Current: *"A great soft cloud of it rises straight up the center of the canvas,
billowing under the dark roof. Get close to the canvas and you can see Monet has
built the cloud out of dabs of nearly white paint, pale yellow paint, washes of
soft blue, and feathered edges of pale gray. The steam isn't a sharp shape; it is
shape *dissolving*."*

That passage is good, and "shape dissolving" is exactly right. But the benchmark
level of looking goes one more step: it shows the reader the *mechanical relationship*
between the painted marks and the visual result. In `sunrise`, the equiluminance
section explains *why* the sun looks the way it looks — it doesn't just say "the sun
glows." Here, the steam-built-from-dabs is named, but the effect isn't: why does
a cloud of dabs of white and pale-yellow and soft-blue *read as steam* rather than as
a bunch of paint marks? The reader who has never stood in front of a misty Monet
doesn't automatically make the connection. Give them the mechanism: at arm's length
the colors resolve into haze; at arm's length your eye blends the marks into the
soft body of water vapor the way the atmosphere itself works.

**Fix:** After "shape dissolving," add: *"Back up a step and the individual marks
disappear; your eye blends the pale dabs into a column of vapor exactly the way your
eye blends coal smoke into a gray sky from across the street. Monet is using the
viewer's own distance as the final ingredient."*

---

**[GS-M2] `looking` — The "limited palette" paragraph buries its best comparison.**

Current: *"The palette is severely limited... Monet has trusted the palette. He hasn't
smuggled in pretty color the scene didn't have."*

"Smuggled in pretty color" is exactly the right phrase — dry, accurate, a genuine
criticism of what lesser painters would have done. But "He hasn't smuggled in pretty
color the scene didn't have" arrives as the last sentence, after a list of what colors
are and aren't present. It should be the *first* sentence of that section, leading
the color inventory rather than closing it.

**Fix:** Open the palette passage with: *"The one thing Monet didn't do was make it
prettier than it was. There is no red, no green, nothing warm has been smuggled in;
the palette is grays, blacks, blue-grays, and dirty whites, which is the palette of
a roofed industrial shed lit through grimy glass, and Monet trusted it."* Then
the inventory of what is and isn't there reads as confirmation rather than buildup.

---

**SHOULD-FIX**

**[GS-S1] `making` — The "cheeky thing" framing is the right register; don't undercut it.**

Current: *"In January 1877 Monet did two practical things, and one slightly cheeky
thing, and out of those three moves came the most ambitious single project of his life
so far."*

That is a great opener. But "slightly cheeky" softens the anecdote before it lands.
Monet walked into a major private railway company, dressed, presumably, as a painter,
and asked them to let him set up an easel in their busiest terminal for four months.
That is not *slightly* cheeky. The benchmark would say "brazenly" or "with the
confidence of a man who knew the worst they could say was no."

**Fix:** Change "one slightly cheeky thing" to "one thing that required a fair amount
of nerve" — or lean into the comedy: "one thing that required him to put on his best
coat and speak in his most authoritative voice."

---

**[GS-S2] `station` — The Turner/Manet lineage is correctly placed but could deploy a comparison.**

Current: *"Monet was not the first painter of railways. He never claimed to be. What
he was about to do was different — and you have to know the lineage to see what was new."*

That promise ("what was new") is set up here and paid off in `making` (the steam
as subject, the series as method). The sentence structure is correct. But "you have
to know the lineage to see what was new" is a statement about the essay rather than
a description of the difference. What *was* different? Turner dissolved the locomotive
into weather. Manet hid the locomotive behind a steam cloud and showed us the woman
on the bench. Monet put the locomotive in the picture *and* made the steam the subject.
That triangulation is the interesting beat, and it's available here.

**Fix:** Add after the Turner/Manet reference: *"Turner dissolved the locomotive into
weather. Manet hid the locomotive and showed the steam behind the fence. Monet was
going to put both in the frame — the black machine and the white cloud above it — and
make the cloud the subject."* This lets the reader arrive at `making` already knowing
the picture's argument.

---

**[GS-S3] `afterlife` — "Nobody planned that" undersells the joke.**

Current: *"The painting of the station is in the station. Nobody planned that. It just
happened, because the building was the building and the museum needed the space."*

"Nobody planned that. It just happened" is accurate but too casual. The benchmark
voice would extract the irony more deliberately — this is one of the great art-
placement coincidences of the twentieth century, and it deserves one more beat. The
reader has just been told that Monet spent four months painting a train shed and now
his painting lives in one. Give that one full sentence of appreciation before moving on.

**Fix:** Replace *"Nobody planned that. It just happened..."* with: *"Nobody intended
this. The French government needed a building to put nineteenth-century art in, and
the old Gare d'Orsay was available. But of all the pictures that could have moved in,
it is Monet's iron-and-glass shed that hangs under the iron-and-glass ceiling — as
if the painting and the museum had always known they belonged together."*

---

### Look-Closer Assessment

**#1 The iron-and-glass roof** — STRONG. "The cathedral comparison is not an accident"
turned into a task: "Look up" is exactly right. The "girders don't quite form a tidy
triangle; they cut the sky into facets" is a genuine observation.

**#2 The two black locomotives** — STRONG. "Heavy black mass below the cloud" — the
reader knows what to look for and why it reads the way it does.

**#3 The central plume of steam** — STRONG. "Shape dissolving" + the Manet comparison
makes this a real task. The benchmark level.

**#4 The distant city through smoke** — GOOD. The Haussmann gloss is useful. The
pointer could add: "the steam is erasing them, the way the station erases the street
it sits on — you can feel the city just outside the shed but you can't quite reach it."

**#5 Small figures along platform** — GOOD. The "porter and fisherman are the same
mark" observation is the right comparison. Could use a direct task: "Find the small
dark vertical marks near the closest engine — those are people."

**#6 Dark vault versus sunlit yard** — GOOD. The instruction to trace from dark upper
left to pale upper right is a genuine task. The "cathedral nave" comparison earns its
place.

---

## TOP 5 FINDINGS — BATCH-WIDE

**1. [GS-M1] Gare Saint-Lazare / `looking` — MUST-FIX.**
The steam is named as the subject but the mechanism of *how dabs of paint become vapor*
is never explained. The sunrise benchmark goes deep on the mechanism (equiluminance,
two visual crews, why the sun shimmers). The steam needs the same treatment. A Monet
who paints steam needs the reader to understand how pigment at arm's-length turns into
atmosphere.

**2. [DC-M2] Dance Class / `looking` — MUST-FIX.**
The Japonisme paragraph is an essay lecture where it should be a fast triple punch.
The draft earns its synthesis ("a record of attention, not arrangement") but makes the
reader wait too long by front-loading the ukiyo-e history. Cut ~80 words, hit the
three moves fast, get to the payoff.

**3. [BP-M1] Boating Party / `looking` — MUST-FIX.**
The most famous crowd in Impressionism gets a cast tour, not a canvas reading. The
chapter needs to open on what the full canvas *feels like* before it names anyone.
Fourteen friends at a scale painting deserve a first-impression beat before the
roll-call.

**4. [AB-M1] L'Absinthe / `looking` — MUST-FIX.**
The drained palette is the picture's whole argument — it is why the two figures
dissolve into the wall — but it is placed at the end of the `looking` chapter, after
the figure descriptions. Move it to immediately after the empty-tables diagonal. The
palette is the lens through which every figure detail should be read.

**5. [DC-S3] Dance Class / `afterlife` — SHOULD-FIX.**
The closing returns to the opening hook almost verbatim ("The work, not the gala")
rather than advancing the argument. The watering can has been built up across three
chapters as the picture's emblem of labor. Close on the watering can; it earns an
ending the opening hook doesn't.
