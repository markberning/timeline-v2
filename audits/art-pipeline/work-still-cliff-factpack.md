## 2. THE STORY

Clyfford Still painted enormous abstractions built from **jagged, torn-edged fields of thickly troweled color** — paint laid on with a palette knife in heavy impasto, the colored fields ending not in clean geometric edges (as in Rothko's soft rectangles or Newman's "zips") but in ragged, **flame-like or torn vertical rifts**, as if one sheet of color had been peeled back to reveal another beneath it. *1957-D No. 1* is a museum-scale example: roughly **9½ by 13¼ feet**, a predominantly **black field** torn through by **jagged jolts of yellow and beige**, with white accents flicking across the yellow and a few patches of **bare, unpainted canvas** near the lower left. The largest yellow form runs the full height of the canvas along the right edge.

Still was the most **fiercely independent and combative** of the New York School. He cut ties with commercial galleries in the early 1950s, **declined to exhibit publicly from roughly 1952 to 1959**, and in 1961 moved to a farm near Westminster, Maryland, deliberately withdrawing from the art market and the New York scene. He saw the gallery-and-critic system as compromising; he wanted his paintings encountered on their own terms, untitled and uninterpreted.

His relationship with Buffalo is central to his legacy. Director **Gordon M. Smith** and board president **Seymour H. Knox, Jr.** courted Still's trust over years; in **1959** Smith gave Still complete control to stage his **first large-scale museum survey** at the Albright-Knox. Satisfied with how the museum handled his work, Still **donated 31 paintings in 1964** — making the Albright-Knox the preeminent public holder of his art for decades. The museum now holds **33 Still paintings (dated 1937–1963)**, one of the largest public collections of his work.

Because Still **sealed the rest of his output** during his lifetime and willed it intact to a single American city, the vast majority of his estate stayed unseen until the **Clyfford Still Museum** opened in **Denver in 2011** — holding roughly **95% of his lifetime output** (≈ 825 paintings plus ~1,575–1,850 works on paper). His paintings read as a **raw, sublime, anti-decorative force**: vertical, vast, matte, refusing prettiness, composition-as-arrangement, and the European picture tradition alike.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK *(required — before / after)*

**Before:** Even within early-1950s abstraction, the dominant logic was still *compositional* — flat planes (Rothko), the single divided field and vertical "zip" (Newman), or gestural calligraphy (Pollock, de Kooning). Behind all of it sat the inherited European idea of a picture as an *arrangement of parts within a frame*, balanced and resolved.

**The break (*1957-D No. 1*):** Still builds an **all-over color field out of torn, vertical color-shards** with **jagged, ragged edges** — not drawn, not composed into a balanced design, but **troweled and torn**, so the field reads as a single continuous skin punctured by rifts rather than a set of related shapes. The result rejects:
- **European composition** — there is no central motif, no figure-ground hierarchy, no balanced arrangement; the field simply runs to (and seems to strain against) the frame's edge.
- **The market and the art world** — Still pushed abstraction past style into an **uncompromising existential statement**, and matched that in his life by withdrawing from galleries, refusing titles, and sealing his estate. The painting and the stance are one gesture.

**After:** Still's torn, full-bleed color field (alongside Rothko and Newman) helped define **Color Field painting** and the move from gesture toward large, enveloping, non-compositional abstraction — abstraction as a total environment and a moral/existential claim rather than a designed picture.

> Sources: Smarthistory; The Art Story; Met Museum object essays on Still.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT *(verbatim, sourced — with variant flags)*

**Primary (on color / what the paint is for):**

> "I never wanted color to be color. I never wanted texture to be texture, or images to become shapes. I wanted them all to fuse into a living spirit."
> — Clyfford Still.

*Source/attribution:* documented via the **Clyfford Still Museum Archives** (City and County of Denver); reproduced by Artnet News and The Art Story. **Variant/uncertainty flag:** one secondary source associates this wording with the work **PH-960 (1960)**; treat the date attribution as approximate and cite the quote itself, which is stable across sources.

**Alternate (on the work as life/death — the brief's "life and death" phrasing):**

> "These are not paintings in the usual sense; they are life and death merging in fearful union. As for me, they kindle a fire; through them I breathe again, hold a golden cord, find my own revelation."
> — Clyfford Still.

*Source/attribution:* Clyfford Still Museum Archives; secondary sources associate it with **PH-385 (1949)** / a 1949 statement. **Flag:** the brief paraphrased a quote as *"I never wanted color to be color… I wanted them to be life and death"* — that is a **conflation of two separate quotes**. The accurate ending of the color quote is *"…fuse into a living spirit"* (not "life and death"); "life and death merging in fearful union" is the *separate* statement above. Use whichever fits the read, but do not splice them.

**On titles / refusing interpretation (best fit for this work's neutral date-code title):**

> "As before, the pictures are to be without titles of any kind. I want no allusions to interfere with or assist the spectator."
> — Clyfford Still.

*Source:* Clyfford Still statement, via The Art Story.

> Sources: Artnet News, "7 Quotes from Artist Clyfford Still" (cites Clyfford Still Museum Archives); The Art Story <https://www.theartstory.org/artist/still-clyfford/>.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS *(~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)*

1. **The jagged, torn color-edges.** Where the yellow meets the black, the boundary is ragged and irregular — like torn paper or a flame's edge, never a ruled line. This is Still's signature: the field looks ripped, not drawn.
2. **The thick palette-knife impasto.** The paint is troweled on in heavy, crusted layers rather than brushed flat; up close the surface is built up and tactile, the marks of the knife visible in the buildup.
3. **A dominant field with sudden rifts.** A single color dominates — here a deep, encompassing **black** — pierced by sudden **jolts of yellow and beige**, with the largest yellow form rising the full height of the canvas along the right edge.
4. **The matte, non-reflective surface.** The oil is dry and absorbent, not glossy; the picture swallows light rather than bouncing it, which is part of its somber, non-decorative weight.
5. **The absence of any image.** There is no figure, horizon, object, or symbol — and no descriptive title to supply one. Nothing depicts anything; you are left with color, edge, and scale alone.
6. **The towering scale.** At roughly **9½ × 13¼ feet**, the canvas exceeds the body and fills the viewer's field of vision — meant to be stood *before*, not looked *at*, so the field surrounds you.

> Sources: Buffalo AKG description of *1957-D No. 1*; Smarthistory; Met Museum object essays.

## 6. PROVENANCE

- Painted by Clyfford Still, **1957**.
- Acquired and given to the Albright-Knox Art Gallery (now Buffalo AKG Art Museum) by **Seymour H. Knox, Jr.**, board president, **in 1959** — credit line **"Gift of Seymour H. Knox, Jr., 1959,"** accession **K1959:26**.
- This 1959 Knox gift **predates** Still's own **1964 donation of 31 paintings** to the museum (a separate event); the two are sometimes conflated. *1957-D No. 1* came in via Knox's gift, not Still's 1964 gift.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS *(corrected, with sources)*

- **Date-code titles vs PH numbers — keep them distinct.** *1957-D No. 1* is a **date-code title** (year + letter + number), the kind of neutral, non-referential title Still used so nothing would steer the viewer. **PH numbers are a different system**: "PH" = **PHotograph** — an inventory number for the *order in which works were photographed* (not made), used in cataloguing his oeuvre (with PP = photo of pastel, PD = photo of drawing, etc., often paired as e.g. "PH-371 (1947-S)"). Do **not** call *1957-D No. 1* a "PH number," and do **not** describe the date-codes as inventory numbers. Both express the same intent — **refuse descriptive titles** — but they are not the same scheme. *(Sources: Clyfford Still Museum FAQ; SFMOMA object pages.)*
- **Quote conflation (see §4).** The accurate color quote ends **"…fuse into a living spirit,"** not "life and death." "Life and death merging in fearful union" is a **separate** statement. Don't splice.
- **"Life-lines" is not Still's documented term.** The brief's phrasing "life-lines" should be used loosely/descriptively, not as a coined Still term. The 2019 documentary is titled *Lifeline: Clyfford Still*, which may be the source of the association — that is a film title, not a Still coinage. Describe the rifts as "torn / flame-like / jagged color-edges" (well attested) rather than asserting "life-lines" as his vocabulary.
- **Confirm the wired image is THIS work.** The wired `ART_IMG.stillCliff` should be Buffalo AKG's **1957-D No. 1**, accession **K1959:26** — a **black-dominant** field torn by **yellow/beige** verticals (largest yellow form full-height on the right, white accents, bare-canvas patches lower-left). If the wired image is instead a **red/orange or differently-colored** Still field, it is a *different* painting and must be reflagged. *(Source: Buffalo AKG description.)*
- **Gift-year trap.** The 1959 Knox gift and the 1964 Still gift of 31 paintings are different events; *1957-D No. 1*'s credit is the **1959** Knox gift (§6).
- **Estate figures vary slightly by source** (≈ 825 paintings; works on paper cited as ~1,575 to ~1,850; total ≈ 2,400–2,800; "~95% of lifetime output"). Use "roughly 95% of his output / ~825 paintings" and avoid a single hard total. *(Sources: Wikipedia; Clyfford Still Museum; Denver Art Museum.)*
- **Biography myths — keep proportionate.** Still's reputation for combativeness and total independence is real and well-documented (he withdrew from galleries, sealed his estate), but his own claim *"My work is not influenced by anybody"* is a **statement of stance, not a verifiable fact** — present it as his self-mythology, not as art-historical truth.
