**Photomontage as a Berlin Dada "invention":** Höch and Raoul Hausmann are usually credited with originating photomontage as an art form (en.wikipedia: "one of the first pioneers"). Both told an origin story from a **1918 holiday on the Baltic Sea**, where they saw mass-produced commercial pictures — German soldiers' heads pasted onto standard uniformed-musketeer bodies, sold so a paying customer could fake "a heroic military life" — and grasped the alienating power of cut-and-paste (TheArtStory/Britannica on Hausmann; Photomontage, Wikipedia). **Present the claim honestly:** the "we invented it" framing is contested. George Grosz, John Heartfield, and Johannes Baader each separately claimed to have invented it (no priority works survive to prove any claim), and photomontage already had a **commercial history dating to the 1850s** (Photomontage, Wikipedia). So the honest line is: *the Berlin Dadaists made photomontage a fine-art / political medium; they did not invent cut-and-paste from nothing, and "who first" inside the group is a memoir feud, not a settled fact.*

## 2. THE STORY

- **The First International Dada Fair (Erste Internationale Dada-Messe), Berlin, summer 1920**, at Dr. Otto Burchard's gallery. *Cut with the Kitchen Knife* hung there and was reportedly **one of the most prominent / popular pieces in the show** (NMWA, Wikipedia/TheArtStory).
- **Höch almost wasn't allowed in.** George Grosz and John Heartfield opposed her exhibiting with the group; she was included only after **Raoul Hausmann threatened to withdraw his own work** if she was kept out (en.wikipedia, NMWA). This is the literal context of a woman's manifesto-painting being made *inside* a movement that nearly excluded her. (Hausmann later wrote in his memoirs that "she was never a member of the club" — en.wikipedia.)
- **Built entirely from mass media.** Höch cut her material from **illustrated magazines, newspapers, broadsides, product catalogues, and journals** (Smarthistory-aligned sources), then rearranged the clippings into a vast, kaleidoscopic, seemingly-chaotic-but-orchestrated composition.
- **The work is a map of Weimar Germany,** organized as **two warring camps**, with the **word "anti-dada"** and **"dada"** placed as labels. A diagonal "kitchen knife" cut runs **lower-right to upper-left**, separating the camps:
  - **"Anti-Dada" / the establishment** (upper right): figures of the old empire and the Weimar government — **Kaiser Wilhelm II** (his iconic moustache replaced by a pair of upside-down wrestlers), **General Hindenburg** and the military/generals.
  - **"Dada" / pro-revolution** (lower left and across): Communists and radicals (**Lenin, Karl Marx, Karl Liebknecht, Karl Radek**), the **Dadaists themselves** (Hausmann, George Grosz, Wieland Herzfelde, Johannes Baader), and **Albert Einstein** as the new science.
- **The dancers and the swapped heads.** A defining device: cut-out **dancers' and performers' bodies carry the heads of political/cultural men**, deflating their authority. The dancer **Niddy Impekoven** appears (her body "juggling"/balancing the head of the artist **Käthe Kollwitz**); other small female-performer bodies carry the caricatured heads of Lenin, Baader, Radek. (Ballerina/dancer bodies also carry Grosz + Herzfelde.) The Russian ballerina **Anna Pavlova** is among the dancers commonly cited as a source. Among the recognizable women: Käthe Kollwitz, silent-film star **Pola Negri**, actress **Asta Nielsen** — all aligned on the Dada axis.
- **The corner map of women's suffrage — her feminist signature.** In the **lower-right corner** Höch pasted a small **map of Europe marking the countries where women had won the vote**, and at the **upper-left of that little map she pasted her own self-portrait photograph**. This puts a woman, and the question of women's political power, in the spot a painter would normally sign — a feminist statement planted inside a male movement. (Khan Academy; this is the most-cited reading. Germany itself had given women the vote in the 1919 Weimar constitution, so the issue was live.)

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required before/after)

**Before:** Avant-garde radicalism still mostly meant *painting* — the artist's hand, oil on canvas, an invented image. Collage existed (Cubist *papier collé*), but it used neutral scraps (newsprint, labels) as form, not as the photographic image-world of mass media turned into political argument. There was no fine-art tradition of building a picture entirely from cut-up press photographs.

**After / what's new — three things at once:**
1. **A new medium made from mass media.** *Photomontage* — a picture assembled from cut press photographs and printed type, the **machine-made, mass-reproduced image** as the raw material of art. No brush, no invented figure; the modern media stream itself is the paint. (Berlin Dada's signature contribution; Höch + Hausmann at the front of it.)
2. **Collage as direct political weapon.** Not abstract play but a **partisan map of a real political moment** — naming the Kaiser, the generals, the Communists, the revolution — and physically *cutting* the establishment apart with scissors. Art as agitprop.
3. **A woman's critique of gender and the new image-world.** Inside a movement that nearly shut her out, Höch turned the same scissors on **how women were pictured** in that mass media — dancers, film stars, the "New Woman" — and planted women's suffrage and her own face where the signature goes. The break is not just *photomontage*; it's photomontage used to question who gets to make, and who gets pictured by, the modern image.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — with variant flags)

**On being the woman in a movement of men** (her most-quoted statement, on the Dada men's hypocrisy about the "New Woman"). Verbatim as transmitted by NMWA / Wikipedia (English translation):

> "None of these men were satisfied with just an ordinary woman. But neither were they inclined to abandon the (conventional) male/masculine morality toward the woman. Enlightened by Freud, in protest against the older generation … they all desired this 'New Woman' and her groundbreaking will to freedom. But — they more or less brutally rejected the notion that they, too, had to adopt new attitudes …"

**Flags:** (a) This is a **translation**, and the wording differs slightly between sources — one transcription reads "they were not *included* to abandon," clearly a typo for "**inclined**." Use "inclined." (b) The ellipses are in the quoted sources; it is excerpted. (c) Verify against the German original (Höch's papers / Lavin's monograph) before treating any single clause as exact.

**On the "kitchen knife" metaphor:** the standard reading — that the *kitchen knife* equates her scissors with a domestic woman's tool used to **cut through the male, beer-belly culture of Weimar** — is given by Smarthistory/Khan Academy and is near-universal, but it is **interpretation, not a sourced Höch quote.** Do **not** put it in Höch's mouth.

**On inventing photomontage:** Höch *claimed* she and Hausmann hit on the technique on the 1918 Baltic trip (paraphrased widely). **No clean verbatim first-person Höch sentence on the invention is confirmed here** — treat the origin story as reported/paraphrased, not as a quotable line, unless Lavin's monograph supplies the exact words.

> If a verbatim quote must ship, the **"None of these men…"** statement is the safest, but cite NMWA (or Lavin) and keep the translation flag.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, sourced; prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **Kaiser Wilhelm II, upper right ("anti-dada" / the old empire).** His head is large; his famous upturned **moustache is replaced by a pair of upside-down wrestlers** — the deflation device in miniature. *(Certain — universally cited.)*
2. **The military / Weimar establishment around him** — generals and government men of the "anti-dada" camp, including **Hindenburg**, whose head is set on a **belly-dancer's body**. *(Hindenburg-on-dancer: cited; treat the exact body-swaps as scholarly readings, see §8.)*
3. **The Dada / pro-revolution camp (lower left and across):** the radicals — **Lenin, Karl Marx, Karl Liebknecht, Karl Radek** — and the **Dadaists themselves** (Hausmann, Grosz, Herzfelde, Baader), with **Albert Einstein** as modern science nearby (framed by clippings reading roughly "*invest your money in dada*"). *(Camp division certain; some individual head-on-body pairings are speculative — see §8.)*
4. **The word fragments.** "**dada**" / "**anti-dada**" act as camp labels; the phrase "**Die grosse Welt dada**" ("the great Dada world") reads out at the **lower right**. Höch **revised this spot** — she earlier had "**Weltrevolution**" (World Revolution) there and replaced it with the milder "Die grosse Welt Dada." *(The Weltrevolution→Die grosse Welt Dada revision is a documented, citable detail.)*
5. **The women's-suffrage map, lower right.** A small **map of Europe marking the countries where women could vote** — Höch's political signature, placed where a painter signs. *(Certain. Map corner: lower **right** is the best-sourced placement; a minority of sources say lower-left — see §8.)*
6. **Höch's own self-portrait,** pasted at the **upper-left of that little suffrage map** — a woman's face and women's votes occupying the signature corner together. *(Certain that a small self-portrait is in the work and tied to the map; cite Khan Academy.)*
   - *(Optional 7th:)* The **dancers** as a motif — Niddy Impekoven's body carrying **Käthe Kollwitz's** head; Pavlova and film stars (Pola Negri, Asta Nielsen) among the cut figures, aligned on the Dada axis. *(Dancer motif certain; specific name-to-figure matches are partly scholarly.)*

## 6. PROVENANCE

- Held by the **Nationalgalerie, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin – Preußischer Kulturbesitz**, accession **NG 57/61** (Neue Nationalgalerie). The "**57**" in the inventory number indicates **acquisition c. 1957** — i.e., the (then West) Berlin Nationalgalerie acquired it in the late 1950s, during Höch's own lifetime. *(Flag: the precise acquisition route — purchase vs. from the artist vs. dealer — was **not confirmed** in this research; verify against the museum's collection record before stating a mechanism. Do not assert a buyer/seller.)*
- The work was first shown publicly at the **1920 First International Dada Fair, Berlin**; it remained associated with Höch through her long life in Berlin (she died there in 1978) before/around its entry into the national collection.

## 7. RIGHTS

- The wired image lives on **English Wikipedia, not Wikimedia Commons**, exactly because of its split status (File:Hoch-Cut With the Kitchen Knife.jpg).
- **United States: public domain (PD-US).** The work was **published in 1920** (at the Dada Fair / in Dada print contexts), i.e. **before 1 January 1930 (now 1 January 1931 under the rolling rule)** — so it is PD in the US. **State plainly: US public-domain status turns on the date of publication, NOT on the artist's death date.** Höch dying in 1978 does **not** affect the US-PD status.
- **Country of origin (Germany / EU): still under copyright** — German law runs life + 70 years, so EU copyright persists until **end of 2048** (Höch d. 1978). Any museum "©" notice (e.g. © Staatliche Museen / VG Bild-Kunst) is an **EU/Germany notice and does not govern US use.**
- Practical line for the app: safe to use as **PD-US**; cite the museum for the object, but the "©" you'll see on museum/agency pages is an EU claim, not a US one.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct, with sources)

1. **Exact title wording.** The English title floats: "*…Dada Through the Last Weimar Beer-Belly Cultural Epoch **of** Germany*" (our wired/assigned form, MoMA-style) vs. "*…**in** Germany*" (Smarthistory, Britannica, Khan Academy) and "*Cut with the **Dada** Kitchen Knife…*" (Artsy/Sartle reorder "Dada"). **All are translations of one German title.** Use the assigned full title; don't "correct" a source that uses *in Germany* — it's a legitimate variant, not an error. Anchor to the German: *Schnitt mit dem Küchenmesser DADA durch die letzte Weimarer Bierbauchkulturepoche Deutschlands.*
2. **Dimensions trap.** The reliable figure is **114 × 90 cm (44⅞ × 35⁷⁄₁₆ in)** — height × width, MoMA/museum. **Watch two errors:** (a) **order flips** — Wikipedia lists "90 × 144 cm," which both flips the order *and* gives **144** instead of 114; **144 cm appears to be a transcription error** — the corroborated long side is ~114 cm. (b) Don't republish "35 × 57 inches" (seen on one secondary site) — that's wrong. Use **114 × 90 cm ≈ 3 ft 9 in × 2 ft 11 in**, and flag if a museum record gives a slightly different measured size.
3. **Which cut figure is who = certain vs. guess.** **Certain:** the two camps (anti-dada establishment vs. dada/revolution); Kaiser Wilhelm with the wrestler-moustache; the suffrage map; "die grosse Welt dada"; Höch's self-portrait by the map; that the Dadaists, Lenin, Marx, Einstein appear. **Scholarly / less certain — flag if named:** the exact *head-on-body* pairings (Hindenburg-on-belly-dancer; Lenin/Baader/Radek on specific performer bodies; Grosz+Herzfelde on one ballerina; Niddy-Impekoven-balancing-Kollwitz). These are well-supported readings (Lavin, Smarthistory) but the picture is dense and some identifications are **interpretation, not labels** — present them as "scholars identify," not as fact. Pavlova / Pola Negri / Asta Nielsen as the dancer sources are commonly cited but **partly inferential**.
4. **Photomontage-invention dispute.** Do **not** write "Höch invented photomontage" or "Höch and Hausmann invented photomontage" flatly. Correct framing: the Berlin Dadaists (Höch + Hausmann prominent) **pioneered photomontage as a fine-art/political medium**; the "who invented it" story is a **contested memoir feud** (Grosz, Heartfield, Baader also claimed it), and **cut-and-paste photo compositing existed commercially since the 1850s** (Photomontage, Wikipedia). Also note Höch worked from **1916 in print/handicraft design at Ullstein**, so she was already steeped in the print-collage world before the famous 1918 Baltic trip.
5. **"Kitchen knife = her scissors" is interpretation,** widely repeated but not a Höch quote — keep it as a reading.
6. **Suffrage-map corner.** Best-sourced placement is **lower right** (Khan Academy, Lavin/Artsy); a few sources say "bottom-left/bottom-right" loosely. If the app needs a side, say **lower right** but it's safest to say "a corner."
7. **"Only woman in Berlin Dada"** is accurate and citable (and load-bearing for the read) — but say *Berlin Dada* specifically; other Dada centers (Zurich, Paris, New York) had other women.
