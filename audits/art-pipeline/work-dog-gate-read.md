# GATE READ — Balla, *Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash* (1912), WORK draft (Part B prose)

Gates run: **STORYTELLING + LOOKING + CLARITY**. Scope = the five `Dog`-prefixed
chapter components in PART B of `work-dog-draft.md` (the reader-facing prose). PART A
(the `DOG` const) and the fact ledger were read for context but are not the prose under
these three gates.

House-voice reference: `absinthe-narratives.tsx.txt`.

**Verdict: PASS with fixes.** No BLOCKERs on any of the three gates. The draft is
story-first, the playful charm lands, the looking section is genuinely guided and
concrete, and the hard terms (Futurism, Divisionism, chronophotography, persistence of
vision, Marey/Muybridge, avant-garde, dynamism, provenance) are all glossed at first use
for a zero-knowledge reader. The FIX items below are small precision/voice tightenings,
not structural. NICE items are optional polish.

Legend: **[BLOCKER]** = ship-stops · **[FIX]** = should-fix before integrate ·
**[NICE]** = optional.

---

## 1. STORYTELLING gate

**Result: PASS.** Story-first throughout. Each chapter opens with a scene or a hook, not
a thesis-restatement. The dry wit is present and is the absinthe register ("the grown-up
in the room," "Balla simply swapped the racehorse for a dachshund," "The twenty legs are
doing serious avant-garde work. They are also, unmistakably, a wiener dog in a hurry").
The central irony — a movement that worshipped the machine gun produced its most beloved
picture by painting somebody's dachshund — is set up in ch1, paid off in the closer, and
is the spine of the whole read. Comparisons are doing work (Pointillism for Divisionism,
sparkler/spinning-wheel for persistence of vision, the coffee-table-top size, Duchamp's
*Nude Descending* in the lineage). Scene-setting is concrete (Montepulciano stone streets
and slow afternoon light vs. the Futurists' boulevard).

No flat stretches that stop the read. No meta-narration of the forbidden kind (no "in
this chapter we will," no "this section is about how the painting links to"). The
forward-reference devices used ("you'll see it again in the dust of the road two chapters
from now"; "the divided color of Balla's Divisionist training... the thing he'd been
teaching for years") are the *absinthe* house pattern (cf. "in two chapters Victorian
critics will get it spectacularly wrong"), so they are in-voice, not a violation.

### Findings

- **[FIX] Hedge-stacking in `DogMaking` para 2 dulls the wit.** The Contessa-Nerazzini
  catch is correct fact-handling (legend, hedged), but the prose carries the hedge three
  times in one paragraph: "the popular books like to say more" / "By one often-repeated
  account that is who she was, but it is not on the museum's own page and is not tied to a
  primary source we can stand behind, so treat the name as a good story rather than a
  settled fact" / "What we know is the lady, the black dress, the dog, and the month."
  The triple-pass reads like a footnote eating the paragraph and flattens the scene. The
  fact must stay hedged; tighten the *delivery*.
  - Fix (suggested): collapse to one clean aside, e.g. replace the parenthetical with —
    "(You'll often see the owner named as a specific countess, the Contessa Nerazzini.
    It's a good story and it might be true, but it isn't on the museum's own page or tied
    to a source we can stand behind, so we'll leave it as the lady in black.)" — drops
    the redundant "What we know is..." sentence and the "the popular books like to say
    more" lead-in, both of which restate the same hedge.

- **[FIX] `DogScience` para 1 leans on a reader-command tic.** "Read that again and then
  look back at the dog." This is the imperative-to-the-reader move the looking gate
  *wants* (where it directs the eye on the canvas), but here it's pointing at nothing
  visible on the page in the science chapter and reads as filler instruction. The
  absinthe ref uses "Reader, file this fact away" sparingly and pointedly; this one is
  closer to a verbal tic. Minor.
  - Fix: cut "Read that again and then look back at the dog." and let the next sentence
    ("Balla simply swapped the racehorse for a dachshund.") carry it — it already does.

- **[NICE] `DogLooking` para 1 ends on a slightly bald instruction.** "Stand in front of
  it and let your eye go where the picture pulls it, which is straight down to the bottom
  center, to the dog. So go there." The "So go there." is a touch curt. Optional: "So
  that's where we'll start." Keeps the guidance, softer landing. Leave if you like the
  snap.

- **[NICE] The afterlife "verdict" reception dates float a little.** "it got called
  'crude' and 'clichéd' in the 1940s" and "By 2014 the mood had flipped... 'elegant and
  accurate'" are in the fact ledger as DOCUMENTED, so this is a storytelling note only:
  the quoted critics are anonymous ("Mid-century critics," "the same device was being
  praised"). Absinthe names its critics (Walter Crane, George Moore) and that specificity
  is part of why its scandal chapter sings. If a named source for either quote exists in
  the fact pack, naming even one would lift the paragraph. Not required.

## 2. LOOKING gate

**Result: PASS — strong.** This is the best-served of the three gates. `DogLooking`
walks the eye in a deliberate path and every required visual is hit with concrete,
see-it-yourself language:

- **blurred multiplied dog legs** — para 2, the whole paragraph: "a dense brown blur, a
  fan of feet, a haze of paws, far too many of them... like the dog has sprouted a
  centipede's worth of legs... Count them and you can't; that is the point." Tells the
  reader *where* (under the body, bottom center) and *what to do* (try to count, fail).
  Excellent.
- **swinging leash arcs** — para 3: "a fan of curved arcs, a set of loops drawn one over
  another... A single slack lead becomes a little nest of curves." Located ("runs up and
  to the right, from somewhere near the dog's collar toward the lady's hand") and used as
  the teaching example ("the easiest place to watch the method work on something
  simple").
- **lady's stepping feet** — para 4: "a staccato row of small dark shoes and the black
  hem of a skirt, the feet multiplied into a quick rhythm of steps." The "no face, no
  body, no figure" / "You supply the rest of her yourself" is exactly the kind of
  guided-seeing the gate exists for.
- **trotting dachshund body** — para 5: "long low body, the sausage itself, stays
  relatively solid and whole... the still core holds the shape together." Crucially this
  paragraph answers the reader's actual question ("why does any of this read as a dog and
  not as a brown explosion?"), which is the smartest looking move in the chapter.
- **the pavement / Tuscan road** — para 6: "a field of small pink-and-green strokes...
  usually read as the pale road dust of a Tuscan summer shimmering in the heat." Ties the
  ground to the Divisionism payoff promised in ch1.
- **repetition-as-motion device** — para 7: "Anything that moves, Balla paints as many
  overlapping copies of itself. Anything that stays still, he paints once... Multiply the
  moving, hold the still." The rule is stated, then re-checked against each element just
  walked through (legs/leash/feet move → multiplied; body still → single). This is the
  device made fully legible.

The eye-path is coherent: down to the legs → follow the leash up-right → lift to the lady
top-left → back to the body as the anchor → down to the ground → pull back for the rule.
A reader could stand at the canvas and follow it.

### Findings

- **[FIX] One small internal inconsistency in eye-direction.** `DogLooking` para 3 sends
  the eye "up and to the right" to the lady's hand for the leash; para 4 then sends it
  "up and a little to the left, to the top of the dog and above it" for the lady's feet
  and hem. So the lady's *hand* is up-right and the lady's *feet/hem* are up-left. That's
  geometrically odd for one figure and may momentarily confuse a reader trying to
  actually locate her on the canvas. Verify against the image which side the figure/leash
  resolve to, and make the two directions consistent (or, if the leash genuinely angles
  across, add half a clause acknowledging the leash crosses the body so the reader isn't
  whipsawed). This is a looking-accuracy point, not prose.

- **[NICE] "the tail, the ears" in para 5 are asserted as dissolving.** "everything
  attached to it (the legs, the leash, the tail, the ears) dissolves into repeated
  copies." Legs and leash are established as visible-and-multiplied earlier; tail and ears
  are introduced here for the first time and asserted as also-multiplied without the
  reader being shown them. If they're clearly multiplied in the image, fine; if they're
  not distinctly visible, drop "the tail, the ears" to avoid pointing at something the
  reader then can't find. Quick image check.

## 3. CLARITY gate

**Result: PASS.** Every load-bearing term is glossed at first use for a reader with zero
prior knowledge, in the absinthe pattern (define inline, in plain language, often with a
parenthetical and a pronunciation):

- **Divisionism** — "(the Italian cousin of French Pointillism)" plus a full mechanism
  gloss ("instead of mixing your colors on the palette... you lay down separate little
  strokes... and you let them mix in the viewer's eye"). Pointillism is glossed by being
  paired and explained in the same breath. Good.
- **avant-garde** — glossed at first use: "(the catch-all term for the experimental
  painters and writers who set out to break with everything that came before; literally
  French for 'advance guard,' the soldiers who go out ahead of the army)." Exemplary.
- **Futurism / Manifesto of Futurism / Technical Manifesto** — set up as a movement, with
  Marinetti, *Le Figaro*, the 1909/1910 split, and what it stood for. Clear.
- **dachshund** — "(the long-bodied, short-legged German breed, the 'sausage dog')."
- **dynamism** — defined where it lands the punch (end of `DogLooking`): "not a picture
  of a moving thing, but a picture of the movement itself." Held to the end deliberately,
  which is good craft, but see FIX below.
- **persistence of vision** — fully glossed with two everyday tests (sparkler, spinning
  wheel) in `DogScience`. Strong.
- **chronophotography** — glossed etymologically and mechanically: "(from the Greek
  *chronos*, 'time': literally 'time-photography')" + "rigged cameras to fire many
  exposures onto a single photographic plate."
- **Marey / Muybridge** — both introduced with dates, nationality, and what each did;
  the distinction between them (single-plate overlap vs. bank of cameras) is drawn, and
  why Marey is "the closer cousin" of the painting is explained.
- **provenance** — glossed at first use in `DogAfterlife`: "(the documented chain of who
  owned a work, from the painter's hand to where it hangs now)."

### Findings

- **[FIX] "dynamism" / "Dynamism" is in the title and used before it is defined.** The
  work's name is *Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash* and the looking-chapter title is "Twenty
  legs on a wiener dog," but the word "dynamism" itself isn't defined until the last
  sentence of `DogLooking`. A zero-knowledge reader meets the word in the title and hook
  long before the gloss. This is *probably* fine because the held-to-the-end definition
  is a real payoff — but confirm the deliberate withholding is acceptable to the gate.
  Lightest fix if not: at the very first in-prose use, a three-word inline tell
  ("dynamism — Futurist for motion-made-visible —") and keep the full payoff at the end.
  I'd lean toward leaving it as-is; flagging so the call is conscious, not accidental.

- **[FIX] "louche"/"divided color"-style term reused without re-anchor across chapters
  is fine, but "divided-color" appears before "Divisionism" is fully a household word.**
  In `DogMaking` para 3: "painted by separate strokes of color in the divided-color habit
  Balla had been teaching for years." A reader who skipped ch1, or whose memory of the
  ch1 gloss has faded, meets "divided-color habit" with only an implicit back-reference.
  The absinthe model re-anchors lightly on reuse. Minor: consider "the divided-color
  habit (those separate strokes of pure color) Balla had been teaching." `DogLooking`
  para 6 *does* re-anchor it correctly ("(separate touches of color meant to mix in your
  eye, the thing he'd been teaching for years)"), so this is only the `DogMaking`
  instance.

- **[NICE] "staccato"** (DogLooking para 4, annotations) is a music borrow used for a
  visual rhythm. It's common enough and the context ("a quick rhythm of steps") carries
  it, so no gloss strictly needed for the lay reader. Leave.

- **[NICE] "impresario and ideologue"** (DogTeacher para 3) for Marinetti — "ideologue"
  is borderline for a true zero-knowledge reader. Context ("He wasn't a painter") and the
  surrounding setup mostly carry it. Optional: "its showman and its theorist." Leave if
  you prefer the bite.
