## MUST-FIX

---

### 1. LOOKING FAILURE — The Angelus section never makes the reader SEE the picture

**Chapter:** 4 — Millet's peasants  
**Section:** "The Angelus — A field that turns into a church"

**The problem in full:**

The Gleaners section is genuinely strong on the looking axis. Three sentences do
the job: the scale, the bent-waist posture, the compositional argument (monumental
women in the foreground; the tiny, golden, busy harvest behind them). The reader sees
it. The Angelus section that follows gets the subject exactly right — the man and
woman bowed in prayer, the potato basket, the bell — but it never grants the reader
the same specificity.

What the draft says:
> "Two peasants — a man and a woman — stand alone in a darkening field at dusk, a
> basket of potatoes and a fork at their feet, and they have stopped work to bow their
> heads. The title names the reason: the Angelus is a Catholic devotion said morning,
> noon and evening at the sound of a church bell, and on the far horizon, almost too
> small to find, the tower of a village church catches the last light."

That inventory is accurate but it is a museum label: it names what is there, it does
not make the reader see what looking at this painting actually *feels like*. The
Angelus has a quality that made it the most reproduced image of the nineteenth century
and that the draft completely omits: the sky. The painting's sky is enormous — almost
two-thirds of the vertical canvas is sky, a flat, gray-gold wash that presses down on
the two tiny figures in a way that makes them look pinned. The scale relationship
between the towering sky and the bowed, small figures below is the emotional mechanism
of the picture. Millet gives you the sky that humans have always prayed under, and he
makes it weigh on them. The church-bell tower catching the last light on the horizon is
almost impossibly small — which is part of the point: the bell fills the whole painting
but the tower is barely a smudge. Without that sky, the prose is describing a label
("two figures in a field at dusk") rather than the painting.

The section then pivots to Millet's argument about the sacred in the dirt — which is
the right intellectual payoff — but because the reader has not been made to *see* the
painting first, the argument floats free. The Gleaners section works because the
description and the argument are fused: you see the composition, you understand the
politics. The Angelus needs the same treatment.

**Required fix:** Before the "far gentler picture" summary, add one beat of genuine
looking. The sky's weight, the smallness of the figures under it, the near-invisibility
of the tower against that last bronze light. Something like: the sky takes up most of
what you're looking at — a flat, still weight — and the two figures under it seem not
just humble but genuinely small, the way people look small under weather. The bell is
everywhere in the painting but the tower is barely findable. That visual — the sky
pressing down, the two bent heads, the just-findable tower — is the picture, and the
prose needs to show it before it explains it.

## SHOULD-FIX

### 2. WIT NOT GIVEN ROOM — Ch 1's best asides are stated and immediately sprinted past

**Chapter:** 1 — Why Realism  
**Section:** "The demand — Paint your own century"

**The line at issue:**
> "It sounds obvious now, which is the surest sign of how completely they won."

This is the draft's single best dry-wit line — the kind of compression the Cubism
narrative does well throughout ("It is a wonderful story, and worth remembering that it
is also a story Picasso told about himself"). It earns a breath. What follows —
"But set it against what the Salon was rewarding in these very years…" — is correct
and useful, but the transition is too fast. The line is stated and then immediately
justified rather than briefly allowed to hang. Voice-lock (a) is specific: when a wry
fact is earned, give it the half-sentence of room to land, don't state the punchline and
sprint on.

**Also in Ch 1:** The closing line of the avant-garde section —
> "The man riding point was a barrel-chested provincial with an ego the size of a
> cathedral, and the next chapter is mostly his."

This is strong. It lands. The only concern is that "barrel-chested provincial with an
ego the size of a cathedral" is doing a lot at once; "a cathedral" is the right scale
for the joke but it's a slightly worn comparison for a big ego. This is a minor note,
not a blocker — but if the revision pass opens Ch 1, consider whether there is a more
specific, visual Courbet-inflected version of the ego comparison (he was also a
self-portraitist obsessed with his own image; something about that particular self-belief
might land more specifically than the generic cathedral).

**Suggested fix direction:** After "which is the surest sign of how completely they won,"
let the sentence end there (period) and start the next sentence fresh. The current
construction runs the wit line into the qualification in the same paragraph breath,
which softens it. Give the win its own sentence — then pick up the camera and show the
reader what it was won against.

### 3. COMPARISON THIN — Ch 3 has the manifesto analysis but no analogy for the reader

**Chapter:** 3 — The Pavilion and the Manifesto  
**Section:** "The manifesto — A name pinned to a flag"

**The problem:**

The analysis of Courbet's manifesto sentence — "*My epoch* — not antiquity; *the customs,
the ideas, the appearance* — ordinary life; *according to my own estimation* — by my
judgment, not the jury's" — is excellent intellectual history. It is exactly the kind of
prose that earns the Cubism narrative's five-star reviews. But voice-lock (b) calls for
a healthy dose of comparisons, and this chapter has almost none. The whole Pavilion
chapter is argument and analysis; it never gives the reader an analogy to grab.

The one comparison the chapter *does* use is the "renting the empty shop next door"
analogy for the Pavilion itself, which is genuinely good — vivid, modern, immediately
understandable. But the manifesto section that follows drops that energy and becomes
more purely analytical. The idea that Courbet was relocating authority from the State
to the individual artist deserves an analogy: this was the art-world equivalent of
something. What was happening in other domains at the same time where individuals were
asserting their own judgment against institutions? The Dickens Meanwhile card gestures
at this, but it is outside the chapter body.

**Suggested fix direction:** One analogy in the manifesto analysis section — even a brief
parenthetical — that makes the "authority relocated from State to artist" move feel as
visceral as the "shop next door" image makes the Pavilion feel. Not long. One
half-sentence. Voice-lock (b) is the requirement.

### 4. LOOKING — Ch 3 is the only chapter with no look at a picture in its prose

**Chapter:** 3 — The Pavilion and the Manifesto

**The problem:**

Chapter 3 is argument and narrative — and for the most part that is fine; the Pavilion
chapter's job is to explain the move Courbet made, not to describe another canvas. But
it does name *The Painter's Studio* in passing ("the era read shows this one — the
canvas that ended up in his own tent instead of the official hall"). The casual name-drop
without a looking beat is a small missed opportunity: the reader has just been told this
is the painting Courbet considered his "summary statement" and that it ended up in a
tent across the street from the Exposition Universelle. That context is extraordinary.
Even two sentences of what the painting looks like — a vast canvas, nearly twelve feet
tall, with Courbet himself at the center surrounded by an allegorical company that
includes a naked model, a journalist, and a skeleton — would make the case for why the
jury refused it far more viscerally than the abstract argument.

This is a SHOULD-FIX rather than a MUST-FIX because the chapter works without it, and
the era read is explicitly established as the place that shows *The Painter's Studio* in
full. The looking gap here is a missed intensification, not a failure. But the chapter
is currently the one stretch in the six-chapter narrative where the reader goes longest
without being asked to look at anything.

### 5. VOICE — The Stone Breakers ghost passage is the draft's best moment; the rhetorical
move at the end should not be buried

**Chapter:** 2 — Courbet's gauntlet  
**Section:** "The Stone Breakers — Two men and a pile of rock"

**The line at risk:**
> "There is a grim aptness to it: the great picture of the people history grinds up
> was itself ground up by history."

This is the single best line in the draft. It is worthy of the Cubism bar. But it
appears at the end of a long paragraph and is immediately followed by the
[RESTRICTED-FIGURE] block, which functions as a figure break. There is a risk that in
the final rendered narrative the rhetorical weight of "the great picture of the people
history grinds up was itself ground up by history" will be swallowed by what comes
before it rather than allowed to land.

**Voice-lock (c):** No italic/muted violation in the draft text as written — the line
is in body text. But the coordinator should confirm at integration time that this line
does not end up in a `proseMutedStyle` or similar muted/italic wrapper in the JSX. The
gate flags it now so it is not accidentally softened at integration.

This is a SHOULD-FIX note for the integration pass, not the author pass.

### 6. HOOK & STAKES — Ch 5 opens on explanation rather than story

**Chapter:** 5 — Daumier's city  
**Section:** "The press — A weapon you could print" (first section)

**The problem:**

The chapter's opening sentence —
> "Realism so far has meant the countryside — Courbet's stone and graves, Millet's
> fields. Honoré Daumier dragged it into the modern city, and he did it first not with
> paint but with the printing press."

— is structurally correct and clear. It does the transition and the stakes. But it is
a road-sign, not a hook. Compared to Ch 1 ("Most art movements begin in a studio. This
one begins in a riot.") and Ch 2 ("The kind of self-believer who makes a movement
possible and a dinner party unbearable"), the Ch 5 opening is journeyman-level
transition writing. Daumier is the chapter's most colorful character in one sense —
a man who went to *jail* for a drawing, who produced thousands of images mocking kings,
who painted his greatest picture and almost nobody saw it in his lifetime — and the
chapter's entry doesn't signal any of that energy.

Compare to the Cubism narrative's Ch 5 opening ("Picasso and Braque never wrote a
manifesto and rarely exhibited. The painters who turned Cubism into a public movement
were the second wave…") — that chapter opens on contrast and immediately makes a
distinction that drives everything that follows. The Daumier chapter's opening is more
of a bridge paragraph.

**Suggested fix direction:** Open with something that puts Daumier and the jailing
anecdote — or the printing-press-as-weapon image — in the first sentence. The current
opening's contrast ("countryside → city") can be preserved but as a subordinate clause,
not the main event. The lede is the man who went to jail for a cartoon.

## Notes on what is already working at or above bar

The following do not need changes and should not be touched in the revision pass:

**The Burial at Ornans passage (Ch 2)** is the draft's anchor. The specific detail —
ten feet tall, twenty-two feet wide, fifty mourners strung in a frieze, the dog, the
flat gray cliff, nobody beautiful, nobody posed — is exactly the kind of looking the
pipeline demands. The "mismatch was the scandal" move is clean and lands hard.
"Critics asked, in effect, who told these *nobodies* they could fill a wall meant for
the gods" is earned and vivid.

**The Gleaners passage (Ch 4)** is the movement narrative's equal of the Burial.
The compositional argument (wealth is tiny and gold and back there; the women who get
the scraps are right here, filling your view) is done with precision. The "There is no
slogan. There is just a decision about who gets to be the size of a hero" formulation
is excellent — the analysis emerges from the looking rather than being imposed on it.
The political reading is handled with fairness: "It is tender. It is also a claim."

**The Horse Fair passage (Ch 6)** is the draft's best looking beat. "You can almost
hear it — the stamp of hooves, the shouts of the dealers, the snorting heave of a
half-ton animal that does not want to be where it is" is exactly right. The
synesthetic "A canvas you can hear" section header earns its title. The Vanderbilt
gift as the payoff — a French quarrel with a French jury crossing the Atlantic — is a
strong close.

**The Stone Breakers ghost passage (Ch 2)** — the whole structure of describing a
painting that no longer exists, via black-and-white reproductions, with the "grim
aptness" closing line — is inventive and well-executed. This kind of meta-formal
device (the story of a picture's destruction becoming part of the story of what the
picture said) is exactly what separates good art writing from a catalog entry.

**The handoff section (Ch 6)** closes the movement cleanly. "The Realists had said:
paint the real world, the thing that is actually there. A circle of younger painters
would soon say something subtler: paint not the thing that is there, but the thing you
actually *see*" is the right level of abstraction for a movement handoff, and "less a
break than a relay" is a genuinely useful comparison. The final sentence —
"Realism was the first shot of the whole modern revolt — the moment a group of painters
decided that their own ordinary century, exactly as it was, deserved the wall the gods
had always hung on" — is a strong close.

**The Bonheur trousers passage (Ch 6)** handles agency and the legend/fact distinction
cleanly ("The permit is so often told that it is worth flagging as reported"). The
conclusion — "She is not a footnote to a movement of men. She is one of the people who
proved the movement could win an audience" — is the right register: firm without being
a lecture.
