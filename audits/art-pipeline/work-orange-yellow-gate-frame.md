## 1. COMPREHENSIVENESS

### [FIX] C-1 — The tragic later arc is named but never delivered; the Seagram murals (the on-theme episode) are missing
The afterlife chapter gives the suicide a single clause ("he took his life in his New York studio in 1970, at the height of his fame") and the *idiom* of the period gets no later-arc at all. The brief explicitly asks for "the tragic later arc." More importantly, the **Seagram murals refusal (1958)** is the cleanest documented proof of the draft's own central claim — that for Rothko these were *not decoration*. Commissioned to paint a mural cycle for the Four Seasons restaurant in the Seagram Building, Rothko worked for ~two years, dined in the room, recoiled at the idea of his paintings as ornament for wealthy diners, returned the money and withdrew the canvases (later gifting nine to the Tate). It is the literal enactment of "if you are moved only by their color relationships, you miss the point," and it also marks his turn into the darker, heavier, more enclosed late palette that the Chapel completes. Its absence is the one major missing facet.

Suggested addition — a short paragraph in `RthAfterlife`, before "The afterlife" header (keeps the work-read tight, no new chapter):

> Two events from his last decade say more about what he thought these pictures were than any statement does. In **1958** he took a commission to paint a cycle of murals for the **Four Seasons** restaurant in the new Seagram Building in Manhattan &mdash; and then, after roughly two years and a dinner in the room, he handed the money back and withdrew the paintings, unable to stomach the idea of his work hanging as expensive decoration over the meals of the rich. (Nine of those murals are now a quiet room of their own at the Tate in London.) The refusal is the clearest thing he ever did: if the paintings were only handsome color, there would have been nothing to refuse.

(Then the existing "Rothko's own end was bleak" paragraph follows, and the Chapel close lands harder for it.)

### [FIX] C-2 — The Rothko Chapel ambition is undersold as a date, not an ambition
The brief flags "the Rothko Chapel ambition." The draft mentions it twice but only as a 1971 culmination ("an octagonal room of his dark canvases that people still enter and sit in and, often, weep in"). The *ambition* — that it was **commissioned in 1964** by John and Dominique de Menil as a non-denominational sacred space, that Rothko controlled the architecture, scale, and light, and that he designed it as a single immersive environment (fourteen near-black canvases) rather than a hang of separate pictures — is the endpoint of everything the read argues about scale and standing-inside-the-color. He did not live to see it open. Suggested tightening of the existing Chapel clause in `RthAfterlife`:

> &hellip;and it reaches its purest form in the **Rothko Chapel** in Houston &mdash; an octagonal, non-denominational room of fourteen of his darkest canvases that he was commissioned to design in **1964** and controlled down to the architecture, the scale, and the light, building not a wall of pictures but a single space to stand inside. He did not live to see it open: it was dedicated in **1971**, a year after his death. People still enter it, sit, and weep &mdash; which was, by his own account, always the point.

(Optional same edit in `RthBreak` where the Chapel is first named — at minimum drop the bare "(1971)" parenthetical there since 1971 is the *opening*, and the commission/intent is 1964.)

### [PASS] Facets covered well
- **Mature multiform/stacked-rectangle style** — `RthIdiom` traces Rothkowitz → figuration → Surrealist myths → multiforms → 1949–50 stacked rectangles. Complete and accurate.
- **Emotion-not-decoration insistence** — carried throughout and anchored to the Rodman quote; the "vehicle, not the point" framing is exactly the fact pack's §4/§8.4.
- **Immersive close viewing** — the ~18 in distance, the 1951 *Interiors* "you are in it" quote, the "field to be in" all present and sourced.
- **Rejection of labels** — colorist / abstractionist / color-field all explicitly refused in his voice; clean.
- **Influence** — Frankenthaler, Morris Louis, Color Field, the Chapel all named as the forward line.

## 2. FRAMING / FAIRNESS

### [PASS] F-1 — Rothko's own framing kept without tipping into mysticism or sentimentality
The draft repeatedly does the hard thing: it gives the reader the high register ("tragedy, ecstasy, doom," "religious experience," "inner light") **in Rothko's mouth via a sourced quote**, then immediately grounds the mechanism in physical paint ("There is nothing supernatural in the mechanism… you can name exactly why it does what it does"; "the means are physical paint" echoing fact-pack §8.5). This is the correct line between honoring his claim and endorsing it as metaphysics. No sentimentality creep — the "people break down and cry" detail is presented as *his* evidentiary claim, not as the narrator's emotional editorializing.

### [PASS] F-2 — "Colorist / color-field / abstractionist" correctly NOT used as self-description — CONFIRMED
Verified against the fact pack (§8.4) and the draft. The draft never asserts these as Rothko's terms; it explicitly stages all three as labels he rejected (`RthIdiom`: "He rejected the label 'colorist.' He rejected 'abstractionist.' He was no fonder of … 'color-field painter'") and keeps "Color Field" only as a museum/movement category ("what the museums call Color Field painting"). This is exactly right and is a genuine strength — flag it as a clean pass so it is not "fixed" backward.

### [FIX] F-3 — Genius-myth / proportion: Rothko is never situated *among* the New York School in the prose
The brief asks to "situate in the NY School" and watch proportion. The draft's prose treats Rothko almost in isolation. Pollock ("The other pole of the movement") and Frankenthaler exist only as **figure cards** (Part A) and as a downstream influence — the reader of the *chapters* never learns that Abstract Expressionism was a crowded scene of which Rothko was one figure, or that he had a distinct wing (the "color" / contemplative side) versus the gestural "action painting" side. Right now an inattentive reader could come away with a lone-genius impression. One sentence fixes it; suggested insert in `RthIdiom` (after the multiforms paragraph) or `RthBreak`:

> Rothko was one figure in a crowded postwar scene in New York &mdash; the loose group later called Abstract Expressionism, or the New York School &mdash; and he sat on its quiet, contemplative wing, as far as you could get from the flung-paint "action painting" of **Jackson Pollock** at the other pole. What follows is his move, not the movement's.

### [NICE] F-4 — "one of the most consequential ideas in postwar painting" — proportion check, passes but watch
`RthAfterlife` calls the color-carries-emotion move "one of the most consequential ideas in postwar painting" and "one of the clearest cases anyone ever made that abstraction could be an emotional encounter." Both are hedged ("one of," "one of the clearest cases") rather than first/only claims, so this passes the overclaim test. No change required; noting only that with F-3's NY-School sentence added, the proportion is fully safe. No "first," "only," or "invented" overclaim anywhere in the draft — good.

### [NICE] F-5 — minor citation nit (not a framing problem)
Web check returns the Rodman quote on **p. 92** of *Conversations with Artists* (1957); the fact pack and the draft's editorial note cite **p. 93**. Immaterial to the reader-facing prose (the draft prints no page number — it says only "his 1957 book *Conversations with Artists*" and "the 1956 Rodman conversation," both correct). Flagging for the fact-checker gate, not this one. The attribution guard in the draft ("it belongs to the 1956 Rodman conversation, not … a 1947 essay or a wall text at the Museum of Modern Art") is correct and worth keeping.
