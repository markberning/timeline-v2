### [FIX] — COMPREHENSIVENESS GAP: the "first woman at MoMA" facet is alluded to but never actually told

This is the single biggest gap. The "first work by a woman acquired by MoMA" / "First Lady of MoMA" framing is
one of the most-repeated facts about this object (Artsy's headline is literally *"Meret Oppenheim's Fur-Lined
Teacup Was MoMA's First Work by a Female Artist"*; The Vintage News, A Women's Thing, and Wikipedia all carry
it). The draft **mentions the claim only to footnote its date wrinkle** — in `afterlife`:

> "...the often-repeated claim that this was the first work by a woman to enter MoMA hangs on that 1946
> study-collection date."

That sentence assumes the reader already knows the claim. A comprehensive read should **state the facet, then
scope it** — it is both a real distinction and a fairness-sensitive overclaim, so it belongs in the prose, not
buried as a dependent clause. This is also exactly the kind of "first/only" claim the framing gate must scope
(see §2). Suggested addition to the `afterlife` provenance paragraph (replacing the bare footnote clause):

> "This acquisition is the root of a claim you will see everywhere: that *Object* was the first work by a
> woman to enter MoMA's collection, which earned Oppenheim the nickname 'the First Lady of MoMA.' It is worth
> stating carefully. The claim hangs on that 1946 study-collection date; the work was not accessioned into the
> permanent collection until 1963, and the wording in the sources runs from the confident ('MoMA's first work
> by a female artist') to the hedged ('she was *thought* to be the first woman in the permanent collection').
> Take it as a widely-repeated distinction with a real asterisk, not a settled record."

This both fills the gap AND discharges the §2 first/only scoping requirement in one move. **[FIX]** — required
for comprehensiveness; without it a top-three fact about the work is effectively absent.

### [NICE] — "young woman in a male movement" could be slightly sharpened

The draft establishes she was "one of its youngest and most striking members," modeled for Man Ray, and later
resented being reduced to "a 'woman Surrealist,' a muse and a mascot." That covers the facet adequately. A
light addition naming that Surrealism's inner circle was overwhelmingly male and that women were more often
muses/models than recognized authors would strengthen it — but the draft already gestures at this ("muse and
a mascot rather than an artist with a full body of work"), so this is **[NICE]**, not required.

### Comprehensiveness — no other major missing facet.
The medium, dimensions, three-title trap, the break (found-object assemblage), the Basel quote, and the
provenance chain (Ratton → London → MoMA) are all present and accurate.

## 2. FRAMING / FAIRNESS

### Gender / fairness — HANDLED WELL (no action)
- The Breton-coined title is named explicitly as **his** framing, not hers: "that French title was **not
  Oppenheim's**... coined by **Breton**... as a literary pun." Web-verified: Breton renamed it *Le Déjeuner en
  fourrure* punning on Manet's *Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe* and Sacher-Masoch's *Venus in Furs*; her own working
  title was *"Cup, saucer and spoon covered with fur."* ✅ Correct and proportionate.
- The erotic/sexual readings are held as **later reception, not stated intent** — the `break` paragraph is
  explicit: "those bodily and erotic readings... came **after**, from critics; Oppenheim neither fully
  endorsed them nor controlled them... Hold them as reception, not as a statement of what she set out to say."
  ✅ This is exactly the fairness posture the brief asks for, and it is **not sensationalized** — the prose
  never lingers on the sexual reading or quotes a lurid critic.

### [NICE] — make the *Venus in Furs* mention land as Breton's, not the work's
In `making` the draft writes the French title glanced "more slyly, at the erotic novel *Venus in Furs*." That
is correct (the pun is Breton's), but a reader skimming could attach the erotic reference to Oppenheim's
*object* rather than to Breton's *title*. One clause — "an association Breton built into the name, not one
Oppenheim put in the cup" — would seal it. **[NICE].**

### Genius-myth / situating her — HANDLED WELL
She is firmly placed inside the Surrealist circle (the café, Man Ray, the group exhibitions, the Ratton show),
not as a lone one-hit figure. The `afterlife` "trap of one perfect object" explicitly fights the one-hit
framing by naming the "long and varied career, poetry, painting, objects, sculpture, jewelry... for another
half-century." ✅ No genius-myth inflation.

### First / only overclaim — see [FIX] in §1
The only first/only claim in scope is the MoMA "first woman" one, and the draft's instinct (scope it, tie it
to the 1946 date) is correct — it just needs to be promoted from a buried clause to a stated-and-scoped facet
(the §1 [FIX]). With that fix, the first/only handling is clean. No other unscoped superlatives found
("quintessential Surrealist object" is correctly attributed to the MoMA visitor vote, not asserted in the
house voice; "canonical Surrealist object" / "type-specimen" are defensible art-historical consensus, not
overclaims).

### [FIX] — name-spelling consistency (fairness-adjacent + accuracy)
The draft uses **"Méret"** (with accent) throughout (const `artist`, `figures`, prose, blockquote
attribution). Web check: **MoMA's own record titles her "Meret Oppenheim" (no accent)**, and most major
sources (Britannica, Khan/Smarthistory, Artnet, NPR) use **Meret**. Wikipedia notes both forms are used
("Meret (or Méret)") with no stated preference, so *Méret* is **defensible, not wrong** — but it diverges from
the institution that holds the work and from the majority of the cited sources. **[FIX]:** either switch to
**Meret** to match MoMA/most sources, or keep *Méret* and add a one-line note that MoMA/most catalogs drop the
accent — but do not leave it silently inconsistent with the museum credit line the read itself cites. (Lower
priority than the §1 facet gap, but it is a clean accuracy call the gate should flag.)

### Proportion — GOOD
The erotic reading gets one careful, hedged paragraph; the fairness/gender material is woven through rather
than front-loaded as a grievance; the tactile-shock and break content carry the read. The balance is
storytelling-first with the fairness guardrails kept as guardrails, not as the subject. ✅
