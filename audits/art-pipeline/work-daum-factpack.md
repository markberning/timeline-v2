## 2. THE STORY

- **Berlin Dada, 1920.** Grosz had returned from WWI service to a Berlin of revolution, hyperinflation, war-cripples and bourgeois hypocrisy. He joined Berlin Dada in 1919; its members (Grosz, Heartfield, Hausmann, Höch, Huelsenbeck) turned Dada from Zurich's nonsense-play into a **savage political weapon** aimed at the German bourgeoisie and the militarist Right.
- **The occasion:** it is, among other things, **a wedding announcement.** Grosz married **Eva Peter** (often given "Eva Peters") on **22 May 1920**. **"Daum" is an anagram of "Maud,"** Grosz's pet nickname for Eva — *Maud → Daum*. (Berlinische Galerie, Wikipedia.) **VERIFY:** sources split on "Peter" vs "Peters"; both appear. The anagram is well-attested.
- **The conceit:** Grosz paints **himself** as the groom — a **pedantic clockwork automaton/robot man**, a head like a grey mask, body and machine parts **assembled from newspaper and magazine photographs (collage/photomontage)**. The **bride "Daum"** stands beside him, rendered in soft traditional ink-and-watercolour: pale, sensual, **largely undressed** (underwear, exposed breast and genitals, a floppy grey hat), reluctant/looking back at him.
- **The joke (per publisher Wieland/Werner Herzfeld):** at the very moment the bride bares her body and her desire, the husband-automaton **turns away to "soberly pedantic arithmetical problems"** — numbers, diagrams. Marriage "comes between the bride and groom like a shadow." Sensuality vs. cold mechanism.
- **The city behind them:** an **irreal, de-peopled urban backdrop** (architectural fragments, perspective lines) explicitly indebted to **Giorgio de Chirico's** metaphysical mannequin-and-piazza paintings — the same source Grosz mined for his other 1920 automaton works.
- **Politics:** the man-machine is Grosz's standing emblem of a **dehumanised, mechanised society** — the rationalised, emotion-dead bourgeois male, kin to the war-veteran rebuilt from prosthetics. Anti-bourgeois, anti-militarist satire is the engine of the whole image; the marriage is the alibi.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (before / after)

**Before:** Zurich Dada (Tzara, Arp, Ball) was abstract, playful, nonsense-driven — sound poems, chance collage, an anti-art *refusal of meaning*. Pre-war collage (Cubist *papier collé*) used pasted paper formally, to build a picture's surface.

**After / the break:** Berlin Dada — and this work — weaponise those tools.
- **The man-machine as political portrait.** Grosz casts himself, and by extension bourgeois man, as a **clockwork automaton** — emotionless, rationalised, sexually dead. The body-as-machine becomes biting social diagnosis, not Futurist celebration of the machine.
- **Photomontage as satire, not formalism.** The groom is literally **cut from press photographs** and reassembled — collage repurposed as **political/sexual montage** (the technique Grosz and Heartfield were pioneering). Pasted reality is turned against the society that produced it.
- **Bitterness vs. play.** Where Zurich Dada laughed, Berlin Dada **accused.** This is Dada with a target: marriage, the bourgeois male, mechanised modernity, all skewered at once.

**One-line break:** *Dada stops playing and starts shooting — collage and the man-machine become a satirical weapon aimed at the bourgeoisie.*

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — flag variants)

Grosz left **no reliably documented quote about this specific painting.** Use his general statements on his art-as-protest, clearly attributed as *general*, not about *Daum*:

> "My drawings and paintings were done as an act of protest; I was trying by means of my work to convince the world that it is ugly, sick and hypocritical."
— George Grosz. **Widely circulated** (Spartacus Educational, AZ Quotes, Quotefancy); ultimate source is Grosz's own writing/autobiography. **FLAG:** exact original publication not pinned; treat as well-attested but cite as "Grosz" generally, not to a verified page.

> "My drawings expressed my despair, hate and disillusionment. I drew drunkards; puking men; men with clenched fists cursing at the moon…"
— George Grosz, cited in **Uwe M. Schneede, *George Grosz: Leben und Werk*** (Hatje, Stuttgart 1975, p. 38). **Best-sourced** of the three.

> "I considered any art pointless if it did not put itself at the disposal of political struggle… my art was to be a gun and a sword."
— attributed to Grosz. **FLAG / USE WITH CAUTION:** widely quoted but I could **not** verify exact wording or a page in *A Little Yes and a Big No* (1946). Do **not** present as verbatim-verified without primary confirmation.

**Recommendation:** lead with the Schneede-sourced "despair, hate and disillusionment" line as the gated key passage; it is the cleanest provenance.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6 — prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **The automaton groom (Grosz's self-portrait), right of centre / smaller figure.** A man pieced together from body and machine parts, with a **whitish-grey head like a mask**; he is the "pedantic automaton 'George.'" Note he does **not** look at his bride. *(Berlinische Galerie.)*
2. **He is literally collaged.** The groom's body is **assembled from photographs cut out of newspapers and magazines** — set against the bride's hand-painted softness. The two halves of the picture use two opposed techniques on purpose. *(Berlinische Galerie.)*
3. **"Daum," the bride.** A pale watercolour woman in **underwear and a floppy grey hat**, breast and genitals exposed, sensual and turned toward the indifferent machine-man — vivacious flesh against cold mechanism. *(Berlinische Galerie / Wikipedia.)*
4. **Machine parts / clockwork.** The groom's gears, fittings and apparatus mark him as a wind-up mechanism — the **man-machine motif** central to Berlin Dada (compare Grosz's *Republican Automatons*, MoMA, same year). *(Dada scholarship.)*
5. **Numbers and arithmetical diagrams.** The automaton attends to **"soberly pedantic arithmetical problems"** — figures/calculations stand in for his emotional deadness at the very moment of intimacy. *(Per Herzfeld, via Wikipedia.)* **VERIFY exact numbers shown against the image before captioning specifics.**
6. **The irreal city behind.** An empty, hard-edged **urban/architectural backdrop with de Chirico-style perspective** — the metaphysical-painting source Grosz drew on for his 1920 mannequins. *(Wikipedia.)*

**Do NOT assert** specific identities for individual collaged scraps (which paper, which photo) — not reliably documented (see §8).

## 6. PROVENANCE

- Grosz reproduced the work (as a "metamechanical construction") in his **1922 Malik-Verlag portfolio *Mit Pinsel und Schere: 7 Materialisationen* ("With Brush and Scissors: 7 Materialisations")**, collecting his Dada works of 1919–1922.
- **Acquired by the Berlinische Galerie in 1995** with funds from the **Stiftung Deutsche Klassenlotterie Berlin (Stiftung DKLB)**. *(Berlinische Galerie — authoritative.)*
- **GAP / VERIFY:** the chain between Grosz's studio (1920) and the 1995 museum acquisition is not documented in accessible sources. Don't assert intermediate owners.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

1. **"Marshal Pétain" is NOT a title of this work — FALSE.** There is **no credible source** calling this painting *Marshal Pétain* or anything similar. The confusion likely comes from blurring it with **Grosz's other 1920 automaton work, *Republican Automatons* (Republikanische Automaten), MoMA** — a different picture with two faceless robot men. **Do not use "Marshal Pétain" as an alternate/short title.** The only real short form is *"Daum" Marries Her Pedantic Automaton "George"*.
2. **"Daum = Maud" anagram — TRUE and well-attested.** Maud was Grosz's nickname for his wife **Eva Peter(s)**; *Daum* reverses/rearranges *Maud*. (Berlinische Galerie, Wikipedia.) Minor uncertainty: **"Peter" vs "Peters"** — both spellings appear; flag, don't pick blindly.
3. **Medium trap — "on canvas" is likely WRONG.** Wikipedia says "on canvas"; the **Berlinische Galerie** (the owning museum) says **watercolour/ink/collage on watercolour board (paper support).** Trust the museum: it is a works-on-paper drawing/collage, not a canvas painting.
4. **Date — solid (1920).** No dispute.
5. **Identifying specific collaged scraps — UNRELIABLE.** No source reliably names which photographs/clippings make up the automaton. Describe the technique (press-photo montage), **not** specific identified elements.
6. **"Subtitle: Meta-mechanical construction by Grosz" — descriptor, verify framing.** Grosz classed the piece as a *metamechanical construction*; this is his genre tag (echoed in the 1922 portfolio), **not** a painted second line. Present as "Grosz called it a meta-mechanical construction," not as part of the inscribed title.
7. **The "gun and a sword" quote — unverified wording (see §4).** Do not present as verbatim-from-the-autobiography without primary confirmation.
