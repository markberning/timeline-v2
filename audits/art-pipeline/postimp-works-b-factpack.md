## 1. Bathers at Asnières — Georges Seurat (id `bathers-asnieres`)

### Identity
- **Title:** *Bathers at Asnières*. Original French: **Une Baignade, Asnières**
  (literally "A Bathing Place, Asnières"). [Wiki-EN, NG]
- **Artist:** Georges-Pierre Seurat (1859–1891). Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN]
- **Date:** 1884. Often given **1883–84** because the oil studies (*croquetons*) and
  drawing began in 1883 and the large canvas was finished in spring 1884.
  Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, Britannica, NG]
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas. [Wiki-EN, NG]

### Dimensions (BOTH units — convert to ft/in for the app)
- **cm:** 201 × 300 cm (h × w). (Some sources round the width to 301 cm; the National
  Gallery records 201.5 × 300.5 cm. Use ~201 × 300.) Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, NG]
- **in:** ~79 × 118 in.
- **ft/in for app:** **6 ft 7 in × 9 ft 10 in** (≈ 6.6 ft × 9.85 ft). It is a very
  large canvas — Salon-scale. Confidence: high.

### Location + accession / acquisition
- **Current location:** The National Gallery, London. Confidence: high. [NG]
- **Accession number:** **NG3908**. Confidence: high. [NG, Wiki-EN]
- **Acquisition:** Acquired **1924 through the Courtauld Fund** (the fund Samuel
  Courtauld endowed to bring French modern art into British public collections).
  Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN]
- **Note on display history:** It hung at the **Tate Gallery** after 1924 and was
  **transferred to the National Gallery in 1961**. Confidence: high (Wiki-EN);
  treat the Tate→NG transfer as solid but secondary. [Wiki-EN]

### Provenance chain
Confidence: high on the spine; some intermediate dates secondary. [Wiki-EN]
1. Remained with **Seurat's family / estate** after the 1884 Salon rejection (Seurat
   died young in 1891; works dispersed via his family and circle).
2. **Félix Fénéon** — the critic who coined "Neo-Impressionism" and championed Seurat —
   purchased it (Wikipedia: c. 1900). ⚠️ Exact intermediate owners between the family
   and Fénéon not fully pinned; treat Fénéon as the key early collector.
3. Acquired for the **British national collection in 1924 via the Courtauld Fund**.
4. Tate Gallery → **National Gallery, 1961**.

### Where it came from (the artist's situation)
- Seurat was ~24, fresh from the École des Beaux-Arts and military service. *Une
  Baignade* is his **first monumental canvas** — his bid to make a major modern
  statement at Salon scale. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, Britannica]
- He built it the academic way: ~**14 small oil panel studies** he called
  **croquetons** ("little sketches," painted on cigar-box lids and small panels) plus
  conté-crayon drawings, done on the riverbank, then composed the big canvas in the
  studio. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, Britannica]
- **Setting depicted:** the riverbank at **Asnières**, a working-class western suburb
  of Paris on the Seine. Across the water: the **factory chimneys of Clichy** smoking
  on the horizon, the **railway bridge** at Asnières, and to the right the tip of the
  **Île de la Grande Jatte** (the island of his *next* monumental canvas). The figures
  are clerks/workers cooling off — modern leisure, not myth. Confidence: high.
  [Wiki-EN, NG] ⚠️ "Côte des Ajoux near Courbevoie" appears in one source as the
  precise spot; safe ground-truth = "the Seine bank at Asnières, factories of Clichy
  across the water, Grande Jatte to the right."

### The making (technique, when, how long)
- Built **1883–84**: studies on-site 1883, large canvas finished **spring 1884**.
  Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN]
- **Technique:** large flat criss-cross strokes Seurat called **balayé** ("swept"),
  a controlled, woven application — NOT yet dots. The smooth, frieze-like calm comes
  from this disciplined hatching. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, NG]
- **[DOCUMENTED — likely true, see Disputed]** Seurat **reworked parts later, c. 1887**,
  adding small **pointillist dots** of orange and blue in select areas (notably around
  the **hat of the boy in the water** and other figures' edges), after he had developed
  divisionism for *La Grande Jatte*. The National Gallery's own technical reading and
  Wikipedia both describe these added dots. Confidence: high that *some* dots were added
  later; see the Disputed section for the nuance. [NG, Wiki-EN]

### Reception
- **[DOCUMENTED] Rejected by the official Salon (Salon des Artistes Français) jury in
  1884.** This rejection is the pivot of the whole story. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN,
  Britannica]
- **[DOCUMENTED]** In response, Seurat became a **founding member of the Groupe des
  Artistes Indépendants**, and the painting was shown at their inaugural exhibition
  (May–July 1884) — the show that became the **Salon des Indépendants** (jury-free,
  prize-free). Catalogued there as **no. 261**. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN]
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Shown by dealer **Paul Durand-Ruel in New York** (National Academy
  of Design) in **1886**. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN]
- Mixed early criticism: Paul Alexis called it "a false Puvis de Chavannes" but
  conceded its conviction; a *Sun* (NY) reviewer called it "monstrous" / "vulgar."
  Confidence: medium-high (quotes via Wiki-EN; treat as representative, attribute
  loosely). [Wiki-EN]

### Afterlife
- Now read as the **launch of Neo-Impressionism** and the companion/prelude to *A
  Sunday on La Grande Jatte* (1884–86, Art Institute of Chicago). The Indépendants it
  helped found became the central venue for the avant-garde for decades. Canonized
  through the 20th c.; a National Gallery centerpiece. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, NG]

### "Look closer" pointers (textual — full painting + where-to-look prose; NO coords)
1. **The boy in the water, right of center, hands cupped at his mouth** — the orange-red
   **hat** on his head carries the clearest patch of **later-added pointillist dots**
   (orange against blue), the one spot where you can see Seurat's two techniques on one
   canvas. [NG, Wiki-EN]
2. **The factory chimneys on the far bank (upper left/center horizon)** — thin
   smokestacks of **Clichy**, smoking. This is the tell that these bathers are not in
   Arcadia: it is industrial suburban leisure. [Wiki-EN, NG]
3. **The railway bridge spanning the river (background)** — the **Asnières bridge**,
   a hard horizontal of modern infrastructure behind the soft bodies. [Wiki-EN]
4. **The seated man in the foreground, in a bowler hat, fully clothed** — note that the
   most prominent figure is *dressed*, not bathing: a clerk on his day off, anchoring
   the social reading. [Wiki-EN]
5. **The reclining boy on the bank, lower left, and the orange dog (if present in repro)
   / pile of clothes** — the still, frieze-like spacing of the figures: each isolated,
   none interacting, giving the stillness people compare to Piero della Francesca and
   Puvis de Chavannes. [Wiki-EN, Britannica]
6. **The brushwork itself across the grass and water** — long, woven **balayé** strokes,
   not dabs: proof this began *before* Seurat's dot technique. [NG]

### Documented vs legend
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Salon rejection 1884 → founding of the Indépendants. Solid. [Wiki-EN]
- **[DOCUMENTED, with nuance — author must handle precisely]** Seurat added pointillist
  **dots later (c. 1886–87)** to an already-largely-finished canvas. This is the
  standard scholarly/National Gallery reading and is **very likely true**. The nuance:
  do NOT say the whole painting was "converted to pointillism" — only **localized
  retouching** (the boy's hat, some contours). Frame as "he went back in and dotted
  parts of it after inventing the technique," not "he redid it." Confidence: high on
  the fact, medium on any *exact* date. [NG, Wiki-EN]
- **[LEGEND/CAUTION]** The specific hostile-quote attributions ("a false Puvis,"
  "monstrous") are real period criticism but attribute loosely; don't build a scene on
  the exact wording. [Wiki-EN]

### Born-verified candidate images (pre-1931 → US public domain; artist d. 1891)

  London. Also `File:Seurat bathers.png`. [Commons]
- **Detail/in-chapter:** Commons category *"Versions of Bathers at Asnières by Georges

## 2. The Card Players — Paul Cézanne (id `card-players`)

> **VERSION FEATURED FOR THIS READ: the Musée d'Orsay two-player version**
> (the smallest, c. 1894–95, generally taken as the *last* of the series and the
> most refined/iconic). The other four versions are tabled below, INCLUDING the
> Qatar-owned version and its record price.

### Identity (featured = Orsay)
- **Title:** *The Card Players*. French: **Les Joueurs de cartes**. Confidence: high.
  [d'Orsay, Wiki-EN]
- **Artist:** Paul Cézanne (1839–1906). Confidence: high.
- **Date (Orsay version):** **c. 1894–95** (sources also write 1890–95 for the series
  as a whole; the Orsay two-player canvas is dated 1894–95 and read as the final one).
  Confidence: high. [d'Orsay, Wiki-EN]
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas. [d'Orsay]

### Dimensions — Orsay version (BOTH units)
- **cm:** **47.5 × 57 cm** (h × w). (d'Orsay records H. 47.0 × L. 56.5 cm; ~47.5 × 57.)
  Confidence: high. [d'Orsay, Wiki-EN]
- **in:** ~18.7 × 22.4 in.
- **ft/in for app:** **1 ft 6 1/2 in × 1 ft 10 1/2 in** (≈ 1.56 ft × 1.87 ft). It is
  the **smallest** of the five — emphasize how intimate it is. Confidence: high.

### Location + accession — Orsay version
- **Current location:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris. Confidence: high. [d'Orsay]
- **Acquisition:** Bequeathed to the State by **Count Isaac de Camondo** (collector,
  d. 1911); accepted **1911** for the national museums (entered the Louvre, later
  transferred to the Jeu de Paume / then the Musée d'Orsay on its 1986 opening).
  Confidence: high on Camondo 1911 bequest; the later transfer to Orsay is standard
  Orsay-collection history. [d'Orsay, Wiki-EN]

### Provenance — Orsay version
Confidence: medium-high. [Wiki-EN, d'Orsay]
1. Cézanne → his dealer **Ambroise Vollard** (Vollard handled most of Cézanne's late
   sales after 1895). ⚠️ Exact first-sale date not pinned here.
2. **Count Isaac de Camondo** collection, Paris.
3. **1911 bequest to the French State** (Camondo bequest) → Louvre → eventually
   Musée d'Orsay.
- **[DOCUMENTED] Theft note:** the Orsay version was **stolen in 1961** and **recovered
  after a ransom payment**. Good color, but Wikipedia is the source — attribute as
  "the Orsay canvas was stolen in 1961 and later recovered." Confidence: medium-high.
  [Wiki-EN]

### The five versions (TABLE — author features Orsay, references the rest)
Confidence: high on which museum holds which; sizes per Wiki-EN cross-check.
| Version | Players | Size (cm) | Date | Location |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Barnes | **5 figures** (3 players + standing man + seated boy) | ~134.6 × 180.3 | 1890–92 | Barnes Foundation, Philadelphia |
| Met | 4 figures | ~65.4 × 81.9 | 1890–92 | Metropolitan Museum of Art, NY |
| Courtauld | 2 players | ~60 × 73 | 1892–95 | Courtauld Gallery, London |
| **Qatar / private** | **2 players** | ~97 × 130 | 1892–93 | Private (Qatar royal family) |
| **Orsay (FEATURED)** | **2 players** | ~47.5 × 57 | 1894–95 | Musée d'Orsay, Paris |
[Wiki-EN]
- The series moves from **crowded** (Barnes, 5 figures) to **pared-down** (the
  two-player canvases) — fewer figures, more monumental stillness. The Orsay one is the
  smallest AND most distilled. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN]

### The Qatar sale (the famous price — author must handle carefully)
- **[DOCUMENTED — but the number is reported, not official]** In **2011**, **one of the
  two-player versions** (the ~97 × 130 cm canvas, dated 1892–93) was **sold by the
  estate of Greek shipping magnate George Embiricos to the royal family of Qatar**.
  Reported price **~$250 million (estimates range $250M–$320M)** — at the time the
  **highest price ever paid for a painting**, a record that stood until 2017.
  Confidence: high that the sale happened and set a record; the **exact figure is
  reported/leaked, never officially confirmed** — write "around $250 million,
  reportedly," NOT a hard number. [Vanity Fair (Feb 2012), NPR, Hyperallergic, Wiki-EN]
- **Author rule:** do NOT attach the price to the *Orsay* version. The record sale is a
  **different** canvas (the Qatar/Embiricos one). Keep them straight.

### Where it came from (the artist's situation)
- Cézanne, in his **50s, working at the family estate (Jas de Bouffan) near
  Aix-en-Provence**, took as models the **local farm laborers / estate workers**, men
  he knew, posed playing cards in plain rustic interiors. No drama, no narrative — just
  weighty, absorbed concentration. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, Britannica]
- **Models:** identified as Aix peasants/estate hands; the gardener **Père Alexandre**
  is named among them. Confidence: medium (commonly stated; specific naming attribute
  loosely). [Wiki-EN] ⚠️ Exact identity of each sitter is not firmly documented.

### The making (technique, sequence)
- Cézanne made **many preparatory studies** — over a dozen oil portraits and drawings
  of single seated players/smokers (the *Man with a Pipe* studies now in the Courtauld,
  Hermitage, Pushkin). The single-figure portraits are masterpieces in their own right.
  Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN]
- He **built the canvas in flat, modulated planes of color** ("constructive stroke"),
  making solid form out of patches rather than line/shadow — the method that fed Cubism.
  Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, Smarthistory]
- **[DOCUMENTED, nuance]** **Met conservation project** X-ray/infrared study **challenged
  the old assumption** that Cézanne worked strictly **large → small**; evidence suggests
  he may have used the **smaller versions and studies to prepare/refine the larger
  ones** — i.e., the chronology is genuinely uncertain. Author should present the
  Barnes-first / Orsay-last ordering as the **traditional reading, not settled fact**.
  Confidence: high that the order is contested. [Met conservation, Wiki-EN]

### Reception & afterlife
- Came to be seen as a cornerstone of Cézanne's late genius and a **direct ancestor of
  Cubism** (Picasso/Braque). The 2010–11 **Courtauld + Metropolitan two-venue
  exhibition** reunited the largest grouping of the series ever shown (Barnes and Qatar
  shown as reproductions due to loan restrictions). Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN]
- The Qatar sale (2011) made *The Card Players* shorthand in the press for "the world's
  most expensive painting." Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, NPR]

### "Look closer" pointers (textual; Orsay two-player version)
1. **The wine bottle standing dead-center on the table** — it splits the canvas into
   two halves and acts as the **visual axis/pivot** between the two men. The single most
   pointed-to element. [Wiki-EN]
2. **The left player — upright, dark coat, taller hat, smoking, pipe/posture formal** vs
   **the right player — lighter loose clothes, shorter hat, hunched forward** — the
   composition is a study in **balanced opposites** facing across the table. [Wiki-EN]
3. **The hands and the cards** — held low, close to the body; **no faces meet, no
   stakes shown**: Cézanne drains all anecdote out of a genre subject (tavern card
   games were usually painted as comedy/cheating scenes). The stillness IS the subject.
   [Wiki-EN, Smarthistory]
4. **The tablecloth / table edge** — built from **flat planes of color**, not modeled
   with smooth shadow; look at how solidity is made from patches. [Smarthistory]
5. **The drape/curtain behind the left man** — its folds echo the seated bodies;
   Cézanne rhymes shapes across the canvas rather than describing a real room. [Wiki-EN]
6. **The downcast eyes / total absorption of both men** — the psychological weight is
   in concentration, not action. [Britannica]

### Documented vs legend
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Five versions; Orsay = smallest, two-player, generally read as last.
  [Wiki-EN]
- **[DOCUMENTED but reported-figure]** Qatar bought a two-player version 2011 for
  ~$250M (record). Different canvas from Orsay. [Vanity Fair/NPR/Wiki-EN]
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Orsay version stolen 1961, recovered. [Wiki-EN]
- **[DISPUTED/CONTESTED]** The painting **order/chronology** of the five (big→small vs
  small→big). Present as open. [Met conservation, Wiki-EN]
- **[DOCUMENTED-LOOSE]** Models were Aix farm laborers incl. gardener Père Alexandre —
  attribute as "men from the estate, including a gardener," don't over-specify. [Wiki-EN]

### Born-verified candidate images (pre-1931 → US PD; artist d. 1906)
- **Main (Orsay):** `File:Les Joueurs de cartes, par Paul Cézanne.jpg` (Commons,
  catalogued Musée d'Orsay). [Commons]
- **For the "five versions" comparison beat:**
  `File:Les Joueurs de cartes, par Paul Cézanne, Metropolitan Museum of Art.jpg`;
  `File:Paul Cézanne, 1892-95, Les joueurs de carte (...) Courtauld Institute of Art,
  London.jpg`. [Commons]
- **Study beat:** *Man with a Pipe* single-figure studies are on Commons under the
  Card Players category — good for "he built it from portrait studies." Verify file
  before use. [Commons]

## 3. Mont Sainte-Victoire seen from Les Lauves — Paul Cézanne (id `mont-sainte-victoire-lauves`)

> **VERSION FEATURED FOR THIS READ: the Philadelphia Museum of Art version**
> (*Mont Sainte-Victoire*, 1902–04, PMA). Chosen because it is one of the most
> reproduced late Les Lauves canvases, is in a US collection, and is firmly US public
> domain. The Pushkin (Moscow) and Kunstmuseum Basel versions are tabled as siblings.

### Identity (featured = Philadelphia)
- **Title:** *Mont Sainte-Victoire* (the late group is often catalogued *Mont
  Sainte-Victoire seen from Les Lauves* / French *La Montagne Sainte-Victoire vue des
  Lauves*). Confidence: high. [PMA, Wiki-EN]
- **Artist:** Paul Cézanne (1839–1906). Confidence: high.
- **Date:** **1902–04** (the PMA records 1902–1904; the broader Les Lauves campaign
  runs 1902–06). Confidence: high. [PMA]
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas. [PMA]

### Dimensions — Philadelphia version (BOTH units)
- **cm:** **73 × 91.9 cm** (h × w). Confidence: high. [PMA via gallerix/Smarthistory]
- **in:** ~28.7 × 36.2 in.
- **ft/in for app:** **2 ft 4 3/4 in × 3 ft 1/4 in** (≈ 2.4 ft × 3.0 ft).
  Confidence: high.

### Location + accession — Philadelphia version
- **Current location:** Philadelphia Museum of Art. Confidence: high. [PMA]
- **Accession number:** **102997** (PMA object id). Confidence: high. [PMA]
- **Credit line / donor:** ⚠️ **UNVERIFIED to the museum's own wording.** Standard
  references attribute it to the **George W. Elkins Collection (1936)**, but PMA's own
  page did not render the credit line on fetch. **Author: do not state the donor as
  fact** until confirmed against philamuseum.org; safe text = "in the Philadelphia
  Museum of Art's collection." [PMA — credit line not confirmed]

### Sibling Les Lauves versions (TABLE — author features Philadelphia)
Confidence: high on holder; sizes per Wiki-EN/Nelson-Atkins/MoMA cross-check.
| Version | Size (cm) | Date | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Philadelphia (FEATURED)** | ~73 × 91.9 | 1902–04 | Philadelphia Museum of Art |
| Pushkin | ~60 × 73 | 1904–06 | Pushkin Museum, Moscow |
| Kunstmuseum Basel | ~60 × 72 | 1904–06 | Kunstmuseum Basel |
| Kunsthaus Zürich | ~63.5 × 83 | 1902–06 | Kunsthaus Zürich |
| MoMA | (late Les Lauves) | 1902–06 | Museum of Modern Art, NY |
| Metropolitan | — | late | Met, NY |
[Wiki-EN, Nelson-Atkins, MoMA]
- Cézanne painted Mont Sainte-Victoire **~11 times in oil from Les Lauves (1902–06)**,
  plus many watercolors — a single motif painted obsessively. Confidence: high.
  [Wiki-EN, Smarthistory]

### Provenance — Philadelphia version
- ⚠️ **UNVERIFIED in detail.** Late Les Lauves canvases were **left in Cézanne's studio
  at his death (1906) and dispersed afterward** (largely via Vollard and the heirs).
  The PMA canvas presumably entered via the Elkins gift (1936). Author: state only
  "left in the studio at his death and later acquired by the museum"; don't invent a
  chain. [Wiki-EN general; PMA chain not confirmed]

### Where it came from (the artist's situation)
- **1902:** Cézanne built a **studio on the hill of Les Lauves**, just north of
  Aix-en-Provence (Chemin des Lauves; he moved there in 1903). From the rise above the
  studio he had a sweeping view east across the plain to **Mont Sainte-Victoire**, the
  limestone ridge that dominates the Aix landscape. Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN,
  Smarthistory]
- The mountain had been his lifelong motif, but the **Les Lauves vantage gives the
  late, most abstract group** — the mountain seen from farther back, the whole valley
  flattening into a mosaic of color patches. Confidence: high. [Smarthistory, Wiki-EN]

### The making (technique, dates)
- Painted **1902–06** across the group; the PMA canvas **1902–04**. Confidence: high.
  [PMA]
- **Technique:** the motif is built from **overlapping, semi-transparent patches /
  planes of color** ("constructive stroke") — green and ochre for the plain, blue and
  violet for the mountain and sky — with **areas of bare canvas left showing**. Forms
  dissolve into a field of color relationships; near and far are knit into one plane.
  This is the work that read as **proto-Cubist** to the next generation. Confidence:
  high. [Smarthistory, Wiki-EN]
- **[DOCUMENTED] The "cylinder, sphere, cone" idea is from THIS period.** In a letter to
  **Émile Bernard dated 15 April 1904** Cézanne wrote (original French):
  *"Traiter la nature par le cylindre, la sphère, le cône, le tout mis en perspective,
  soit que chaque côté d'un objet, d'un plan, se dirige vers un point central."*
  Standard English: **"Treat nature by means of the cylinder, the sphere, the cone,
  with everything put in perspective, so that each side of an object or a plane is
  directed toward a central point."** The autograph letter is in the **Courtauld Gallery**
  collection. Confidence: high on attribution, date, and wording; multiple English
  translations exist (treat/deal with/interpret) — quote one and note it's a
  translation. [Courtauld letter MS-1932-SC-1-1, quotepark, arthistoryproject, MoMA
  *Letters of Cézanne*]

### Reception & afterlife
- Cézanne died largely under-recognized by the establishment but **lionized by the young
  avant-garde**. The **1907 Cézanne retrospective at the Salon d'Automne** (year after
  his death) was the revelation that hit Picasso, Braque, Matisse — the late Mont
  Sainte-Victoire canvases especially. He became "the father of modern art."
  Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, Smarthistory] ⚠️ The 1907 retrospective detail is standard
  art history; cross-check date if the author leans on it hard.
- The Les Lauves Mont Sainte-Victoires are now seen as **the bridge from Impressionism
  to Cubism / abstraction**. Confidence: high. [Smarthistory]

### Cézanne's death (the storm — author must handle as DOCUMENTED with care)
- **[DOCUMENTED]** On **15 October 1906** Cézanne was **caught in a storm while working
  outdoors**; after a couple of hours he started home, **collapsed**, and was carried
  back by a passing laundry-cart driver. He developed **pneumonia**. He went out to
  paint once more (the *Portrait of the Gardener Vallier*), fainted again, was put to
  bed, and **died on 22 October 1906**, aged 67, buried at Saint-Pierre Cemetery, Aix.
  Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, Britannica]
- **Author rule:** he was painting **outdoors near Les Lauves**, but the records say
  "working in the field / out painting," NOT specifically "painting Mont Sainte-Victoire
  at the moment he collapsed." Do **not** claim he died *at the easel painting the
  mountain* — that is the romantic exaggeration. Safe text: "caught in a storm while out
  painting, he collapsed and died of pneumonia days later." [Wiki-EN]

### "Look closer" pointers (textual; Philadelphia version)
1. **The mountain peak (upper right) against the sky** — built from **blue and violet
   patches**, its edge **dissolving into the sky** rather than drawn as a hard line;
   the mountain and the air share the same color-touches. [Smarthistory]
2. **The patches of bare/unpainted canvas** showing between strokes — Cézanne **left
   gaps**; the picture is openly unfinished-looking by design. A concrete, verifiable
   thing to point to. [Smarthistory, Wiki-EN]
3. **The plain/valley in the middle ground** — a **mosaic of small green and ochre
   blocks** standing for fields, houses, viaduct; representation is reduced to
   color-architecture. [Smarthistory]
4. **The lack of a single vanishing point** — near and far are pulled into **one shallow
   plane**; the valley tips up toward the viewer instead of receding "correctly." This
   is the proto-Cubist flatness. [Smarthistory, Wiki-EN]
5. **The horizontal banding** — foreground plain / mid-valley / mountain / sky read as
   stacked colored zones, the composition's quiet scaffolding. [Smarthistory]
6. **The repeated directional brush-strokes ("constructive stroke")** — parallel
   diagonal touches that knit unrelated objects (a roof, a tree, the slope) into one
   continuous fabric of paint. [Wiki-EN, Smarthistory]
