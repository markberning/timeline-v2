# PART A — Movement metadata

## hook (one sentence)
A handful of refugees in neutral Zurich looked at the war chewing up Europe and decided the only honest response was a urinal, a mustache on the Mona Lisa, and a name that means nothing.

## hookLong (2–4 sentences)
Dada is the moment art turned on itself. Born in Zurich in 1916, in the middle of a war feeding a whole generation into the mud, a few exiled poets and painters decided the civilization that had produced both the Mona Lisa and the machine gun no longer deserved to be taken seriously, so they made an art of nonsense, accident and provocation: sound poems with no words, poems pulled out of a hat, a mass-produced urinal signed and called sculpture. Under the clowning sat a deadly serious invention that changed art for good, the idea that the artist's choice, not the artist's hand, is what makes a thing art, and alongside it two more inventions, photomontage (the art of cutting up photographs and reassembling them into a new image) and chance, which turned out to matter as much as the jokes. It jumped from Zurich to New York, Berlin, Hannover, Cologne and finally Paris, where it burned out around 1923 and was swallowed by the movement it had hatched, Surrealism.

## artists row (~6)
1. **Tristan Tzara** (1896–1963): the ringmaster. Romanian poet at the Cabaret Voltaire; wrote the famous "Dada Manifesto 1918" (a manifesto being a public declaration of what a movement stands for, or, in Dada's case, against), ran the journal *Dada*, carried the movement to Paris in 1920, and feuded with both Huelsenbeck and Breton over who "owned" Dada.
2. **Hugo Ball** (1886–1927): a founder. German writer-director who opened the Cabaret Voltaire in 1916 alongside the poet and performer Emmy Hennings, wrote the first Dada manifesto, and performed sound poems in a cardboard costume, then quit Dada within a couple of years for Catholic mysticism.
3. **Hans (Jean) Arp** (1886–1966): the gentle one. Alsatian artist who pioneered chance collages in Zurich, worked across Zurich and Cologne Dada, and bridged into Surrealism and biomorphic sculpture.
4. **Marcel Duchamp** (1887–1968): the most consequential. Invented the readymade (*Bicycle Wheel* 1913; *Fountain* 1917; *L.H.O.O.Q.* 1919) and reframed what art could be; central to New York and Paris Dada.
5. **Francis Picabia** (1879–1953): the machine-painter. Wealthy and restless; ran the journal *391*, painted humans as absurd machines, and helped link New York, Zurich and Paris Dada.
6. **Hannah Höch** (1889–1978): the great photomontagist, and the woman the men sidelined. Berlin Dada's sharpest cut-and-paste satirist (*Cut with the Kitchen Knife*), kept out of the 1920 Dada Fair until her partner Hausmann threatened to pull his own work.

(Optional 7th if room: **Kurt Schwitters**, 1887–1948, the one-man "Merz" movement; or **Raoul Hausmann**, 1886–1971, the Berlin "Dadasoph" and *Mechanical Head*; or **Sophie Taeuber-Arp**, 1889–1943, the serious Zurich abstractionist too often left off the list.)

## parallels (2–3 "meanwhile")
1. **1914–1918 · Europe**: *The First World War.* Not background but cause: while the Western Front fed millions into the trenches, the Dadaists in neutral Zurich answered the "civilization" that built the machine gun with nonsense and ridicule.
2. **1917 on · Russia**: *Revolution and Constructivism.* In the same years Dada was clowning and raging, the Bolshevik Revolution and the rise of Constructivism (a Soviet movement, Tatlin and Rodchenko, that used hard geometric abstraction and industrial design to build a visual language for the new communist state) tried to make a brand-new machine-age art for a new society. Where Berlin Dada used photomontage to attack, the Constructivists used it to build: Dada's pure negation against Constructivism's utopian engineering.
3. **Early 1920s · Paris**: *Surrealism arrives.* Less "meanwhile" than "next": Paris Dada's split (Breton against Tzara) handed straight off to Surrealism (Breton's 1924 manifesto), which kept Dada's chance and collage but aimed them at the unconscious and the dream.

## lineage
**Parents (gave):**
- **Futurism** (Italy, 1909): gave: the manifesto as a weapon and the provocation evening, the scandal staged as art. (Reference the Futurism read, do not re-tell it.)
- **Cubism** (Picasso & Braque, 1907–14): gave: collage, the cut-and-pasted fragment that Berlin Dada turned political as photomontage. (Reference the Cubism read.)
- **The First World War and its trauma**: gave: the engine. A generation watched the most "civilized" nations butcher each other and decided reason and beauty were a fraud.
- **Duchamp's proto-readymades**: gave: *Bicycle Wheel* (1913) and *Bottle Rack* (1914) predate Zurich Dada and feed straight into its most durable idea.

**Children (took):**
- **Surrealism** (Paris, 1924): took: chance, collage, automatism and the readymade attitude, pointed at the unconscious and the dream. Dada's Paris wing simply became Surrealism.
- **Pop Art** (1950s–60s): took: the found commercial image and the deadpan appropriation, the everyday object as art.
- **Conceptual art** (1960s on): took: the readymade logic, that art is a decision, not a craft.
- **Fluxus & performance** (1960s): took: chance procedures, nonsense events, and the anti-art attitude wholesale.
- **Punk** (1970s): took: the cut-up photomontage, the DIY collage aesthetic, and the nihilist provocation. (Heartfield's graphics descend straight into the protest poster and the punk sleeve.)

## influenceSummary (one italic sentence)
*Dada took Futurism's manifesto-as-weapon and Cubism's collage, set them against a civilization that had just produced the trenches, and out of the wreckage handed forward three ideas that changed art permanently, the readymade (choosing, not making, is what makes art), photomontage (the cut-up image as a political weapon), and chance as a working method, and bred Surrealism outright.*

## whatChanged — "Why it was a break"

**heading:** Why it was a break

**before:**
- img: Leonardo, *Mona Lisa* (PD-US; the painting Dada defaced by name in *L.H.O.O.Q.*). A Cubist or Futurist canvas from the adjacent reads also works, to show that even radical modern art still believed in the picture.
- title: `Before · Leonardo, Mona Lisa (c.1503)`
- caption: "What art had always been: a thing made with skill, aimed at beauty and meaning, that earned its place by being revered. The most worshipped painting in the world. Even the wildest pre-1916 modern art still believed in the picture and the artist's hand."

**after:**
- img: Duchamp, *Fountain*, 1917, via the Alfred Stieglitz 1917 photograph (the only surviving image of the original). Alternative: Höch, *Cut with the Kitchen Knife…*, to make the "art becomes a political cut-and-paste weapon" point instead.
- title: `After · Duchamp, Fountain (1917)`
- caption: "What Dada did instead: a factory-made urinal, signed 'R. Mutt,' laid on its back and called sculpture. The skill is gone, the beauty is gone, the hand is gone. What is left is the choice, and the argument that the choice is the art."

**prose (3 paragraphs):**

1. For centuries a work of art was something an artist made with skill, a painting painted, a sculpture carved, that aimed at beauty or meaning or at least craft, and earned its place by hanging where the public took it seriously. Even the wildest modern art before 1916 still believed in all of that. Fauvism's screaming colors, Cubism's broken planes, Futurism's blurred speed (all in the reads alongside this one) were violent quarrels with how to make a picture, but they never doubted that a picture was the thing to make, by hand, and that art itself was worth the trouble.

2. Dada doubted exactly that. Look at the urinal. Duchamp bought a mass-produced piece of plumbing, signed it with a fake name, laid it on its back, called it sculpture, and argued that his choosing it was the entire artistic act. There is nothing to admire in the making, because he did not make it. There is no skill, no beauty, no subject, no meaning you are meant to feel. The only thing the artist did was point at an object and say "this." That single move, the readymade, is the most disruptive idea in modern art, and it is the whole break in one glance.

3. Three more moves stack behind it. Chance: Arp tore up paper, let the scraps fall, and glued them where they landed, handing the composition to accident instead of to the trained eye. Photomontage: in Berlin, Hannah Höch and John Heartfield cut up news photographs and reassembled them into political weapons, an art made of scissors and the daily paper. And nonsense: sound poems with no words, a name chosen because it means nothing, a mustache scribbled on the Mona Lisa. The skill, the beauty, the seriousness, even the made-by-hand part are all thrown out. What is left is the idea and the gesture, and almost everything in art that comes after is built on that.

**prose (how to write the presence and the joke):**

1. Most movements write a manifesto to explain themselves earnestly. (A manifesto is a public declaration of a movement's aims; Futurism, in the read next door, had practically invented the art-world version, and Dada took the weapon straight from it.) Dada wrote manifestos to prove that manifestos were worthless. In 1916 Hugo Ball stood up in a Zurich guildhall and announced "Dada is a new tendency in art," then cheerfully explained that the whole point was that it meant nothing: "How does one achieve eternal bliss? By saying dada."

2. Two years later Tzara wrote the most famous one of all. Partway through it he flatly declares "DADA DOES NOT MEAN ANYTHING," and elsewhere he admits that he is writing a manifesto while being against manifestos and against principles. It is a manifesto that argues itself out of existence, which is exactly the joke, and exactly the point. There is no creed to sign, no program to follow, only the refusal of creeds and programs. That is as close to a founding statement as a movement built on negation can get.

## Chapter 1 — The cabaret at the end of the world
**eyebrow: Zurich · 1916**

### Neutral ground in a burning Europe
**[SectionHeader: Zurich · 1916 | An island in the war]**

In February 1916, while the armies of Europe were settling into the second year of grinding each other to paste, two German refugees opened a nightclub in Zurich. One was the writer and theater director Hugo Ball. The other was the poet, singer and performer Emmy Hennings, a published author in her own right before any of this began and the equal co-founder of what they were about to make, not a hired act. (She tends to get filed under "Ball's partner who sang," which is the first small injustice in a story that has several.) Both had seen enough of the war's logic up close to conclude that the whole civilization producing it had gone insane. Switzerland was neutral, an island of peace in a continent at war, which is exactly why the artists, the deserters, the pacifists and the exiles had all washed up there. To Ball, the language, the art and the manners of that civilization were complicit in the slaughter. You do not answer a society that feeds its young men into machine guns by painting it a nice landscape.

So on 5 February 1916 the two of them opened the Cabaret Voltaire, a cabaret being a small nightclub with a stage, this one tucked into a back room of a tavern called the Holländische Meierei at Spiegelgasse 1. (They named it after Voltaire, the eighteenth-century French writer who had spent his life mocking the powerful, which tells you the program before the curtain even goes up.) A press notice invited "young Zurich artists of all tendencies" to come and contribute. What turned up was stranger than anyone advertised.

### The little stage
**[SectionHeader: The acts | Verses without words]**

Around the cabaret a circle gathered fast: the Romanian poet Tristan Tzara, who would become the movement's loudest mouth and its tireless impresario, the one forever organizing the evening, printing the journal, and picking the fight; the Romanian painter Marcel Janco; the Alsatian artist Hans Arp (he also went by the French "Jean"); the German writer Richard Huelsenbeck; and the Swiss artist Sophie Taeuber, later Taeuber-Arp, a serious abstract artist who was making some of the most formally rigorous work in the room. (Picture them as the early Zurich circle that formed over the spring of 1916, not as one fixed cast literally on stage opening night, the line-up was looser than the legend.)

What they did on the little stage had no precedent and was meant to have none. Poems were shouted in three languages at once, deliberately drowning each other out. There were chants and drumming borrowed from African performance, Hennings singing her cabaret songs, Janco's masks made of cardboard, jagged and totem-like, and Arp's abstract collages on the walls. And there were Ball's "sound poems," which he called "verses without words": strings of invented syllables that meant nothing and were arranged purely for their noise, language stripped of sense and used as music. If that sounds like a child banging pots, that was rather the idea. Sense, they had decided, was what had built the trenches. Nonsense was the cure.

The most famous night came in June 1916, when Ball performed his sound poem "Karawane" dressed in a costume of stiff blue cardboard tubes for legs, a huge gold cardboard collar he flapped like wings, and a tall shaman's hat. He could not walk in it. He had to be carried onto the stage and carried off again, intoning his made-up syllables like a priest of a religion that did not exist. It is the founding image of Dada: total seriousness and total absurdity wearing the same costume, and you genuinely cannot tell which one is the joke.

[FIGURE: Ball in the cardboard costume reciting "Karawane," 1916 | Hugo Ball intoning a sound poem (a poem of invented, meaningless syllables) in a costume of blue cardboard tubes and a flapping gold collar at the Cabaret Voltaire. He could not walk in it and had to be carried on and off the stage. | Hugo Ball performing at the Cabaret Voltaire, Zurich, June 1916 (historic photograph)]

Not all of Zurich Dada was shouting and costume. Off to one side, quietly, Sophie Taeuber-Arp was making the most disciplined objects in the whole circle: spare, geometric, abstract works built out of clean shapes and flat color, the opposite of the cabaret's deliberate chaos. A few years later she carved her *Dada Head*, a smooth wooden head turned on a lathe with the word "DADA" and a date painted across its face like a tiny manifesto in three dimensions. It is serene where the men were aggressive, and it is proof that the movement had real women artists at its founding, not just performing on its margins. The fact that she is most often remembered under her husband's name is the same injustice the movement kept running, one we will hit again and again.

[FIGURE: Taeuber-Arp, Dada Head, 1920 | A smooth wooden head turned on a lathe, abstract and serene, with the word "DADA" and the date painted across its face like a miniature manifesto. The most formally rigorous object Zurich Dada produced, made by one of the serious women the movement tended to file under a husband's name. | Sophie Taeuber-Arp, Tête dada (Dada Head), 1920 · Centre Pompidou, Paris. RIGHTS WATCH: 3D wooden head, the museum photograph is the rights surface; Taeuber-Arp d.1943; verify the file.]

There was a verbal version of the cabaret's nonsense too, and it became one of Dada's most quoted recipes. Tzara's instruction for making a Dada poem was, roughly: take a newspaper, cut out every word of an article, shake the words up in a bag, then draw them out one by one and copy them down in the order they come. The poem is whatever chance hands you. It is the same idea as the painter Hans Arp's torn-and-dropped paper collages (Chapter 4), but done with language: remove the author's taste and judgment from the work and let accident write it instead. Sense, again, was the enemy. Accident was the cure.

### The name that means nothing
**[SectionHeader: The name | A word born no one knows how]**

At some point that spring the group needed a name for what they were doing, and they chose the word "Dada." Where it came from is the most-disputed fact in the whole movement, and the honest thing is to tell the famous story as a legend rather than a fact. The legend goes that someone (usually Richard Huelsenbeck, who said he and Ball did it together) stabbed a paper-knife blindly into a French-German dictionary and hit "dada," which in French is a child's word for a hobby-horse. It is a wonderful story. It may even be true. But Tzara later claimed he had invented the name himself, which set off a lifelong feud between him and Huelsenbeck over who "owned" Dada, and the most reputable accounts simply shrug. Tzara's own chronicle of those months says, under June 1916, that "a word was born no one knows how."

What is not in doubt is that the word was chosen because it means nothing, or rather because its meanings are all trivial. In French it is the hobby-horse. In German it is roughly "good-bye" or "get off my back." In Romanian, "da, da" means "yes, yes." So it means a child's toy, a brush-off, and an agreement, all at once, which is to say it means nothing serious at all. That was the appeal. A movement that had decided sense was the enemy could hardly march under a sensible banner.

On 14 July 1916, Bastille Day, at the first public Dada soirée in the Zunfthaus zur Waag (an old Zurich guildhall), Ball stood up and read the first Dada manifesto, the founding statement told in full in the manifesto block above. It announced "Dada is a new tendency in art" and then gleefully explained that the whole point was that it meant nothing. A manifesto that argues itself out of existence. It would not be the last.

[MEANWHILE: Europe, 1916 | The war that built the cabaret | Twenty feet of stage in Zurich is hard to understand without the thing it was reacting to. In 1916 the war the Dadaists had fled was at its worst: the battles of Verdun and the Somme between them killed or wounded well over a million men, much of it for a few miles of churned ground. The Dadaists were not painting the war. They were refusing the entire mindset, reason, order, patriotism, beauty, that they believed had marched a generation into it. The nonsense on the cabaret stage was a moral position.]

## Chapter 2 — A urinal in New York
**eyebrow: New York · 1913–1919**

### A second Dada, before the name
**[SectionHeader: New York | Dada before Dada]**

While Zurich was inventing the cabaret, a parallel version of the same revolt was already running in New York, and it had started even earlier. Two French artists, Marcel Duchamp and Francis Picabia, had crossed the Atlantic to escape the war, and in New York they found a small avant-garde scene (the avant-garde being art's experimental front edge, the scouts out ahead of respectable taste) with two hubs: the gallery "291" run by the photographer Alfred Stieglitz, the most important showcase for modern art in America, and the apartment of the wealthy collectors Walter and Louise Arensberg, where artists, poets and hangers-on gathered most nights to argue, drink and play chess. With the American photographer Man Ray, the painter Beatrice Wood (later nicknamed the "Mama of Dada"), and the writer Henri-Pierre Roché, they made up a New York Dada that was every bit as anti-everything as Zurich's but cooler in temperature, drier, more deadpan, less drumming and shouting, more the quiet sabotage of a single well-placed object.

Picabia, in particular, painted a peculiar specialty: machines. He drew people as deadpan mechanical diagrams, a portrait of Stieglitz as a broken bellows camera, a couple as a pair of pumping engine parts, the "mechanomorphic" (man-as-machine) style. The joke was bleak and exact: in the age that had just industrialized killing, maybe a person really was just a machine, and a not very well-made one. Picabia also poured his energy into a magazine, *391*, named in homage to Stieglitz's own little journal *291*, which he kept publishing across Barcelona, New York, Zurich and Paris from 1917 to 1924, almost the exact span of Dada. The magazine was itself a Dada form: not a place to report on the art but a place to commit it, full of nonsense, machine drawings and insults. Dada's journals (*391*, the New York *Blind Man*, Tzara's Zurich *Dada*) were artworks in their own right, and they are how the scattered cities stayed one movement.

And the single well-placed object was Duchamp's specialty. Years before Zurich, he had begun making what he would eventually call "readymades," and the readymade is the single most consequential idea in modern art, so it is worth slowing down.

### The readymade explained
**[SectionHeader: The readymade | Choosing instead of making]**

In 1913 Duchamp took a bicycle wheel, bolted it upside-down onto an ordinary kitchen stool, and set the thing in his studio. He could spin the wheel idly, like a fireplace. He did not call it art at first; he called it, later, an "assisted readymade," assisted because he had at least combined two objects. The next year, 1914, he went one step further and bought a galvanized iron bottle rack, the spiky rack you stand bottles on to dry, from a Paris department store, and declared that too, untouched, exactly as the shop sold it. The *Bottle Rack* is the purer move: a single ordinary object, not altered or combined at all, simply chosen and named art. He coined the actual word "readymade" around 1915. So the dates matter, and we should be honest about them: the first readymade object is usually dated to the 1913 *Bicycle Wheel*, but Duchamp did not yet have the concept or the word when he made it. He arrived at the idea by doing it, then named what he had done.

Here is the idea, stated plainly. A readymade is an ordinary, mass-produced object that an artist declares to be a work of art simply by choosing it and presenting it as one. No carving, no painting, no skill, no making. The whole artistic act is the choice. That sounds like a shrug, and it nearly is, but follow it through: if choosing is enough, then art is not a craft you practice with your hands. It is a decision you make with your mind, and anything at all can be art the moment an artist points at it. Every Conceptual artist and most of what came after is downstream of that sentence.

### The R. Mutt case
**[SectionHeader: Fountain · 1917 | A urinal called sculpture]**

In 1917 a new exhibiting society started up in New York, the Society of Independent Artists, on the radical principle of "no jury, no prizes," meaning that unlike the old Salon system (the jury-policed official exhibitions that had ruled European art for centuries, the tradition the earlier reads in this app trace), absolutely anyone who paid the fee could exhibit anything. Duchamp was on the board. To test whether they really meant it, someone in his circle submitted a work: a standard porcelain urinal, laid on its back, signed "R. Mutt 1917," and titled *Fountain*.

The "no jury, no prizes" society panicked and hid it from view. Duchamp and the collector Walter Arensberg resigned from the board in protest. And an anonymous defense ran in *The Blind Man*, the little magazine Duchamp ran with Beatrice Wood and Henri-Pierre Roché, which laid out the entire philosophy of the readymade in two sentences: "Whether Mr. Mutt with his own hands made the fountain or not has no importance. He CHOSE it." [GATED QUOTE: source = *The Blind Man* no. 2, May 1917, "The Richard Mutt Case," anonymous; English original, no translation. Period after "Mr." per the 1917 source.] That is the argument in full. The skill is irrelevant. The choice is the art.

The name "R. Mutt," at least, Duchamp explained himself. "Mutt," he said, came from the J.L. Mott Iron Works, the plumbing-supply firm, altered slightly and crossed with the popular comic strip *Mutt and Jeff*. So the most famous prank in art history is signed with the name of a plumbing company and a newspaper cartoon.

### Who really sent it?
**[SectionHeader: The dispute | An honest unknown]**

Now the part most books skip. We do not actually know for certain who sent *Fountain* in, and there is a serious case that it was not Duchamp at all.

What is documented is solid: a porcelain urinal, signed "R. Mutt 1917," submitted to the 1917 Independents show and hidden; the original lost, surviving only in the Stieglitz photograph; Duchamp and Arensberg resigning the board. What is disputed is whether Duchamp personally submitted it, and here his own words complicate things. In a letter to his sister Suzanne dated 11 April 1917, he wrote: "One of my female friends under a masculine pseudonym, Richard Mutt, sent in a porcelain urinal as a sculpture." A female friend. A vocal scholarly minority (notably Glyn Thompson and Julian Spalding, building on Irene Gammel's biography of the Baroness) reads that letter to mean the work was the idea of the German-born artist-poet Baroness Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven, a wild performance artist before the term existed, who was central to the New York circle and entirely capable of the gesture.

The counter-case (Dawn Ades, Francis Naumann and others) reads the very same letter as evidence against any theft: Duchamp openly told his own sister that a woman had fronted the entry, which is hardly what a man covering up a stolen idea would do, and they identify the "female friend" as another associate, Louise Norton, whose address connects to the submission. Mainstream museums still credit Duchamp. The Baroness case rests on stylistic affinity and circumstantial ties.

The honest position is the one this read takes: the debate is real and unresolved, and we will not pretend to settle it. What it does cleanly is puncture the myth of the lone male genius. The most famous artwork of the century may not even have a single sure author, and that is worth sitting with.

### A mustache on the Mona Lisa
**[SectionHeader: L.H.O.O.Q. · 1919 | Defacing the masterpiece]**

If the urinal asked "what counts as art," Duchamp's next big provocation asked "what do we worship, and why." In 1919 he took a cheap printed postcard of Leonardo's *Mona Lisa*, the single most revered painting in the world, and drew a mustache and a little goatee on her face in pencil. Then he captioned it with five letters: *L.H.O.O.Q.* Read those letters aloud in French and they sound out "Elle a chaud au cul," which translates, roughly and rudely, to "she has a hot backside," meaning she is aroused. (Duchamp's own later, looser gloss was "there is fire down below.")

This is what Duchamp called a "rectified readymade," a found object (the postcard) that the artist has altered, as opposed to a plain readymade he merely chose. With a pencil and a dirty joke he had defaced the most worshipped image in Western art, and the target was not Leonardo but the worship itself: the museum cult, the reverence, the idea that a painting becomes holy because enough people agree to genuflect. Five letters and a mustache, aimed straight at the cathedral of good taste. It is, not coincidentally, the perfect "before and after" for this whole movement, the masterpiece, and then the masterpiece with a mustache.

## Chapter 3 — Berlin cuts it up
**eyebrow: Berlin · 1918–1920**

### Dada turns political
**[SectionHeader: Berlin · 1918 | From nonsense to fury]**

When Richard Huelsenbeck carried Dada from Zurich back to Berlin, it landed in a completely different country and turned into a completely different thing, not just louder but pointed at real targets. Zurich Dada had been nonsense as an end in itself, refusal made in a safe neutral bubble. Berlin in 1918 was no bubble: Germany had lost the war, the Kaiser had fled, the streets were full of demobilized soldiers and revolutionaries (the Communist Spartacist uprising was crushed and its leaders murdered that same winter), people were genuinely starving, and a fragile new republic (later called the Weimar Republic, after the town where its constitution was drafted) was being born in chaos. Dada here could not afford to be a private joke. The Berliners thought pure Zurich nonsense was a luxury they could not afford; they wanted the same anti-everything energy aimed at something. So it got savagely political, pointed squarely at the militarists who had run the war and the new establishment trying to paper over the wreckage.

The Berlin circle (it formally constituted itself as "Club Dada" in early 1918) was a who's-who: Raoul Hausmann, who styled himself the "Dadasoph" (the Dada-philosopher); Hannah Höch; John Heartfield, who had anglicized his German name, Helmut Herzfeld, as a protest against the wartime German nationalism that taught children to hate the English, and who would later become the great anti-Nazi photomontagist; the savage caricaturist George Grosz; the self-declared "Oberdada" Johannes Baader; and the publisher Wieland Herzfelde. Their politics ran hard left, toward the new Communist movement, and their art was a weapon.

### Photomontage, the weapon
**[SectionHeader: The technique | Scissors and the daily paper]**

That weapon was photomontage. The word needs unpacking for a reader meeting it cold. A collage (a technique Cubism had developed a few years earlier, see the Cubism read) is any picture assembled by gluing found materials onto a surface, scraps of paper, fabric, newsprint. Photomontage is the special case where the found materials are photographs, cut out of newspapers and magazines and reassembled into a new image, usually a jarring, satirical one: a general's head on a baby's body, a machine where a brain should be, faces sliced and recombined. Berlin Dada used scissors and the daily paper to make pictures that attacked the people in the daily paper.

Here we have to hedge, because Dada loved to claim firsts it could not prove. Both Grosz and Heartfield, and separately Hausmann and Höch, claimed to have invented photomontage. (Höch and Hausmann said the idea came to them when they spotted a framed commercial photomontage, a soldier's portrait pasted into a generic barracks scene, on a 1918 holiday by the Baltic.) But the cut-and-paste of photographs was not new at all: commercial and Victorian "combination printing" went back to the 1850s. So nobody in Dada "invented" photomontage. What Berlin Dada actually did, and this is the real innovation, was seize the technique and turn it into a political weapon, an art made from the same mass media it was attacking.

Hausmann made the same indictment in three dimensions. His *Mechanical Head*, subtitled *The Spirit of Our Time*, is a wig-maker's wooden dummy head with objects screwed and clamped to it: a folding ruler, a tape measure curled over one ear, a tin cup, a small wooden box, the inner workings of a pocket watch, a printer's measuring tool. The head has no thoughts of its own. Its only contents are the measuring and counting instruments bolted to the outside of it. That, Hausmann is saying, is what the war-machine society has produced: a person who is nothing but the gadgets done to him, a head whose entire mind is administration. It is funny and it is a little horrifying, which is the Berlin Dada register exactly.

### Cut with the kitchen knife
**[SectionHeader: Höch · 1919–20 | The whole republic, cut up]**

The masterpiece of the form has one of the longest titles in art: *Cut with the Kitchen Knife Dada through the Last Weimar Beer-Belly Cultural Epoch of Germany*, made by Hannah Höch in 1919–20. The title is itself a sneer: the "Weimar Beer-Belly cultural epoch" is the comfortable, complacent respectability of the new republic, the well-fed politicians and generals who had survived the war and the revolution and now ran things as if nothing had happened. Höch proposes to take a kitchen knife to all of it. Stand in front of the work (it is large, nearly four feet tall) and it is overwhelming on purpose: a teeming, churning field of hundreds of cut-out fragments from the illustrated press, generals' heads and dancers' legs and machine cogs and crowds and bits of text, the whole new German republic shredded and reassembled. Höch literally split the composition into camps, scattering the words "die grosse Welt dada" (the great dada world) and "anti-dada" across it, sorting the figures of Weimar Germany into those she counts as Dada (the radicals, the new) and those she does not (the old military and political order).

And down in one corner, easy to miss, she pasted a little map showing the European countries where women had won the right to vote, a quiet flag planted by one of the very few women the movement let near its center. It is no accident that the work which most thoroughly cuts up the men's world of generals and politicians is the one that smuggles the women in. There is a small irony in the raw material, too: Höch had a day job at the Ullstein publishing house, designing knitting and embroidery patterns for women's magazines, and Ullstein was the very firm that printed the illustrated papers she was now cutting to ribbons. The woman the men barely tolerated had her hands on the movement's signature raw material because she went to the office every morning.

### The Dada Fair, and the women shoved aside
**[SectionHeader: 1920 | The Fair, and the sandwiches]**

In the summer of 1920 the Berlin group staged its great spectacle, the First International Dada Fair, at Dr. Otto Burchard's gallery from late June to late August: around two hundred works, slogans on every wall, and hanging from the ceiling a stuffed dummy in a German officer's uniform with a papier-mache pig's head, the "Prussian Archangel" (made by Heartfield and Rudolf Schlichter), a gesture so contemptuous of the military that it later drew prosecution. It was provocation as a total environment.

But the Fair is also where one of Dada's ugliest truths shows clearly. Hannah Höch, who made the work that became the emblem of the whole movement, was nearly left out of the Dada Fair entirely. Grosz and Heartfield wanted her excluded; she got in only after Raoul Hausmann, her partner, threatened to withdraw his own work if she was kept out. And the contempt was not subtle. Years later one of the Dadaists, Hans Richter, summed up Höch's contribution to the group as "the sandwiches, beer and coffee she managed somehow to conjure up despite the shortage of money." The line was both contemptuous and false: Höch held a paying day job at the Ullstein publishing house and was a working artist the whole time, not the group's hostess. She wrote that the men "continued, into their old age, to look on us women artists as charming and gifted amateurs, denying us any real professional status."

She was not alone in being written out. In Zurich, Sophie Taeuber-Arp was a serious abstract artist usually filed under her husband's name; Emmy Hennings co-founded the entire Cabaret Voltaire and is remembered mostly as a singer; and in New York the Baroness Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven turned her own body and apartment into art (she wore tin-can bras and stamps on her face and made assemblages of found junk years before the men got around to it), a performance artist before the word existed, who is now best remembered for the urinal called *Fountain*, which may be hers and is credited to a man (Chapter 2). The movement that wanted to tear up every hierarchy kept one of them carefully intact. Name them, because their own colleagues mostly did not.

## Chapter 4 — Trash and one-man movements
**eyebrow: Hannover & Cologne · 1919–1921**

### The man Dada blackballed
**[SectionHeader: Hannover | Schwitters and Merz]**

Dada had no headquarters and no membership card, and nowhere is that clearer than in the smaller German cities, where it splintered into private versions that barely resembled each other. In Hannover one man, locked out of the official club, built a whole movement by himself out of garbage. In Cologne a tiny cell ran the most aggressive show of the entire movement and pointed straight at what came next. And running between them was the quiet Alsatian who handed art its third great Dada idea by dropping paper on the floor. Three small stories, one point: by now Dada was less a thing than a way of refusing, and it could be done alone, in any town, out of anything.

Not everyone got to be a Dadaist. Kurt Schwitters, working in the city of Hannover, wanted into Berlin Dada and was blackballed by Huelsenbeck. The reason, as far as it is documented, is revealing: the Berlin group thought Schwitters' art was too pretty, too crafted, too tangled up with Expressionism and the gentler avant-garde gallery Der Sturm, in short too aesthetic and too bourgeois to count as Dada. (Other accounts add plain rivalry to the mix.) So Schwitters did the most Dada thing imaginable: rejected for caring about beauty, he founded his own one-man movement, made it out of garbage, and called it "Merz." The name itself is pure Dada accident. It is a meaningless fragment, the middle of the words "Commerz und Privatbank" (a German bank's name), which had turned up on a scrap of paper in one of his collages. He liked the sound, cut it loose, and named his entire life's work after a piece of a bank's signage that happened to land in a picture.

What Merz meant in practice was art made from literal garbage. Where Berlin Dada cut up the glossy printed press, Schwitters worked from what he found in the street and the gutter: tram tickets, candy wrappers, bits of wood and wire and cloth, newspaper, broken junk. He glued and nailed and built these into collages and assemblages (an assemblage being a collage that comes off the wall into three dimensions, made of bulkier found objects). His 1921 *Cherry Picture* is a good specimen: a surface of pasted trash with a child's flashcard of cherries stuck on, a real broken pipe poking out, and the penciled, deliberately ungrammatical line "Ich liebe dir!" (a wrong-cased "I love you"). It is tender and ridiculous at once, beauty assembled out of what everyone else had thrown away.

### The house that ate itself
**[SectionHeader: The Merzbau | A cathedral of trash]**

Schwitters' most ambitious work no longer exists, and the only reason we know it is photographs. Starting in the mid-1920s he began building the Merzbau (Merz Building) inside his own house in Hannover: an architectural sculpture of white plaster grottoes, columns and niches that he never stopped adding to. Each cavity was a shrine to a friend or an idea, sometimes containing a small stolen relic of the person it honored. Over years it grew, broke through walls and the ceiling, climbed into the rooms above, ate through the building. It was a single sculpture you could walk inside, an entire house slowly being converted into art.

It was destroyed in an Allied air raid on Hannover in October 1943. All that survives are a few photographs (notably Wilhelm Redemann's from 1933) and later reconstructions. The most extraordinary Dada object became, fittingly for a movement born out of the First World War, a casualty of the Second.

### Cologne, the toilet and the axe
**[SectionHeader: Cologne · 1920 | Enter past the urinal]**

A short train ride away, in Cologne, another cell ran the rawest Dada show of all. Max Ernst, Hans Arp (over from Zurich) and Johannes Theodor Baargeld staged an exhibition in April 1920 called "Dada-Vorfrühling" (Dada Early Spring) in the courtyard of a pub. The documented core of it is astonishing on its own. Visitors had to enter past the men's urinal, where a girl in a child's communion dress recited lewd verse. One of Ernst's sculptures was displayed with a hatchet attached, an open invitation for the public to smash it. The police shut the show down for obscenity, then dropped the charges when the supposedly obscene image turned out to be a print of Albrecht Dürer's *Adam and Eve* from 1504, four hundred years old. The show reopened in triumph. (The exact staging details get embroidered in the retelling, so hold the trimmings loosely; the core is solid.)

Ernst's own work here pointed somewhere new. His 1920 collage *The Hat Makes the Man* stacks rows of catalogue-cut hats into wobbly, vaguely human, faintly absurd columns, captioned with its own punning title. It is funny, but it is also dreamlike in a way the cooler Dada objects were not, an early sign of where Ernst was heading.

Cologne Dada collage already tilting toward the dream logic of Surrealism. | Max Ernst, The Hat Makes the Man (C'est le chapeau qui fait l'homme), 1920 · The Museum of Modern Art, New York. ### Letting the scraps fall where they may
**[SectionHeader: Arp · chance | Handing the picture to accident]**

Running through Zurich and Cologne both was Hans Arp, and Arp's great contribution was the third durable Dada idea after the readymade and photomontage: chance. The story is that Arp, frustrated with a drawing, tore it up and let the pieces drop to the floor, then noticed that the way they fell pleased him more than anything he had arranged on purpose, so he glued them down where they had landed.

Look at the result, his 1916–17 *Collage with Squares Arranged according to the Laws of Chance*. It is small and quiet: a field of pale, soft-edged squares of torn paper, a few darker, scattered across a lighter ground in no pattern you can name, none of them lined up, none of them quite straight, like confetti caught at the instant it settles. There is no composition in the old sense, no focal point, no arrangement by a trained eye. The picture is a snapshot of where gravity left the scraps.

[FIGURE: Arp, Collage with Squares Arranged according to the Laws of Chance, 1916–17 | A field of pale, soft-edged squares of torn paper scattered across a lighter ground, none aligned, none straight, glued where they fell. The founding object of "chance" as an art method (though the orderly result hints Arp kept a hand in). | Hans (Jean) Arp, Untitled (Collage with Squares Arranged according to the Laws of Chance), 1916–17 · The Museum of Modern Art, New York. RIGHTS WATCH: Arp d.1966, verify the file (MoMA object 37013, not 37166).]

Handing the picture to accident sounds like the least amount of effort possible, and that is half the point: if the artist's skilled judgment had been the thing that made art, then deliberately removing it was the perfect Dada sabotage. But here is an honest caveat worth keeping. The actual collage looks rather tidy and balanced, which strongly suggests Arp did not fully surrender control, that he nudged, selected, and arranged at least a little. Even Dada's purest surrender to randomness had a human thumb on the scale. Chance as a working method, though, was now loose in art, and it would run straight into Surrealist automatism, John Cage's music and Fluxus decades later.

So by 1921 Dada had no single style and barely a single city. It was a readymade in New York, a political photomontage in Berlin, a cathedral of trash in Hannover, an axe in a Cologne courtyard, and a pile of dropped paper in Zurich. It was less a movement than a shared refusal, wearing a different costume in every town. Which made it very hard to kill, and, as it turned out, very hard to hold together.

## Chapter 5 — Paris, and the family feud
**eyebrow: Paris · 1920–1923**

### Tzara comes to Paris
**[SectionHeader: Paris · 1920 | The ringmaster arrives]**

The last great Dada chapter, and the one that ended it, was Paris. In 1920 Tristan Tzara moved there (Picabia was already on the scene) and was welcomed like a prophet by a group of young French writers gathered around a journal called *Littérature*: André Breton, the most serious-minded and imperious of them, a born organizer who wanted a cause to lead; Louis Aragon; Philippe Soupault; and the poet Paul Éluard in the circle. These were brilliant, ambitious, restless young men who had read Tzara's manifestos and wanted the genuine article, and for a couple of years Paris Dada was the loudest avant-garde act in the city.

It was almost pure provocation, much of it staged as theater and meant to detonate the audience. Nonsense plays, deliberately tedious or insulting performances, evenings designed to start fights. Man Ray, who had crossed over from New York, fit right in. On the afternoon of his first Paris show he built an object he called *Gift*: he took a flatiron, the heavy clothes-iron you heated on a stove, and glued a single neat row of fourteen pointed tacks down the middle of its smooth sole. Stop and picture using it. The one surface whose entire job is to be flat and harmless now bristles with spikes; the moment you set it to a shirt, it shreds the shirt. A domestic tool turned, with one row of tacks, into something useless and faintly menacing, a small machine for ruining things. Pure Dada in a single found object, and the title is the joke twisting the knife: he made it as a gift.

### The trial of a living man
**[SectionHeader: 1921 | Dada in the dock]**

The strangest event of Paris Dada was a mock trial. In May 1921 the group staged the "Trial of Maurice Barrès," putting a real, living, famous French writer (a celebrated nationalist) on trial, in absentia, for crimes against the security of the spirit, with Breton presiding as a black-robed judge, other Dadaists as prosecution, defense and witnesses, and a dummy standing in for the accused. It was theater, satire and genuine ideological combat all at once, and it ended, as Dada events tended to, in chaos in the audience.

But look closely at who was running it. A solemn judge, a structured trial, a real political target, a verdict to be reached: this was Dada being organized, serious, even moralistic, and that was Breton's instinct, not Tzara's. The trial that was supposed to mock authority had quietly built a little authority of its own, and that small crack would split the movement open.

### The break
**[SectionHeader: 1922–23 | Two men who could not share a room]**

The split was, at bottom, a disagreement about what Dada was for. Breton wanted to turn it into a serious instrument, an organized force pointed at remaking art and the mind. Tzara wanted it to stay exactly what it had always been: pure negation, pure nonsense, an attack with no program and no future, because a program and a future were precisely the bourgeois things Dada existed to refuse. To Tzara, a Dada with goals was a contradiction in terms, the moment you had a goal you had stopped being Dada and started being just another art movement with a committee. To Breton, a Dada with no goals was a machine that could only ever smash, never build, and he was tired of smashing. Picabia, characteristically, picked a fight with everyone and drifted away from both. The personal and the philosophical were tangled together: these had been friends, and the friendship was curdling into contempt.

In 1922 Breton tried to organize a grand "Congress of Paris" to federate the whole modern spirit under one roof and decide, in an orderly way, where art should go next. It was a thoroughly un-Dada idea (Dada existed precisely to refuse being federated or decided), and Tzara refused to take part. Breton attacked him in print, in language that questioned whether Tzara had even invented Dada at all, reviving the old Zurich feud. The friendship became open warfare. It came to blows the next year. In July 1923, at a Paris soirée built around a performance of Tzara's own play *The Gas Heart*, Breton and his allies stormed the stage mid-show, swinging a cane, and a brawl broke out that reportedly left one performer with a broken arm before Tzara called the police on his former comrades. You cannot run a movement that ends in its leaders calling the police on each other. The break was total. Dada in Paris, and effectively everywhere, was finished by 1923, killed less by the bourgeois world it had attacked than by its own internal argument about whether nonsense could be put to serious use.

## Chapter 6 — What Dada left
**eyebrow: The afterlife · 1924 on**

### Surrealism takes the inheritance
**[SectionHeader: 1924 | The direct heir]**

Dada did not so much die as get reorganized by the man who had broken it. Having driven Tzara out, André Breton took Dada's best tools, its love of chance, its collage, its appetite for provocation, and pointed them somewhere new. Instead of pure negation, he aimed them at the unconscious mind and the dream, the hidden interior that the new psychology said drove us all. In 1924 he published the Surrealist Manifesto and Surrealism was born, a movement that kept Dada's methods but gave them a purpose Dada had refused to have. The Paris Dada circle simply walked across the bridge: Ernst, Arp, Man Ray, Éluard, Aragon, Breton himself all became Surrealists. Ernst's strange Cologne pictures had been pointing this way all along. Look at his 1921 *Celebes*. A vast, rounded, dark body fills the middle of the canvas, built like a cast-iron boiler or a grain silo on legs, with a ribbed metal tube curling up from it like a trunk that ends in a blunt mechanical head. A small headless mannequin stands beside it with one arm raised. Behind, an empty greenish sky and a bare plain. It is part machine, part beast, part nightmare, and it makes no rational sense at all, which is exactly the point: this is Dada's nonsense turning into the Surrealist dream, and the picture is often called the first masterpiece of Surrealist painting. Dada had hatched the movement that ate it.

### The three things that outlived the joke
**[SectionHeader: The legacy | A prank that rewired art]**

Strip away the clowning and three of Dada's inventions ran forward and changed everything downstream. The readymade, Duchamp's claim that choosing can be art, became the foundation of Conceptual art (where the idea matters more than the made object) and of Pop Art (which took the found commercial image and the deadpan appropriation), and is, in a real sense, the seed of a great deal of the art made after 1950. Photomontage, the political cut-and-paste, found its full power in the 1930s when John Heartfield (Berlin Dada's own) used it for savage anti-Nazi covers on the mass-circulation left-wing illustrated weekly *AIZ* (the Workers' Illustrated Paper), which at its peak reached hundreds of thousands of readers, the very same cut-up-the-daily-press weapon turned on fascism in the daily press itself, and from there the line runs straight to the protest poster and the punk record sleeve. And chance, the willingness to hand decisions to accident, fed Surrealist automatism (the practice of drawing or writing without conscious control, to let the unconscious speak), the music of John Cage, the events of Fluxus (a 1960s movement of deadpan art-happenings) and the whole idea of art as a procedure rather than a craft. The nonsense events at the Cabaret Voltaire are the direct ancestors of performance art and the happening.

There is a clean way to measure how far the joke traveled. In 2004 a poll of five hundred art-world professionals (commissioned by Gordon's, then the sponsor of the Turner Prize) was asked to name the single most influential artwork of the twentieth century. They did not choose a Picasso. They chose Duchamp's urinal. A factory-made piece of plumbing, signed with a cartoon character's name, submitted to a show that hid it, surviving only in a photograph and a debate over who even sent it, was judged the most influential object of its century.

### Holding both halves
**[SectionHeader: The verdict | The gag and the earthquake]**

It is tempting to tidy Dada up into one thing or the other, to call it either a silly prank or a profound revolution, but the truth is that it was emphatically both, and the refusal to choose is the point. The Dadaists genuinely meant the horse-laugh. They genuinely believed that after the trenches, reason and beauty and seriousness were a fraud, and that the only honest art was an art that attacked art. The sound poems, the urinal, the mustache, the dropped paper, the axe in the courtyard were sincere acts of demolition, and for a few years it really did feel like the end of art.

And it really was the seedbed of everything that came after. Hold both halves at once, the prank that felt like an ending and the idea that turned out to be a beginning, because that double nature is exactly what Dada built and exactly what it left behind. A movement whose name means nothing turned out to mean almost everything.
