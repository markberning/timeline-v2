# Framing / Fairness Gate — NRO Era (Neoclassical & Romantic, c.1750–1850)

Critic role: FRAMING / FAIRNESS (gate #5 in the art pipeline).
Scope: distortion only — contested-memory failure modes. Not a rewrite-for-taste pass.
Drafts audited: nro-era-narrative.tsx.txt, nro-neoclassicism-narrative.tsx.txt,
nro-romanticism-narrative.tsx.txt, and their matching factpacks.

Hunt axes:
1. Romantic-genius myth (lone male titan; erased collaborators / studio / patrons)
2. Erasure of women (Kauffman, Vigée Le Brun)
3. Colonial / race question (Orientalism; Raft of the Medusa apex figure; Slave Ship / Zong)
4. Eurocentric overclaim (stated as universal, not Western-scoped)
5. Political framing (David / the Terror; Goya under the Occupation)

## SECTION 1: ERA READ ("Lay of the Land")

**Overall verdict: TILTED**

Three axes carry a distortion risk; none is egregious, but together they tip the era
framing in a way that the movement reads partially correct but do not fully fix.

---

### Finding 1.1 — Women entirely absent from era-level framing
**Grade: SHOULD-FIX | Axis: Erasure of women**

Not one female painter appears anywhere in the era read — not in the five numbered
sections, not in the MeanwhileSheet, not in the blurbs. The era read is the reader's
first encounter with this hundred years of European painting. Framing it as a world of
Fragonard, David, Goya, Géricault, Delacroix, Turner, Friedrich, Ingres, and Courbet
alone sets a baseline that is already skewed before the movement reads try to correct it.

Kauffman is present in the Neoclassicism MeanwhileSheet (finding 2.1 below) but absent
here. Vigée Le Brun is absent from the entire body of work (finding 2.2 below).

The fix does not require a separate section; one sentence in "The first answer · reason"
is enough: e.g., "The movement's reach went further than France — Angelica Kauffman and
Elisabeth Vigée Le Brun were two of its most celebrated practitioners, one a founding
member of Britain's Royal Academy, the other the most sought-after portrait painter in
pre-Revolutionary France." Both claims are documented fact; no new fact-check needed
before adding.

---

### Finding 1.2 — Turner's Slave Ship: atrocity named but humanity not
**Grade: SHOULD-FIX | Axis: Colonial / race question**

Exact lines (era read, "The sublime, out of doors"):
> "J.M.W. Turner's The Slave Ship (1840) burns a whole sky and sea orange and blood-red
> over a slaver that has thrown the dead and dying overboard ahead of a coming typhoon,
> the atrocity half-dissolved into pure turbulent light."

The Romanticism narrative (finding 3.2 below) does this correctly: it names the Zong
massacre, names the 132 enslaved Africans, names the insurance-fraud motive, and names
the 1840 abolition convention. The era read reduces all of that to "the dead and dying"
and "the atrocity" — vague enough that a reader learns only that something bad happened
at sea, not what it was. The era read is the first and for many readers only point of
contact with this painting. The stripped-down treatment here is in tension with the
fuller, fairer treatment in the Romanticism movement read.

Fix: add one clause — "a slaver that has thrown enslaved Africans overboard to claim
them as insurable cargo" — before "the atrocity." All facts are verified in the
Romanticism factpack §8. No new fact-check needed.

---

### Finding 1.3 — Géricault's Raft: racial dimension not raised at era altitude
**Grade: SHOULD-FIX | Axis: Colonial / race question**

Exact lines (era read, "The second answer · feeling"):
> "Théodore Géricault heaped the survivors of a real, recent shipwreck onto The Raft
> of the Medusa, a canvas the size of a wall, the dead and dying piled into a straining
> pyramid reaching toward a speck of a rescue ship on the horizon."

The painting's compositional apex — the highest point of the pyramid, the figure
waving at the horizon — is a Black man. This is a documented, visible fact of the
canvas, and the placement was deliberate: at the moment Géricault painted it, France
had re-established colonial slavery in its Caribbean territories (Napoleon's law of
1802), and the frigate ran aground off the coast of Senegal. Placing a Black figure at
the compositional summit — the most active, most hopeful, most prominent position in the
painting — is a politically significant choice that scholarship since the 1980s has
discussed at length.

Neither the era read nor the Romanticism narrative (chapter 3, "Géricault and the raft")
names this. The factpack for Romanticism also does not flag it as a framing axis (a
gap in the factpack itself). The Romanticism narrative describes the apex figure only as
"a man hoisted on his fellows' shoulders, waving a rag at a ship."

This is the one finding in the era read where the factpack itself is missing the
framing axis. ⚠️ New material: the VISUAL FACT (the figure is Black; the placement is
at the apex) can be stated without additional fact-checking — it is visible and
uncontested. The POLITICAL INTERPRETATION (anti-slavery reading) requires hedging
("scholars have read…") and should be flagged for fact-check verification before any
interpretive claim is added to prose. A one-sentence fix at the era level: "…the dead
and dying piled into a straining pyramid, a Black figure at its apex reaching toward a
speck of a rescue ship" is sufficient and requires no new fact-check.

### Finding 1.4 — Eurocentric overclaim: minor, contained
**Grade: FAIR | Axis: Eurocentric overclaim**

"Is the truest art the one that orders the world by reason, or the one that tells the
truth of feeling?" is stated as THE question the whole modern age inherits. Given that
the app's scope is explicitly Western, this is within bounds. The text consistently says
"European painting," which keeps the scope honest. No fix needed.

### Finding 1.5 — David's politics: handled well
**Grade: FAIR | Axis: Political framing**

Exact lines (era read, "David"):
> "He was elected a deputy to the National Convention, voted for the death of King Louis
> XVI and signed the warrant (a regicide), sat on a committee that signed arrest orders,

This is precise, fair, and neither hagiographic nor cheap-villain. The exile is named

## SECTION 2: NEOCLASSICISM MOVEMENT READ

**Overall verdict: TILTED — approaching DISTORTED on the women axis**

The political and factual framing of David and Ingres is strong. The era's female
painters are badly handled: Kauffman is reduced to a sidebar sentence in direct
contradiction of the factpack's own instruction; Vigée Le Brun is absent from the
entire read.

---

### Finding 2.1 — Angelica Kauffman: relegated to sidebar against factpack instruction
**Grade: MUST-FIX | Axis: Erasure of women / Romantic-genius myth**

The factpack Neoclassicism §10 (Honesty) says explicitly:
> "Women + scope: name Angelica Kauffman as a major Neoclassical history painter and
> a Royal Academy FOUNDER (not a footnote)."

The narrative puts Kauffman in a single sentence in the MeanwhileSheet at the end of
chapter 4 (the Ingres chapter):
> "Angelica Kauffman, one of only two women among the founders of the Royal Academy in
> 1768, painted classical history at the highest level."

A MeanwhileSheet is a sidebar. The factpack said "not a footnote." A sidebar is a
footnote with a background color. The move contradicts the factpack's own instruction
on framing, which is the canonical source for this read.

Kauffman's actual standing: she was one of only two women among the 36 founding members
of the Royal Academy (1768), a working history painter in Rome, London, and Vienna who
operated at the highest prestige tier the movement recognized. She studied in Rome the
same decade as David. Treating her as a MeanwhileSheet aside and giving David three
full chapters plus Ingres a full chapter is a structural fairness failure.

Fix: move Kauffman into chapter 1 body text ("A scholar's creed and a buried city"),
where Rome's international workshop community is already discussed. The MeanwhileSheet
at the end of chapter 1 already names her ("Angelica Kauffman and Benjamin West all
passed through the same ancient streets") — upgrade that to body prose and expand to
one paragraph naming her as a history painter, not just a name in a list. No new
fact-check needed (the factpack §6 has her bio verified).

---

### Finding 2.2 — Elisabeth Vigée Le Brun: entirely absent
**Grade: MUST-FIX | Axis: Erasure of women**

Elisabeth Louise Vigée Le Brun (1755–1842) is not named anywhere in the Neoclassicism
narrative or its factpack. She was:
- Marie-Antoinette's primary portrait painter (roughly 30 portraits of the queen)
- Admitted to the Académie Royale in 1783 (unusual; opposed at the time for being
  "merely" a portraitist and a woman)
- Fled France at the Revolution in 1789
- Painted portraits across Europe (Vienna, London, St. Petersburg, Naples) for decades
- Returned to France and continued working through the Empire and Restoration

Her absence from a read about Neoclassical painting is an omission. She worked in a
genre (portraiture) distinct from David's history painting, but she is a major figure in
the same era and institutional world — admitted to the same academy David dominated.

⚠️ New material: the factpack does not include her. Adding her requires a fact-check
pass on:
- Her Academy admission date and the controversy surrounding it (1783, yes —
  documented; the king had to intervene to force the admission)
- Her relationship to the Neoclassical style vs Rococo portraiture (she bridges both)
- Appropriate scope placement (portraiture vs history painting)

Flag for the coordinator: add Vigée Le Brun to the factpack §6 artists row before the
fix is written into the narrative.

### Finding 2.3 — Ingres / Grande Odalisque: Orientalist projection not named
**Grade: SHOULD-FIX | Axis: Colonial / race question**

Exact line (chapter 4, "Grande Odalisque"):
> "His Grande Odalisque (1814) is a reclining harem nude (an odalisque is a woman of a
> sultan's household) whose cool back is drawn impossibly, sinuously long…"

And from the narrative draft at the same point:
> "a favorite excuse for the period's exotic nudes"

"A favorite excuse" is dismissive shorthand for what is a documented and contested
dimension of the painting: the Orientalist fantasy. Ingres never visited the Ottoman
world. The harem, the "odalisque," the Turkish accessories (hookah, peacock fan,
incense burner) are props assembled from European imagination. The woman depicted is
not an observed sitter but a Western projection of "the East."

The Romanticism narrative correctly names Orientalism when it reaches Delacroix's
Morocco works (chapter 4, "Morocco · 1832"). Ingres's odalisques are arguably the
earliest and most canonical example of the same tendency in French painting, and the
Neoclassicism narrative does not connect them to it.

Fix: replace "a favorite excuse for the period's exotic nudes" with something
like: "a Western fantasy of an Ottoman harem Ingres never saw; the woman, her
accessories, and her setting are assembled from European imagination, an early instance
of the Orientalist projection that would run through the era's French painting." No new
fact-check required (the Delacroix/Orientalism treatment in the Romanticism factpack
§8 and the Women of Algiers precedent are sufficient).

### Finding 2.5 — Romantic-genius myth: partial concern, acceptable

## SECTION 3: ROMANTICISM MOVEMENT READ

**Overall verdict: FAIR with one MUST-FIX**

The two most sensitive axes — Turner's Slave Ship and Delacroix's Orientalism — are
handled well. One significant factual/framing omission remains: the racial dimension of
the Raft of the Medusa's composition.

---

### Finding 3.1 — Géricault's Raft: apex figure's identity not named
**Grade: MUST-FIX | Axis: Colonial / race question**

Exact lines (chapter 3, "The raft"):
> "Géricault piles the figures into a pyramid that climbs from the dead slumping off
> the front of the raft, up through the despairing and the dying, to a single straining
> peak: a man hoisted on his fellows' shoulders, waving a rag at a ship so tiny on the
> horizon you have to look twice to find it."

The man at the compositional apex — the highest point of the pyramid, the most hopeful
and most visually prominent position in the painting — is Black. This is a visible,
documented, uncontested fact of the canvas. Géricault's deliberate placement of a Black
figure at the summit of a history-painting composition was noted by contemporaries and
has been a subject of sustained scholarly analysis.

The political context is significant: France had restored colonial slavery in its
Caribbean territories in 1802 (six years before the Méduse sailed). The frigate ran
aground off the coast of Senegal. Géricault researched the wreck obsessively. The
compositional choice to elevate a Black figure to the apex, in that context, in that
decade, was not neutral.

The narrative currently discusses the research process (survivors interviewed, morgue
visits, limbs in the studio) but says nothing about the painting's racial dimension.
This is the one place in the Romanticism draft where a politically significant fact is
simply not on the page.

⚠️ New material levels:
- LEVEL 1 (visual fact, no new fact-check needed): "a Black man hoisted on his fellows'
  shoulders, waving a rag at a ship" — this simply names what is visible in the
  painting. Can be added without fact-checking.
- LEVEL 2 (contextual fact, verify before adding): France's 1802 restoration of slavery
  and the wreck's location off Senegal. Both are verified in the factpack (the Romanticism
  factpack does say "off the African coast" and Wikipedia/Britannica sources are cited).
  Verify the 1802 law before writing the connection in prose.
- LEVEL 3 (interpretive claim — flag for fact-check): the anti-slavery / political
  reading of the apex placement. Scholars who have written on this include Albert Boime
  (*The Art of Exclusion*, 1990) and Simon Lee. If this reading is cited, it should be
  attributed to scholarship, not asserted as Géricault's stated intent.

Minimum fix (Level 1 only, no new fact-check): change "a man hoisted on his fellows'
shoulders" to "a Black man hoisted on his fellows' shoulders." That one word is a MUST,
is simply true, and requires nothing beyond what any viewer can see in the painting.

### Finding 3.2 — Turner's Slave Ship: well handled
**Grade: FAIR | Axis: Colonial / race question**

Exact lines (chapter 5, "The Slave Ship"):
> "Down in the water, half-swallowed by the spray, are shackled human legs and reaching
> hands, with fish gathering. Turner's full title names the subject: Slavers Throwing
> overboard the Dead and Dying. The picture is built on a real and specific horror, the
> 1781 massacre aboard a British slave ship called the Zong, whose crew threw roughly
> 132 enslaved Africans overboard alive so the owners could claim insurance on them as
> 'cargo' lost at sea. Turner exhibited it in 1840, the year the World Anti-Slavery
> Convention met in London…"

The Zong massacre is named. The 132 enslaved Africans are named. The insurance-fraud
motive is named. The abolition context is named. The historical precision here is
appropriate and honest. The narrative correctly says "made against the slave trade" and
"the beauty is not decoration laid over the horror. It is the same paint." This is good
framing. No fix needed.

### Finding 3.3 — Delacroix / Orientalism: honestly handled
**Grade: FAIR | Axis: Colonial / race question**

Exact lines (chapter 4, "Morocco · 1832"):
> "Out of it came Women of Algiers (1834) and a string of harem and hunt and street
> scenes that fed a whole nineteenth-century European fashion called Orientalism: the
> 'Orient,' meaning North Africa and the Middle East, painted by Western artists as a
> place of erotic languor, exotic color, and timeless mystery. The pictures are real and
> often beautiful, and they are also a projection. The people in them are mostly props
> for a Western daydream, an East imagined more than observed, the harem as a fantasy a
> European man could never actually have entered."

This names the problem precisely: projection, props, Western daydream, impossible-to-
enter fantasy. It does not sanitize and does not lecture. The Romanticism factpack §8
references Linda Nochlin's critique; the narrative reaches the same judgment in plain
language without attributing it. No fix needed.

---

### Finding 3.4 — No women as makers in the Romanticism read
**Grade: SHOULD-FIX | Axis: Erasure of women**

Women appear in the Romanticism narrative only as:
- Concubines killed in Sardanapalus
- The allegorical figure of Liberty (explicitly flagged in the text as NOT a real woman)
- Shackled bodies in the Slave Ship
- The Wordsworth/Coleridge MeanwhileSheet (unnamed literary context)

No female painter appears. The major figures of Romantic painting are indeed
predominantly male, but the complete absence of women as makers reinforces the same
lone-male-genius structure the era critiques. The closest candidate is Marie-Guillemine
Benoist, whose 1800 "Portrait of Madeleine" (known until recently as "Portrait d'une
Négresse") is contemporaneous with Géricault's raft research and directly relevant to
the slavery/abolition axis. Rosa Bonheur is slightly too late for this movement's range.

⚠️ New material: Benoist's 1800 portrait is documented (it is in the Louvre), and its
connection to the abolition debate is discussed in scholarship. However, it is not in
the Romanticism factpack, so adding it requires a fact-check pass on:
- Whether Benoist fits the Romanticism movement scope or Neoclassicism (she trained
  under David — she may belong in the Neoclassicism read instead)
- The painting's proper title (the "Portrait d'une Négresse" title was updated in 2019;
  verify current Louvre usage)

Flag for coordinator: decide where Benoist fits before adding. Minimum fix without
new material: note the lone-male character of the Romantic pantheon directly in the
afterlife section, e.g. "The Romantic cast was almost entirely male…" — an
acknowledgment that is itself honest framing and requires no new fact-checking.

### Finding 3.5 — Goya's political complexity: simplified but not distorted
**Grade: FAIR (border SHOULD-FIX) | Axis: Political framing**

The narrative frames Goya as an unaligned lone witness — "He had a country falling apart
and an unblinking eye." This is a recognizable reduction of a more complex historical
position. Goya:
- Continued working as court painter during the French occupation under Joseph Bonaparte
- Painted the portrait of the French-installed mayor of Madrid
- Painted the Third of May and Second of May in 1814 partly to curry favor with the
  restored Ferdinand VII, who had him investigated for his "Naked Maja" in 1815
- Was investigated by the Inquisition multiple times

The narrative's Family of Charles IV treatment does hedge the "satirical intent" reading
correctly ("That reading is interpretation, not documented fact"). But "a deaf court
painter gets there a generation early" and "He had a country falling apart and an
unblinking eye" mythologize Goya as a pure outsider in a way that elides his service to
competing regimes.

This stops short of distortion — the narrative doesn't state anything false — but the
framing leans toward the romantic-artist-as-witness myth. A SHOULD-FIX: one sentence
noting Goya continued working as court painter under the occupation would make the
complexity visible without derailing the narrative. New material needed (his service
under Joseph Bonaparte): flag for fact-check before writing.
