## Work-by-work findings

### 1. Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe — Manet, 1863
> "A naked woman picnicking with two clothed men, staring straight out with no mythological alibi. Its scandal at the 1863 Salon des Refusés announced that modern life could be a serious subject — and lit the fuse for the whole movement."

✅ CONFIRMED. The painting was rejected by the 1863 Salon jury and shown at the Salon des Refusés, where it became the principal attraction and generated substantial scandal. The nude's direct gaze without mythological alibi was the core offense. The "lit the fuse" framing is appropriate as a figurative claim.

### 2. Olympia — Manet, 1863
> "A nude who is plainly a contemporary courtesan, not a goddess, meeting your eye in flat, blunt paint. The shock of the 1865 Salon, and the work that made Manet the reluctant father-figure the younger painters gathered around."

✅ CONFIRMED. *Olympia* was painted 1863, exhibited at the 1865 Salon, and caused major scandal — the museum placed guards to prevent physical attacks. Manet's role as "reluctant father-figure" is well-documented.

**Note:** The blurb says "1863" as the year under the canon entry but the Salon showing was 1865 — these are two different things (painted 1863, exhibited 1865). The `year` field is 1863 (correct for when it was made), and the blurb correctly says "shock of the 1865 Salon." No error.

### 3. La Grenouillère — Monet, 1869
> "Painted on the spot beside Renoir at a river café, the water reduced to quick separate dashes — the loose, broken-color sketch treated as a finished picture. This is the new way of seeing being invented in real time."

✅ CONFIRMED. Monet and Renoir painted side by side at La Grenouillère in the summer of 1869. The characterization of the broken-color sketch as a revolutionary "finished picture" is standard art-historical framing.

---

### 4. La Grenouillère — Renoir, 1869
> "Renoir's view of the same spot on the same day, easel to easel with Monet — the twin canvas that shows two friends working out Impressionism together, stroke for stroke."

🟡 LEGEND-FRAME-IT — SHOULD-FIX.

The phrase **"on the same day"** overstates what the evidence supports. Monet and Renoir painted together at La Grenouillère across multiple sessions in the summer of 1869 — they were not necessarily standing side by side on *one single day*. Between them they produced six paintings of the site. Sources confirm they "worked side by side" and "competed to capture their impressions," but the "same day" claim treats an extended collaborative season as a single photographic moment.

**Suggested fix:** Change "on the same day, easel to easel" to "side by side over the summer of 1869, easel to easel."

### 5. Impression, Sunrise — Monet, 1872

✅ CONFIRMED. All claims check out: the harbor, the single orange stroke, Leroy's hostile review, the painters eventually adopting the name. No errors.

### 6. The Cradle — Morisot, 1872
> "A mother watching her sleeping infant through gauzy netting, painted in a few translucent breaths. The female world seen from inside it by a founding member — the signature work of what a respectable woman was allowed to show."

❌ WRONG — MUST-FIX.

The figure in the painting is **not Morisot herself** watching an infant. The painting depicts **Berthe Morisot's sister Edma** (Edma Pontillon) watching over **Edma's daughter Blanche** — Morisot painted her sister and niece, not herself as a mother. The blurb says "A mother watching" without specifying who — which is technically accurate as description — but by calling it "the female world seen from inside it" the blurb strongly implies the painter is the mother observing her own child, which is false. Morisot painted her sister's domestic scene; she herself was not yet married or a mother at this point (she married Eugène Manet in December 1874, two years later).

The error is compounded by the fact pack's own description: "The female gaze on the world it was allowed: a mother watching her sleeping child." The fact pack is also misleading on this point — the painter is not the mother.

**Exact blurb:** "A mother watching her sleeping infant through gauzy netting, painted in a few translucent breaths."
**The correct fact:** The subject is Morisot's sister Edma watching Edma's daughter Blanche. Morisot herself is the painter, not the mother in the scene.
**Suggested fix:** "Her sister Edma watching her sleeping daughter through gauzy netting, painted in a few translucent breaths — the female world observed from inside it by a founding member."

**Source:** Wikipedia, *The Cradle (Morisot)*; Musée d'Orsay collection record.

### 7. Hoarfrost — Pissarro, 1873
> "A frost-stiff field built from broken strokes and colored shadows, hung in the first 1874 exhibition, where a critic sneered it was all palette-scrapings. Pure Impressionist landscape by the one painter who showed in all eight exhibitions."

✅ CONFIRMED with one nuance. The painting was indeed in the 1874 show. Louis Leroy's review does include language about "palette scrapings" in reference to Pissarro's Hoarfrost: "Are these furrows, is this frost? … These are but palette scratches … on a dirty canvas." Pissarro's exclusive status as the only painter in all eight shows is confirmed by multiple sources.

### 8. Boulevard des Capucines — Monet, 1873–74
> "The new Paris boulevard seen from an upstairs window, the crowd dissolved into flecks of paint — Haussmann's city as pure modern motion. Shown at the first Impressionist exhibition, 1874."

✅ CONFIRMED. The painting was shown at the first Impressionist exhibition, 1874. The year in the canon entry is 1874, but the painting is generally dated 1873–74, with the Moscow version (believed to be the one shown at the 1874 exhibition) dated 1873. No material error in the blurb itself. "Upstairs window" is accurate — the exhibition was held at Nadar's former studio and Monet painted from a high vantage point.

### 9. The Dance Class — Degas, 1874
> "A rehearsal seen at a tilt, dancers scattered off-center like a snapshot — Japanese cropping and the caught, ungraceful instant, with no plein air at all. Degas's indoor Impressionism at its purest."

🔵 MINOR IMPRECISION — SHOULD-FIX.

The blurb is artistically accurate. However, the canon entry year is 1874 and the blurb does not claim a specific exhibition showing — which is prudent, because the Wikipedia entry for the Metropolitan Museum version clarifies that it was **loaned to the second Impressionist exhibition in 1876** by its owner (not displayed in 1874 despite being painted then). No specific exhibition claim is made in the blurb, so this is not a stated error. Mark as clean, with a note.

✅ CONFIRMED as written.

### 10. The Floor Planers — Caillebotte, 1875
> "Three shirtless workmen scraping a parquet floor, muscled and anonymous — rejected by the Salon as vulgar, then a sensation at the 1876 exhibition. Modern urban labor at unflinching scale."

✅ CONFIRMED. Wikipedia and museum sources confirm the painting was rejected by the 1875 Salon as depicting "vulgar subject matter" (working-class men not fully clothed), then shown at the second Impressionist exhibition in 1876, where it "achieved instant notoriety." All claims verified.

### 11. Woman with a Parasol — Monet, 1875
> "Madame Monet and their son on a windy rise, seen from below against a racing sky — a full figure dashed off outdoors in a single session, light and movement chosen over likeness."

⚠️ UNSUPPORTED — MUST-FIX.

The "painted in a single session" claim cannot be verified from available sources and is not supported by the fact pack. The painting is a well-documented Impressionist work showing Camille Monet and their son Jean, and it is widely described as capturing a spontaneous, fleeting moment — but the assertion that it was completed **in a single session** is a specific factual claim that goes unverified. It may be legend; it may be true. Art historians describe the work as conveying freshness and spontaneity but do not confirm a single-session completion.

**Exact blurb sentence:** "a full figure dashed off outdoors in a single session"
**Verdict:** MUST-FIX — either verify and source this claim (single-session plein air is occasionally documented for Impressionist works, but needs a specific citation), or soften to "painted quickly outdoors" or "dashed off in the open air."

### 12. Eugène Manet on the Isle of Wight — Morisot, 1875
> "Her husband seated at a window, the garden beyond compressed into bright strokes — a woman painting a man hemmed into a domestic interior, quietly inverting the era's usual gaze."

✅ CONFIRMED with one observation. Morisot married Eugène Manet in December 1874; the painting was made in 1875 during their honeymoon at Cowes, Isle of Wight. "Her husband seated at a window" is accurate. The view is actually through the window to the harbor and streets — sources describe "boats visible at the shoreline" and "two elegantly dressed women in white pass by." The claim that the garden is seen "beyond" is slightly imprecise (it is the harbor/street scene, not strictly a garden) but this is a minor descriptive framing, not a factual error. The "inverting the gaze" observation is a standard and well-supported art-historical reading.

✅ CONFIRMED.

### 14. Bal du moulin de la Galette — Renoir, 1876
> "A Montmartre dance garden dappled with sun falling through the trees, painted at the size the Salon kept for history. The great warm crowd-scene of the movement, and modern leisure made monumental."

✅ CONFIRMED. The painting is at the Musée d'Orsay. "Painted at the size the Salon kept for history" is accurate — the canvas is 131 × 175 cm, unusually large for a leisure scene, typically reserved for history painting. Renoir showed it at the third Impressionist exhibition in 1877.

### 15. L'Absinthe — Degas, 1876
> "Two hollow figures shoved into the corner of a café, the foreground all empty tables, the one note of color a milky-green glass — the loneliness of the modern city, and a fresh scandal when it was later shown in London."

🟡 LEGEND-FRAME-IT — SHOULD-FIX.

The blurb says "a fresh scandal when it was later shown in London." This is true — the Grafton Gallery, London, 1893 showing did cause major controversy — but the phrasing "fresh scandal" understates/misframes the timing. The London scandal was in **1893**, seventeen years after the painting was made. It was not "later shown in London" as if this were a near sequel — it was kept in storage for years after 1876, then the London showing gave it its current title ("L'Absinthe") and made it notorious. Calling it a "fresh" scandal is vague. More importantly, the blurb implies the London showing was a second scandal, but the 1876 initial Paris showing ("it was panned by critics, called ugly and disgusting") was itself controversial, though not on the same scale.

The claim as written is not factually *wrong* but is loosely framed in a way that could mislead. The London scandal is worth mentioning, but the framing should be clearer about the 17-year gap.

**Suggested fix:** "…the loneliness of the modern city, and the source of a fresh uproar when it was shown in London in 1893 — where Victorian critics called it a study in degradation."

### 16. Floods at Port-Marly — Sisley, 1876
> "Floodwater turning a village street into a still mirror of the sky — quiet, exact, and now counted the high point of his art, though his prices only soared after he died poor."

✅ CONFIRMED. Sisley died on 29 January 1899 in financial difficulty, weakened by throat cancer. His prices rose dramatically after his death — a single *Flood at Port-Marly* was later sold for 43,000 francs, while he had sold 27 paintings in his lifetime for barely twice that. The posthumous auction Monet organized for Sisley's children in 1899 raised 112,320 francs. The blurb is accurate.

✅ CONFIRMED. Monet showed multiple works from this series (seven canvases, possibly more) at the third Impressionist exhibition in 1877. The description is accurate.

### 18. Paris Street; Rainy Day — Caillebotte, 1877
> "A huge, cool, almost photographic canvas of Parisians under umbrellas on a wet new boulevard — Haussmann's rebuilt city as subject, rendered with an architectural precision the looser Impressionists never aimed for."

✅ CONFIRMED. The canvas is indeed very large (212.2 × 276.2 cm). It was shown at the third Impressionist exhibition in 1877. The "almost photographic" and "architectural precision" characterizations are standard critical descriptions. The "Haussmann's rebuilt city" framing is accurate. Clean.

### 19. Little Girl in a Blue Armchair — Cassatt, 1878
> "A bored child sprawled sideways in an armchair, a small dog asleep nearby — childhood caught unposed and ungraceful. Cassatt's breakthrough, painted as Degas was drawing her into the group."

🟡 LEGEND-FRAME-IT — SHOULD-FIX.

The phrase **"painted as Degas was drawing her into the group"** is imprecise in a way that implies the painting was made during Cassatt's recruitment process — which is off. Degas **invited Cassatt to join in 1877**, and she accepted. *Little Girl in a Blue Armchair* was painted in **1878**, the year *after* she joined, not during the courtship. She debuted publicly with the group at the **fourth** Impressionist exhibition in **1879**. So she was already in the group when she painted this, not being drawn into it.

The phrasing "as Degas was drawing her into the group" also risks the anachronistic reading that this painting helped secure her membership, which is not documented. Degas did work on the painting (he contributed to the background, confirmed by infrared photography), but that collaborative act was between colleagues already in the same circle.

**Exact blurb sentence:** "painted as Degas was drawing her into the group"
**Correct framing:** Cassatt was invited to join in 1877 and accepted; she painted *Blue Armchair* in 1878 after she was already in the group, before her 1879 debut. Degas even worked on the painting itself as a collaborator.
**Suggested fix:** "Cassatt's breakthrough, painted the year before her 1879 Impressionist debut, with Degas himself contributing to the background."

### 20. A Box at the Théâtre des Italiens — Gonzalès, 1874
> "An elegant couple in a theatre box, painted in the new manner by Manet's only formal pupil — evidence that the circle's women, so often dropped from the story, were working at its center."

✅ CONFIRMED. Gonzalès was indeed Manet's only formal pupil. The painting is dated 1874 and is in the Musée d'Orsay. The note that Gonzalès is "so often dropped from the story" is a legitimate and well-supported characterization. Confirmed.

**One nuance (not a blurb error):** The Musée d'Orsay and Wikipedia note that the painting was actually *submitted to the 1874 Salon* (and rejected), then later shown at the 1879 Salon where it was praised. It was *not* shown at an Impressionist group exhibition. The blurb doesn't claim an Impressionist-show exhibition, so no error — but curators should know the exhibition history differs from the other Impressionist-show works.

### 21. The Little Dancer Aged Fourteen — Degas, 1881
> "A two-thirds-size wax sculpture of a ballet student in a real tutu and real hair, so lifelike it disturbed the 1881 exhibition as almost taxidermic. Now, in its bronze casts, one of the most beloved objects of the century."

🟡 LEGEND-FRAME-IT — SHOULD-FIX.

The word **"taxidermic"** (or "almost taxidermic") appears to be the author's own vivid gloss, not a documented contemporary critical term. The actual 1881 critical language was striking but different: critics described her as "a repulsive monster," compared her to "an anatomical specimen," said the work belonged in "a museum of anthropology," and found her "uncomfortably realistic." Huysmans wrote of "terrible realism." The NGA's own sources describe the reaction as "waxwork figures at Madame Tussauds or a strange doll." "Taxidermic" conflates the "specimen" reactions with an anachronistic framing that sounds more like modern commentary than 1881 criticism.

This is a "charming gloss stated as if it were a documented contemporary reaction" — exactly the legend-frame category. The facts are right (the wax, the real tutu, the real hair, the 1881 show, the disturbance), but "taxidermic" should be framed as the modern reader's apt word for what the critics meant, not as the documented reaction.

**Suggested fix:** "…so lifelike it disturbed the 1881 exhibition — critics called her a repulsive monster and said she belonged in a natural-history museum. Now, in its bronze casts, one of the most beloved objects of the century."

### 22. Luncheon of the Boating Party — Renoir, 1881
> "Friends and a little dog at a riverside lunch, light sieving through a striped awning onto wine and skin — the warmth and ease of the new Sunday leisure, painted at full scale."

✅ CONFIRMED. The setting is the Maison Fournaise restaurant on the Seine at Chatou. The dog is documented (held by Aline Charigot, whom Renoir later married). Shown at the sixth Impressionist exhibition, 1881. "Full scale" is accurate (130 × 175 cm). Clean.

### 23. A Bar at the Folies-Bergère — Manet, 1882
> "A barmaid facing us, the whole glittering hall behind her in a mirror that doesn't quite add up. Manet's last great painting — modern life and modern doubt in a single canvas."

✅ CONFIRMED. *A Bar at the Folies-Bergère* is universally described as Manet's last major work (he died April 1883, one year after it was shown at the 1882 Salon). Multiple museum and scholarly sources call it "the last major work" or "last great painting." The "mirror that doesn't quite add up" is the standard and well-documented observation about the painting's impossible reflection. Clean.

### 24. Haystacks (End of Summer) — Monet, 1891
> "The same grainstacks painted again and again at different hours and seasons, then hung together so the light itself — not the hay — becomes the subject. Impressionism pushed to its serial extreme."

✅ CONFIRMED. The Haystacks series comprises 25 canvases, painted 1890–91. The "End of Summer" (Meules, fin de l'été, 1891) is in the Musée d'Orsay. Durand-Ruel exhibited the series in 1891 and sold nearly all within a month. The "hung together so light becomes the subject" framing is standard and accurate. Clean.

### 25. The Child's Bath — Cassatt, 1893
> "A woman and child seen from a high, flattened, Japanese-print angle, the two heads bent together over a basin — Cassatt's masterpiece of the mother-and-child theme she made her own."

✅ CONFIRMED. The painting is at the Art Institute of Chicago. The high vantage point and flattened perspective are directly influenced by Japanese ukiyo-e prints (Cassatt visited the 1890 Beaux-Arts ukiyo-e exhibition three years before). Called a masterpiece of the mother-and-child theme in multiple sources. Clean.

### 26. Rouen Cathedral — Monet, 1893
> "The cathedral front dissolved into pure colored light, one of a series tracking the same façade from dawn to dusk — solid stone turned into nothing but atmosphere."

🔵 MINOR IMPRECISION — SHOULD-FIX.

The canon entry gives the year as **1893**, and the blurb implies this is a single representative canvas. The series as a whole comprises more than 30 canvases, painted **1892 and 1893** (then reworked in studio in 1894), and was exhibited in 1895 at Durand-Ruel's gallery. The statement "tracking the same façade from dawn to dusk" is accurate but slightly narrow — Monet tracked across multiple weather conditions, seasons, and times of day, not just dawn to dusk in a single day.

These are minor framing imprecisions rather than factual errors. The blurb does not claim this is the only canvas, and "one of a series" is stated. The 1893 year for the canon entry is defensible (the bulk of the on-site painting was 1892–93).

✅ CONFIRMED as written, with advisory note on precision.

### 27. Boulevard Montmartre — Pissarro, 1897
> "The grand boulevard seen from a hotel window across the seasons — the eldest Impressionist, late in life, painting the modern city he had helped make a fit subject thirty years earlier."

✅ CONFIRMED. Pissarro painted 14 views of Boulevard Montmartre from a room at the Grand Hôtel de Russie from February to April 1897. The series covers different seasons, times of day, and weather. He was 67 in 1897, making "late in life" accurate (he died in 1903). "Thirty years earlier" is a reasonable figurative reference to the 1860s–70s when the Impressionists established the modern city as subject. Clean.
