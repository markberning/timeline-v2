**[FIX] — The "carefully reproduced from a study" point is dropped, and its absence slightly oversells the spontaneity.** MoMA's own *Chief* commentary explicitly notes: *"The dynamic curves and slashes of Chief may seem spontaneous, but this painting, like many of his so-called action paintings, was likely carefully reproduced from a preliminary study."* (web-confirmed, MoMA). The draft leans hard on "thrown," "the speed of the body," "fast and loaded" (esp. `looking` and `break` chapters) without ever surfacing that the *finished canvas* was very likely a deliberate scaling-up of a small drawing — which is the same insight the projector chapter establishes ("a small drawing brought up to the scale of a wall… the offhand gesture fused with the deliberate decision to enlarge it"). The two halves of the draft are in mild tension: the projector chapter says deliberate, the looking/break chapters say pure velocity. This is the central paradox of action painting and the draft already has the pieces; it just doesn't close the loop in the looking section.
- **Suggested fix (add to `ChfLooking`, after the "speed" paragraph, or as a clause in the speed annotation):** "What you are reading as velocity is partly staged: like many of these canvases, *Chief* was very likely worked up deliberately from a small drawing, so the thrown-looking stroke is a fast gesture that has been studied and enlarged, not necessarily made in one pass. The speed is real in the mark; it is not a guarantee that the painting was made fast."
- Severity FIX (not BLOCKER): nothing asserted is *false* — the edges genuinely record velocity — but leaving out the "reproduced from a study" caveat lets the prose imply more spontaneity than MoMA itself claims, on a work where that exact tension is the point.

**[NICE] — Tenth Street is named in the cover note's modeling reference but never reaches the reader.** The framing brief asks to "situate him… (Tenth Street, de Kooning)." The draft situates him well via the New York School roster and de Kooning's studio, but never names the Tenth Street / downtown-studio scene as such. Optional one-clause add in `railroad` ("a loose downtown crowd, the Tenth Street painters") would make the milieu concrete. Not required — the situating already works.

## 2. FRAMING / FAIRNESS

**Calligraphy-rejection — handled correctly throughout (no finding).** Every surface that touches it frames it as a reading Kline *refused*: the cover note ("Presented as a reading he refused, never as fact"), the `afterlife` "The reading he refused" header, and the closing logic tying the refusal to the white-is-painted point ("calligraphy is black signs on a blank page, and in *Chief* the white is painted too"). The Sylvester-interview line *"calligraphy is simply the art of writing"* is correctly dated to **1960** and attributed as his own pushback. Web-confirmed. This is exactly the fairness posture the gate wants.

**NY-School situating — good, not lone-genius (no finding).** Kline appears among Pollock, de Kooning, Rothko, Gottlieb; the projector story is set in de Kooning's studio; the `railroad` chapter explicitly frames him as the *stuck* one "watching younger and louder friends rewrite what American art could be." He is the opposite of a lone gesture-man here.

**[NICE] — Projector hedge is excellent; one phrasing risks slightly overstating the hedge.** The `projector` chapter is a model of scoped storytelling ("hold the projector as the *standard account*… a story that has been tidied up… not a documented record of one instant"). One nuance: the de Kooning quote in the wild is *"Franz had a vision of something and sometimes it takes quite a while to work it out"* (web-confirmed). The draft's "took quite a while to work it out" is a faithful clip and used correctly. But the draft's gloss — "which is not the language of a man describing a thunderclap" — slightly recruits de Kooning *against* the eureka story more than his actual sentence does (de Kooning was affirming Kline's vision, just noting it took time to realize). The point survives, but consider softening "is not the language of a man describing a thunderclap" → "describes a slow working-out, not a thunderclap." Minor; the substance is right.

**[NICE] — "one of the defining American paintings of its decade" appears twice (afterlife blurb + afterlife prose) — watch for proportion drift.** Each instance is hedged ("one of," "one of the defining") and defensible. But the claim lands twice in the same short chapter. Recommend keeping ONE. No overclaim per se — just repetition that edges toward boosterism on re-read.

**[NICE] — Genius-myth check: clean.** The `railroad` and closing prose deliberately de-mythologize ("a skilled and stuck painter in his late thirties," "He was a stuck representational painter into his late thirties. By 1950 he had found the one move that was entirely his"). This is the anti-genius-myth framing the gate rewards. No "tortured genius" / "born great" language anywhere. No finding.

**[NICE] — Solinger contextual claim is accurate and if anything understated.** Draft: "later a notable figure at the Whitney Museum" / "later president of the Whitney Museum's 'Friends' / a Whitney figure" (factpack). **Web-confirmed:** Solinger was elected the Whitney's **president in 1966** (the first non-Whitney-family president). So "later a notable figure at the Whitney" is safe and correct — no overclaim risk. (Bonus corroboration found, not required in body: MoMA founding director **Alfred Barr asked Solinger to buy MoMA's first Kline, *Chief***; the draft's "acquired early by the collector" is compatible and need not change.)

**First/only overclaim scan — clean.** No "first abstract…", no "only painter who…", no "invented…". "Chief is one of the first major paintings of that breakthrough" is true and hedged ("one of").
