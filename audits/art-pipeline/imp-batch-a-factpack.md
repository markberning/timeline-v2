# Impressionism Batch A — Combined Fact Pack

Four WORK-level deep reads for the art vertical's gated authoring pipeline. Each
section below is its own fact pack. Imperial dimensions only in the prose-facing
fields; cm appear once as the sourced footnote (museum-authoritative number) so
the converter is always traceable. Every anecdote is tagged DOCUMENTED or LEGEND;
every "what's on the canvas" line is placement-checked against the actual image
descriptions on the museum/Wikipedia pages.

Conversion key (used throughout):
- 1 cm = 0.3937 in
- ft/in rendered as `X ft Y in` (rounded to nearest half-inch on the picture's
  long side, nearest quarter-inch on the short side; the cm footnote is the
  source of truth, the ft/in is reader-facing)

Works in order: Monet *La Grenouillère* (1869) · Renoir *Bal du moulin de la
Galette* (1876) · Caillebotte *Paris Street; Rainy Day* (1877) · Morisot *The
Cradle* (1872).

## 1. Claude Monet, *La Grenouillère* (1869)

### Hard facts

- **Title (FR / EN):** *La Grenouillère* / "La Grenouillère" (also catalogued as
  *Bain à la Grenouillère*, "Bathing at La Grenouillère").
- **Artist:** Claude Monet (1840–1926).
- **Date:** Summer 1869 (signed and dated lower right: *Claude Monet 69*).
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas.
- **Dimensions (reader-facing, imperial):** about **2 ft 5½ in × 3 ft 3¼ in**.
  - Footnote (source of truth, Met catalogue): 74.6 × 99.7 cm.
- **Museum:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.
- **Accession no.:** 29.100.112.
- **Credit line:** H. O. Havemeyer Collection, Bequest of Mrs. H. O.
  Havemeyer, 1929.
- **Signature/inscription:** Signed and dated lower right, *Claude Monet 69*.

### Provenance chain (in order)

1. Probably with **Édouard Manet**, Paris (the Met records the painting as
   "possibly bought from Mme Manet" by Durand-Ruel — Manet was a close ally and
   one of Monet's earliest informal patrons).
2. Possibly **Suzanne Manet** (Manet's widow), Paris, after 1883.
3. **Durand-Ruel**, Paris — possibly acquired from Mme Manet in 1886, certainly
   in Durand-Ruel stock by 1891.
4. **H. O. (Henry Osborne) and Louisine Havemeyer**, New York — bought from
   Durand-Ruel **27 September 1897** for **12,500 F**.
5. **Louisine W. Havemeyer**, New York — by inheritance, 1907–1929.
6. **Metropolitan Museum of Art**, New York — Havemeyer bequest, **1929**.

### What is literally on the canvas (for "Look closer" pointers)

The view looks across a slack arm of the Seine at Croissy-sur-Seine on a hot
summer afternoon.

- **Centre, middle distance:** a small round wooded island connected to a
  rectangular floating platform by a narrow gangplank. The platform is the
  bathing-and-dance pavilion of **La Grenouillère** ("the frog pond"); the
  round island is nicknamed the **Camembert** (or "flowerpot island") for its
  shape. Painted with broad horizontal slabs of olive green and warm grey.
- **On the platform and gangplank:** small standing figures in dark dress
  clothes — day-trippers, not bathers — rendered as paint-strokes a few inches
  high, no facial detail. They lean and cluster; Monet treats them as silhouettes.
- **Left foreground / left edge:** the bow ends of several **rowboats and
  skiffs** tied along the bank, painted as tilted dark wedges with pale ribs.
- **Right foreground:** **bathers in the water** — half-immersed figures in
  white and dark shirts, painted as a few broken brushstrokes each. They form
  a small cluster near a wooden pontoon edge.
- **Water:** the dominant surface. **Horizontal broken strokes** in slate-blue,
  emerald, white, and warm ochre run across nearly two-thirds of the picture;
  the reflections of trees and boats are painted as continuous horizontal bars,
  not as mirrored shapes. This is the technical headline of the picture.
- **Background:** dense summer **trees** in unblended greens and yellow-greens
  fill the upper third, with no sky visible above them (the sky reads as
  reflected light in the water, not as a band above).
- **Light direction:** high overhead summer sun, slightly from the upper left;
  the platform casts a clear dark reflection into the water beneath it.
- **Palette:** olive and emerald greens, slate blue, white, warm ochre, dark
  brown — saturated and high-contrast, not pastel.

### Exhibition history

- **Not exhibited** at any Impressionist exhibition during Monet's most active
  Impressionist decade in any well-documented way. Some references mention the
  2nd Impressionist exhibition (1876), but this is not confirmed in the
  museum's catalogue entry; treat as **uncertain** in prose. Safer phrasing:
  "Painted four years before the First Impressionist Exhibition of 1874, it
  was not shown publicly at the time it was made."
- The picture is now permanently on view at the Met.

### Context the WORK read needs

- **1869, the Seine resort summer:** the new western railway lines out of
  Saint-Lazare made the Seine villages — Bougival, Chatou, Croissy — a same-day
  trip for Parisian clerks and shopgirls. La Grenouillère was a working-class
  floating café-bathing-spot-dance-hall combined; Napoleon III himself had
  visited the year before. It was *not* a respectable upper-bourgeois retreat;
  it was rowdy and a little dubious.
- **Monet and Renoir, side by side:** both were broke. Renoir was staying with
  his parents nearby in Voisins; Monet was at Saint-Michel near Bougival with
  Camille Doncieux and their infant son Jean. They walked over to La Grenouillère
  together across the summer of 1869 and set their easels up next to each other
  — across **several painting sessions, not one day** (LEGEND-CORRECTION below).
- **What's not in this read** (defer to movement read): the full broken-color
  thesis statement and "Impressionism as a method"; that belongs to the
  movement-level Sunrise chapter.

### Documented vs. legend ledger

- **DOCUMENTED:** Monet and Renoir painted at La Grenouillère together in the
  summer of 1869. Each made a closely-related canvas; Renoir's twin (oil on
  canvas, similar composition from a slightly different vantage) is in the
  **Nationalmuseum, Stockholm**.
- **DOCUMENTED:** Monet wrote to Frédéric Bazille in September 1869:
  "I do have a dream, a painting (*tableau*), the baths of La Grenouillère,
  for which I have made some bad sketches (*pochades*), but it is only a
  dream." The Met version is one of those *pochades*; the large finished
  *tableau* was never realised. A larger version once in the Arnhold collection,
  Berlin, is **lost** (presumed destroyed in WWII).
- **LEGEND (REFUSE):** "Monet and Renoir set up next to each other one
  morning and painted these canvases the same day." **Wrong.** They painted
  there over the course of the summer of 1869 across **multiple sessions**;
  the side-by-side canvases are the product of weeks of company, not one
  morning. Use phrasing like "over the summer," "across the summer," or
  "across several afternoons" — never "one day," "one morning," "the same
  afternoon."
- **LEGEND (REFUSE):** "La Grenouillère is where Impressionism was invented."
  Overstated for a WORK read — it's one of the generative moments, but the
  word "Impressionism" was coined five years later (1874) off Monet's
  *Impression, soleil levant*. Phrase as "one of the moments where the
  Impressionist method first showed up on canvas," not as a single birth.
- **LEGEND (REFUSE):** "The painting was shown at the First Impressionist
  Exhibition in 1874." Not documented. Do not put La Grenouillère in the
  1874 show.

### Falsehoods to refuse (per-work)

- Never "the same day," "the same morning," "the same afternoon" for the
  Monet/Renoir session.
- Never claim Monet finished the larger *tableau* he wrote to Bazille about
  — he didn't.
- Never date the picture 1868 or 1870 — it is firmly 1869.
- Don't call La Grenouillère a "respectable bourgeois retreat" — its
  reputation was rowdy; that's part of the point.

### Proposed 5-chapter map (this work)

1. `grenouillere-arrival` — **A summer at the frog pond.** The railway out
   from Saint-Lazare, Croissy, the floating café — what kind of place this was
   and why two broke young painters walked there in the summer of 1869.
2. `grenouillere-making` — **Easel next to easel, week after week.** Monet,
   Renoir, and a season of side-by-side painting; the *pochade* (sketch) treated
   as a finished picture; the moment broken-color becomes a method, not a
   shortcut.
3. `grenouillere-reception` — **The picture nobody saw.** Why this canvas was
   never shown at an Impressionist exhibition, why Monet thought of it as a
   sketch for a *tableau* he never made, and how it travelled quietly through
   Manet's circle to a Durand-Ruel storeroom.
4. `grenouillere-meaning` — **The day painting changed shape.** What is
   actually happening on this canvas (the horizontal water-strokes, the
   refusal to mirror reflections, the figures as marks) and why those choices
   pre-figure the rest of Impressionism.
5. `grenouillere-afterlife` — **From Manet's widow to Fifth Avenue.** The
   Durand-Ruel sale to the Havemeyers in 1897, the 1929 bequest, and a
   *pochade* that ended up arguably more famous than the lost *tableau* it
   was a sketch for.

## 2. Pierre-Auguste Renoir, *Bal du moulin de la Galette* (1876)

### Hard facts

- **Title (FR / EN):** *Bal du moulin de la Galette* / "Dance at the Moulin de
  la Galette" (sometimes "Le Moulin de la Galette").
- **Artist:** Pierre-Auguste Renoir (1841–1919).
- **Date:** 1876 (signed and dated lower right).
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas.
- **Dimensions (reader-facing, imperial — the d'Orsay primary version):**
  about **4 ft 3¾ in × 5 ft 9 in**.
  - Footnote (source of truth, Musée d'Orsay): 131 × 175 cm (some catalogues
    131.5 × 176.5 cm). With frame: 161.8 × 206 cm.
- **Museum:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris.
- **Accession no.:** RF 2739.
- **Credit line:** Caillebotte bequest, accepted by the French State 1894,
  entered the national collections 1896.
- **Signature/inscription:** Signed and dated lower right, *Renoir. 76*.

### Provenance chain (in order)

1. **Pierre-Auguste Renoir** (the artist), Paris — 1876–1879.
2. **Gustave Caillebotte**, Paris — bought directly from Renoir in **1879**
   (price not securely documented; Caillebotte was Renoir's friend and main
   patron during these years).
3. **French State**, by **Caillebotte bequest** — Caillebotte died 21 February
   **1894**; the bequest was negotiated against state resistance and the
   painting was formally accepted in **1894**, entering the **Musée du
   Luxembourg** (the state's contemporary-art museum) in **1896**.
4. **Musée du Luxembourg**, Paris — 1896 to 1929.
5. **Musée du Louvre**, Paris — transferred 1929 (the standard route for
   "consecrated" modern works leaving the Luxembourg).
6. **Jeu de Paume**, Paris — installed there with the Impressionist collection,
   1947–1986.
7. **Musée d'Orsay**, Paris — when d'Orsay opened in **1986**.

### What is literally on the canvas (for "Look closer" pointers)

A Sunday-afternoon outdoor dance at the *guinguette* (open-air café-dance)
attached to the windmill called **le Moulin de la Galette** on the Butte
Montmartre.

- **Upper third:** the **canopy of trees** (acacias) with **round paper-globe
  lanterns** hung in the branches, unlit (it is still daylight). Light falls
  through the leaves as scattered round patches.
- **Middle ground, behind the figures:** couples **dancing** — a sea of bowler
  hats and pale dresses, painted as a soft crowd, no facial detail. A few of
  the dancing women's dresses pick up pure pink and pure pale-blue strokes
  that read as the strongest colour in the picture.
- **Foreground, lower right — the table group:** three named friends of Renoir
  sit around a small round café table with a green bottle and glasses.
  - The man **in the dark suit and straw boater, seen in profile facing
    right**, his arm on the chair, is the painter **Norbert Goeneutte** (some
    sources also identify him as Pierre-Franc Lamy here — see note below).
  - The man **seated opposite, facing the viewer**, leaning forward toward the
    woman, is the writer **Georges Rivière** (Renoir's friend and the chronicler
    of this whole circle).
  - The young woman **leaning back against the bench**, wearing a **striped
    pink-and-blue dress**, is **Estelle**, the younger sister of the actress
    Jeanne Samary.
- **Foreground, lower left:** **Margot** (Marguerite Legrand), a favourite
  Renoir model, in a striped pink-and-blue dress, dancing with a Cuban-painter
  friend, **Pedro Vidal de Solares y Cárdenas** (often given as "Don Pedro
  Vidal de Solares y Cárdenas").
- **Other identifiable figures in the dancing crowd:** the painters **Henri
  Gervex**, **Frédéric Cordey**, **Pierre-Franc Lamy**, the journalist **Paul
  Lhote**, and **Jeanne** (sister to Estelle Samary).
- **Light:** the picture's signature trick is the **dappled sunlight** — round,
  warm patches of light falling on hats, shoulders, jackets and the ground,
  painted as discrete pink-blue-violet spots, not as a continuous wash. The
  young man in the foreground in the dark jacket has a clear **pink-violet
  patch on his back** that is the same colour as nothing else in the picture.
- **Palette:** pale pinks, lilac, pure blue, straw-yellow, warm gray, soft
  black; almost no pure white and no hard black shadows.

### Exhibition history

- **3rd Impressionist Exhibition**, Paris, **April 1877** — the painting's
  public debut. Hung as one of the show's centrepieces.
- (Note: the older Wikipedia phrasing "first exhibited at the 1877 Salon" is
  **wrong** — the picture was rejected from / not shown at the official Salon
  and was shown at the breakaway Impressionist exhibition. Refuse this if it
  surfaces.)
- After Caillebotte's bequest entered the Luxembourg in 1896, the painting
  has been permanently on view in Paris (Luxembourg → Louvre → Jeu de
  Paume → d'Orsay) ever since.

### Context the WORK read needs

- **The Moulin de la Galette site:** a real windmill on the Butte de
  Montmartre, converted by the Debray family into an open-air dance garden
  serving a flat sweet pancake (the *galette*) the place was named for. Open
  Sunday afternoons; admission cheap; clientele was working-class Montmartrois
  young people, plus the bohemian Montmartre art crowd. Renoir rented a studio
  nearby on rue Cortot specifically to work on this picture and painted on
  site over weeks.
- **History-painting scale, modern subject:** the canvas is enormous — nearly
  six feet across. That scale was reserved by Salon convention for ancient
  battles, mythology, and the death of kings. Painting a Sunday dance at it is
  a deliberate provocation. (Defer the full "modern life as a worthy subject"
  argument to the movement read.)
- **Defer to movement read:** the Salon-system context, the 1874–1886
  Impressionist exhibitions overview, the Caillebotte-bequest fight in detail
  (the state initially refused 27 of the 67 works and the negotiation dragged
  for years). The bequest gets gestured at here; it is *told* in the movement
  read's last chapter.

### Documented vs. legend ledger

- **DOCUMENTED:** Renoir rented a studio on rue Cortot in Montmartre in 1875
  expressly to work on this picture; he painted at the dance garden on
  Sunday afternoons over weeks.
- **DOCUMENTED:** Caillebotte bought the painting from Renoir in 1879 and
  bequeathed it to the French State on his death in 1894.
- **DOCUMENTED:** The friends named above are all identifiable from Renoir's
  and Georges Rivière's accounts and from cross-referenced photographs.
- **LEGEND / DISPUTED — the smaller version:** there is a **smaller canvas**
  of the same composition, roughly **78 × 114 cm** (about 2 ft 6¾ in × 3 ft
  9 in). Historically catalogued as a study by Renoir; sold from the John Hay
  Whitney collection at **Sotheby's New York in May 1990** for **$78.1 million**
  (then a record). Its current location is **a private Swiss collection**.
  Scholars do not agree whether it is (a) Renoir's preparatory study, (b) a
  later replica by Renoir, or (c) a copy by another hand. **Phrase carefully:
  "a smaller version exists, attributed to Renoir but contested." Never say
  "the study in the Barnes Foundation" — that is wrong; the Barnes does not
  hold a *Bal du moulin de la Galette*. Refuse "Barnes Foundation" as a
  location for any version of this picture.**
- **LEGEND (CAREFUL):** "Renoir painted the whole thing *en plein air* in the
  garden." Partially documented. He painted there on site; the picture was
  finished in studio. Phrase as "painted partly on site, finished in the
  rue Cortot studio."

### Falsehoods to refuse (per-work)

- **No Barnes Foundation version exists.** Refuse.
- The painting was **not** exhibited at the 1877 Paris Salon; it was at the
  3rd Impressionist Exhibition (also 1877). Different show.
- Renoir was not yet famous in 1876. He was 35, broke, and Caillebotte's
  patronage was the lifeline. Don't write "the celebrated Renoir."
- The dappled-light effect is **paint trick**, not photograph: don't write
  "photo-realist" for this picture.

### Proposed 5-chapter map (this work)

1. `galette-arrival` — **The mill on the hill.** Montmartre as a working-class
   village just inside the new city, the Debrays' galette, why a 35-year-old
   broke painter rented a studio next door.
2. `galette-making` — **Weeks of Sundays, then weeks in the studio.** The
   on-site sessions, the friends who sat (Goeneutte, Rivière, Estelle,
   Margot), the rue Cortot finish, and the trick of painting sunlight as
   round spots of pure colour.
3. `galette-reception` — **April 1877, the third exhibition.** The painting's
   debut at the 3rd Impressionist Exhibition; mixed reviews; Caillebotte's
   purchase two years later (1879) for the price of a saved friendship.
4. `galette-meaning` — **History-painting scale, Sunday-afternoon subject.**
   Why nearly-six-feet-across of a dance garden is a small revolution; what
   the dappled light is doing technically; what's actually in the picture
   (named friends, the pink-and-blue dress, the lanterns).
5. `galette-afterlife` — **From Caillebotte's wall to d'Orsay's wall.** The
   1894 bequest, the state's reluctance, the Luxembourg → Louvre → Jeu de
   Paume → d'Orsay journey, and the smaller contested version that broke an
   auction record in 1990.

## 3. Gustave Caillebotte, *Paris Street; Rainy Day* (1877)

### Hard facts

- **Title (FR / EN):** *Rue de Paris, temps de pluie* / "Paris Street; Rainy
  Day."
- **Artist:** Gustave Caillebotte (1848–1894).
- **Date:** 1877 (signed and dated lower left).
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas.
- **Dimensions (reader-facing, imperial):** about **6 ft 11½ in × 9 ft 0¾ in**.
  - Footnote (source of truth, Art Institute of Chicago): 212.2 × 276.2 cm.
- **Museum:** Art Institute of Chicago.
- **Accession no.:** 1964.336.
- **Credit line:** Charles H. and Mary F. S. Worcester Collection.
- **Signature/inscription:** Signed and dated lower left, *G. Caillebotte. 1877*.

### Provenance chain (in order)

1. **Gustave Caillebotte** (the artist), Paris — 1877–1894.
2. **Martial Caillebotte and Marie Minoret Caillebotte** (the artist's
   brother and sister-in-law), Paris — by inheritance, **1894**.
3. **Georges Minoret** (Martial's brother-in-law), Château de Montglat,
   Provins, France — by deposit, **1900**.
4. **Albert and Geneviève Chardeau (née Caillebotte)** (Martial's daughter
   and son-in-law), Paris — by inheritance, **1950**.
5. **Walter P. Chrysler Jr.**, New York — **1954/55**.
6. **Wildenstein & Co.**, New York — **1964**.
7. **Art Institute of Chicago** — **1964** (purchased through the Worcester
   Collection fund).

### What is literally on the canvas (for "Look closer" pointers)

A vast, almost-photographic view of a Paris intersection just east of the
Gare Saint-Lazare, the morning after rain.

- **The setting / identifiable streets:** the intersection now called **Place
  de Dublin** (in Caillebotte's day, **Carrefour de Moscou**), where five
  streets meet in Haussmann's rebuilt 8th arrondissement: **rue de Turin**,
  **rue de Moscou**, **rue Clapeyron**, **rue de Saint-Pétersbourg** (today
  rue de Léningrad / rue de Saint-Pétersbourg), and **rue de Bucarest**. The
  streets are identifiable from the painting; the intersection still exists
  and the wedge-shaped Haussmann building visible behind the central lamppost
  still stands.
- **Centre, foreground right of centre:** the **central couple** walking
  toward the viewer under a single black umbrella, the **man on the woman's
  right**. The man wears a black **top hat**, dark overcoat, **bow tie**, and
  has a trim mustache; the woman wears a small dark hat with a **soft veil
  over her face**, a **single pearl/diamond earring** glinting at her ear, and
  a **fur-trimmed coat**. Her **right hand is hooked into the crook of his
  left arm**; the umbrella is held in his **left hand**.
- **Centre, exact middle of the canvas:** a **dark-green cast-iron gas
  lamppost** rises the full height of the picture, splitting the composition
  vertically into two near-equal halves.
- **Right edge, severely cropped:** a **man in a top hat with his back
  three-quarters to us**, walking past the central couple toward the viewer's
  right; only his left side, umbrella, and right arm are visible. The crop is
  abrupt — half a body is simply gone off the edge of the canvas. This is the
  picture's headline photographic-influence detail.
- **Left half, middle ground:** smaller figures under umbrellas crossing the
  intersection from left to right, and a **horse-drawn cart** (one source
  reads it as a green wagon) heading away from us up the slope of rue
  Clapeyron.
- **Background:** the wedge-shaped pale Haussmann apartment building with
  its uniform stone facade, balcony courses, and mansard roof fills the
  upper-centre of the canvas. The streets recede past it.
- **Ground:** roughly the lower **quarter** of the canvas is wet cobblestones
  — grey-blue, slick, with reflections.
- **Light direction:** flat, diffuse, no direct sun. The picture has **no cast
  shadows** of any consequence. The umbrellas are protective, not against rain
  in progress so much as against the wet sky.
- **Palette:** the picture's other headline. **Cool greys, slate blue, oyster
  white, a few greens** (the lamppost, the wagon). No warm colour anywhere.

### Exhibition history

- **3rd Impressionist Exhibition**, Paris, **April 1877** — the painting's
  public debut. Caillebotte was both an exhibitor and one of the show's
  organizers and financial backers.
- Subsequent 19th-century / early-20th-century exhibition history: limited —
  the picture stayed in family hands and was rarely seen.
- Acquired by the Art Institute in 1964; permanently on view in Gallery 201
  (Grand Staircase, east side).

### Context the WORK read needs

- **Haussmann's Paris just finished:** Napoleon III's prefect Baron
  Georges-Eugène Haussmann ran the 1853–1870 demolition-and-rebuild that
  turned medieval Paris into the boulevard city. By 1877 the new boulevards
  are seven years old at most; the **Place de Dublin / Quartier de l'Europe**
  is some of the **newest construction in Paris**. This is not a nostalgic
  street scene — it's a deliberately new street scene, painted by an engineer
  who liked the geometry.
- **Caillebotte the engineer-painter:** trained as an engineer, independently
  wealthy (his father owned a military-textile concern; Gustave inherited at
  26 in 1874). Painted seriously, was the financial backer of several
  Impressionist exhibitions, and assembled the great collection that became
  the **Caillebotte bequest** to the French State after his death in 1894.
  The bequest gets gestured at here; it is *told* in the movement read's
  last chapter.
- **The photographic influence:** by 1877 cheap photographic prints were
  everywhere in Paris. The severe cropping of the right-edge figure, the
  almost-vertical perspective, and the flat diffuse light read as photographic
  on purpose.
- **Defer to movement read:** Haussmann in detail, the Caillebotte bequest
  fight, the railway-and-leisure context.

### Documented vs. legend ledger

- **DOCUMENTED:** the intersection is **Place de Dublin** (then Carrefour de
  Moscou), east of Gare Saint-Lazare. The wedge-shaped building is still
  there and identifiable. The streets are real and named.
- **DOCUMENTED:** the painting debuted at the 3rd Impressionist Exhibition in
  April 1877. Caillebotte was 28 when it was shown.
- **DOCUMENTED:** the picture stayed in the Caillebotte family from 1894 to
  1950, passed by inheritance through Martial Caillebotte and Geneviève
  Chardeau, and only left France in 1954/55 (to Walter Chrysler Jr.) before
  Wildenstein resold to the Art Institute of Chicago in 1964.
- **LEGEND (REFUSE):** "Caillebotte was a wealthy amateur." Wrong on two
  counts — he was wealthy, but he was a fully serious painter, not a
  hobbyist, and he was a working organizer of the exhibitions. "Engineer-
  painter who could afford to paint without selling" is the accurate phrase.
- **LEGEND (REFUSE):** "The man at the right edge is Caillebotte himself."
  Not documented and contested by the Art Institute's own catalogue. Treat
  the cropped figure as anonymous, not as a self-portrait.
- **LEGEND (CAREFUL):** "Caillebotte bought this painting from his own
  estate." No — *this* picture stayed in the family privately. **Other**
  Renoirs / Monets / Pissarros / Cézannes went into the Caillebotte bequest
  to the French state. Don't confuse the two. *Bal du moulin de la Galette*
  went in the bequest; *Paris Street; Rainy Day* did not.

### Falsehoods to refuse (per-work)

- The picture was **not** part of the Caillebotte bequest. It stayed in the
  family until 1954.
- The intersection is **not** "somewhere in the Marais" / "the Left Bank" /
  "near Notre-Dame." It is the **Quartier de l'Europe** in the 8th
  arrondissement, east of Saint-Lazare. Place de Dublin.
- The light is **not** sunset / sunrise / golden-hour. It is flat overcast.
  Refuse warm-light readings.
- The man at the right edge is **not** identified.

### Proposed 5-chapter map (this work)

1. `rainy-day-arrival` — **A new intersection.** The 8th arrondissement of
   1877: Haussmann's demolitions just finished, the boulevards seven years
   old, the Place de Dublin five minutes from the new Gare Saint-Lazare and
   one of the freshest stretches of pavement in Paris.
2. `rainy-day-making` — **The engineer at the easel.** Caillebotte the
   independently-wealthy engineer-painter; the studies, the geometric
   perspective scheme, the seven-by-nine-foot canvas; how someone with no
   need to sell painted *this big* on a contemporary street scene.
3. `rainy-day-reception` — **April 1877, third exhibition.** Public debut at
   the 3rd Impressionist Exhibition; how a vast wet-cobblestone picture
   landed in a year that was meant to be about colour and light; the
   contemporary reviews.
4. `rainy-day-meaning` — **What's actually here.** The cropped man at the
   right edge, the woman with the veil and the pearl earring, the single
   green lamppost down the centre, the wedge-shaped Haussmann building you
   can still find — and how the picture borrows from photography without
   ever pretending to be one.
5. `rainy-day-afterlife` — **The painting that stayed home.** The Caillebotte
   bequest of 1894 (and the famous works that *did* go to the State); this
   picture's quiet 60 years in the family; Walter Chrysler, Wildenstein, and
   the 1964 Chicago purchase that made it American.

## 4. Berthe Morisot, *The Cradle (Le Berceau)* (1872)

### Hard facts

- **Title (FR / EN):** *Le Berceau* / "The Cradle."
- **Artist:** Berthe Morisot (1841–1895).
- **Date:** 1872 (signed lower left).
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas.
- **Dimensions (reader-facing, imperial):** about **1 ft 10 in × 1 ft 6¼ in**.
  - Footnote (source of truth, Musée d'Orsay): 56 × 46 cm (some catalogues
    56 × 46.5 cm).
- **Museum:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris.
- **Accession no.:** RF 2849.
- **Credit line:** Acquired by the Louvre in 1930.
- **Signature/inscription:** Signed lower left, *Berthe Morisot*.

### Provenance chain (in order)

1. **Berthe Morisot** (the artist), Paris — 1872; offered for sale at the
   First Impressionist Exhibition 1874 at **800 francs**, **did not sell**.
2. **Edma Pontillon (née Morisot)** (the artist's elder sister, the model
   for the seated woman), Paris and Cherbourg — by gift, after 1874.
3. **Blanche Pontillon (later Blanche Forget)** (Edma's daughter, the sleeping
   baby in the painting), Paris — by inheritance.
4. **Musée du Louvre**, Paris — purchased from Blanche Forget in **1930** for
   **300,000 francs**.
5. **Musée du Jeu de Paume**, Paris — installed with the Impressionist
   collection, 1947–1986.
6. **Musée d'Orsay**, Paris — when d'Orsay opened in **1986**.

### What is literally on the canvas (for "Look closer" pointers)

A young mother in a domestic interior, watching her sleeping infant in a
cradle draped in white gauze.

- **Right two-thirds of the canvas — the woman:** a young dark-haired woman
  in a dark grey-black dress, seated in three-quarter profile facing the
  viewer's **left**. She is **Edma Pontillon**, née Morisot, the artist's
  elder sister. She is **not Berthe Morisot.** (In 1872 Berthe was 31,
  unmarried, and lived with her parents; she only married Eugène Manet —
  Édouard Manet's brother — in December 1874. The painting is therefore
  *not* a self-portrait of Morisot as a mother.)
- **Her right elbow** rests on the edge of the cradle; **her right hand is
  raised to her chin**, fingers loosely curled, in a posture of thought.
- **Her gaze** is **directed downward and to the viewer's left, into the
  cradle.** The diagonal of her gaze and the diagonal of the cradle's gauze
  are the picture's main compositional line.
- **Left third of the canvas — the cradle:** a wooden cradle entirely draped
  in **translucent white gauze netting**. Through the gauze, the baby's
  **closed eyes and small fisted left hand** are visible. The baby is
  **Blanche Pontillon** (later Blanche Forget), Edma's infant daughter, born
  in 1871.
- **The baby's small bent left arm** mirrors the mother's bent right arm —
  the visual rhyme is deliberate.
- **Top left — a pale curtain:** a sheer **vertical curtain** falls from
  upper-left, its drape rhyming with the cradle's gauze.
- **Background:** a darker wall to the right of the mother, with no specific
  detail — Morisot keeps it almost monochromatic so the white gauze reads as
  pure light.
- **Gauze technique:** Morisot paints the netting in a few rapid translucent
  strokes — you read it as fabric, but the baby's face is visible *through*
  it. This is the picture's signature technical move and the look-closer
  headline.
- **Light direction:** soft daylight from the upper left, falling on the
  cradle and the mother's face; no hard shadow.
- **Palette:** white, oyster, pale grey, soft black, a single warm flesh
  tone on the mother's face and the baby's hand.

### Exhibition history

- **First Impressionist Exhibition**, Paris, **15 April 1874**, at Nadar's
  former studio, 35 boulevard des Capucines. Morisot was **the only woman**
  among the 30 exhibiting artists. *Le Berceau* was one of the nine works
  she showed. Listed at 800 francs; **did not sell**.
- After 1874, the painting was effectively unseen for the rest of the 19th
  century — it stayed with the Pontillon family.
- Modern exhibition history begins after the 1930 Louvre purchase.

### Context the WORK read needs

- **The Morisot sisters:** Berthe and Edma trained together as painters
  under Joseph Guichard and Camille Corot. Both were serious; both exhibited
  at the Salon in the 1860s. Edma married a naval officer, **Adolphe
  Pontillon**, in 1869 and largely stopped painting — the standard pressure
  on a bourgeois Frenchwoman. **Berthe did not stop painting.** That choice
  is the personal core of this picture.
- **Bourgeois women's constraints, 1872:** a respectable upper-middle-class
  Frenchwoman in 1872 could not walk alone in a Paris café; could not paint
  at La Grenouillère next to Renoir; could not loiter at the racetrack
  alone. The subjects available to Morisot were the interior, the garden,
  the nursery, the boudoir — and she made *that* her field. (Defer the
  movement-level "women's space" argument to the movement read.)
- **Manet, the brother-in-law to be:** Berthe had been close to Édouard
  Manet's circle since 1868 (she modelled for *The Balcony*, 1868–69). She
  married his younger brother **Eugène Manet** in December 1874, eight
  months *after* the First Impressionist Exhibition. Édouard Manet famously
  did not show with the Impressionists; Berthe did.
- **Defer to movement read:** the full 1874 First Impressionist Exhibition
  blow-by-blow; the Salon refusals; the larger feminist reception history.

### Documented vs. legend ledger

- **DOCUMENTED:** the seated woman is **Edma Pontillon** (née Morisot), the
  artist's sister. The baby is **Blanche Pontillon**, Edma's daughter
  (later Blanche Forget). This identification is recorded in family
  correspondence and is the consensus reading on the Musée d'Orsay catalogue
  page.
- **DOCUMENTED:** the painting was shown at the 1874 First Impressionist
  Exhibition; Morisot was the only woman among the exhibiting artists;
  the picture was offered at 800 francs and did not sell.
- **DOCUMENTED:** Morisot married Eugène Manet in **December 1874**, eight
  months after the First Impressionist Exhibition.
- **DOCUMENTED:** the Louvre bought the painting from Blanche Forget in
  **1930** for **300,000 francs**.
- **LEGEND (REFUSE):** "Morisot painted herself watching her own daughter."
  **Wrong.** Morisot was unmarried in 1872. Her own daughter Julie Manet
  wasn't born until 1878 — six years after this canvas. Refuse any reading
  that puts Morisot in the picture as the seated woman.
- **LEGEND (REFUSE):** "The Cradle was acclaimed in 1874." Reviews were
  mixed-to-warm at best, and the picture did not sell. Refuse "acclaimed"
  / "triumphal debut."
- **LEGEND (CAREFUL):** "Berthe Morisot married Édouard Manet." **Wrong** —
  she married his brother **Eugène Manet**. Édouard was already married
  (to Suzanne Leenhoff) and was her brother-in-law, never her husband.
  Refuse the Édouard-Manet-husband error every time it surfaces.
- **LEGEND (CAREFUL):** the cradle drape was painted "alla prima in one
  sitting." Not documented; Morisot's process for this picture is not fully
  recorded. Phrase as "painted in a few translucent strokes" — describes the
  *look*, not a claimed technique.

### Falsehoods to refuse (per-work)

- The mother in the painting is **not** Berthe Morisot. It is her sister
  **Edma Pontillon**.
- The baby is **not** Berthe's daughter Julie (born 1878). It is **Blanche
  Pontillon**, Edma's daughter.
- Berthe Morisot did **not** marry Édouard Manet. She married **Eugène
  Manet**, his younger brother, in December 1874.
- *The Cradle* was **not** at the Salon. It was at the **First Impressionist
  Exhibition** (the break-away).
- Morisot was **not** an amateur or "Manet's pupil." She trained under
  Guichard and Corot, exhibited at the Salon from 1864, and was a working
  professional painter when she met Manet in 1868.

### Proposed 5-chapter map (this work)

1. `cradle-arrival` — **Two sisters who painted.** Berthe and Edma Morisot:
   their shared training under Corot, their early Salon successes, and the
   1869 marriage that made one of them stop painting and the other dig in.
2. `cradle-making` — **The picture of someone else's choice.** Edma in
   Cherbourg with her infant Blanche; Berthe at the easel; a quiet
   tender-but-not-sentimental portrait of the life Berthe is choosing not
   to have. The translucent gauze, the matched bent arms, the diagonal of
   the mother's gaze.
3. `cradle-reception` — **April 1874, Nadar's studio.** The only woman in
   the room; nine works shown; *Le Berceau* listed at 800 francs and
   passed over; the mixed reviews; eight months later Morisot marries
   Eugène Manet.
4. `cradle-meaning` — **What the gauze is doing.** The technical headline
   (transparency as a few breaths of paint); the conceptual headline
   (a respectable woman's enforced domestic field made into a serious
   modern picture).
5. `cradle-afterlife` — **From Edma's parlour to the Louvre.** The painting
   stays with Edma, then with Blanche; nearly sixty years out of public
   view; the 1930 Louvre purchase from Blanche Forget; the Jeu de Paume,
   then the d'Orsay, then a permanent place in the Impressionism story.
