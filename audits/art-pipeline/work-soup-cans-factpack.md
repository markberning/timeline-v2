## 2. THE STORY

Thirty-two canvases, one for each variety of Campbell's condensed soup the company sold in 1962 — the count tracks the product line, not a chosen number [S2][S4]. Each can is the same red-and-white label; **only the variety name on the front changes** (Tomato, Beef, Onion, etc.) [S1][S4].

First shown at the **Ferus Gallery, Los Angeles**, opening **9 July 1962** — Warhol's first solo painting show — arranged by gallery director **Irving Blum**. The canvases sat in **a single row on a narrow ledge/shelf, like cans on a grocery-store aisle** [S2][S4]. Blum had first seen soup-can canvases at Warhol's Lexington Avenue apartment in 1961 and offered the show. Few sold (around five or six, one reserved by actor Dennis Hopper) [S4].

Warhol's stated reason was deadpan and autobiographical: he ate Campbell's soup for lunch, the same thing, for ~20 years (see §4). The work's argument is the brand itself as subject — the supermarket commodity treated with the flat, repetitive uniformity of advertising, painted by hand but engineered to look mass-produced.

**Technique caveat that matters for the caption:** these specific **1962 cans are largely hand-painted** — among the last works Warhol made substantially by hand. The mechanical **silkscreen** process he is famous for (Marilyns, Disasters, etc.) came **slightly later in 1962 and after** [S4][S2]. Do not describe the Soup Cans set as silkscreens.

MoMA **acquired the full 32-canvas set intact** in **1996** (~$15 million, partial gift of Blum + partial purchase), deliberately keeping the set together rather than dispersing it [S4][S1].

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (before / after — REQUIRED)

**Before:** Postwar American high art was dominated by Abstract Expressionism — gestural, heroic, individual, emotionally expressive, the canvas as the record of the artist's unrepeatable hand (Pollock, de Kooning). Subject matter was inward or abstract; the supermarket aisle was beneath fine art.

**The break:** Warhol puts a **mass-market supermarket commodity — a grocery soup can — on the wall as the entire high-art subject**, in **deadpan, serial, near-identical repetition** across 32 panels [S2][S6]. There is no expressive gesture, no hierarchy, no "best" can; the image is the packaging. This collapses the line between commercial illustration and fine art and stages **liking ordinary commercial things as the content of the art** (see §4 quote).

**What it puts in question:** **authorship and originality.** If the picture is a faithful copy of a label, repeated, deliberately impersonal and made to look mechanical (and soon literally mechanical, via silkscreen), then the value of the unique artist's touch — the core claim of the preceding generation — is openly challenged [S2][S6].

**After:** opens Pop art as a dominant 1960s movement and a long line of appropriation/brand/commodity art; the idea that anything mass-produced can be the subject, and that the artist can be a deadpan reproducer rather than an expressive originator.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — with variant flags)

**Quote A (the soup / repetition — recommended primary):**
> "I used to drink it. I used to have the same lunch every day, for twenty years, I guess, the same thing over and over again."
— Andy Warhol, interview with **G. R. (Gene) Swenson, "What is Pop Art? Answers from 8 Painters, Part I," ARTnews, November 1963** [S7][S8]. Widely reproduced by MoMA and academic sources.

**Quote B (Pop art / liking things):**
- **Verbatim from the published Swenson interview:** when asked "Is that what Pop Art is all about?" Warhol answered, **"Yes. It's liking things."** [S8][S9]
- **Common paraphrase (FLAG):** the widely-quoted form **"Pop art is about liking things"** (e.g. cited by The Andy Warhol Museum) is a **smoothed paraphrase**, not Warhol's exact words in the interview. If quoting, prefer the verbatim "It's liking things"; if the paraphrase is used, attribute it as a paraphrase, not a direct verbatim quote.

**Caveat on the Swenson interview generally:** the published 1963 ARTnews text was heavily edited from the recordings (a revised transcript was later published in the *Oxford Art Journal*, 2018). The two quotes above are the standard, widely-cited published forms; flag that the published interview is an edited document, not a raw transcript [S9].

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **Thirty-two near-identical cans in a grid.** Scan the whole set first: the same can, the same scale, the same head-on view, repeated 32 times — the repetition is the point, not any single can.
2. **The only thing that changes is the name.** Move can to can and notice the sole variable is the **variety name** on the label's lower band — Tomato, Beef, Onion, Pepper Pot. The product line, not the painter, sets what you see.
3. **The red-and-white label.** The flat division — red lower half, white upper half — is the actual Campbell's packaging, reproduced deadpan, with the script "Campbell's" wordmark across the white.
4. **The gold fleur-de-lis band.** Look at the ring of small gold fleur-de-lis ornaments near each can's base — these were applied with a **hand stamp**, a tell that the "mechanical" look is in fact handwork.
5. **Slight hand-painted irregularities.** Compare a few cans closely: small inconsistencies in the lettering, the outline, the gold band give away that each is **individually hand-painted**, not printed — the human hand peeking through the machine surface.
6. **The grocery-shelf / grid display.** The way they hang — an even rank, originally on a literal shelf at Ferus — reads like a **supermarket aisle**, importing the retail display into the gallery.

## 6. PROVENANCE

1. **Made by the artist**, New York, 1961–62 [S4].
2. **Ferus Gallery, Los Angeles** — exhibited July 1962; director **Irving Blum**. A handful of individual canvases were initially sold to buyers [S4].
3. **Irving Blum reassembled the set** — Blum bought back / reclaimed the sold canvases and **purchased the full 32 from Warhol for $1,000, paid in installments** (~$100/month over about a year) to keep the set intact [S4].
4. Blum held the set; placed it on **long-term loan to the National Gallery of Art, Washington (from 1987)** [S4].
5. **The Museum of Modern Art, New York, 1996** — acquired the intact 32-canvas set for ~$15 million as a **partial gift of Irving Blum + partial purchase** (Varnedoe-led acquisition); kept together [S4][S1]. (Full credit line in §1.)

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

1. **"They're silkscreens." — FALSE for this set.** The **1962 Soup Cans are hand-painted** (projector-traced outlines, hand-stamped fleur-de-lis). They are among Warhol's last largely hand-made works; the silkscreen technique came slightly later. Common error [S2][S3][S4].
2. **"Warhol fixed the order of the 32 canvases." — NOT established.** Warhol did **not** specify a fixed sequence. **MoMA arranges them by the date each soup variety was introduced to market** (Tomato, the oldest, first), but this is a museum decision and the arrangement has varied for special displays [Wikipedia/MoMA, S2][S4]. Don't claim Warhol mandated an order.
3. **"32 because Campbell's had 32 varieties." — TRUE, with care.** There were 32 because that was the number of Campbell's condensed-soup varieties then sold — the count tracks the product line, not a symbolic or chosen number [S2][S4].
4. **The Ferus "shelf" display — TRUE.** They were shown in a single row on a narrow ledge like grocery-store cans at Ferus, July 1962 — a real, documented detail, not legend [S2][S4].
5. **Dimensions — get them right.** Each canvas is **20 × 16 in (50.8 × 40.6 cm)**. Any "overall" figure (~97 × 163 in) depends on the hang/spacing and is not an intrinsic dimension — don't cite it as the size of "the painting" [S1].
6. **"Casein and pencil" medium.** Some sources list casein/graphite for *individual* Warhol soup-can canvases; MoMA's published medium line for **this 32-canvas set** is "synthetic polymer paint on thirty-two canvases." Use MoMA's exact wording for the set [S1].
