## 2. THE STORY

Paris Dada, 1920, at its most provocative. Picabia — a wealthy ex-Cubist who had become the movement's chief publisher and provocateur — ran an irregular, deliberately anarchic little review called ***391*** (1917–24, printed in Barcelona, New York, Zurich, and Paris). The magazine traded in lewd drawings, multilingual jokes, scrambled typography, and "exuberantly nihilistic" attacks on "good taste and sound morals" (Public Domain Review).

Into issue 12 (March 1920) Picabia dropped *La Sainte Vierge*: a single splatter of black ink flung onto a gray sheet, with the title **LA SAINTE VIERGE** lettered above it and his signature beneath, both in blue-violet ink. A portrait of the Virgin Mary — Catholic France's holiest figure, the very emblem of purity — rendered as a **stain**.

That is the whole work. **Blasphemy as economy.** It is the most minimal possible provocation: no skill, no subject, no labor — just a flick of ink and a sacred caption, the two colliding to insult both Church and Art at once. As scholars put it, the blot is "anti-religious, anti-pictorial, anti-aesthetic" (Public Domain Review). The joke cuts in several directions at once: an inkblot offers the Virgin **precisely the feature she theologically lacks — a stain** (a "homage in the form of an insult"); and the mark is "at once both not writing and not drawing," refusing to privilege word over image (the Dawn Ades / *Word & Image* reading).

It is also a **readymade-style gesture**: like Duchamp's ready-mades, the art is the *naming*, not the making. The mark is largely a product of **chance** — ink let fall and allowed to run — and the title is what converts an accident into "a work." Picabia called Dada itself the art of making the public "understand nothing" (see §4); *La Sainte Vierge* is that program compressed into one drop of ink.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required: before / after)

**Before.** A "Holy Virgin" / Madonna had been, for centuries, the supreme test of devotional craft — Raphael, Murillo, Bouguereau — where reverence for the subject and mastery of the hand were the entire point. Even Cubism, which Picabia had just left, still prized composition, facture, the considered mark.

**After.** *La Sainte Vierge* dethrones **both skill and subject in a single gesture.** A flick of the wrist — an accident, a stain, a chance mark — is titled as a sacred image, and that is the whole picture. What is on the page is no longer *depiction* and no longer even *drawing*; the content is the **gesture and the chance of the ink** plus the provocation of the name. Authorship shrinks to a choice and a caption.

**Why it matters downstream:**
- **Chance and gesture as content** prefigures the automatism the Surrealists made into method, and *art informel* / *tachisme* (Wols, Mathieu — the *tache*, the "blot," is literally Picabia's medium here).
- **The flung/dripped mark** as the work itself anticipates gestural abstraction / **Action Painting** (Pollock's poured skeins).
- **The title doing the work, not the hand** anticipates the Conceptual provocation — the idea/naming as the art (Duchamp's lineage, on to Manzoni, Klein, and after).

*(Synthesis from the cited scholarship — Public Domain Review, Ades, the* Dada/Surrealism *Joan-of-Arc essay — flagged as interpretive, not a single sourced sentence.)*

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — with the trap flagged)

There is **no reliably sourced quotation by Picabia *about this specific drawing*.** What is sourced is Picabia's own framing of Dada provocation/blasphemy in *391* no. 12 — the **same issue that carried *La Sainte Vierge*** — and in the cannibal manifesto of the same month. Use these as the "statement," attributed to Dada-the-program, not to the drawing.

**A — From Picabia's "Dada Manifesto," *391* no. 12, Paris, March 1920** (the issue that printed *La Sainte Vierge*):
> "Dada … wants nothing, absolutely nothing, and what it does is to make the public say: 'We understand nothing, nothing, nothing.'"

> "Art is a pharmaceutical product for idiots."

*(Source: Ubu Web text of the March 1920 Dada Manifesto; published in 391 no. 12. Translation wording varies — see variants below.)*

**B — From Picabia's "Manifeste Cannibale Dada" (read at the Salle Gaveau Dada festival, 27 March 1920):**
> "… like your idols: nothing / like your heroes: nothing / like your artists: nothing / like your religions: nothing."

*(Source: 391.org text. NOTE: this manifesto was performed/published in March 1920 but in the *Dadaphone* / festival context — **not** inside* 391 *no. 12. Keep it separate from A so we don't claim both ran beside the drawing.)*

**Variant flags:**
- The "pharmaceutical product" line appears as **"for idiots"** and as **"for imbeciles"** across translations of the same French original ("un produit pharmaceutique pour imbéciles"). Either is the same source line; prefer one and note it's a translation.
- "nothing, nothing, nothing" recurs across both manifestos and the closing signature ("Francis Picabia, who knows nothing, nothing, nothing"); cite the specific manifesto, not "Picabia said."

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **The splatter itself.** The "image" is one generous blot of black ink dropped onto the sheet — irregular, organic, with no contour, no figure, nothing you could call a Madonna except the words.
2. **The drips and their direction.** Fine runs trail off the main mass where the ink ran before it dried — the literal record of gravity and chance. (In the original drawing they run one way; the *391* plate is mirror-flipped, so the drips run the other way — a quick tell for which version you're looking at.)
3. **The caption lettering — "LA SAINTE VIERGE."** Hand-lettered across the top, plain and unceremonious; it is the title that performs the whole provocation, turning a stain into "the Blessed Virgin."
4. **The signature.** "Francis Picabia" inscribed below in the same blue-violet ink — the only conventional mark of "art" on the page, claiming authorship over an accident.
5. **The bare ground.** A near-empty gray sheet around the blot — no setting, no halo, no composition. The emptiness is the point: maximum sacred subject, minimum means.
6. **The deliberate crudeness.** Nothing is refined, corrected, or "finished." The roughness is the message — skill and finish are exactly what Dada is refusing.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct, with sources)

1. **"291" vs "391."** The Commons/Wikipedia file page text says the work was "Reproduced in **291**" — that is a **typo**. It ran in **Picabia's *391*, no. 12, March 1920.** *291* was a different (earlier, New York) magazine. ✅ Correct to *391*.

2. **One version vs several.** There is no single *La Sainte Vierge* object. At minimum: **(a)** the **drawing** now at the Pompidou (the wired image); **(b)** the **printed plate in *391* no. 12**, which is **cropped and mirror-reversed** relative to the drawing; **(c)** a separate **lithograph** (e.g. the Yale sheet). Scholarship even argues Picabia likely made **two closely related drawings in one session** on the same paper stock (cut from a larger sheet), one possibly rejected. **Do not** say "the original" as if singular without specifying which object. *(Sources: Centre Pompidou; Yale University Art Gallery; the* Dada/Surrealism *Joan-of-Arc essay.)*

3. **Medium — not just "an ink splatter."** It is **India ink (the blot) + graphite + ink (title/signature) on paper.** Calling it purely "an ink splatter" is close but undercounts the lettering/signature, which are part of the work and carry the blasphemy. The Pompidou wording is "Encre de Chine, mine graphite et encre sur papier." ✅

4. **"How it was made" — flung? thrown? dropped?** Popular accounts say Picabia *flung* or *threw* the ink. The careful scholarly reading is that the blot was **most likely made with an ink dropper / pipette from the bottle** and the ink allowed to **run by chance**, not necessarily hurled across the room. The honest line: **a chance ink blot with drips, applied (probably dropped/spattered) and let run** — avoid asserting a dramatic "flung across the studio" as fact. ⚠️ *Method is interpretive; sources differ. (Dada/Surrealism essay vs. popular retellings.)*

5. **Direction of the drips.** Because the magazine plate is reversed, captions that describe "drips running down-left" etc. will be **wrong for one of the two versions.** Describe the drips generically, or specify "in the drawing" vs "in the *391* plate."

6. **Dimensions.** Use **33 × 24 cm (~13 × 9.4 in)** — that is the **Pompidou drawing.** The Yale **lithograph** sheet is larger (55.5 × 37.4 cm); don't mix the two. ✅

7. **Date.** "1920" is correct; the *391* printing is **March 1920** specifically. Don't drift to 1919/1921.

8. **Quote attribution.** No verbatim Picabia line is documented *about the drawing itself.* Attribute the "nothing, nothing, nothing" / "pharmaceutical product" lines to the **manifesto**, and keep the *391*-issue manifesto (A) separate from the festival *Manifeste Cannibale* (B). See §4. ⚠️

1. **33 × 24 cm (~13 × 9.4 in)** — the size of the actual drawing; smaller than a sheet of letter paper. A "monument" of blasphemy you could hold in one hand.
