## 3 · NARRATIVE BEATS (~5; the spine the author builds chapters from)

### Beat 1 — The painter who would become the noise man
Russolo (b. 30 April 1885, Portogruaro; d. 1947) came from a musical family — father an organist, two elder brothers at the Milan Conservatory — but trained as a **painter**. He befriended **Umberto Boccioni** at the end of 1909 and joined the founding circle of Futurist painters. He **signed the *Manifesto of the Futurist Painters* (11 February 1910)** and the *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting* (1910), alongside Boccioni, Carrà, Balla, and Severini. `[DOCUMENTED]` ⟦S4⟧⟦S5⟧
**Precision flag:** Russolo did NOT sign Marinetti's *1909* founding manifesto (that was Marinetti's own literary launch in *Le Figaro*); Russolo's signatures are on the **painters'** manifestos of **1910**. Do not write "signed the 1909 manifesto." `[DOCUMENTED correction]` ⟦S5⟧

### Beat 2 — The car as the icon of the new age (Marinetti's roaring car)
Futurism worshipped the machine, the city, and above all **speed**. The founding text is Marinetti's *Manifesto of Futurism*, first published in French in ***Le Figaro*, Paris, 20 February 1909**. Its most famous line, **point 4**:
> "We affirm that the world's magnificence has been enriched by a new beauty: the beauty of speed. A racing car whose hood is adorned with great pipes, like serpents of explosive breath — a roaring car that seems to ride on grapeshot — is more beautiful than the *Victory of Samothrace*." `[DOCUMENTED — but verify wording; see flag]` ⟦S6⟧⟦S7⟧
**WORDING FLAG `[DISPUTED translation]`:** the English wording varies by translator. Common variants: "roaring motorcar / motor car," "machine-gun fire" vs "grapeshot," "Winged Victory of Samothrace" vs "Victory of Samothrace." The *Victory of Samothrace* is the ~2nd-c. BC Hellenistic marble in the **Louvre**. The author must quote ONE reputable published translation and cite it, or paraphrase — never blend variants into an invented composite. ⟦S6⟧⟦S7⟧
The painting is Russolo's attempt to put *that* idea on canvas: not a car, but the speed a car makes. `[DOCUMENTED link is thematic/interpretive — frame as "the Futurist program," not as a Marinetti instruction to Russolo]`

### Beat 3 — How Futurism painted motion (force-lines)
Futurist painting's signature device is the **force-line** (*linea-forza*) — directional lines that carry an object's movement and energy out into the space around it, so the painting shows not a frozen instant but the *passage* through space. Boccioni's *Technical Manifesto* (1910) called for putting "the spectator in the centre of the picture" and rendering "universal dynamism." In this canvas the car is driven left-to-right by repeated **chevron / arrowhead wedges**. `[DOCUMENTED — device + manifesto; the specific chevron reading is descriptive of the canvas, verify against image at look-closer gate]` ⟦S5⟧

### Beat 4 — Near-abstraction: the car dissolves into its own speed
The vehicle is barely legible as an object: the body breaks into faceted planes and the forward arrowheads, so the subject is the **sensation of velocity** rather than the machine. This pushes Futurism toward abstraction — a contemporary of Robert and Sonia Delaunay's Orphism and Balla's abstract speed studies (e.g. Balla's *Speed of an Automobile* / *Abstract Speed* series, 1913). `[DOCUMENTED that the work is near-abstract and contemporary with Balla's speed series; specific comparison framed as parallel, not influence-claim]` ⟦S2⟧⟦S4⟧

### Beat 5 — Russolo quits painting for noise (the bridge to *The Art of Noises*)
**In 1913 Russolo effectively abandoned painting** to pursue music. On **11 March 1913** he wrote *L'arte dei rumori* (*The Art of Noises*) — a manifesto in the form of a letter to the Futurist composer **Francesco Balilla Pratella**. He argued the industrial age had remade the human ear and that music must "break out of this limited circle of [pure] sounds and conquer the infinite variety of noise-sounds." With the painter **Ugo Piatti** he built the **intonarumori** ("noise-tuners") — boxes with hand-cranks and levers that produced families of noises (howlers, roarers, cracklers, hummers, gurglers, etc.). The **first concert of Futurist noise instruments** was in **Modena, 2 June 1913**; a larger London series followed in 1914. Russolo is now counted a founding ancestor of noise music, *musique concrète*, and electronic music (cited as influence on Varèse, Cage, Pierre Schaeffer). `[DOCUMENTED]` ⟦S3⟧⟦S4⟧
**This is the through-line that makes the work worth a deep read:** the same man who painted a car's *roar* as wedges of force, the next year set out to *play* the roar — the painting and the intonarumori are two attacks on the same target, the modern machine's energy. `[interpretive frame — fine if voiced as such]`

The image is a landscape canvas; the car reads as driving toward the right.

1. **The arrow / chevron force-lines** — *where:* repeated across the canvas, the nested V-shaped wedges pointing in the direction of travel. *detail:* These are *force-lines* (linee-forza), the Futurist device for carrying an object's energy out into the space around it. Stacked like arrowheads, they don't describe the car — they describe its push. `[device DOCUMENTED; exact placement → verify on image]`

2. **The dissolved car body** — *where:* the denser mass of faceted planes from which the wedges spring. *detail:* The actual vehicle is barely a vehicle. Its body shatters into overlapping planes, so you read "car" more from the title and the thrust than from any fender or wheel. The subject is the speed, not the machine. `[verify on image]`

3. **The directional thrust, left-to-right** — *where:* the overall sweep of the wedges and planes across the wide canvas. *detail:* The whole composition leans and drives one way, so the eye is pulled along the same path the car takes. On a canvas wider than it is tall, the format itself is a runway. `[verify direction on image — do NOT assert left/right until checked]`

4. **The color planes** — *where:* the bands and shards of color stepping back from the leading edges. *detail:* Color is laid in flat-ish planes rather than modeled rounded form, each plane a stage in the car's passage — closer to a diagram of motion than a portrait of a thing. `[verify palette on image]`

5. **The wedge composition** — *where:* the way the wedges fan out from a denser core toward the open field ahead. *detail:* The picture is built out of one repeated shape — the wedge — multiplied and rotated, so the structure of the painting *is* the structure of acceleration. `[verify on image]`

6. **Speed-as-form (the near-abstraction)** — *where:* anywhere you try, and fail, to find a recognizable car. *detail:* Step back and there is no clear automobile to point to — only the sensation of one tearing past. That refusal to render the object, in favor of rendering the *experience* of the object moving, is the whole Futurist wager, and it puts this canvas at the edge of pure abstraction. `[DOCUMENTED interpretive frame]`

## 6 · DATE DISPUTE (must be handled in stats + prose)

- Pompidou dates the work **1912–13**. ⟦S1⟧
- The canvas is **signed and dated 1911** by Russolo; the museum marks this **`[sic]`**, i.e. treats the painted date as unreliable. ⟦S1⟧
- Some commercial/print sources caption it **"1911"** (e.g. muzeo print listing) — this is the artist's own inscription, not the scholarly date. ⟦S8⟧
**Author handling:** use **1912–13** as the date (follow the museum), and you MAY note the wry fact that Russolo back-dated/signed it 1911 — but frame it as "the canvas is signed 1911, though the museum dates it 1912–13," `[DISPUTED]`, never assert a flat single wrong year.
