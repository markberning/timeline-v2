## 0. THE LOAN-STATUS WRINKLE — read this first (coordinator note)

The brief asked me to **verify** the collection/loan line "Mattioli Collection, on
long-term loan to the Peggy Guggenheim Collection, Venice." It is the canonical,
born-verified phrasing (it IS the Wikimedia Commons file title and the Guggenheim's
own press wording) — but it describes a loan that ran **1997–2015/16**, and the work
has since left Venice. Treat it as the *documented historical loan*, not a guaranteed
present-tense location. Specifics:

- **[DOCUMENTED]** Twenty-six works from the Mattioli Collection went on long-term
  loan to the Peggy Guggenheim Collection, Venice, opening **6 September 1997**. The
  Guggenheim's own press release calls *Dynamism of a Cyclist* "his most beautiful."
  (Guggenheim press release; Guggenheim "Masterpieces of Futurism" exhibition page.)
- **[DOCUMENTED / DISPUTED end date]** Sources put the END of the Venice loan at
  **2015 or 2016** — EN-Wikipedia (Gianni Mattioli article) says "1997 to 2015"; the
  Guggenheim's later material says "1997 to 2016." Use **"from 1997 to about 2016."**
- **[DISPUTED — current location]** After the Venice loan, sources diverge on where
  the Boccioni now hangs. The current EN-Wikipedia *Dynamism of a Cyclist* infobox
  reads **"Museo del Novecento, Milan (on long-term loan from the Gianni Mattioli
  Collection)."** Other tertiary listings still say "Mattioli Collection, Milan" or
  repeat the old Venice line. I could NOT find a single authoritative museum page
  confirming the present-day (2026) wall. **RECOMMENDATION for the author:** describe
  the Venice loan as the documented 1997–~2016 arrangement, name the Mattioli
  Collection (now under Laura Mattioli's stewardship) as the OWNER, and frame the
  current location with a hedge ("the Mattioli Collection, whose Futurist masterpieces
  were on long-term loan to the Peggy Guggenheim Collection, Venice, from 1997 to
  about 2016") rather than asserting a present-tense wall. Do NOT state flatly "now at
  the Peggy Guggenheim Collection" — that loan has ended. **This is the section's

## 2. HOOK (candidate, author may rework)

A racing cyclist, head down, hammering forward — except there is no man and no
bicycle left to find, only a single burst of curving force where rider, machine, and
the rushing air have fused into one speeding shape. Boccioni's bid to paint not a
cyclist but cycling itself.

## 3. KEY FIGURES (nameplates / cast)

- **Umberto Boccioni (1882–1916)** — [DOCUMENTED] Italian Futurist painter and
  sculptor; **the movement's leading visual artist and chief theorist.** Born 19 Oct
  1882 in Reggio Calabria; moved to Milan 1907, where he joined the circle around
  Marinetti. Died **17 Aug 1916** near Verona, age 33 — thrown and trampled by his
  horse during a cavalry/artillery training exercise the previous day (drafted May
  1916). (Wikipedia; Italian Art Society.) NOTE: he was a **founding signatory** of
  the painters' manifestos, NOT the founder of Futurism — that was Marinetti.
- **Filippo Tommaso Marinetti (1876–1944)** — [DOCUMENTED] poet; **founder of
  Futurism**, launched it with the *Manifesto of Futurism* (Paris, *Le Figaro*, 20
  Feb 1909). Boccioni met his Milan circle in 1907. (Wikipedia; Met essay.)
- **Gianni Mattioli (1903–1977)** — [DOCUMENTED] Milan textile businessman and
  collector; built one of the great private collections of early-20th-c. Italian art,
  especially Futurism, with Boccioni as its centerpiece. Knew the Marinetti/Futurist
  circle by the early 1920s. His daughter, art historian **Laura Mattioli (Rossi)**,
  inherited the collection and arranged the Peggy Guggenheim loan (opened 1997).
  (Guggenheim press release; Met "Modern Art Index"; Wikipedia.)
  - **[DISPUTED — formation dates]** EN-Wikipedia: collecting began early 1920s, "most
    of his collection built in the late 1930s and 1940s," and he bought 87 works from
    collector Pietro Feroldi in **1949**. The Guggenheim press release says the
    collection "was formed between **1949 and 1953**." Use the broader, sourced framing
    ("assembled mainly in the late 1930s–1940s, with a major 1949 purchase from the
    Feroldi collection") and do NOT pin a single tidy span.

## 4. NARRATIVE BEATS (~5, the author weights *looking*)

**Beat 1 — Late-1913 push toward near-abstraction.** [DOCUMENTED] *Dynamism of a
Cyclist* belongs to a 1913 burst of "dynamism" canvases — *Dynamism of a Human Body*,
*Dynamism of a Soccer Player* (*Dinamismo di un footballer*), and *Plastic Dynamism:
Horse + Houses*. Across this series Boccioni used a more vivid palette and thicker,
denser paint than before, and pushed the recognizable subject closer to dissolving
than at any earlier point in his work. (Wikipedia.)

**Beat 2 — The cyclist as pure speed/force, not a depicted man.** [DOCUMENTED] The aim
(stated in the Futurist painters' theory) was to paint "the dynamic sensation of a
cyclist moving through time and space rather than a snapshot of a particular moment."
The bicycle, the figure, and the surrounding space "seemingly fuse together in a
single form." There is no portrait, no readable face, no clean machine — the subject
is the *event* of speeding, not the speeder. (Wikipedia.)

**Beat 3 — "Plastic dynamism" and lines of force.** [DOCUMENTED] Boccioni's program
word was *plastic dynamism* (*dinamismo plastico*) — his idea that a moving body and
the space it tears through are one continuous plastic whole. The Futurists' signature
device was **"force lines"** (*linee-forza*): lines they claimed showed "how an object
would resolve itself if it followed the tendencies of its own forces." Boccioni's
preparatory drawings render the cyclist as head-down, "behind in the air," his motion
carried entirely by these curving force lines and echoing arcs. [DOCUMENTED] The
underlying idea drew on the philosopher **Henri Bergson**, whom the Futurists admired,
who held that material objects are in constant flux. (Wikipedia.)

**Beat 4 — How it differs from his figurative 1910 work.** [DOCUMENTED] Only a few
years earlier Boccioni was painting legible, peopled modern scenes — e.g. *The City
Rises* (1910–11), a recognizable scene of laborers and rearing horses building a city.
By 1913 the readable figure is nearly gone: in the *Cyclist* the body is broken into
interlocking planes and cones and threaded through its own motion. [DOCUMENTED]
Technically he carried over the **Divisionist** stroke he'd learned from Giacomo Balla
(separate dabs/strokes of color) but now combined it with **Cubist** plane-segmentation
absorbed on his Paris visits — while rejecting Cubism's static, still-life calm in
favor of speed. (Wikipedia; Met essay.) NOTE the source also remarks the "discordant
colour choices reflect the failure of the Futurists to develop a coherent colour
theory" — usable as a wry, sourced aside, not as praise.

**Beat 5 — Dissolving the object into its motion (the theory made paint).** [DOCUMENTED]
The Futurist *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting* (1910) had already declared the
goal; the *Cyclist* is that goal carried almost to abstraction. The work sits one short
step from non-representational painting while still insisting on a real subject in the
real modern world — the bicycle as an emblem of modern speed (still idealized in 1913,
though by then a decades-old machine). The "afterlife" beat: this near-abstract
treatment of motion fed directly into Boccioni's sculpture of the same moment (*Unique
Forms of Continuity in Space*, 1913) and into 20th-c. attempts to paint movement
itself. (Wikipedia.)

## 5. "LOOK CLOSER" ANNOTATIONS (6) — PROSE pointers, verified against the image

The page shows the whole canvas (tap to zoom); each pointer is a `{label, where,
detail}` describing what is literally there. **Verified by looking at the born-verified
Commons full-res image** (landscape, W 95 > H 70; the figure reads left-to-right). Where
a reading is interpretive it is hedged. The author should re-look at the image and may
refine `where` phrasing.

1. **The rider bent into the speed** — *where:* upper / center-left, the strongest
   forward-leaning thrust. The one foothold of the human subject: the cyclist's
   head-down, crouched-forward racing posture survives as a *direction* and a lean, not
   as a drawn man. [DOCUMENTED the pose is a head-down racer "behind in the air" from
   the prep drawings; that it survives only as a lean is observation of the canvas.]

2. **The wheel as circular force** — *where:* lower portion, the rounded, repeating
   arc-forms. Curved and circular shapes echo the spinning wheel, but no complete,
   drawn wheel exists; the circle has been smeared into the arc of its own rotation.
   [Observation of the canvas; consistent with the "no readable bicycle" sourced claim.]

3. **The diagonal force lines** — *where:* driving across the whole canvas, lower-left
   up toward the right. The *linee-forza*: long diagonal and curving lines that carry
   the eye and the motion across the picture, the Futurists' claimed device for showing
   an object "resolving itself" along its own forces. [DOCUMENTED the device and intent;
   their presence is plain on the canvas.]

4. **The near-dissolved body** — *where:* the center mass, where figure and machine
   meet. Rider and bicycle are broken into interlocking planes and cones and fused into
   one shape — you cannot cleanly say where the man stops and the machine starts. [DOCU-
   MENTED "the bicycle, figure, and surrounding space seemingly fuse into a single form."]

5. **The color planes of speed** — *where:* throughout, the patches of contrasting hue.
   The form is built from separate planes/strokes of vivid, often clashing color (the
   Divisionist stroke pushed into Cubist faceting). [DOCUMENTED the divisionist+cubist
   technique and the "more vivid palette / thicker paint" of the 1913 series.] HEDGE: do
   not over-claim a color *system* — sources note the Futurists never settled a coherent
   color theory, so describe it as energetic and deliberately discordant, not scientific.

6. **The absence of a fixed contour** — *where:* the outer edges of the form, where it
   bleeds into the surrounding ground. There is no closed outline sealing the cyclist
   off from the space around him; the body opens into, and is continued by, the space it
   is tearing through — Boccioni's "plastic dynamism," the object and its environment as
   one continuous whole. [DOCUMENTED the plastic-dynamism / fusion-with-space idea;
   the open contour is visible on the canvas.]
