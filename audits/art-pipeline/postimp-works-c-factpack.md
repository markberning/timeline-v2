## 1. Vision after the Sermon (Jacob Wrestling with the Angel) — `vision-sermon`

### Identity / catalog
- **Full title (EN):** *Vision after the Sermon (Jacob Wrestling with the Angel)*. **High.** (Also catalogued
  by NGS as "Vision of the Sermon.")
- **Original French:** *La Vision après le sermon (La Lutte de Jacob avec l'ange)*. **High.**
- **Artist:** Paul Gauguin (French, 1848–1903). **High.**
- **Date:** 1888, painted in Pont-Aven, Brittany. **High.**
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas. **High.**
- **Dimensions:** 72.2 × 91 cm = **28.4 × 35.8 in** (≈ 2 ft 4.4 in × 3 ft 0 in). **High.** (Wikipedia + NGS.)
- **Location:** Scottish National Gallery (National Galleries of Scotland), Edinburgh. **High.**
- **Accession:** **NG 1643**. **Med** (search-surfaced from NGS/Grokipedia; not seen on a fetched primary page).
- **Acquisition:** Purchased by the gallery in **1925 for £1,150**, by then-director **Sir James Caw**. **Med**
  (search convergence; primary NGS page blocked).

### Provenance chain (handle as Med — primary catalog page blocked)
- 1888–: retained by Gauguin in Brittany after he failed to gift it to a local church (below). **Med.**
- Through private hands after Gauguin's death (1903); ⚠️ **UNVERIFIED** intermediate owners/dealers
  (no reliable full chain surfaced — e.g. whether Theo van Gogh, Vollard, or a Russian collector held it; do
  NOT assert a specific dealer line).
- 1925: acquired by the Scottish National Gallery (NG 1643). **Med.**
- Note for author: a 2006 *Studies in Conservation* article ("Vision of the Sermon by Paul Gauguin: An
  Exploration of Making and Meaning") is the technical reference if a deeper provenance/material claim is needed.

### Four-altitude story
**Where it came from.** Summer 1888, Pont-Aven, an artists' colony in Brittany where Gauguin had been working.
The younger painter **Émile Bernard** arrived with theories aimed at overturning Impressionism. Out of their
exchange came **Synthetism**, and *Vision after the Sermon* is generally called its first complete statement.
**High.** Bernard's flat, outlined manner — itself drawn from Japanese woodblock prints and from the look of
medieval stained glass and *cloisonné* enamel — is the direct stylistic spur. **High.**

- **Define Cloisonnism** (be accurate): a style of flat areas of pure, unmodeled color separated by bold dark
  contour lines, named after **cloisonné enamelwork**, where thin metal strips (French *cloisons*, "compartments"
  / "partitions") hold each field of colored enamel apart — and visually echoing the lead cames between panes of
  **stained glass**. The dark outlines do the work that shading would in naturalistic painting. **High.**
- **Define Synthetism:** the broader Pont-Aven program of which Cloisonnism is the visual signature —
  *synthesizing* the motif, the artist's memory/feeling, and pure decorative form rather than copying nature.
  Core principles: abandon faithful representation, build from memory and emotion, lay down bold flat color,
  drop perspective and modeling. **High.**

**The making.** Gauguin compresses two worlds into one frame. In the foreground, a crowd of **Breton peasant
women in starched white coifs (*coiffes*) and black dresses** bow their heads, fresh from a sermon. **High.**
Behind/above them, against an **unnaturally pure red ground**, the biblical **Jacob wrestles the angel**
(Genesis 32:22–31). **High.** The red field is not landscape: Gauguin makes the ground itself a vision-color.
Some readings tie the red to "the river Jabbok" of the Jacob story (**Med/Low** — interpretive, sourced to
popular analysis, not to Gauguin). A **diagonal tree trunk** — repeatedly described as an **apple tree** (the
Edenic tree of knowledge) — cuts across the canvas and **divides the real (praying women) from the vision
(the wrestling match)**. **High** for the dividing diagonal; **Med** for "apple tree / tree of knowledge"
(secondary-source interpretation). The Jacob-and-angel figures are widely said to derive from **Hokusai's
wrestlers** (Bernard "designated" the source); flag as **Med** (attributed to Bernard, popular repetition).

**The reception.** Gauguin himself described and sketched the picture in a letter to **Vincent van Gogh**
(c. 26 Sept 1888), the same year. **High** (the van Gogh letters archive carries it). It was an early banner for
Synthetism among the Pont-Aven and Nabi circles.

**The afterlife.** Its 1925 purchase by Edinburgh was a bold move given strong British public hostility to
Post-Impressionism after Roger Fry's 1910/1912 London shows. **Med.** It is now treated as Gauguin's first
fully Synthetist masterpiece and one of the foundational works of the move away from Impressionism toward flat,
symbolic color. **High.**

### "Look closer" pointers (literally on the canvas)
1. **The red ground** — the entire field behind the figures is a flat, saturated red, not grass or earth; color
   here signals vision/inner state, not a real meadow. **High.**
2. **The white Breton coifs** — the tall starched white *coiffe* headdresses fill the foreground in a rhythm of
   pale shapes against black dresses. **High.**
3. **The diagonal tree trunk** — slices the canvas corner-to-corner, splitting the praying women (lower/near)
   from the wrestling vision (upper). **High.**
4. **Jacob and the angel, top right/upper area** — small relative to the women's heads and backs; the "subject"
   is deliberately pushed back and shrunk. **High** (this scale-inversion is exactly what the priest objected to).
5. **A cow** appears small near the dividing tree — a leftover of ordinary Breton field life intruding on the
   vision. **Med.**
6. **Dark contour lines everywhere** — every coif, face, and limb is bounded by a hard outline with no
   blended shading: the cloisonné/stained-glass effect made literal. **High.**

### Documented vs. legend
- **The church refusal — DOCUMENTED in outline, but pin the details carefully.** Gauguin tried to **donate the
  painting to a local Breton church and was turned down**. **High** that this happened. The *specifics* are
  shakier: **Émile Bernard recounted (1904)** that Gauguin offered it to the **priest at Nizon** (the parish
  church near Pont-Aven), who judged it non-religious — objecting that giant bonnets and peasants' backs filled
  the canvas while the sacred subject shrank to insignificance. **Med** (single-witness, told 16 years later).
  Some sources name **Névez** or say "two local churches." **Treat the village/priest specifics as Med and
  attribute to Bernard, not as flat fact.** The author may say "Gauguin offered it to a Breton parish church,
  which refused it" with full confidence.
- **Red = river Jabbok / apple = tree of knowledge** — **interpretive [LEGEND]-adjacent.** Popular and useful,
  but not anchored to Gauguin's own words. Mark as one reading, not established intent.
- **Hokusai wrestlers as source** — **Med**, attributed to Bernard; fine to state as "said to draw on."

## 2. Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going? — `where-do-we-come-from`

### Identity / catalog
- **Full title (EN):** *Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?* **High.**
- **Original French:** *D'où venons-nous ? Que sommes-nous ? Où allons-nous ?* **High.**
- **Artist:** Paul Gauguin. **High.**
- **Date:** **1897–98**, painted in **Tahiti** (signed/dated "P. Gauguin / 1897"). **High.**
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas (a coarse, heavy jute/sackcloth-type canvas). **High** (oil on canvas); **Med** for
  "jute/sackcloth" (widely reported).
- **Dimensions:** **139.1 × 374.6 cm** = **54.8 × 147.5 in** ≈ **4 ft 6.8 in × 12 ft 3.5 in** (the
  ~4.5 ft × ~12 ft mural scale the brief expects). **High.**
- **Location:** Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. **High.**
- **Accession:** **36.270**. **High** (Commons file page; matches "acquired 1936").
- **Credit line:** Tompkins Collection — Arthur Gordon Tompkins Fund. **Med** ⚠️ (standard MFA credit reported
  in secondary sources; the MFA primary page was blocked — verify exact donor-fund wording before publishing).

### Provenance chain (Med–High; from Wikipedia)
1. Sent to Paris 1898; first exhibited at **Galerie Vollard, Paris, 17 Nov–10 Dec 1898** (mixed reviews). **High.**
2. **Gabriel Frizeau** bought it from **Ambroise Vollard** for 2,500 francs, 1901. **Med.**
3. To **Galerie Barbazanges**, c. 1913. **Med.**
4. Norwegian collector **Jørgen Breder Stang** (by 1920). **Med.**
5. Sold via dealer **Alfred Gold**, 1935. **Med.**
6. **Marie Harriman Gallery**, New York, 1936. **High.**
7. **Museum of Fine Arts, Boston**, acquired **16 April 1936 for $80,000**. **High.**

### Four-altitude story
**Where it came from.** Gauguin's second, final Tahitian period, amid poverty, illness, and grief (his
favorite daughter Aline had died in early 1897). **Med.** He set out to make a summary statement — a
mural-scale philosophical testament he ranked above everything else he had done, "comparable to the Gospels"
in ambition. **Med** (paraphrase of Gauguin's own letters).

**The making.** A roughly **12-foot frieze** to be **read right to left**, tracing a human life: at the **right**,
a **sleeping infant** with three crouching women = birth/beginning; the **center** group (a standing figure
**picking fruit**, an Eve-like gesture of "sin"/knowledge) = the daily life of young adulthood; at the **far left**,
an **old woman drawn into herself near death** appears reconciled. **High.** Behind the central figures stands a
**blue idol** with raised arms, which Gauguin said represents **"the Beyond."** **High.** A **white bird** clutching
a lizard, which he said stands for **"the futility / uselessness of vain words."** **Med.** The whole title is
**inscribed in paint on the canvas, upper LEFT corner** (no question marks or dashes, all caps), and **signed/dated
upper RIGHT** ("P. Gauguin / 1897"). **High.**

**The reception.** First shown at Vollard, Nov–Dec 1898, to **mixed reviews.** **High.** The picture's
deliberate inscrutability divided critics from the start.

**The afterlife.** Now the centerpiece of Gauguin's late myth and one of the MFA's signature holdings; routinely
read as his testament and the high-water mark of Symbolist/Post-Impressionist ambition. **High.**

### "Look closer" pointers (literally on the canvas)
1. **The painted title, upper-left corner** — the three French questions lettered straight onto the canvas in a
   yellow field. **High.**
2. **Right edge — the sleeping infant** + three crouched women: where the life-cycle *begins* (so the eye must
   travel right→left). **High.**
3. **Center — the standing figure reaching up to pick fruit** (the Eve/knowledge gesture). **High.**
4. **The blue idol, left-of-center background**, arms raised — Gauguin's "Beyond." **High.**
5. **Far left — the old woman**, hunched, hands to her face, beside a white bird. **High.**
6. **The yellowed corners** — Gauguin painted the two upper corners as if it were a damaged fresco / gold-cornered
   tapestry, framing the frieze. **Med.**

### Documented vs. legend — THE SUICIDE ATTEMPT (handle with care)
- **What's documented:** In a **letter to Daniel de Monfreid (Feb 1898)** Gauguin wrote that he had **finished the
  painting and then gone into the mountains to kill himself with arsenic**, and that the dose failed (he vomited it
  up). He had foreshadowed the plan to Monfreid in **December 1897.** **High** that *he wrote this.*
- **Why it's [LEGEND]-flagged:** the entire account is **self-reported, retrospective, and dramatized.** Gauguin was
  a relentless self-mythologizer (his biographer Wayne Andersen: few men worked harder at building a self-image).
  The arsenic story dovetails suspiciously neatly with the painting's life-death-meaning theme. **Recent forensic
  work on teeth attributed to Gauguin found no arsenic** (cited re: syphilis treatment, but it weakens the
  "lived with arsenic on hand" picture). **No independent witness corroborates the attempt.**
- **Author guidance:** say plainly that *Gauguin claimed* he tried to kill himself after finishing it, that the
  only source is his own letter, and that scholars treat it as part-fact, part-self-promotion — neither flatly
  asserted nor dismissed. **This is a framing-gate item.**

### FRAMING FLAGS — colonial / ethical context (REQUIRED; surface, don't sanitize, don't lecture)
- **Adolescent "wives."** During his Tahitian years Gauguin took a series of very young Tahitian girls as
  partners/"wives" (*vahine*). **Teha'amana** (also "Tehura"), the model behind several Tahiti works, was
  **about 13** per Gauguin's own account in *Noa Noa*; the "marriage" was arranged in a single afternoon. **High**
  that this is what Gauguin recorded and what scholarship now foregrounds. Such arrangements were common among
  French colonists and were not legally binding; whether the girls consented in any meaningful sense is unknowable
  and the power imbalance was total. **The author MUST name this for *this artist* even though Teha'amana is not
  in this specific canvas** — the Tahitian "paradise" *Where Do We Come From?* depicts is inseparable from it.
- **Primitivism on a colonized people.** Gauguin's "primitive" Tahiti was a French colony whose culture French
  colonialism (and missionary Christianity) was already dismantling; much of the "authentic" imagery he painted —
  the blue idol included — was **invented or imported**, not living Tahitian religion. His project **romanticized
  and exoticized** a colonized people for a European art audience. **High** (this is the standard current scholarly
  framing — Concordia "Colonial Myth of Primitivism," Art UK, New Lines). **Framing-gate item.**
- **Tone:** state it honestly and briefly as context the modern viewer needs; do not turn the read into a tribunal,
  and do not let the masterpiece-status erase it.

## 3. At the Moulin Rouge — `moulin-rouge`

### Identity / catalog (from artic.edu JSON API — verbatim)
- **Full title (EN):** *At the Moulin Rouge*. **High.**
- **Original French:** *Au Moulin Rouge*. **High.**
- **Artist:** Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec (French, 1864–1901). **High.**
- **Date:** **1892–95** (AIC's official date display). **High.** (Some sources say 1893–95; AIC = 1892–95.)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas. **High.**
- **Dimensions:** **123 × 141 cm** = **48 7/16 × 55 1/2 in** ≈ **4 ft 0.4 in × 4 ft 7.5 in**. **High** (AIC API).
  (Wikipedia rounds to 123 × 140 cm.)
- **Location:** Art Institute of Chicago. **High.**
- **Accession:** **1928.610**. **High** (AIC API + Commons).
- **Credit line:** **Helen Birch Bartlett Memorial Collection.** **High** (AIC API).
- **Inscription:** stamped lower left with the artist's monogram. **High** (AIC API).
- **First displayed at AIC:** 23 Dec 1930. **Med** (Wikipedia).

### Provenance chain (from AIC, Med–High)
Maurice Joyant (by 1902) → Manzi → Jean Laroche (1926) → Paul Rosenberg → Reid & Lefevre, London (1928) →
**Frederick Clay Bartlett** → **Art Institute of Chicago, 1928** (given as part of the Helen Birch Bartlett
Memorial Collection, in memory of Bartlett's late wife). **Med** (chain via AIC API summary; the
Bartlett-memorial detail **High**).

### Four-altitude story
**Where it came from.** The **Moulin Rouge**, the Montmartre cabaret that opened in **1889** and became the icon
of Belle Époque Paris nightlife. **High.** Lautrec was a fixture there with **his own reserved table**, and the
painting is a **group portrait of the cabaret's regulars and performers** rather than a generic crowd. **High** (AIC).

**The making.** A steep, tipped-up composition: a long **diagonal balustrade/railing** drives the eye back to a
table of recognizable people, with the green-lit face of a woman lunging in at the right edge and two figures
(one tiny, one tall) crossing the back. Lit by the harsh **artificial electric light** of the venue — which is
exactly what gives the acid greens and chalky faces. **High** (AIC).

**The identifiable people (AIC's own list + Wikipedia placement):**
- **At/around the central table:** writer **Édouard Dujardin**; dancer **La Macarona**; photographer **Paul Sescau**
  (also spelled Secau); vintner/photographer **Maurice Guibert**; and, **seen from behind with flaming red-orange
  hair**, dancer/entertainer **Jane Avril** (the focal hairdo). **High** (AIC lists all; Wikipedia gives the seating).
- **Background, crossing left-to-right:** the painter **himself — a tiny figure — beside his much taller cousin,
  the physician Gabriel Tapié de Céleyran.** **High** (AIC).
- **Background right, fixing her hair at a mirror:** dancer **La Goulue** ("The Glutton," the Moulin Rouge's star
  can-can dancer). **High.**
- **Right foreground, face harshly under-lit a shocking acid green:** English dancer/singer **May Milton.** **High.**

**The reception / afterlife.** Now one of the most reproduced images of fin-de-siècle Paris and a cornerstone of
the Art Institute's Post-Impressionist holdings. **High.**

### "Look closer" pointers (literally on the canvas)
1. **The green-lit face, far right (May Milton)** — chin and cheek washed an unnatural acid/lurid green by the
   electric gaslight, lunging into the frame at the edge. **High.**
2. **Jane Avril from behind, center** — the burst of **orange-red hair** above the dark-clad table group is the
   visual anchor. **High.**
3. **Lautrec himself, tiny, in the back** — a short top-hatted figure beside the **conspicuously tall** Tapié de
   Céleyran; the height gap is the point, a wry self-insertion. **High.**
4. **La Goulue at the mirror, back right** — a woman arranging her hair, reflected in the greenish glass. **High.**
5. **The diagonal balustrade** sweeping in from the lower left — the tipped, cropped, photographic/Japanese-print
   framing that pitches you into the room. **High.**
6. **The monogram stamp, lower left** — Lautrec's interlocked-initials mark. **High.**

### Documented vs. legend — THE CUT-DOWN CANVAS (verify-and-state)
- **DOCUMENTED in substance:** the canvas was **cut down** (an **L-shaped strip along the bottom and right was
  removed**), and **later reattached** — by **1914** the cut section had been rejoined, restoring the composition
  to its present extent. **Med–High.** The removed strip contains the **right-edge figure, May Milton** (her
  green-lit face) and part of the foreground. **High** that the right strip = May Milton.
- **Open question:** **why** and **by whom** it was cut is **not settled** — possibly Lautrec or his dealer, to make
  it more saleable; the reason is **⚠️ UNVERIFIED.** State it as "for reasons that aren't known, the canvas was cut
  down and later (by 1914) rejoined." Do not assert a motive as fact.
- Author note: this is the source of the slightly odd, off-balance crop on the right — useful "look closer" beat.

### Toulouse-Lautrec — the man (state plainly, NOT as freak-show)
- Born **Henri Marie Raymond de Toulouse-Lautrec-Montfa, 24 Nov 1864, Albi**, into an old aristocratic family
  (Counts of Toulouse / Viscounts of Montfa); his parents were **first cousins.** **High.**
- As a teenager he **fractured his right femur at 13 and his left at 14**; the breaks did not heal properly and his
  **legs stopped growing**, leaving him about **1.52 m / 5 ft 0 in** with an adult torso on short legs.
  Modern medicine attributes this to a **genetic bone disorder (possibly pycnodysostosis, "Toulouse-Lautrec
  syndrome")**, not an accident alone. **High.**
- Trained under **Léon Bonnat** then **Fernand Cormon**, where he met Van Gogh and Émile Bernard; spent his short
  career embedded in Montmartre's dance halls, cafés, and brothels, which became his subject. **High.**
- **Died 9 September 1901, age 36**, at his mother's estate **Château Malromé**, of complications of **alcoholism
  and syphilis.** **High.**
- **Framing guidance:** present the bone disorder and short stature factually and with dignity — it shaped his life
  and his eye for the margins of Montmartre society; avoid period "freak"/spectacle language.

- `File:Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, At the Moulin Rouge.jpg` — alternate full reproduction. PD. **Med.**
