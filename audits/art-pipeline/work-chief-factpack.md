## 2. THE STORY

Franz Kline started as a representational painter — cityscapes, interiors, portraits, small black ink drawings. *Chief* is from the breakthrough that made him famous: huge, fast, **black-on-white gestural abstraction**, broad black bars and slashing strokes laid across a white field. The black forms swell, taper, and run off the edges of the canvas, reading as girders, tracks, rolling stock, speed — without ever depicting any of those things.

**The title.** *Chief* is the name of a **locomotive** Kline remembered from his Pennsylvania childhood (he grew up in the coal-and-railroad country around Wilkes-Barre and Lehighton and loved the railway). Many of his black-and-white canvases carry titles of this kind — names of trains, places, mechanical things — and viewers read engines, bridges, and machinery into them, but Kline insisted the titles and forms were not pictures of those subjects "because they actually resemble the subject." (MoMA Learning; Wikipedia; Smarthistory paraphrase.)

**The white is painted too.** This is the load-bearing point for any caption. *Chief* is **not** black signs drawn on a blank white ground. Kline painted the white as actively as the black; the white pushes back into and around the black, so figure and ground trade places and both are doing structural work. His own line: *"I paint the white as well as the black, and the white is just as important."* (See §4.)

**NOT calligraphy / not Zen.** Because they're black marks on white, the paintings were endlessly compared to Japanese/Chinese calligraphy and to Zen brushwork. Kline **rejected** this. He said he wasn't "painting black signs on a white ground," and in a 1960 interview pushed back directly: *"calligraphy is simply the art of writing,"* and he denied that the work was about "the equalization of the proportions of black… against a form of white." There were real points of contact with the Japanese avant-garde calligraphy world (the Bokujinkai group around Morita Shiryū; the journal *Bokubi*), but Kline later denied that connection. Treat "Kline = Zen calligraphy" as a reading he refused, not as fact. (MoMA Learning; ASX / David Sylvester 1960 interview; Wikipedia.)

**The discovery story (standard account, possibly embellished — see §8).** The familiar origin story: in 1948, in Willem de Kooning's studio, Kline projected a small (~4 × 5 in) black brush drawing — often told as a drawing of a rocking chair (Elaine de Kooning's account names a "rocking chair") — onto the wall using a **Bell-Opticon projector**. Enlarged, the little marks "loomed in gigantic black strokes which eradicated any image," and the abstract, sweeping force of the magnified line set him on the black-and-white path. Many of his subsequent canvases reproduce a small drawing at mural scale, fusing the improvised with the deliberate. (MoMA Learning; Smarthistory; Wikipedia.) This is the **standard account** but is widely flagged as romanticized (§8).

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK *(required — before / after)*

**Before** — In the late 1940s, ambitious American abstraction mostly still trafficked in color, in all-over fields (Pollock's drips), in pictographic or mythic signs (Gottlieb, early Rothko), or in de Kooning's churning, color-loaded figuration. Even gestural painting kept a lot of *stuff* on the canvas — color, atmosphere, multiplicity of marks.

**The break (what *Chief* does that's new):**
1. **Radical reduction to black-and-white gesture, at wall scale.** Kline throws out color, atmosphere, and depicted objects and bets everything on a handful of huge black strokes against white. The painting is almost entirely *one gesture, enlarged* — drawing made monumental.
2. **The stroke as structure, not as contour or sign.** The black bar isn't outlining a thing or writing a character; it *is* the architecture of the picture. The marks behave like girders and beams — they bear weight, brace against each other, span the canvas. And because the white is painted too, the composition is a genuine two-way structure of black and white, not marks on a blank.
3. **Speed and the body.** The strokes look thrown — fast, wide, loaded — so the painting registers the **physical act and velocity** of making it. This is exactly the territory the critic Harold Rosenberg would name **"action painting"** in 1952: paint as the trace of a bodily event. *Chief* (1950) is a defining early instance.

**After** — Black-and-white gestural abstraction at architectural scale becomes a recognized idiom; Kline's "girder" stroke and the action-painting reading feed directly into how 1950s–60s painting (and later Minimalism's interest in scale and the literal mark) gets discussed.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced)

> **"I paint the white as well as the black, and the white is just as important."**
> — Franz Kline

**Source / status:** This is Kline's best-known statement and is the one MoMA itself quotes in its *Chief* commentary, in the context of rejecting the calligraphy comparison: *"…Kline did not see himself as painting black signs on a white ground; 'I paint the white as well as the black,' he said, 'and the white is just as important.'"* (MoMA Learning page on *Chief*.) **HIGH confidence on wording.**

**Variants / flags:**
- The quote is often split/punctuated as two clauses (*"I paint the white as well as the black," … "and the white is just as important."*) — same words, just MoMA's parenthetical framing. Quote it as the single sentence above.
- Some secondary sources attach a **date of 1958** to the statement and tie it to his pushback on the calligraphy label; the date is not firmly nailed down across sources, so **do not assert a specific year/venue** unless a primary citation is found. (Secondary sources via search; **MED**.)
- A related, separately useful primary line, from his **1960 interview with David Sylvester** (publ. *Interviews with American Artists*, 2001): *"calligraphy is simply the art of writing"* — usable if the looking-points want Kline's own rejection of the calligraphy reading. **MED-HIGH** (single secondary transcript, ASX; verify against the printed Sylvester interview before quoting in body).

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The broad black bars.** A few wide, loaded black strokes dominate the canvas — they don't outline anything, they *are* the structure. Follow how they cross, brace, and lean on one another like beams or girders.
2. **The white is painted, not blank.** Look at the white around and *through* the black: it's worked, brushed, sometimes overlapping the black at the edges. The white shoulders into the black so the two trade places — neither is just "background."
3. **Speed at the stroke edges.** The strokes look thrown fast: watch the ragged, dragging, dry-brush and dripped edges where the loaded brush moved quickly — the painting carries the velocity of the gesture.
4. **Architectural / girder-like structure.** The whole thing reads like framing, trusses, track, or rolling stock — a built, weight-bearing scaffold — even though nothing is depicted.
5. **No depicted object.** There is no figure, no scene, no thing. Resist the urge to find the locomotive: the title points at a *feeling* of power and speed, not a picture. The forms run off the canvas edges, implying motion continuing past the frame.
6. **Scale and the body.** At roughly 5 × 6 ft, a small drawing's gesture has been blown up to room/body scale — your whole body, not just your eye, reads the strokes. The bigness is the point: drawing made monumental.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

1. **The Bell-Opticon projector "discovery" story — STANDARD ACCOUNT, likely embellished.**
   The story (1948, de Kooning's studio, a tiny black drawing — often "a rocking chair" — projected huge, "eradicating any image" and launching the black-and-white style) is the *standard, repeated-by-MoMA account* and should be told as such. **But flag it as possibly romanticized:** it compresses a gradual shift into one eureka moment. Wikipedia notes (via Elaine/Willem de Kooning) that de Kooning himself said it "took quite a while to work it out" — i.e., the overnight-conversion framing oversimplifies. **Tell it as the standard story; signal that it's the standard account, possibly tidied up, not a documented single instant.** (MoMA Learning; Wikipedia.)

2. **"Chief is Zen / Japanese calligraphy" — a reading Kline REJECTED.**
   The black-on-white look invites the calligraphy/Zen comparison, and Kline knew the Japanese calligraphy scene (Bokujinkai, *Bokubi*). **But Kline explicitly rejected the equation:** he did not see himself "painting black signs on a white ground," said *"calligraphy is simply the art of writing,"* and later denied the Bokujinkai connection. Do **not** present *Chief* as calligraphy or Zen brushwork; present that reading as one Kline refused. (MoMA Learning; ASX/Sylvester 1960; Wikipedia.)

3. **"Chief" = a person / a Native American chief — NO. It's a LOCOMOTIVE.**
   The title is the name of a **train/locomotive** from Kline's Pennsylvania railroad-town childhood, not a person or a portrait. (MoMA Learning; Smarthistory.) (Aside: the Santa Fe's famous "Chief" trains ran out West; sources tie Kline's title to a train he knew growing up in PA — keep the claim to "named after a locomotive he remembered," don't over-specify which line unless a source nails it.)

4. **"The white is just blank canvas / unpainted ground" — WRONG.**
   The white is **painted and active**; black and white are co-equal structural elements. This is the single most-corrected misreading and is backed by Kline's own statement (§4). Captions must not call the white "empty" or a "blank ground."

5. **Dimensions — keep MoMA's figures, don't round wrong.**
   MoMA: **148.3 × 186.7 cm = 58 3/8 in × 6 ft 1 1/2 in.** Some sources cite it as "148.3 cm × 186.7 cm" only; convert/quote consistently and use ft/in in the app per house rule (≈ 4 ft 10 3/8 in × 6 ft 1 1/2 in). Don't invent a square or a giant-mural size.

6. **Credit line precision.** It's **"Gift of Mr. and Mrs. David M. Solinger,"** not "Gift of David M. Solinger" — use the full MoMA wording.
