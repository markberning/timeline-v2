## 1. Eurocentric overclaim

**Verdict: FAIR**

The opening sentence plants the scope firmly: "In France around 1850 there was exactly
one way to become a painter…" That frame is carried throughout — the Salon, the
Académie, the École, the hierarchy, the daguerreotype impact, Haussmann — all are
France-specific or Paris-specific claims. No sentence states or implies "the only way
to be a painter" or "modern art begins here" as a universal. The closing "make it new"
and avant-garde passage gestures toward a larger movement, but the framing reads as
French cultural history, not a claim about the world. Nothing to fix.

## 2. Fairness to the establishment

**Verdict: FAIR**

The Académie is not a cartoon villain here. The draft consistently acknowledges that
the system had coherent logic, real authority, and worked on its own terms:

- "it had worked beautifully for the people inside it" (stakes section, final paragraph)
- The hierarchy of genres is explained as having principled internal logic ("gods at the
  top, a bowl of fruit at the bottom"), not mocked as arbitrary
- The Prix de Rome is described as a genuine prize — State-funded, career-making — not
  a sham
- The Cabanel section reads the painting as "technically faultless," giving it its due
  before calling it "slightly too sweet"
- The closing paragraph explicitly says the Académie's position was "settled and serene"
  and frames its coming fall as a structural problem ("could no longer hold") driven by
  market change, not moral failure

The parenthetical at the close of Section 5 — "(That is the institution's attitude put
into plain words, not a quotation — nobody at the Académie said it quite like that.)"
— is an especially clean move: it lets the author characterise the system's stance
without inventing a quote and explicitly flags it as an author gloss. That is a model
of honest framing at era altitude.

One minor observation (SHOULD-FIX, not MUST-FIX): the phrase "It was a closed system"
in that same paragraph could read as a slight: it names a structural fact but without
the prior softening. Given that the whole passage already acknowledges the system
worked, the phrase is defensible — it's accurate and necessary for the "could no
longer hold" turn. Not blocking.

## 3. Erasure of women — MUST-FIX

**Verdict: TILTED**

### The finding

The chapter describes "a student" in the standard training pipeline who "spent years
drawing — first from plaster casts of antique statues, then from the live model —
before he was trusted to touch serious paint at all." The pronoun "he" is not a generic
or default usage here: it accurately describes who the student actually was, because
women were structurally barred from that exact path for the entire period the chapter
covers (pre-1850 to c.1863). The chapter never acknowledges this.

This is a meaningful omission at era altitude, not a pedantic one, for three reasons:

1. **The life-drawing prohibition was the central mechanism of exclusion.** The
   academic system placed mastery of the nude model at the core of the curriculum —
   especially for history painting, the top genre in the hierarchy just described. The
   door to the highest rungs was literally locked by the denial of life drawing, not
   merely by convention or sexism. The chapter's detailed account of that training
   pipeline, without noting women were excluded from it, implies the ladder was open
   to all.

2. **The chapter argues this ladder was "the only way."** The opening paragraph's
   central claim is "there was exactly one way to become a painter." If that way was
   formally closed to women, saying so in one clause is part of the honest account
   of what the system was.

3. **Women did exhibit at the Salon.** Roughly 15–16% of exhibitors at the 1848 Salon
   were women; that share grew through the century. The existence of women in the
   exhibition does not resolve the training exclusion — they were reaching the Salon
   despite the system, not through the same ladder — but it means this is not a topic
   the chapter can silently skip.

### Verified facts (for the author's use; all must be fact-checked before integration)

- Women were not admitted to the École des Beaux-Arts as regular students until **1897**,
  after a campaign by reformists. This is the figure cited by multiple scholarly sources
  (Wikipedia's École des Beaux-Arts article; TheCollector; DailyArt Magazine; search
  results from the UC Press *Journal of the Society of Architectural Historians* 2010
  article "The École des Beaux-Arts: Toward a Gendered History").
- Even after 1897 admission, women were taught in a **separate, women-only atelier** and
  could not compete for the Prix de Rome until 1902–03 (sources vary by one year). The
  first woman to win the Prix de Rome was Lili Boulanger in 1913 (music section; the
  fine arts section admitted women even later).
- Throughout the chapter's period (1840s–1860s), women who wanted serious training went
  to **private ateliers** (Académie Julian, Académie Colarossi, Académie Vitti) or
  private studio study — the Académie Vitti being notable as one of the few that allowed
  women to sketch nude male models.
- The exclusion from life drawing was **explicitly tied to the hierarchy of genres**: it
  was the mechanism that kept women out of history painting (the top rung) and funnelled
  them toward still life, genre, and portraiture (the lower rungs the chapter describes).
  Source: Met Museum essay "Women Artists in Nineteenth-Century France"
  (metmuseum.org/essays/women-artists-in-nineteenth-century-france).

### What the fix should look like

A single parenthetical or brief clause in the training section — not a sub-chapter, not
a lecture, not an apology. Era altitude: acknowledge the structural fact, show the
mechanism, move on.

**Suggested location:** The sentence currently reads: "At the École des Beaux-Arts
(the state art school) a student spent years drawing — first from plaster casts of
antique statues, then from the live model — before he was trusted to touch serious
paint at all."

**One approach (coordinator to tune the voice):**
> At the École des Beaux-Arts a student spent years drawing — first from plaster casts
> of antique statues, then from the live model — before he was trusted to touch serious
> paint at all. (The "he" is not a generic pronoun. The École did not admit women until
> 1897, and the life-drawing class — the one step above all others that gated entry to
> history painting — was the locked door. Women who wanted serious training went to
> private ateliers, not the State machine.)

Or more briefly, if that parenthetical is too heavy for the pace:
> …before he was trusted to touch serious paint at all — and "he" is the right pronoun:
> the École did not admit women until 1897, so this entire ladder ran on male feet.

The author should choose the approach; the essential content is: (a) women excluded from
the École for the whole period discussed, (b) the year (1897), (c) the mechanism
(life-drawing prohibition as the bar to history painting), (d) what women did instead
(private ateliers). Items (a) and (b) are MUST-ADD; (c) and (d) are SHOULD-ADD as
context that makes (a)/(b) meaningful rather than decorative.

## 5. Romantic-genius myth

**Verdict: FAIR**

The closing reference to "the rebels" and the hand-off to Courbet's tent ("he simply
built his own tent across the street") does not pre-glorify a lone titan. Courbet is not
named, the move is described as a structural response (the jury turned his pictures down;
he went around the jury), and the closing "avant-garde" definition explicitly describes
it as a collective noun — "the painters of the chapters ahead," plural. The "make it
new" framing is likewise plural and attributed to no individual. No romantic-genius myth
present.
