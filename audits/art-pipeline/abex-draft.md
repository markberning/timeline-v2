## hook (one sentence)

After the war, painting crossed the Atlantic: New York decided a picture did not have to be *of* anything.

## hookLong (2-4 sentences)

For the first time an American movement led the world, or so the standard account goes, and Paris handed the avant-garde to New York. A loose group later called the New York School made the canvas, in one critic's phrase, "an arena in which to act," big enough to swallow the viewer and built to record the body, the gesture, and the unconscious directly. The single idea split into two wings that look nothing alike: the gesture painters who put the act of painting on the wall, and the color-field painters who built vast glowing fields aimed at the sublime.

**prose (3 paragraphs):**

1. Every radical European movement before the war still organized a picture around something. Cubism shattered a room into planes, but it was a room. Surrealism, in the read right before this one, painted dreams, but a dream is still an image of something, built by a deciding mind and arranged with a focal point. The painting was a window or a design you read, and the avant-garde that made it lived in Paris. de Chirico's eerie square, with its Greek head and red glove nailed to a wall, is that whole tradition in one canvas: strange, yes, but still a composition of recognizable things.

2. In New York after the war, painting dropped the subject entirely and got very big. The canvas became, in the critic Harold Rosenberg's phrase, "an arena in which to act." There were two opposite ways to do it. In the gesture wing, the painting is the record of the artist's body and unconscious moving: Pollock pouring from all four sides of a canvas laid on the floor, de Kooning slashing a figure into being, Kline's wall-sized black strokes. There is no focal point and no center; the marks run edge to edge, what critics called the **all-over composition** (a picture with no top, bottom, or middle, the same density everywhere). In the color-field wing, the painting is a vast field of color built for stillness: Rothko's floating rectangles, Newman's single vertical band splitting an open expanse.

3. That is why this counts as a new movement and not a new style. It did not change how the window looked. It threw the window out. The subject of the painting is no longer what you see in it; it is the act of making it, or the field of color itself, at a scale meant to surround you. And it happened in New York, which is the other half of the break. For three hundred years the new thing in painting came from Europe. After 1945, for the first time, the new thing came from America, and the rest of the world started watching New York.

## manifesto — ABSENT (`absent: true`, with KEY TEXTS as surrogates)

**absent:** true

**prose (tell the silence, then the surrogates):**

1. Surrealism got a manifesto and a pope. Abstract Expressionism got neither. It was never a club with a rulebook; it was a loose set of New York painters who mostly distrusted programs, plus two critics who fought over what they were doing. There is no founding document that names the movement and lays down its rules. The nearest thing to a founding statement is a letter two of them sent the *New York Times* in 1943, and after that the key texts arrive one at a time, by individual hands, often years apart.

2. The 1943 letter is the closest thing to a quasi-manifesto. Adolph Gottlieb and Mark Rothko wrote it (with Barnett Newman a co-editor) in reply to a critic, Edward Alden Jewell, who had dismissed their work, and it was published on 13 June 1943. Its most-quoted line draws the whole future of the movement in one sentence: there is no such thing as good painting about nothing. The subject, they insisted, is everything, and only the tragic and timeless will do. Note the attribution carefully: it is Gottlieb and Rothko's, with Newman, not "Rothko's manifesto," and it is a letter, not a manifesto in the strict sense.

3. After that, four voices, no chorus. Pollock published a short statement called "My Painting" (in the journal *Possibilities*, which Robert Motherwell co-edited, winter 1947–48) describing how he worked on the floor so he could, in his words, "literally be in the painting." Newman published "The Sublime Is Now" (in *The Tiger's Eye*, December 1948), the color-field wing's credo: a new American art reaching for the sublime, free of European "beauty." And the critic Harold Rosenberg published "The American Action Painters" (in *ARTnews*, December 1952), the essay that coined "action painting" and called the canvas "an arena in which to act." Two of these are painters' statements, two are a critic's framing, and "action painting" was a label Rosenberg imposed, never the painters' own chosen name. Four voices, no manifesto.

## Chapter 1 — The center of art crosses the ocean

### New York · the 1940s · The capital moves

For three centuries, if you wanted to find the newest thing in painting, you went to Europe, and usually to Paris. The Impressionists, the Cubists, the Surrealists were all Paris stories. Then the war broke Europe, and the capital of art moved across an ocean. By the late 1940s the new thing in painting came out of New York, and for the first time in the history of Western art the rest of the world had to look to America to see what came next. That, at least, is the standard account, and it is the one large fact this whole read sits on. How it happened is a story of refugees, a New Deal jobs program, a teacher with a German accent, and a bomb. (It is also, fair warning, a movement whose painters held a frankly enormous opinion of what they were doing; a little of that self-belief is the price of admission.)

Start with the refugees. As the Nazis overran Europe, its artists ran, and a remarkable number of them ran to New York. The Surrealists came almost as a group: André Breton, the movement's organizer, plus Max Ernst, André Masson, and the younger Roberto Matta, along with the abstract painters Piet Mondrian and Fernand Léger. The previous read, on Surrealism, tells that exile in full, so we will not retell it here. What matters is what the émigrés (a French word for people forced into exile, pronounced roughly "EM-i-grays") carried in their luggage: a method. That method was **automatism**, the Surrealist practice of making marks straight from the unconscious with the editing, reasoning mind switched off, so that chance and buried impulse do the composing rather than a deliberate plan. The whole point was **abstraction**, painting with no recognizable objects or scenes in it, and these refugees handed the Americans the tool that would get them there.

### Gorky, the human hinge

If you want a single person who is the bridge from Surrealism into this new American painting, it is **Arshile Gorky**, and he deserves more than the word "bridge." He was born Vostanik Manoug Adoian, around 1904, in what was then Ottoman Armenia. He survived the Armenian Genocide, the mass killing and deportation of Armenians during the First World War, reached America as a young man, and reinvented himself wholesale, taking the name Arshile Gorky and a half-invented past to go with it. He spent the 1930s absorbing Cézanne and Picasso almost in sequence, like a student working through a syllabus, and in the 1940s he finally found his own voice by fusing Surrealist automatism with abstraction. His great painting *The Liver Is the Cock's Comb* (1944, now at the Buffalo AKG) is a large, turbulent canvas of biomorphic near-shapes in acid color, somewhere between a body-map and a hallucination, the literal hinge from Surrealist automatism to Abstract Expressionism. [IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only.] Then his luck collapsed: a 1946 studio fire destroyed much of his work, cancer followed, and in 1948 he died by suicide, just as the movement he had done so much to seed was taking off. He helped open the door and did not live to walk through it.

### Automatism becomes gesture

Automatism was the single most important thing the young Americans took from the Europeans, and they changed it on the way. The Surrealists had used automatism mostly to dredge up dream-images, recognizable things from the unconscious. The Americans, Pollock and Gorky and the young Robert Motherwell among them, kept the part where you let the hand run unsupervised, but dropped the dream-pictures. What was left was pure gesture: the movement of the arm and the loaded brush across the surface, recorded as paint, meaning nothing but itself. Automatism turned into the act of painting. Hold that word, **gesture** (here it means the physical mark of the artist's moving hand and body, the brushstroke or poured line as a direct trace of motion), because half this movement is built on it.

There was a second European in the story, and he came not as a refugee passing through but as a teacher who stayed. **Hans Hofmann** (1880–1966) had left Germany before the war and set up an art school in New York and Provincetown, and nearly every important American modernist of the next generation passed through one of his classrooms or his orbit. Hofmann preached a theory he called **"push and pull"**: that flat patches of color and shape, arranged right, can make a flat canvas seem to advance and recede, to have depth and tension, without ever pretending to be a window onto a real room. He gave the Americans a way to think about a painting as an arrangement of color and force on a flat surface, an object in its own right rather than a picture of something. Lee Krasner, who becomes important later in this read, studied with him.

### The WPA, the bomb, and "tragic and timeless"

None of these painters could have afforded to keep working through the 1930s without help, and the help came from the United States government. The **WPA Federal Art Project** (part of the Works Progress Administration, the New Deal program that put millions of unemployed Americans to work during the Great Depression) paid artists a modest salary to make art: murals, easel paintings, prints. Pollock, de Kooning, Gorky, Krasner, and Rothko all drew WPA paychecks in the 1930s. It kept them alive, and just as important, it kept them in the same city, broke and ambitious together, building the dense New York scene that would become the movement. The avant-garde (the artists who get somewhere first) needs a place to gather, and the WPA accidentally built one.

Then came the war's end and the bomb. The painters who emerged in the mid-1940s had lived through a global slaughter and the arrival of a weapon that could erase a city in a flash, and they wanted a painting equal to that weight. Many of them turned toward myth, ritual, and the unconscious, looking for subjects big and old enough to carry the moment. Pollock went into Jungian analysis (the therapy of Carl Jung, who held that beneath the personal unconscious lay a deeper layer of universal myths and symbols shared by all humans), and his early titles call up totems and rites. Their ambition got a public statement in 1943, when Adolph Gottlieb and Mark Rothko, with Barnett Newman, wrote a letter to the *New York Times* in reply to a critic, Edward Alden Jewell, who had dismissed their strange new work. Its most famous line drew the line they all stood on: there is no such thing as good painting about nothing. The subject was everything, they insisted, and only the tragic and timeless would do.

### The bar and the Club

A movement is not only paintings; it is the room where the painters argue. For Abstract Expressionism there were two such rooms, a block apart in Greenwich Village. The first was a bar. The **Cedar Tavern** (properly the Cedar Street Tavern, at 24 University Place) was the movement's social engine from about 1950: a plain, smoky, unglamorous place where Pollock, de Kooning, Kline, Joan Mitchell, Krasner, and Frankenthaler drank, argued theory and women and money, threw punches, slept with each other, and made and broke reputations over the course of a night. Pollock once tore the door of the men's room off its hinges. If you want the noise of this movement, it is here: cigarette haze, raised voices, a painter being told to his face that his last show was garbage.

The second room was the brain. **The Artists' Club**, known simply as "the Club," was founded in 1949 at 39 East 8th Street as the organized intellectual forum the bar could never be. Friday nights it held panels, lectures, and debates: artists, critics, and the odd visiting philosopher hashing out what abstraction was for, whether the figure was dead, what "the sublime" meant in a Manhattan loft. The Cedar supplied the heat and the Club supplied the talk, and between them they were the movement's Montmartre, the cheap, crowded neighborhood where a generation decided together what it was doing.

That is the ground. Refugees who brought a method, a hinge-figure who fused it with abstraction, a teacher who gave them a theory, a jobs program that kept them together, a war that demanded a heavy painting, and finally a bar and a club where it all got argued into shape. Out of that ground grew one big idea, that a painting need not be *of* anything, which is what people meant by **Abstract Expressionism**: abstract, with no recognizable subject, and expressionist, made to deliver raw feeling. That one idea promptly split into two wings that look like opposites. The next two chapters take them one at a time.

[MEANWHILE: Paris, the 1940s. The city that had been the capital of art for a century came out of the Occupation exhausted and broke, its leading avant-garde scattered or in exile. The center of gravity did not so much leave Paris as fail to return to it. Critics still argue about exactly when New York "won," and the claim that this was the first American movement to lead world art is a widely held judgment rather than a measured fact, but by the early 1950s the international art world was unmistakably watching New York, not Paris, for the next move.]

## Chapter 2 — The canvas as an arena

### The floor · Pollock and the pour

The picture most people have in their heads when they hear "Abstract Expressionism" is one man flinging paint, and the man is Jackson Pollock (1912–1956). He was a Westerner, born in Cody, Wyoming, and raised in Arizona and California, and he carried the cowboy-loner persona into the New York art world, romanticizing Native American sand-painting (made by trickling colored sand onto the ground) as a model for an art made flat on the floor; the heavy drinking that would eventually kill him was already part of his life during these peak years, not just at the end. Around 1947 Pollock did something that sounds simple and was not: he took the canvas off the easel and laid it flat on the floor of his barn studio in Springs, on the eastern end of Long Island. Then, instead of touching brush to cloth, he walked around all four sides of it and flung, dripped, and poured thinned house enamel from a stick or a stiff brush or straight from the can, letting the paint fall in long looping skeins. He could move over and into the canvas, working from every direction, with no top and no bottom while he worked. In his short statement "My Painting," he described why the floor mattered: down there he felt nearer to the work, able to walk around it, work from the four sides, and "literally be in the painting."

Picture the result, because none of it can be shown here. *Autumn Rhythm (Number 30)* of 1950 is almost nine feet tall and more than seventeen feet wide, a wall of poured and dripped line in black, white, and tan, threads of paint crossing and looping and tangling over the whole surface. There is no figure, no horizon, no center. Your eye cannot rest on a main thing, because there is no main thing; the density is the same in the corners as in the middle. Critics gave this a name: the **all-over composition**, a picture with no focal point and no hierarchy, the same all the way across, more like a field or a weather system than a scene. *One: Number 31, 1950*, a companion from the same peak year, is an even denser tangle of black, white, and grey drips at the same mural scale.

### "Action painting," and a caveat

The critic Harold Rosenberg, whom the fourth chapter is partly about, gave this kind of work a name that stuck: **action painting**. The phrase means exactly what it says: the painting is the record of an action, the physical act of the artist moving and marking, frozen on the surface. The canvas, Rosenberg wrote, had become "an arena in which to act," a place to do something rather than a space to depict something. Two warnings about that label, both load-bearing. First, it is a critic's word, coined in 1952, not a name the painters chose for themselves; Rosenberg invented it, and Pollock never called himself an action painter. Second, it describes this wing only, the gesture painters. The color-field painters in the next chapter are not action painters, and calling them that gets the whole movement wrong.

One more caveat, because the legend oversells it. Pollock did not invent mark-making by accident from nothing. Pouring and dripping paint had been tried before him, and the deeper idea, letting the unconscious and chance compose, came straight from Surrealist automatism by way of the émigrés. What Pollock did was take that idea and build a whole new way of painting out of it, at a scale and with a commitment nobody had matched. He turned automatism into the act itself. That is the honest claim, and it is plenty.

### de Kooning's Woman, Kline's girders

The gesture wing was not all abstraction. Willem de Kooning (1904–1997), a Dutchman who had stowed away to America in 1926, kept the human figure when nearly everyone around him dropped it, and he paid for it. His *Woman I* (1950–52) is a ferocious, toothy, more-than-life-size female figure, built out of slashing, scraping, violent strokes, her grin baring teeth, her eyes huge, the paint dragged and reworked over months until the whole surface looks fought-over. It scandalized people on two fronts at once. The avant-garde thought he had betrayed abstraction by keeping a recognizable woman; traditionalists thought the woman was a monstrous insult to painting. De Kooning had managed to be figurative and abstract in the same picture, and to make both camps angry, which is one definition of being ahead of your time.

Franz Kline (1910–1962) went the other way, into pure black-and-white abstraction at the scale of a wall. His *Chief* (1950) looks like a few enormous brush-marks, thick black girders of house-paint crossing a white ground, blown up so large they read like architecture or the skeleton of a bridge. The title is the name of a locomotive Kline knew as a boy, and the pictures have that kind of heavy, mechanical, structural weight, as if a single calligrapher's stroke had been enlarged until you could walk into it. Pollock's web, de Kooning's slashing figure, Kline's black beams: three painters who agreed only that the act of painting, the gesture itself, was now the thing worth putting on the wall.

[MEANWHILE: Long Island, 1950. A photographer named Hans Namuth filmed and photographed Pollock at work in the barn, circling a canvas on the floor, paint trailing from the stick, even shooting through a sheet of glass so the camera looked up through the painting as it was made. The films turned the studio into a stage and made Pollock a celebrity, fixing the image of the heroic male genius in his element, which is part of why the myth attached so hard to this one man.]

## Chapter 3 — Fields of color

### The opposite move · Rothko's rectangles

The other half of Abstract Expressionism looks like the calm after Pollock's storm, and it is just as radical. The **color-field** painters (the term means painting built from large, simple, mostly unbroken areas of color rather than from gesture or drawing) wanted not the trace of a frantic act but stillness, vastness, and a kind of glow. Their patron saint is Mark Rothko (1903–1970). A mature Rothko is two or three soft-edged rectangles of color, stacked one above another, hovering on a larger field of a different color, the edges feathered so the blocks seem to float and pulse rather than sit flat. *No. 14, 1960* is a field of bright orange over deep cobalt blue on an eggplant-dark ground, nine and a half feet tall. *Orange and Yellow* (1956) is a luminous yellow block above an orange one, serene and radiant.

The point was scale and closeness. Rothko made the canvases big and wanted them hung low and seen from a few feet away, so the color fills your whole field of vision and you stand inside it rather than in front of it. He hated being called an abstractionist or a colorist. He insisted his real subject was raw human emotion, tragedy, ecstasy, doom, and that a person who broke down weeping in front of his paintings had understood them better than a person admiring the color relationships. The rectangles were not the subject; they were the door. You can also read the man straight off the canvas: over the years his palette darkened with his mood, from the glowing orange-and-yellow of the mid-1950s to the near-black of the Seagram and Chapel canvases as his depression deepened, so that the pigment itself becomes a record of where he was.

### The Seagram refusal, the Houston chapel

Two stories show how seriously Rothko meant it. In 1958 he took a lucrative commission to paint murals for the Four Seasons, an expensive restaurant in the new Seagram Building in Manhattan. He worked for over a year on a series of dark, wine-colored canvases built around looming portal-like shapes, then walked away from the money and pulled the commission, reportedly unwilling to let his art hang as decor for rich diners. He later gave nine of the Seagram murals to the Tate in London, where, by a grim coincidence, they arrived on 25 February 1970, the day he was found dead in his studio, having taken his own life. His final project was the **Rothko Chapel** in Houston, an eight-sided, octagonal room holding fourteen of his canvases, nearly black, made not as pictures to look at but as an environment to sit inside: a place, not a painting.

### Newman's zip, Still's cliffs

If Rothko was the wing's mystic, Barnett Newman (1905–1970) was its theorist of the void. Newman's whole art turns on a single device he called the **"zip"**: one thin vertical band of color running the full height of the canvas, dividing and charging an otherwise empty field. He found it in 1948 in a modest maroon canvas, *Onement I*, split top to bottom by a single ragged orange stripe, and he built the rest of his career on it. (The title comes from an old root of the word "atonement," the state of being made one.) The masterpiece is *Vir Heroicus Sublimis* (1950–51), a vast red field nearly eight feet tall and almost eighteen feet wide, crossed by a few thin zips, meant to be stood in front of so close it fills your sight. The Latin title means roughly "man, heroic and sublime," and that is the claim: that an abstract American painting could reach the **sublime** (the old idea of a beauty so vast it overwhelms you, tipping past pleasure into awe and even dread). Newman wrote the wing's credo in 1948 and titled it, with no hedging, "The Sublime Is Now."

The most extreme of the field painters, and arguably the first of them to the pure field, was Clyfford Still (1904–1980). He arrived at the large vertical color-field format early, in the early-to-mid 1940s, before either Rothko or Newman had settled into theirs, which makes him a founder of color-field painting and not merely its hermit. His canvases are huge and dark, built from torn, ragged, vertical sheets of thick paint that look like cliffs or peeling bark; bright flashes of color are stranded and violent against the charcoal dark, like a torn map of a country that does not exist. Still despised the art market and the gallery world, hoarded most of his own output, and arranged for it to go to a single dedicated museum (now in Denver) rather than be scattered and sold. Three painters, one wing: Rothko's floating veils, Newman's single charged line, Still's torn cliffs, all of them aiming at stillness and immensity, the same towering ambition as the gesture painters but pointed in the opposite direction. Not the record of an act. The field itself.

## Chapter 4 — Two critics, one fight

### The painting as an event · Rosenberg

Here is a thing the legend leaves out: the painters did not make their own reputations alone. Two New York critics did much of it for them, and they could not stand each other. The first was **Harold Rosenberg**, the poet-critic of the pair, a man who drank with the painters at the Cedar and wrote about painting as if he were flinging the paint himself, all heat and motion. In December 1952 he published an essay in *ARTnews* called "The American Action Painters." It is the single most influential piece of writing the movement produced, and its core idea is that a painting is no longer a picture but an **event**. The canvas, Rosenberg wrote, had become "an arena in which to act." What ends up on the wall is the trace of the artist's gesture, the record of a man facing a blank surface and doing something on it, an existential act more than an image. (Existential here points to the fashionable philosophy of the day, the idea that a person's choices and acts, made in the face of a meaningless universe, are what define them.) For Rosenberg the drama was the doing; the painting was the leftover proof that the doing happened. "Action painting" was his coinage, and he gave it to the gesture wing.

That reading fit the gesture wing like a glove, which is why "action painting" became their nickname. It fit Pollock circling his canvas and de Kooning fighting his *Woman* into shape. It did not fit Rothko's still glowing fields at all, and Rothko and Newman wanted nothing to do with the label, which is one more reason the term belongs to one wing only.

### The painting as an object · Greenberg

The rival was **Clement Greenberg**, and he was the opposite kind of man as well as the opposite kind of critic: a cold-eyed ex-Trotskyist (a follower, once, of the revolutionary Leon Trotsky) who had turned into the most exacting formalist of his generation and who believed, simply and without much doubt, that he was right. He had the power to match it; a Greenberg review could make or break a career. He championed Pollock early and, years later, turned on him. He saw the same paintings as Rosenberg in almost the opposite way. Greenberg was a **formalist**, meaning he believed the meaning of a painting lives in its physical form, its color, shape, and surface, not in any story, emotion, or "act" behind it. His big idea was **flatness**. Painting, he argued, had spent five centuries pretending a flat canvas was a deep space, and the whole forward march of modern art was painting slowly admitting and embracing the one thing that made it itself: that it is flat, a stretched cloth covered in color. Abstract Expressionism, in his reading, was the next logical step in that purification, and the best of it (he championed Pollock, then increasingly the staining color painters who came after) was important not because it recorded anyone's heroic act but because it pushed painting closer to pure, flat, optical color and shape. He laid this out in an essay called "American-Type Painting" in 1955.

It is worth saying that Greenberg backed Pollock from the start. From the early 1940s, writing the "Art" column in *The Nation* (which he began in 1942), he was praising Pollock in print by 1943, before the drip paintings, before the films, before the fame. The critic helped build the reputation early, which is exactly why a critic's power was real and not imagined.

### Why the rivalry matters

So you have two clean opposites fighting over the same pictures. Rosenberg: the painting is an event, the record of a self in action. Greenberg: the painting is an object, pure flat optical form, the next move in modernism's logic. They partly backed different artists, they wrote in different magazines, and their dislike was personal and vicious, not merely a clash of theories, and that quarrel is not a footnote. It is part of how the canon got built. Critics, dealers, and the press, as much as the painters, decided which of these men became famous and which got forgotten, and two warring critics with strong theories and good prose are a powerful reputation machine.

And the dealers were the other half of that machine. The single most important early platform was the **Betty Parsons Gallery**, which opened in 1946 and gave Abstract Expressionism its first real commercial home: Pollock (from 1947), Rothko, Newman, and Still all showed there between 1947 and 1952, before Sidney Janis lured several of them away to a more established gallery. A movement that distrusted the market still ran on it. When the next two chapters talk about who got crowned and who got buried, remember that a painting did not become a masterpiece by hanging on a wall. Someone with a typewriter and a magazine, and someone with a gallery and a price list, had to say so first.

## Chapter 5 — The women written out

### The myth and the names

The story Abstract Expressionism told about itself starred a handful of heroic men: the lone genius in his studio, fighting paint, drinking hard, making history with his body. It is a thrilling story, and it is missing half the people who were in the room. A whole cohort of serious women painters helped make this movement and were written out of it, dismissed as wives or "lady painters," left out of the big shows and the big essays, and largely forgotten until art historians went looking for them again in recent decades. Naming them is not a courtesy. It is correcting the record.

Start with Lee Krasner (1908–1984), because her erasure is the textbook case. Krasner was a rigorously trained painter (she had studied with Hofmann) and a major artist in her own right, and she happened to marry Jackson Pollock. For decades the art world filed her under "Pollock's wife," which suited the genius myth and buried a real career. In fact Krasner kept painting at full power for nearly thirty years after Pollock's death, and some of her boldest, largest work, surging fields of organic pink-and-green forms like *The Seasons* (1957), came in exactly those years when the legend had decided she was a footnote.

### Frankenthaler's soak-stain, and the rest

The single most consequential thing any woman did in this movement may be the most consequential thing anyone did for what came after it. In 1952, at the age of twenty-three, Helen Frankenthaler (1928–2011) made *Mountains and Sea* by pouring thinned-down paint directly onto raw, unprimed canvas, so the color soaked into the weave like a stain on cloth rather than sitting on top of it as a brushed skin. The result was soft, luminous veils of color, suggesting a landscape without depicting one. She had invented the **soak-stain** technique, and it became the literal bridge from Abstract Expressionism to the next movement.

There were more. Joan Mitchell (1925–1992) made lush, slashing, gestural abstractions that turn memories of landscape into storms of color, and worked for much of her life in France. Elaine de Kooning (1918–1989) was a painter and a sharp, widely read art critic at the same time, writing the reviews that helped shape opinion in the very scene that overlooked her own canvases. Grace Hartigan (1922–2008) was among the most respected painters of the younger New York generation in the 1950s. Each of them was a central New York School artist; each was, for decades, treated as a minor character in a story about men. None of their work can be shown in this read either, because all of it is still in copyright, which means even the act of correcting the record runs into the same wall.

Here is the sharpest version of the injustice. Two younger painters, Morris Louis and Kenneth Noland, saw *Mountains and Sea*, picked up Frankenthaler's soak-stain, and built it into Color Field painting, and it was those two men, not the woman who invented the method, who collected most of the founding credit for the movement that grew straight out of her experiment. A young woman worked out how paint should meet cloth, and two men walked through the door she opened and got their names on it.

## Chapter 6 — A weapon and a wreck

### The CIA, told straight

In the 1950s, Abstract Expressionism became a tool of American foreign policy, and most of the painters never knew. This is a real story with a documented record, and it is easy to tell badly in either direction, so here it is carefully. During the early Cold War the United States was competing with the Soviet Union not only in weapons but in culture, and the Soviets pointed to their own official art, **socialist realism** (heroic, legible, propagandistic paintings of happy workers and strong leaders), as proof of a healthy society. American officials wanted a counter-image, and Abstract Expressionism made a perfect one: free, individual, expressive, abstract, the visual opposite of state-dictated realism, a poster for what a free society could produce.

The promotion was covert. Working largely through a front organization called the **Congress for Cultural Freedom** and a web of foundations, the CIA secretly funded exhibitions and magazines that showcased this American painting abroad, roughly from 1950 into the late 1960s. The historian Frances Stonor Saunders documented the operation in detail in *The Cultural Cold War* (1999). Now the careful part, both ways. The CIA did not create Abstract Expressionism; the painters had invented it on their own years earlier, out of automatism and Hofmann and the war, and most of them never learned their work had been used this way, and they were not agents of anything. The accurate statement is narrow and strange enough on its own: a secret arm of the American state quietly used these paintings, made by mostly left-leaning artists who would have been horrified, as a Cold War advertisement for American freedom. Not a fake movement, not a conspiracy that painted the pictures. State patronage that hijacked art the painters thought was nobody's business but their own.

### Pollock's wreck

The man at the center of the myth did not get to enjoy it for long. Jackson Pollock was a lifelong alcoholic, and his drinking got worse as his fame grew and his painting stalled. On the night of 11 August 1956, drunk, he drove his car off a road near his home in Springs and crashed into the trees. He was killed, and so was a young woman riding with him, Edith Metzger; a second passenger, Ruth Kligman, survived. Pollock was forty-four. There is nothing romantic in it, and it is worth resisting the urge to make the death part of the legend, the way the legend wants. A man whose drinking had worsened as his fame grew drank himself into a state where he killed himself and an innocent passenger with a car. That is the fact, and the heroic-genius story tends to launder it into tragic poetry, which is one more thing the honest version refuses to do.

### What it left

What the movement left behind is enormous, and it outlasted every one of these quarrels. Abstract Expressionism moved the center of the art world to New York and kept it there for a generation, and it set the terms everything afterward had to answer: the giant scale, the abstraction, the heroic gesture, and the whole modern machinery of dealers, critics, and a market that turns living artists into stars. Its direct heir was **Color Field** painting, which ran straight out of Frankenthaler's soak-stain through Morris Louis and Kenneth Noland and gave you the pure glowing field with the gesture drained out.

The two movements that came next had a simpler choice: match this art's huge, earnest, self-serious scale, or mock it. **Pop Art**, being built in a read parallel to this one, chose mockery, trading the heroic inward self for the cool surfaces of advertising, comics, and the supermarket. **Minimalism** kept the scale and dumped the soul, holding onto the big empty field but stripping out the drama and the personality for something impersonal, industrial, and literal. Either way, the next generation needed something enormous to push against, and Abstract Expressionism was it.

Hold the two halves together one last time, because that is the whole point of telling it honestly. This was the most ambitious painting America ever made, the moment its art stopped following Paris and started leading the world, and it produced two of the most recognizable looks in all of modern art, Pollock's poured webs and Rothko's glowing blocks. It was also a reputation machine that crowned a few men, buried a roomful of women, romanticized a fatal drinking problem, and got quietly drafted into a Cold War it never asked to join. Both are true. Both are the story.
