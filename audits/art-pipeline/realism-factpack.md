## Throughline (the one idea)
Realism is the first shot in the whole modern revolt: painters decide to paint the
**real, ordinary, contemporary world** — laborers, peasants, the urban poor,
animals — at the scale and seriousness the academy reserved for gods and kings.
"Paint your own epoch, as it actually is." It runs ~1848 (the Revolution) to ~1870,
and it hands the baton to Impressionism (paint what you actually *see*).

## CAST
- **Gustave Courbet** (1819–1877) — the firebrand who names + leads Realism.
- **Jean-François Millet** (1814–1875) — peasant labor; Barbizon (the village S of
  Paris where painters worked from rural life).
- **Honoré Daumier** (1808–1879) — caricaturist/lithographer for the press AND
  painter; the modern city + its poor; satire that got him jailed.
- **Rosa Bonheur** (1822–1899) — the era's most celebrated woman painter; animals.

## VERIFIED WORKS / FACTS
- **Courbet, *The Stone Breakers*, 1849** (shown Salon 1850–51) — two laborers, old
  + young, breaking rock; ordinary backbreaking labor at history-painting scale.
  Was in Dresden (Gemäldegalerie); **DESTROYED Feb 1945** in the Allied bombing of
  Dresden (lost in transit toward Königstein with 153 other works). → only B&W /
  reproductions survive; treat as a documented LOSS in the prose; image = a PD
  reproduction or a `RestrictedFigure`-style note (coordinator resolves at gate 6).
- **Courbet, *A Burial at Ornans*, 1849–1850** — a vast (~10×22 ft) canvas of an
  ordinary village funeral in Courbet's hometown, life-size, no idealization;
  scandal for treating "nobodies" like a history painting. **Musée d'Orsay** (in
  the Louvre until 1986). PD.
- **Courbet, *The Painter's Studio*, 1855** — shown in his Pavilion of Realism.
  Musée d'Orsay. PD. (Already in `ART_IMG.courbetStudio`.)
- **The Pavilion of Realism, 1855** — refused works at the Exposition Universelle →
  Courbet builds his own one-man pavilion in sight of the official show; the
  catalogue carries his **"Realist Manifesto,"** aim: "to translate the customs, the
  ideas, the appearance of my epoch, according to my own estimation." This is where
  "Realism" gets its banner. (The era ch1 mentions the tent — go deeper on the
  manifesto/idea here.)
- **Millet, *The Gleaners*, 1857** — three peasant women stooping to gather leftover
  grain after the harvest (the poorest's right to scraps); read by critics as
  dangerously political (the rural poor, monumentalized). **Musée d'Orsay.** PD.
- **Millet, *The Angelus*, 1857–1859** — two peasants pause in a field at dusk to
  pray at the church bell; became one of the most reproduced images of the century.
  **Musée d'Orsay.** PD.
- **Daumier, *The Third-Class Carriage*, c. 1862–64** (unfinished) — the urban poor
  packed in a train's cheapest car; modern life, no sentimentality. **Metropolitan
  Museum of Art, New York.** PD. (Daumier: thousands of lithographs for the satirical
  press; jailed 6 months in 1832 for caricaturing King Louis-Philippe as *Gargantua*
  — `[VERIFY]` the 6-month figure with the fact-checker.)
- **Rosa Bonheur, *The Horse Fair*, begun 1852, shown Salon 1853, finished 1855** —
  a huge, thundering canvas of the Paris horse market; a sensation. **Metropolitan
  Museum** (gift of Cornelius Vanderbilt II, 1887). Bonheur got a **police permit to
  wear men's clothing** to sketch the market unbothered — `[VERIFY]` the permit
  detail (often repeated; confirm documented vs. legend). PD.
