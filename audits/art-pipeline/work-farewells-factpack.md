## ⚑ THE TWO-VERSION FINDING (verify-this-first, locked)

Boccioni painted the *States of Mind* (Italian *Stati d'animo*) triptych — **Gli addii
/ The Farewells**, **Quelli che vanno / Those Who Go**, **Quelli che restano / Those
Who Stay** — **TWICE in 1911.** [DOCUMENTED — Italian Art Society; MoMA; Met essay]

- **FIRST version** — begun **late summer 1911**, **before** his Paris trip. Style is
  **Divisionist** (color built from separate strokes, the technique Boccioni came up
  in), "pure" pre-Cubist Futurism. **NOT at MoMA.** Now in **Milan** — sources split
  on which Milan museum (see DISPUTED below). [DOCUMENTED]
- **SECOND version** — completed **toward the end of 1911**, **reworked AFTER his
  October/November 1911 trip to Paris**, where he saw Cubism firsthand. It shows "an
  inflection of Cubism" — the swirling Divisionist haze of v1 is restructured with
  harder Cubist faceting/intersecting planes. **THIS is the MoMA version** — the more
  famous one. [DOCUMENTED — Met essay; Wikipedia; Italian Art Society]

**→ MoMA holds the SECOND (post-Paris, Cubist-inflected) version of the triptych.**
The difference in one line: **v1 = Divisionist haze, "pure" Futurism, before Paris;
v2 (MoMA) = the same scene rebuilt with Cubist faceting/planes, after Boccioni saw
Cubism in Paris.**

> ⚠️ **AUTHOR/FACT-CHECK CAUTION — do not conflate two things:**
> 1. **Two PAINTED versions** (Milan Divisionist v1 / MoMA Cubist v2) — the headline finding.
> 2. **Separate preparatory DRAWINGS.** MoMA ALSO owns a set of *States of Mind*

## ② Hook (raw material — author to voice it)
A railway-station goodbye painted not as a scene but as a feeling: people fused into a
swirling vortex of steam, the one cold-sober thing in the chaos a locomotive's stencilled
number — and the version that hangs in New York is the *second* one Boccioni painted, redone
after a trip to Paris taught him Cubism.

## ③ Narrative beats (~5)

1. **Emotion, not event.** The triptych's whole premise: paint *states of mind* (the
   literal title), the inner psychological drama of a modern train-station parting, not a
   documentary record of a station. [DOCUMENTED — title; Boccioni's stated program]
2. **The railway-station farewell.** Unknown figures meet on a platform, embrace, say
   goodbye; some board and leave (*Those Who Go*), some remain (*Those Who Stay*). *The
   Farewells* is the embrace-and-chaos panel. [DOCUMENTED — Italian Art Society]
3. **The Paris trip that produced the second version.** In **October 1911** Boccioni,
   Carlo Carrà and Luigi Russolo made a fast trip to Paris, urged on and guided by their
   Paris-based Futurist colleague **Gino Severini**; there they saw Cubism. Boccioni
   **reworked** *States of Mind* afterward — the MoMA set is that reworked, Cubist-inflected
   second version. [DOCUMENTED — Met essay; Wikipedia ("trip to Paris in November")]
   *(Source wobble: "October" vs "November 1911" for the trip — say "late 1911 / autumn
   1911" to be safe. [DISPUTED — exact month])*
4. **"Lines of force" carrying feeling.** Boccioni's Futurist device: *linee-forza* /
   force-lines — directional lines that carry energy and emotion through the picture and
   tie figure to environment. In *The Farewells* the lines swirl/curve (chaos of parting);
   in *Those Who Go* they slant oblique (departure, flight); in *Those Who Stay* they fall
   strictly vertical (the weight of those left behind). [DOCUMENTED for the term + per-panel
   line-direction; see §"lines of force" below]
5. **The locomotive number 6943.** Amid the dissolving vortex, the one stable, legible,
   "calm" element is the engine's painted number — **6943**. [DOCUMENTED — see §look-closer]

## ⑥ Key figures (cast nameplates)

- **Umberto Boccioni** (1882–1916) — Italian Futurist painter and sculptor; the leading
  visual theorist of Futurism; died at 33 in 1916 after a fall from a horse during WWI
  cavalry training. *States of Mind*'s author. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Filippo Tommaso Marinetti** (1876–1944) — poet; founder of Futurism (1909 manifesto);
  first owner of the triptych. [DOCUMENTED]
- **The Cubists / Paris** — Boccioni's October/late-1911 Paris trip exposed him to Cubism;
  that exposure is what produced the reworked second (MoMA) version. (Gino Severini, the
  Paris-based Futurist, urged and guided the trip; Carrà and Russolo went too.) [DOCUMENTED]
- **Nelson A. Rockefeller** (1908–1979) — collector/politician; bought the panels from
  Marinetti's widow (1949), gifted them to MoMA (1979). [DOCUMENTED]

## "Lines of force" (linee-forza) — term provenance [DOCUMENTED, with a caveat]

- The concept of **force-lines / lines of force** is a core Futurist painting device:
  directional lines that carry the energy/emotion of an object and bind it to its
  environment. [DOCUMENTED]
- It is theorized in the **Preface-Manifesto to the catalogue of the first Futurist
  exhibition in Paris (Galerie Bernheim-Jeune, February 1912)** — i.e. the term is
  formalized right around the same Paris moment that reshaped *States of Mind*. The broader
  Futurist-painting program dates to the **Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting (1910)**.
  [DOCUMENTED] **[CAVEAT/VERIFY — one search summary dated the catalogue "October 1911";
  the standard date for the first Futurist Paris show + its catalogue preface is **February
  1912**. Use "the 1912 Paris-exhibition catalogue" and have the fact-checker confirm before
  any dated claim.]**
- **Per-panel line behavior** (safe to state): *The Farewells* — swirling/curved lines;
  *Those Who Go* — oblique/slanting lines; *Those Who Stay* — strict vertical lines.
  [DOCUMENTED — Italian Art Society]
