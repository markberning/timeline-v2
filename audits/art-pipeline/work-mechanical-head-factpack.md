## 0. SOURCE-RELIABILITY NOTE (read first — this work has a known error surface)

There are **two competing object lists** circulating for this work:

- **The Centre Pompidou authoritative caption** (the museum that owns it) — the canonical, conservative list.
- **A popular secondary-source list** (TheArtStory, Wikipedia, museum-tour blogs, Tate-adjacent write-ups) that names a *camera segment, a typewriter, a "crocodile" wallet, a pocket-watch mechanism,* and a *tape measure.*

These DO NOT fully agree. The art-read MUST follow Pompidou's list and may mention the popular list only as "often also described as." Treating the popular list as fact is exactly the kind of caption error the pipeline is built to catch. See §1 and §8.

## 2. THE STORY

- **Berlin Dada.** Hausmann was a founder of the Berlin wing of Dada (with Hannah Höch, John Heartfield, George Grosz, Johannes Baader, Richard Huelsenbeck) — the most overtly political Dada group, formed in the wreckage of Germany's defeat in WWI and the chaos of the early Weimar Republic. The *Mechanical Head* is the only assemblage of this kind by Hausmann to survive.
- **The core idea.** The modern ordinary person's inner life — his "spirit" (*Geist*) — is **empty**. What defines him is not anything inside the skull but the **gadgets and measuring instruments stuck to the outside of his head**: he is the sum of the cup he drinks from, the money in his wallet, the ruler and tape that measure him, the watch that times him, the type that prints on him. The brain is vacant; identity is bolted on from without.
- **Satire of rationalized modern man.** The attached objects are overwhelmingly instruments of **measurement, money, time, and standardization** (ruler, tape measure, the "22," watch movement, wallet, type-roller). The piece mocks the post-war ideal of efficient, quantified, money-and-clock-ruled man — a person reduced to what can be measured and processed. The mechanization theme also reads as a response to the industrial slaughter of WWI: man as machine, life as statistics.
- **Relation to Hausmann's other work.** Hausmann is one of the (disputed) inventors of **photomontage** — he and Höch claimed the technique came to them on a 1918 Baltic holiday seeing framed soldier-portraits. The *Mechanical Head* is the **three-dimensional cousin of photomontage**: instead of assembling a face from cut photographs, he assembles a head from cut-loose manufactured objects. He was also a **sound / "optophonetic" poet** (poster-poems, phonemes) — the assemblage shares Dada's drive to make meaning by combining the found and the nonsensical.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required before/after)

- **BEFORE:** Sculpture meant a head **carved or modelled** from a noble material (marble, bronze, clay) by the artist's hand, idealizing or portraying a person — the human head as the seat of mind and soul. Even avant-garde portrait busts (Rodin, Brancusi) still *shaped* the head.
- **THE BREAK:** Hausmann **assembles** the head from **found, ready-made junk** — a barber's wooden dummy plus office and household oddments — and **fixes them rather than fashioning them**. This is **assemblage / found-object sculpture**: the artist's choice and combination, not his carving hand, is the art. The human head becomes **a thing built from cast-offs**, and its meaning is the **mechanization-of-man theme** — identity as externally bolted-on apparatus, the spirit emptied out.
- **AFTER:** Sits in the line that runs from Duchamp's readymades into Dada/Surrealist object-sculpture and onward to Rauschenberg's combines and later assemblage. It made the **found object glued to a base** a legitimate way to make a "portrait" / sculpture of the modern condition.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (Hausmann quote)

**The most-cited verbatim line, on the empty modern head:**
> "An average / everyday man has no more capabilities than those which chance has glued to the outside of his skull; his brain remains empty."

**Reliability flag:** This is **genuinely Hausmann**, from his own retrospective writing on the work (it recurs across Tate-adjacent, TheArtStory, Smarthistory, and Pompidou-linked accounts), **but it circulates in several English translations** — wording varies ("average man" vs "everyday man" vs "the average German"; "glued to the outside of his skull" vs "glued to his skull, on the exterior"; "his brain remains empty" vs "the brain was vacant"). **Quote it as a translation, not as one fixed English original, and pick ONE variant — do not splice.**

**Second, on intent (paraphrase-grade — flag as such):**
> "I wanted to reveal the spirit of our time, the spirit of everyone in their rudimentary state."
Attributed to a 1967 Hausmann statement; treat as **looser / paraphrased** across sources — use with care or skip.

**If a single clean verbatim is needed and the source cannot be pinned to Hausmann's German original at authoring time, say "Hausmann described the everyday man as having no more capabilities than chance had glued to the outside of his skull" rather than quoting marks.** A sourced paraphrase beats a mis-transcribed quote.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers, no coordinates)

All identifications follow **Pompidou's list** (§1); flag the camera/typewriter as "often described, not in the museum's caption."

1. **The blank wooden face.** The base is a barber's wig-maker's dummy — a smooth, expressionless wooden head with closed, carved-shut features. It is deliberately vacant: the "spirit" is absent from the face itself.
2. **The seam and grain of the dummy head.** It reads plainly as a manufactured wooden object, not a sculpted likeness — you can see it is a found shop-fitting, which is the whole point (no artist's modelling hand).
3. **The collapsible/telescopic cup and the leather wallet/case** fixed to the head — the apparatus of daily consumption and money, bolted onto the skull as if they were the man's faculties.
4. **The measuring instruments** — the **double-decimetre wooden ruler** and the **piece of dressmaker's tape measure** — the rationalized, quantified modern man literally wearing the tools that measure him.
5. **The number "22"** on a white card — a bare numeral, identity reduced to a label/figure (often read as the man-as-statistic motif).
6. **The watch gear and the printer's type-roller** — time (the clock movement) and standardized print/text, the machinery that runs and processes modern life, attached where thought should be.

(If the read wants to mention the popularly-cited **camera segment / typewriter part / pocket-watch mechanism**, frame it as "many descriptions also identify…", not as Pompidou's catalogued list.)

## 7. RIGHTS

- Hausmann **died 1971**, so the work is **under copyright in the EU (life + 70 = through 2041)** — Centre Pompidou and ADAGP assert © there.
- **In the US:** the work was **made and published c. 1919–20, i.e., before 1930**, so it is **public domain in the United States** (pre-1930 publication). The wired Wikimedia file is hosted on that PD-US basis.
- **Plain statement for the read:** US-PD (made/published c. 1920); EU museum © is a separate, EU-only claim and does not bind US use of a faithful photograph of the object.
- **Caveat to verify:** "publication" of a sculpture is fuzzier than for a print; the PD-US claim rests on the c.1920 date. State the date plainly and rely on the pre-1930 rule.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct these)

1. **TRAP — the object list (the #1 error surface).** The popular description (Wikipedia, TheArtStory, tour blogs) adds a **bronze segment of an old camera** and a **typewriter** and calls the wallet a **"crocodile" wallet / jewel box**. **Pompidou's authoritative caption names neither a camera nor a typewriter** — it lists a telescopic cup, leather case, pipe stem, the "22" card, dressmaker's-tape piece, double-decimetre ruler, watch gear, and a printer's-type roller. **Use Pompidou's list. Do not assert the camera or typewriter as fact.** If mentioned, attribute to "often-repeated descriptions."
2. **TRAP — the date.** Frequently flattened to a confident "1919" OR "1920." It is **uncertain**: Pompidou records 1919 but notes scholarship pointing to **1920–21**. **Use "c. 1919–20" and acknowledge the uncertainty.**
3. **TRAP — the title translation.** *Der Geist unserer Zeit* = "The Spirit of Our Time" / "The Spirit of Our Age" (both are used; "Age" is common in Smarthistory's title "Spirit of the Age"). The full German is **Mechanischer Kopf (Der Geist unserer Zeit)** — keep the head-noun and the spirit-subtitle distinct; don't merge into one invented German string.
4. **TRAP — the quote.** Genuine Hausmann but **multiple English translations**; "the average German" is one tightening that narrows Hausmann's "average/everyday man." Don't present any one English wording as THE original. See §4.
5. **TRAP — "only surviving."** Often called "the only surviving assemblage Hausmann made around 1919–20." Reasonable per sources, but state as "the only one of its kind to survive," not "the only assemblage he ever made."
6. **TRAP — dimensions.** ~32.5 cm tall = ~12.8 in (just over a foot); do not round to "life-size." It is a small object.
