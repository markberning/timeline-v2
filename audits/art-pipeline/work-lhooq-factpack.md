## 1. CORE FACTS

- **Title:** *L.H.O.O.Q.* It is a **gramogram** — a "word" spelled by the sound of its letters. Read aloud in French the five letters [ɛl–aʃ–o–o–ky] sound out the sentence **"Elle a chaud au cul."** (Wikipedia; Daily Art Magazine.)
- **The pun / translation:**
  - **Literal:** "She has a hot ass" / "She is hot in the arse." `cul` is vulgar French for backside/arse. (Wikipedia gives "She has a hot ass"; the brief glosses it as "a vulgar expression implying sexual restlessness.")
  - **Idiomatic sense:** the phrase implies the woman is sexually aroused / "hot" — i.e. randy. (Wikipedia.)
  - **Duchamp's own loose English gloss (late interview):** *"there is fire down below"* / *"there is a fire down below."* (Wikipedia; the Medium critical essay both cite this.) **FLAG:** Duchamp's English rendering is deliberately polite/loose — it is NOT a literal translation of `cul`. Treat the literal "hot ass / hot in the arse" and Duchamp's softer "fire down below" as two registers of the same line, not as competing facts.
- **Type:** A **"rectified readymade"** — Duchamp's own term for a found mass-produced object that he altered (as opposed to a plain, untouched readymade like the urinal *Fountain*). (Wikipedia; USC Scalar figure caption: "Rectified readymade.")
- **Object / medium:** A **cheap printed postcard reproduction of Leonardo da Vinci's *Mona Lisa* (La Joconde)**, onto which Duchamp drew a **moustache and goatee in pencil** and **lettered the title "L.H.O.O.Q." below**. Catalogued medium: **pencil on a printed reproduction (postcard) of the *Mona Lisa*.** (Wikipedia; USC Scalar caption "added mustache, goatee, and title in pencil.")
- **Date:** **1919** (made in Paris). (All sources.)
- **Dimensions:** **19.7 × 12.4 cm** = **7¾ × 4⅞ in** (7.8 × 4.9 in). (USC Scalar caption "7-3/4 x 4-7/8 inches (19.7 x 12.4 cm)"; Wikipedia "19.7 cm × 12.4 cm (7.8 in × 4.9 in)" — the two are the same object, just rounded differently; postcard-scale.)
- **Multiple versions (this is the single biggest trap — see §8):**
  - **1919 original** — the small postcard with pencil moustache + goatee + lettering. **Current location: not publicly fixed / private; widely described as in a private collection (was Mary Sisler collection).** (Wikipedia notes the multiple-version situation; FLAG location precisely below.)
  - **1930 (and later) authorized replicas / a black-and-white version** — Duchamp made several versions over the years in differing sizes/media; one is held at the **Musée National d'Art Moderne, Centre Pompidou, Paris.** (Wikipedia.)
  - **1964 *Boîte-en-valise* / readymade reproduction series** — miniature collotype reproductions of *L.H.O.O.Q.* were issued as part of Duchamp's portable-museum boxes and editioned readymades; the **Philadelphia Museum of Art** and others hold these. (Wikipedia; multiple sources reference a *Box in a Valise* example.)
  - **1965 — *L.H.O.O.Q. Shaved* (*L.H.O.O.Q. rasée*)** — a plain, **unmodified** black-and-white *Mona Lisa* reproduction mounted on card, issued as an invitation/edition; the joke is that the moustache has been "shaved off." Held in collections including **IMMA (Irish Museum of Modern Art), Norton Simon, and others.** (Wikipedia; IMMA "Rasée L.H.O.O.Q.")
  - A version is catalogued at the **Norton Simon Museum, Pasadena** as **"L.H.O.O.Q. or La Joconde," accession P.1969.094.** (search result; museum page returned 403 — VERIFY exact year/medium before quoting.)

## 2. THE STORY

- Made in **Paris in 1919**, in and around the nascent **Paris Dada** circle (Duchamp had returned from New York; the Dada movement was crystallizing in Paris with Picabia, Tzara, Breton). The work became, in the words of one museum text, his **most famous readymade and a symbol of international Dada**, which "rebelled against everything that art represented, particularly the appeal to tradition and beauty." (Search/museum text; Daily Art Magazine.)
- **Picabia and *391*:** Francis Picabia published the image in his Dada magazine ***391* (issue 12, March 1920)**. Picabia could not wait for Duchamp's actual object to arrive, so — **with Duchamp's consent, Picabia drew the moustache himself and FORGOT the goatee.** Decades later (1942) Duchamp added the missing goatee in ink to a print and inscribed it: **"Moustache par Picabia / barbiche par Marcel Duchamp / avril 1942"** ("Moustache by Picabia / goatee by Marcel Duchamp / April 1942"). (Wikipedia; Medium essay.) **FLAG:** the *391* reproduction is therefore moustache-only — a separate object from the 1919 original.
- **The gender play:** the moustache/goatee turns the icon of female beauty male/androgynous. This dovetails with Duchamp's own cross-gender alter ego **Rrose Sélavy** (pronounced "Eros, c'est la vie" — "Eros, that's life"), which he was developing around the same period (Rrose first appears 1920–21). (Wikipedia.)
- **What it attacks:** the target is the **cult of the masterpiece and museum/bourgeois reverence**, not Leonardo's skill. By 1919 "Jocondisme" — *Mona Lisa* worship — was near-religious in French bourgeois culture (intensified by the painting's sensational 1911 theft from the Louvre and 1913 recovery); defacing the postcard was a textbook act of *épater le bourgeois* ("shock the middle class"). (USC Scalar / Goodyear; Daily Art Magazine; Wikipedia.)

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (before / after — REQUIRED)

**Before:** A masterpiece was something you copied, revered, hung in a gilt frame, made pilgrimages to. The artwork was the irreproducible original object touched by genius; the artist's job was to make beautiful, skilled originals. The *Mona Lisa* was the supreme sacred object of that order.

**After:** Duchamp takes a **ten-centime postcard of the most revered painting on earth and vandalizes it with a graffiti moustache and a dirty pun — and calls the result his art.** Three breaks land at once:
1. **Defacement as a creative act** — the gesture of *altering / desecrating* a revered image is itself the artwork; iconoclasm becomes a medium.
2. **The readymade turned on art itself** — Duchamp had already declared found manufactured objects (a bottle rack, a urinal) to be art; here he applies that move to *an existing canonical artwork*, collapsing the line between making and selecting/altering.
3. **Appropriation** — taking someone else's already-finished image, recontextualizing it, and authoring the result.

This is widely cited as a **seed of later appropriation art** (Pop's reuse of mass imagery; Sherrie Levine, Elaine Sturtevant, the Pictures Generation re-photographing/re-making existing works). *L.H.O.O.Q.* is the early proof that the source image can be the medium. (USC Scalar / Goodyear essay; standard Dada art-history accounts.)

## 4. KEY STATEMENT — Duchamp's own words (VERBATIM, SOURCED)

**Primary quote (the gender discovery):**

> "The curious thing about that moustache and goatee is that when you look at the *Mona Lisa* it becomes a man. It is not a woman disguised as a man; it is a real man, and that was my discovery, without realizing it at the time."

- **Source:** Marcel Duchamp, in conversation with **James Johnson Sweeney, "A Conversation with Marcel Duchamp,"** televised NBC interview conducted 1955, broadcast January 1956; reprinted in *The Writings of Marcel Duchamp* (Salt Seller / Marchand du Sel), p. 133. Cited via the USC Scalar / *The Space Between* scholarly essay and the Medium critical essay.
- **FLAG — wording varies:** One widely circulated version reads **"…It is a woman disguised as a man; it is a real man…"** (the Medium essay's paraphrase) which **inverts the meaning** (drops the "not"). The scholarly/Writings version with **"It is NOT a woman disguised as a man; it is a real man"** is the correct, sourced reading — the point is that the figure becomes genuinely male, not merely a woman in disguise. **Use the "not" version.** Confirm against *The Writings of Marcel Duchamp* p. 133 before final ship.

**Secondary quote (the pun gloss):** Duchamp's own loose English translation of the letters as **"there is fire down below"** (also seen as "there is a fire down below"). — late interview, via Wikipedia. Treat as Duchamp's polite gloss, not a literal translation.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The moustache** — two short pencil strokes added across the upper lip, sitting on top of the printed face. Hand-drawn graffiti quality, deliberately crude against the smooth printed image.
2. **The goatee** — a small pointed pencil beard at the chin, completing the masculinization. (Remember the *391* version omitted this; the 1919 original has it.)
3. **The untouched *Mona Lisa* beneath** — the famous half-smile, folded hands, and the hazy receding landscape of Leonardo's portrait are reproduced unaltered. Duchamp changed almost nothing; the whole work is the moustache, the beard, and the caption.
4. **The lettering "L.H.O.O.Q." below the image** — five capital letters, pencilled in the margin under the postcard, separated by periods. The dirty pun lives entirely in how you pronounce them in French.
5. **The cheap printed quality** — this is a small commercial postcard reproduction, not a painting: flat printed ink, postcard scale (roughly 8 × 5 inches), the mechanical look of mass reproduction. That cheapness is part of the point.
6. **The collision of registers** — a sublime Renaissance icon + schoolyard doodle + an obscene wordplay, all on one tiny card. The humor and the attack are inseparable.

## 6. PROVENANCE (1919 original)

- Created by Duchamp, Paris, 1919.
- The 1919 object passed through private hands; it is most often associated with the **Mary Sisler collection** and is generally described as **privately held / location not publicly fixed.** (Wikipedia notes the multiple-version situation and the original's uncertain public location.)
- **FLAG — VERIFY before ship:** the precise current owner/location of the 1919 original is not authoritatively pinned down across the sources consulted (museum holdings cited — Centre Pompidou, Philadelphia, Norton Simon, IMMA — are **later replicas / editions / the "Shaved" version**, NOT the 1919 original). Do not state a museum as holding "the original 1919 *L.H.O.O.Q.*" without a primary catalog confirmation. The safe statement: *"the 1919 original is in a private collection; museums hold authorized later replicas and editions."*

## 7. RIGHTS

- **Status: Public domain in the US (PD-US).**
- **Underlying *Mona Lisa*:** Leonardo da Vinci, c. 1503–1506 — copyright long expired; ancient public domain. A faithful photographic/printed reproduction of a 2-D PD work carries no new US copyright (*Bridgeman v. Corel*).
- **Duchamp's 1919 addition:** the moustache, goatee, and lettering were made and the work published in 1919 — **before 1930, hence public domain in the United States** (works published before 1929/1930 are PD-US). The Wikimedia file page hosts the image under a public-domain rationale on exactly this basis.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct, with sources)

1. **TRAP — "It's an original *Mona Lisa* that Duchamp drew on." NO.** It is a **cheap printed postcard reproduction** of the *Mona Lisa*, not Leonardo's panel (which never left the Louvre). Duchamp defaced a mass-produced copy. (Wikipedia; all sources.)
2. **TRAP — which version is "the" original.** The **1919 pencil-on-postcard with moustache AND goatee** is the original. The version reproduced in Picabia's *391* (1920) is **moustache-only** (Picabia forgot the goatee). The pieces at **Centre Pompidou, Philadelphia, Norton Simon, IMMA** are **later replicas / editions / the *Shaved* version** — do NOT call any of them "the 1919 original." (Wikipedia.)
3. **TRAP — *L.H.O.O.Q. Shaved* (1965) confused with the main work.** *L.H.O.O.Q. rasée* is a **separate, later (1965) work**: a **plain, unaltered** *Mona Lisa* reproduction — the joke is the moustache has been "shaved off." It has no moustache. Don't conflate it with the 1919 mustachioed work. (Wikipedia; IMMA.)
4. **TRAP — the translation.** `cul` is **vulgar (arse/ass)**, not neutral. Literal = "She has a hot ass / She is hot in the arse." Duchamp's own English "there is fire down below" is a **softened gloss**, not the literal meaning — present both, don't let the polite version stand alone as "the translation." (Wikipedia.)
5. **TRAP — the quote's "not."** The gender quote is **"It is NOT a woman disguised as a man; it is a real man."** A widespread mis-citation drops the "not," reversing the meaning. (USC Scalar; cf. Medium essay's flawed paraphrase.) Use the "not" version; confirm against *The Writings of Marcel Duchamp* p. 133.
6. **TRAP — the date.** Conceived/made **1919.** Picabia's *391* publication is **1920.** Don't slide the date to 1920. (Wikipedia.)
7. **NUANCE — it's not an attack on Leonardo.** The target is the **masterpiece-cult and museum/bourgeois reverence**, not Leonardo's painting or skill. Frame it as iconoclasm against worship, not against the *Mona Lisa* as art. (USC Scalar / Goodyear; Daily Art Magazine.)
