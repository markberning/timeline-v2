**How to center the read.** Treat the two paintings as a deliberate **pair** and lead on *It's a Man's World I*. Boty made them as counterparts under one title — *I* is the world of men, *II* is the world's image of women — and the read only lands if you hold both. ## 1. CORE FACTS

**Titles.** *It's a Man's World I* and *It's a Man's World II*. Two separate oil-on-canvas paintings, conceived and read as a pair. Lead the work-read on *I*.

**Artist.** Pauline Boty (British, b. 6 March 1938, Carshalton, Surrey; d. 1 July 1966, London, age 28). The only widely acknowledged **woman** of the British Pop art movement and one of its co-founders; nicknamed "the Wimbledon Bardot" by her art-school classmates for her resemblance to Brigitte Bardot.
- Sources: Wikipedia (*Pauline Boty*); TheArtStory; paulineboty.org.

**Dates.**
- *I*: **1964** (paulineboty.org; Wikipedia).
- *II*: **1964–5** (paulineboty.org gives "1964/5"; some sources say "1965–66"). Use **1964–65** for the pair; flag *II* as the slightly later of the two. The brief's "1963–65" range is **too early on the front end** — no reliable source dates either canvas to 1963. Use **1964–65**.

**Medium.**
- *I*: **oil on canvas with collage** — paulineboty.org lists "oil on canvas with collage"; Wikipedia's source-key calls it "painted figures with collaged items pasted onto canvas." So: painted **and** collaged. Describe it as oil + collage, not pure collage.
- *II*: **oil on canvas** (paulineboty.org; WikiArt). The figures in *II* are **painted** (appropriated from photographic/print sources and re-painted), not pasted clippings.

**Dimensions.**
- *I*: **153 × 122 cm** = **~60 × 48 in** (5 ft 0 in × 4 ft 0 in). (paulineboty.org)
- *II*: **125 × 125 cm** = **~49 × 49 in** (4 ft 1 in × 4 ft 1 in), square. (paulineboty.org; WikiArt)
- Both verified from paulineboty.org (the estate's own catalogue site). Flag as the most authoritative dimension source available.

**Current location / owner.** Both **private collection** (paulineboty.org marks each "Private collection unless otherwise noted"). No public-museum permanent home is recorded for either. This ties to the rediscovery story (§6): most Boty paintings sat unseen in a barn for ~30 years and re-entered circulation through 1990s–2010s exhibitions and the art market, largely into private hands.

## 2. THE STORY

Pauline Boty was the **only prominent woman** in British Pop — a founder-generation figure alongside Peter Blake, Derek Boshier, Peter Phillips, and (older) Richard Hamilton and Eduardo Paolozzi — and for decades she was written out of the story almost entirely. She was glamorous, blonde, and photogenic (the "Wimbledon Bardot"), which cut against her: she was treated as a face more than a painter. In Ken Russell's 1962 BBC *Monitor* film **Pop Goes the Easel** (aired 22 March 1962), which featured her with Blake, Boshier and Phillips, sixteen of her works appear on screen — yet, unlike her three male co-stars, **she is never asked to talk about her own work**. That asymmetry is the whole subject of *It's a Man's World*.

*It's a Man's World I* (1964) gathers a field of famous **men** — admired, desired, powerful, dangerous — re-painted and collaged into one canvas: the Beatles (Lennon, Ringo Starr), Elvis Presley, Marcel Proust, Albert Einstein, Lenin, Muhammad Ali, a matador (likely El Cordobés), Fellini with Marcello Mastroianni, a Greek statue (Hermes), a B-52 nuclear bomber, and the **frame of John F. Kennedy's assassination lifted from the Zapruder film**. It is, at once, a fan's love-letter and an indictment — the world of achievement, genius, glamour, power and violence, all of it **male**.

*It's a Man's World II* (1964–65) is the answer: a stacked field of **female nudes** culled from soft-porn magazines and the life-class / old-master tradition alike, set against the blue sky of an 18th-century English landscape garden (the **Pantheon at Stourhead**, Wiltshire). The women are headless or anonymous, defined by their "parts," interchangeable. Boty deliberately flattens the supposed gap between "high" art nudes and "low" pin-ups: **both** treat women as objects to be looked at. Where the men in *I* have names, achievements, slogans ("I am the greatest"), the women in *II* have none.

Together the pair is a **feminist critique built from Pop's own raw material** — the same appropriated media imagery Hamilton and Blake used, turned to ask why men get to *be* people and women only get to be looked at. Then Boty died: diagnosed with cancer (a malignant thymoma) during a pregnancy check-up in 1965, she refused both an abortion and the radiotherapy that would have required it, carried her daughter to term, and died on **1 July 1966 at 28**. Her paintings went into a barn on her brother's farm in Kent and were forgotten for nearly thirty years until curator **David Alan Mellor** tracked them down (c. 1990) and showed them in his 1993 Barbican exhibition *The Sixties Art Scene in London*; scholar **Dr. Sue Tate** then built the art-historical case. The 1990s–2010s rediscovery is now inseparable from how the work reads.

- Sources: Wikipedia; paulineboty.org (works + sources key + "in her own words"); TheArtStory; The Art Newspaper (30 Dec 2023); DailyArt Magazine; Artsy.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required — before / after)

**Before.** British Pop, by 1962–64, was a recognized, celebrated, *male* enterprise: Hamilton, Paolozzi, Blake, Boshier, Phillips clipping ads, comics, film stars and consumer imagery into a knowing celebration-and-critique of mass culture. Women appeared in Pop almost exclusively **as imagery** — Monroe, pin-ups, ad-bodies — the object on the canvas, rarely the hand holding the brush. The appropriated-media language had no built-in place for a woman speaking about the position of women.

**After / the break.** Boty takes Pop's exact toolkit — appropriated, re-painted media imagery, celebrity faces, the deadpan grid — and turns it back on the system that produced it. *It's a Man's World I* names and ranks the men who run the world; *II* shows what that world does with women. The break is a **woman using Pop against the male gaze from inside Pop**, staging the asymmetry of who gets to be a person — a fully **feminist Pop voice years before second-wave feminism gave it a vocabulary** (Nell Dunn's *Talking to Women* is 1965; *The Feminine Mystique* had only just reached Britain). That this critique was then literally lost in a barn for 30 years is part of why it reads as a break recovered, not a break absorbed.

- Sources: paulineboty.org; Wikipedia; The Art Newspaper; Artsy; TheArtStory.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced)

**Primary quote (use this):**
> "Lots of women are intellectually more clever than lots of men. But it's difficult for men to accept the idea."
— Pauline Boty, interviewed by **Nell Dunn** for *Talking to Women* (MacGibbon & Kee, 1965).
Source: TheArtStory (artist/boty-pauline); the Nell Dunn 1965 interview is the documented origin (paulineboty.org, "In her own words" / "Nell Dunn" tag).

**Note on variants.** paulineboty.org's own "In her own words" excerpt renders the related sex line as **"I think sex can be as varied as being alive can be as varied,"** while TheArtStory shortens it to **"Sex can be as varied as being alive can be varied."** Both trace to the same 1965 Nell Dunn interview; **prefer the paulineboty.org phrasing** when quoting the sex line, and flag the wording variance. The "intellectually more clever" line above is the cleanest, most on-theme quote for this work and is well-attested.

**Secondary, also from the 1965 Dunn interview (paulineboty.org, verbatim):**
> "he accepted me intellectually which men find very difficult."
> "Painting. But… painting you do alone… it's really terribly alone and you make the whole thing yourself."

**Caution.** The widely circulated lines about marrying Clive Goodwin because "he really did accept me as a human being, a person with a mind" and "A revolution is on the way…" appear in secondary roundups (TheArtStory) — usable but confirm against the primary Dunn text before quoting as gospel. Do **not** attribute anything to *Pop Goes the Easel*: in that film **she is never given a speaking analysis of her own work**, so no work-statement quote exists there.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6 — prose pointers, SOURCED figures only, no coordinates)

For ***It's a Man's World I*** (figures per paulineboty.org's published sources key — "certain" unless flagged):

1. **The grid of famous men.** A packed field of male faces and bodies, each lifted from print/photo media and re-painted or collaged in — read it as a *ranking* of the world's men: genius, glamour, power, violence, all in one frame.
2. **The thinkers and the stars.** Look for **Albert Einstein** and **Marcel Proust** (the intellect), beside **Elvis Presley**, **the Beatles** (John Lennon and Ringo Starr), and **Fellini with Marcello Mastroianni** (the glamour of film) — the men she admired and desired, openly.
3. **Power and violence, top and bottom.** A **Boeing B-52 nuclear bomber** (with an **X-15** aircraft) flying over a grand Italian estate (the **Palazzina di caccia di Stupinigi**); **Lenin** (revolution); and the **frame of John F. Kennedy's assassination taken from the Zapruder film** — the world's men also run its wars and its killings.
4. **The bodies and the slogans.** **Muhammad Ali** (boxer) and a classical **Greek statue (Hermes and the infant Dionysus)** — physical ideals — with slogan-text in the field ("I am the greatest," "The creative adventure").
5. **The rose / flower motif.** A recurring painted **rose** threads through the canvas — Boty's signature soft, sensual counter-note running against all the male hardware.
6. **Tentative IDs — point gently.** A **matador (probably El Cordobés)**; a jazz musician (**possibly Thelonious Monk**, though Sonny Boy Williamson and Lester Young are also suggested); an **American Plains Indian in a warbonnet**; a **USAF pilot**. paulineboty.org flags these as uncertain — describe by type, don't over-name.

For ***It's a Man's World II*** (the answering canvas):

7. **The stack of nameless nudes.** Reclining/standing **female nudes**, re-painted from soft-porn magazines *and* the old-master / life-class nude tradition, tiled together and set against the blue sky of an **18th-century landscape garden** (the **Pantheon at Stourhead**, Wiltshire). The central figure is pale, headless, arms at her sides — anonymous, defined by her "parts." Where the men of *I* are named and ranked, the women here are interchangeable and unnamed — that contrast **is** the painting's point.

- Sources: paulineboty.org ("Sources: It's a Man's World I" key, with its own certain/tentative flags); Wikipedia (figure list, Zapruder source); Artsy; Artchive.

## 6. PROVENANCE

Both paintings are in **private collections** (paulineboty.org). Neither has a recorded permanent museum home. The provenance is the rediscovery story:

- After Boty's death (1966), the bulk of her output went into storage in a **barn on her brother's farm in Kent** and sat, largely forgotten, for **nearly 30 years**.
- In the late 1980s–c.1990, curator **David Alan Mellor** searched out her surviving family, located the barn, and recovered/conserved a body of work; he exhibited it in ***The Sixties Art Scene in London* at the Barbican, 1993**. Academic **Dr. Sue Tate** then built the scholarly reassessment.
- *It's a Man's World II*'s exhibition trail (paulineboty.org) runs from the **1966 Spring Exhibition, Cartwright Hall, Bradford**, through Barbican 1993, Brighton 1997, Wolverhampton & Chichester 2013, Łódź 2014, the Thyssen-Bornemisza Madrid "Pop Myths" 2014, and Whitford 2016. *I*'s trail includes Barbican 1993, Paris/Brighton 1996–97, Mayor+Whitford 1998, Whitford 2008, Christie's "When Britain Went Pop" 2013, and Lugano 2018.
- The general pattern: works re-entered view through these exhibitions and the art market (Boty pieces have since sold at auction), settling largely into **private hands** — hence "private collection."

- Sources: paulineboty.org (paintings list + exhibition history); The Art Newspaper (30 Dec 2023, "barn on her brother's farm," Mellor, Barbican 1993); Wikipedia (Mellor + Sue Tate rediscovery).

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

1. **The I-vs-II swap (real trap).** *It's a Man's World **I*** = the field of famous **MEN**. *It's a Man's World **II*** = the field of nameless female **NUDES**. One secondary source (**TheArtStory**) garbles this — it describes the work as a single **"diptych"** with nudes on the **left panel** and clothed men on the **right**. That is **wrong**: they are **two separate canvases** (I = 153×122 cm rectangular; II = 125×125 cm square), and the men are in *I*, the nudes in *II*. Follow paulineboty.org, Wikipedia, Artsy, DailyArt. Do not call them a single diptych or put both subjects on one canvas.

2. **Dates.** No reliable source dates either painting to **1963** (the brief's lower bound). *I* = **1964**; *II* = **1964–65** (paulineboty.org "1964/5"; some say 1965–66). Use **1964–65** for the pair.

3. **"Pure collage" vs painted.** *I* mixes **painted figures + pasted collage**; *II* is **painted** (nudes re-painted from sources, not pasted). Don't call the pair "collages" flatly — say oil on canvas, with collage in *I*.

4. **Figure IDs in *I* — verify, several are uncertain.** **Certain** (paulineboty.org / Wikipedia source key): Einstein, Proust, Lenin, Elvis, Lennon, Ringo Starr, Muhammad Ali, Fellini + Mastroianni, the JFK/Zapruder frame, the B-52 + X-15, the Stupinigi estate, Hermes statue, the rose. **Tentative / flagged uncertain:** the matador ("probably El Cordobés"), the jazz musician ("possibly Thelonious Monk," also proposed: Sonny Boy Williamson, Lester Young), the Plains Indian, the USAF pilot. **Trap:** popular write-ups confidently list "JFK, Lenin, Einstein, Proust, Elvis, Muhammad Ali, Cassius Clay…" — note **Muhammad Ali = Cassius Clay** are the *same person* (he changed his name in 1964, the year of the painting); do not list "Muhammad Ali AND Cassius Clay" as two figures.

5. **The cancer.** Specifically a **malignant thymoma** (a tumour of the thymus gland), found at a pregnancy check-up in 1965. She declined the abortion that radiotherapy would have required, gave birth to her daughter Katy (Feb 1966), and died **1 July 1966, age 28** (Royal Marsden). Don't call it leukemia. (Wikipedia / Grokipedia / Infinite Women converge on malignant thymoma; some general sources say only "cancer.")

6. **Biography proportion.** Keep the beauty/"Wimbledon Bardot"/early-death material *proportionate* — Marc Kristal (*Pauline Boty: British Pop Art's Sole Sister*, 2023) warns against flattening her into a tidy proto-feminist martyr; she had real inner conflict and middle-class-conventional pulls too. But the **erasure-and-rediscovery is genuinely part of the work's meaning** and belongs in the read. (The Art Newspaper, 30 Dec 2023.)

7. **Stourhead, not generic.** The landscape in *II* is specifically the **Pantheon at Stourhead, Wiltshire** — name it if you describe the setting (paulineboty.org / source commentary), rather than "an 18th-century garden" alone.
