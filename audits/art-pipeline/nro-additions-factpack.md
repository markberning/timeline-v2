## 1. Antoine-Jean Gros (1771–1835) — Neoclassicism / Bridge to Romanticism

**Who he is:** French painter, born 16 March 1771, died 25 June 1835 (by suicide, drowning). Entered the studio of Jacques-Louis David in 1785; David's most celebrated pupil. Known as the "painter of Napoleonic France." Today recognized as the principal bridge between Neoclassicism and Romanticism: he kept David's monumental format and historical-painting ambition but filled it with Romantic emotional intensity, vivid color, and the horror of war — directly influencing Géricault and Delacroix. He never fully accepted Romanticism as a movement, and when critics rejected his late return to Neoclassical subjects, he took his own life.

---

### Work 1: *Bonaparte Visiting the Plague Victims of Jaffa*

**Exact Wikipedia/Louvre title:** *Bonaparte Visiting the Plague Victims of Jaffa*
French: *Bonaparte visitant les pestiférés de Jaffa*
(Also referred to in English as *Napoleon Bonaparte Visiting the Pest House in Jaffa*)

**Date:** 1804. Exhibited at the Salon of 18 September 1804 — exactly between Napoleon's proclamation as Emperor (18 May 1804) and his coronation at Notre-Dame (2 December 1804).

**Medium:** Oil on canvas

**Dimensions (Wikipedia/standard):** 532 cm × 720 cm (h × w) = approx. 209 in × 283 in (17 ft 5 in × 23 ft 7 in)
Note: some sources list ~523 × 715 cm; the Wikipedia and most scholarly sources use 532 × 720 cm. The painting is landscape format.

**Location:** Musée du Louvre, Paris

**Propaganda function:** Napoleon is shown reaching out to touch a soldier's plague bubo (armpit swelling) — deliberately evoking the medieval "royal touch" tradition, in which kings healed scrofula through touch, a sign of divine favor. The painting was commissioned to neutralize reports that Napoleon had ordered fatal opium doses for plague-stricken French soldiers at Jaffa to prevent them slowing the army. By portraying him as fearlessly touching the sick, Gros recasts him as healer-king rather than murderer. Napoleon does appear to have actually visited the plague hospital on 11 March 1799 at the Armenian Saint Nicholas Monastery in Jaffa; the literal touching gesture is unverified but the visit itself is historically attested. The propagandistic construction (healer-king framing) is not a legend but an intentional artistic choice.

### Work 2: *Napoleon on the Battlefield of Eylau*

**Exact Wikipedia title:** *Napoleon on the Battlefield of Eylau*
French: *Napoléon sur le champ de bataille d'Eylau*

**Date:** 1807–1808. Depicts the scene on 9 February 1807 (the day after the Battle of Eylau). Exhibited at the Paris Salon of 1808.

**Medium:** Oil on canvas

**Dimensions:** 521 cm × 784 cm (h × w) = 205 in × 309 in (17 ft 1 in × 25 ft 9 in)

**Location:** Musée du Louvre, Paris

**Significance:** Described by scholars as "by far Gros's most realistic work depicting Napoleon" — it shows the vast carnage of the winter battlefield, frozen corpses, wounded soldiers begging for mercy, while Napoleon surveys the devastation. The emotional scale and unflinching horror are the Romantic core planted inside a Davidian commission.

## 2. Henry Fuseli (1741–1825) — Romanticism / Dream and Nightmare Strand

**Who he is:** Born Johann Heinrich Füssli, Zurich, Switzerland, 6 February 1741; died London, 16 April 1825. Swiss-born, spent the bulk of his career in Britain (moved to London permanently c.1779). Professor of Painting at the Royal Academy of Arts (1799–1805 and 1810–1825). Keeper of the Royal Academy Schools from 1804 until his death. His work explores the supernatural, dreams, nightmares, and the erotic uncanny — he is one of the founders of the dream/horror strand of Romanticism.

---

### Work: *The Nightmare*

**Date:** 1781

**Medium:** Oil on canvas

**Dimensions:** 101.6 × 127 cm (h × w) = 40.0 × 50.0 in (3 ft 4 in × 4 ft 2 in)
Landscape format: the sleeping woman's body stretches horizontally across the canvas.

**Location:** Detroit Institute of Arts, Detroit, Michigan (acc. 55.5.A; Founders Society Purchase)

**Subject:** A woman in white lies on her back across a bed, arms flung below her, in deep sleep. A small dark incubus (a demon of nightmares) crouches on her chest/belly, squatting and staring out at the viewer. From behind curtains at upper right, a horse's head with blank, glowing eyes peers into the scene. The three elements — sleeping woman, incubus, horse — encode multiple layers: sexual nightmare, folkloric night-terror, the horse as "night-mare" pun.

**Chronological note:** Painted 1781 — predates Goya's *Los Caprichos* (aquatints published 1799) by 18 years. Fuseli and Goya both explored the irrational and the supernatural in the same decades, but independently; Fuseli's nightmare imagery was circulating widely before Goya's series appeared.

**Multiple versions note:** The 1781 DIA painting is the original. Fuseli made at least one later version (c.1790–91), now at the Goethe-Museum, Frankfurt. The DIA work is the primary citation.

## 3. William Blake (1757–1827) — Romanticism / Visionary and Prophetic Strand

**Who he is:** English poet, painter, and printmaker. Born 28 November 1757, London; died 12 August 1827, London. Largely unknown in his own time, now recognized as a founding figure of English Romanticism and one of the most original visual artists of the era. Developed his own mythology (populated by figures like Urizen, Los, Orc) and his own printing method (relief etching), hand-coloring every copy of his books himself.

---

### Work: *The Ancient of Days*

**Date:** 1794. Served as the frontispiece to Blake's illuminated book *Europe: a Prophecy* (1794).

**Medium:** Relief etching with hand-applied watercolor. Blake's method: he etched the design in relief on a copper plate, printed in color, then added individual washes of watercolor to each copy — meaning every surviving copy is unique. The British Museum copy uses blue, black, red, and yellow with added hand-coloring.

**Dimensions:** 23.3 × 16.8 cm (h × w) = 9.2 × 6.6 in (British Museum copy; other copies vary slightly)

**The figure:** Urizen — Blake's mythological demiurge (creator-tyrant, analogous to a false god who imposes limits on the human imagination), NOT the biblical God of the Old Testament in Blake's intended meaning, though most viewers read the figure as God/the Creator. Urizen crouches inside a circle of light, arms spread wide, one hand holding a compass or dividers outstretched over the dark void below — the act of measuring, dividing, and limiting the infinite.

**Multiple copies:** Approximately 13 copies survive. Major holding institutions include: British Museum, London (copies a, d); Fitzwilliam Museum, Cambridge (k, m); Morgan Library & Museum, New York (g); Library of Congress, Washington D.C. (e); Houghton Library, Harvard University (c, h). **No single authoritative location** — cite as "various collections."

**Final copy:** Blake was reportedly painting a new copy of this image in the days immediately before his death in 1827. This is widely cited but should be treated as possibly legendary (the anecdote appears in early biographies; the copy he was working on at death is not definitively identified).

## 4. Elisabeth Vigée Le Brun (1755–1842) — Neoclassical Era / Women Painters

**Who she is:** Born 16 April 1755, Paris; died 30 March 1842, Paris (aged 86). French painter, the preeminent woman painter of the late 18th century and arguably the most financially successful portraitist of her era, male or female. From around 1778, she was the primary court portraitist of Marie Antoinette, painting more than 30 portraits of the Queen and her family. When the Revolution erupted, she obtained passports and fled Paris at midnight on 5 October 1789, spending 12 years in exile across Italy, Austria, Russia, and Germany. A committed royalist throughout. Published her memoirs (*Souvenirs*, 3 vols., 1835–1837). Career output: approximately 660 portraits and 200 landscapes. Elected to art academies in ten cities. She returned to France after Napoleon's amnesty and died in Paris in 1842.

---

### Work: *Self-Portrait with Her Daughter Julie*

**Date:** 1786

**Medium:** Oil on panel

**Dimensions:** 105 × 84 cm (h × w) = 41.3 × 33.1 in (3 ft 5.3 in × 2 ft 9.1 in)

**Location:** Musée du Louvre, Paris (acc. INV 3069)

**Subject:** Vigée Le Brun holds her daughter Jeanne Julie Louise Le Brun (born 1780) in her arms, both looking directly at the viewer. Vigée Le Brun smiles with her lips parted and teeth showing — a compositional choice that was controversial: academic doctrine held that painted portraits should not show open mouths or teeth. The intimacy and emotional warmth make it one of the most personally revealing self-portraits of the 18th century.

**Note on a second version:** A later version of the same composition (1789, different clothing — Neoclassical dress rather than contemporary attire) is also at the Louvre (130 × 94 cm). The 1786 work is the primary and more celebrated one.

## 5. Angelica Kauffman (1741–1807) — Neoclassicism / Founding RA Member

**Verification task:** Confirm "founding member of the Royal Academy (1768)" and "she and Mary Moser were the only two women founders."

**CONFIRMED — all three claims verified:**

**Dates:** Born 30 October 1741, Chur, Graubünden, Switzerland; died 5 November 1807, Rome. **Nationality:** Swiss-born (father Johann Joseph Kauffmann was from Schwarzenberg, Vorarlberg, then part of the Habsburg Empire — hence the "Swiss-Austrian" description sometimes used, though she is primarily identified as Swiss). She lived and worked in London (1766–1781), then Rome (1781–1807).

**Royal Academy founding:** In 1768, Kauffman was one of the 34 founding signatories of the Royal Academy of Arts, established by charter of King George III. She and Mary Moser (1744–1819, known as a flower painter) were **the only two women** among the 34 founding members. The RA did not elect another woman as a full Academician until Dame Laura Knight in 1936 — a gap of 168 years. Kauffman's quasi-exclusion is visible in Johann Zoffany's group portrait *The Royal Academicians* (1771–72), where she and Moser are depicted only as framed portraits on the wall, not present in person with the male members.

**History paintings (Neoclassical):** Kauffman specialized in history painting — the genre ranked highest by academic tradition — at a time when women were generally directed toward portraiture and still life. Notable works: *Penelope at Her Loom* (1764); *Ariadne Abandoned by Theseus* (1774); *Zeuxis Selecting Models for His Painting of Helen of Troy* (c.1778); *The Sorrow of Telemachus* (1783); *Virgil Reading the Aeneid to Augustus and Octavia* (1788).

## Legend Flags

- **Blake / *The Ancient of Days* — "painting at death" anecdote:** The claim that Blake was hand-coloring a copy of *The Ancient of Days* in the final days before his death is widely repeated but derives from early biographies; the specific copy he was working on at death is not definitively identified. Use as a vivid biographical note but flag as possibly embellished.
- **Gros / Jaffa — Napoleon touching bubo:** Napoleon's visit to the plague hospital is historically attested. Whether he literally touched plague sores is unverified; the gesture in the painting is an artistic construction for propaganda effect, not a documented act. Do not assert the touch happened — assert that *the painting shows* him touching.
- No other legend flags on the five subjects above.
