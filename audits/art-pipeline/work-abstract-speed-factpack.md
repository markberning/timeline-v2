**CONFIRMED: this is the CENTRAL panel of a three-part work (a narrative triptych).**
The triptych depicts a single landscape altered by a car passing through the atmosphere
along a white road, with green and blue forms (earth and sky) behind. The three panels

## 3. NARRATIVE BEATS (~5 — verifiable spine)

**Beat 1 — From the dog to pure abstraction (the 1912 → 1913 turn). [DOCUMENTED]**
In **May 1912** Balla painted *Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash* (*Dinamismo di un cane al
guinzaglio*; oil; **Buffalo AKG Art Museum** since 1984) — a dachshund's blurred legs and
its owner's skittering feet, motion shown by multiplying and blurring *recognizable things*,
indebted to Étienne-Jules Marey's chronophotography (stop-motion photos of animals in
motion, from the 1880s). Within roughly a year Balla pushes past depicting things-in-motion
to depicting **motion itself**: by 1913–14 the car, the figure, the dog are gone, and only
abstract forces, planes and lines remain. *Abstract Speed + Sound* is that arrival point.
[Buffalo AKG / Britannica / Wikipedia "Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash"; Guggenheim-Venice]

**Beat 2 — A car implied, never shown.** The subject is a car speeding along a **white road**,
with **green and blue forms** behind it standing for **earth and sky** — but **no car is
depicted.** It is conveyed entirely through the disturbed landscape, the force-lines, and the
atmosphere it leaves behind. (Tate's catalogue on the right panel: pinkish areas suggest the
**exhaust fumes** left by the passing car.) [Tate; Guggenheim-Venice]

**Beat 3 — "+ rumore": sound made visible.** The "+ Sound" in the title is not decoration.
Balla renders the car's **noise** as **crisscross / crisscrossing motifs** — a buzzing
lattice of lines laid over the speed-forms — an attempt to paint a sound the way Futurism
wanted *all* sensation (speed, noise, smell) fused into one picture. [Guggenheim-Venice, Wikipedia]

**Beat 4 — The triptych, and the painted frame that won't stop.** The work was (per the
leading reconstruction) the **centre of a three-panel sequence** reading one continuous
landscape across all three — Tate holds the right panel (*The Car has Passed*). Crucially,
**Balla painted the original frames** of the panels with **continuations of the same forms
and colours**, so the picture spills out of its own frame "into the spectator's own space" —
the image refuses to be contained, which is the whole Futurist point about speed. [Guggenheim-Venice, Tate]

**Beat 5 — Abstraction arrives in Italian art.** Balla is the Futurist who goes furthest
toward **pure abstraction** earliest; these 1913–14 speed pictures are among the first fully
abstract works in Italian painting. Frame this **Western-scoped and hedged** ("among the
first," not "the first abstract painting") — abstraction was emerging simultaneously across
Europe (Kandinsky, Kupka, Delaunay, Malevich); MoMA placed this very work in its 2012
*Inventing Abstraction* survey. Do NOT claim a sole "first." [MoMA *Inventing Abstraction*; framing-gate caution]

## 4. "LOOK CLOSER" ANNOTATIONS (6 — prose pointers, NO crop coordinates)

Each is `{label, where, detail}`; `where` is a written location phrase. **Fact-checker MUST
re-verify each against the actual painting image** (the colour-element calls below are from
museum text on the triptych, not yet eyeballed by the coordinator — flag any that don't match
the canvas). Hedge interpretive readings.

1. **The green landscape band** — *where:* "the lower zone, the broad green forms beneath the
   speed-lines." *detail:* The green stands for the **earth** the car races across — one of
   the two landscape colours (green = land, blue = sky) carried through all three panels of
   the triptych. It is a *form*, not a depicted field: abstraction, not scenery. [Tate, Guggenheim-Venice]

2. **The white road / speed-lines** — *where:* "the pale diagonal channel cutting through the
   composition." *detail:* The **white road** the unseen car travels; the long directional
   strokes are Balla's **lines of force**, the trajectory of speed itself. [Tate]

3. **The sky arcs** — *where:* "the upper zone, the curving blue forms." *detail:* **Blue =
   sky**, the second landscape colour; the arcs read as atmosphere bent and swept by the
   car's passage rather than as a literal horizon. [Tate, Guggenheim-Venice]

4. **The painted frame continuing the image** — *where:* "the border / frame all the way
   around the panel." *detail:* **Not an ordinary frame** — Balla painted it as a continuation
   of the picture's own forms and colours, so the composition runs off the canvas and onto
   its frame, "implying the overflow of the painting's reality into the spectator's own
   space." (This is documented museum fact, the single most distinctive feature.) [Guggenheim-Venice]

5. **The directional thrust** — *where:* "across the whole field, the repeated lines and
   planes all leaning the same way." *detail:* Balla multiplies lines and planes to drive a
   single sense of velocity; the eye is pushed along the road's direction. The "multiplication
   of the number of lines and planes" is the museum's own phrase for how this central panel
   differs from its flanking ones. [Guggenheim-Venice]

6. **The absence of any depicted car** — *where:* "look for the car — there isn't one."
   *detail:* The entire subject is a speeding automobile, yet **no car, wheel, road-sign or
   figure is shown.** Speed is conveyed only by force-lines, the disturbed landscape, the
   crisscross noise, and (in the Tate right-hand panel) pinkish exhaust. This is the proof
   the work is pure abstraction, not a stylized picture of a thing. [Tate, Guggenheim-Venice]

*(Optional 7th if the author wants it: the crisscross "+rumore" sound lattice — the buzzing
network of crossed lines standing for the engine's noise. [Guggenheim-Venice, Wikipedia])*

## 6. KEY FIGURES (cast nameplates)

- **Giacomo Balla** (1871–1958) — *The painter.* Turin-born, Rome-based; the eldest of the
  Futurist painters; signed the **Manifesto of the Futurist Painters** and the **Technical
  Manifesto of Futurist Painting** (both 1910); pushed Futurism furthest toward pure
  abstraction with the 1913–14 speed pictures; later co-author of *Futurist Reconstruction
  of the Universe* (1915, with Depero). [Wikipedia; Buffalo AKG; Britannica]
- **Filippo Tommaso Marinetti** (1876–1944) — *The movement's founder & impresario.* Poet
  who launched Italian Futurism with the **Manifesto of Futurism**, published in *Le Figaro*,
  Paris, **20 February 1909**, glorifying speed, the machine, the automobile, danger and
  modern war. Balla's worship of the car-as-emblem-of-the-age comes straight out of
  Marinetti's program — the conceptual engine behind *Abstract Speed + Sound*. [Britannica; EBSCO; Wikipedia]
- *(Secondary, for context only:)* **Umberto Boccioni, Carlo Carrà, Luigi Russolo, Gino
  Severini** — Balla's co-signatories on the 1910 painting manifesto (name only if the author
  needs the Futurist circle). [Proa / academia.edu manifestos source]
