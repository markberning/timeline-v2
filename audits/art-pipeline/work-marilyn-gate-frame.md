### [FIX-1] — Missing essential facet: the wider Death-and-Disaster series context

The prompt explicitly lists "Warhol's wider death-and-disaster series of 1962–63" as an essential facet, and it is genuinely absent. The draft situates the diptych only inside the *consumer-product* burst (soup cans, dollar bills, Coke) — which is correct and well-done — but never tells the reader that 1962–63 was *also* Warhol's death period. The Marilyn silkscreens sit at the hinge of these two strands: the diptych is both the last great commodity-image and the opening of the death works (car crashes, electric chairs, suicides, race-riot photos), which use the *same* silkscreen-repetition-drains-feeling logic on images of actual catastrophe. This is not trivia; it is the context that makes the "fade to death" half of the work legible as something Warhol was deliberately, repeatedly working on, not a one-off poetic accident. Web sources (Wikipedia, the gallery/critical literature) consistently frame Marilyn as the work that "established the artistic approach and concerns — death, mass media, repetition — that Warhol developed in the Death and Disaster series."

Suggested fix — add one or two sentences to the `break` "key statement" section (after the repetition-drains-meaning passage), or to `afterlife`. Suggested text (drop into `MarBreak`, after the blockquote-payoff paragraph):

> That draining-by-repetition was not a one-off. Across 1962 and 1963 Warhol turned the same screen-and-repeat process onto images of actual death — car crashes, the electric chair, suicides, news photos of catastrophe — his so-called <strong>Death and Disaster</strong> works. The dead movie star sits right at the hinge between the soup cans and the disasters: the celebrity image and the catastrophe image, both reproduced until the feeling wears off. Seen that way, the fade on the right of the diptych is less a poetic accident than the opening of a subject Warhol would work for the next two years.

This converts an "MAJOR missing facet" flag into a one-paragraph fix; it is corroborated and on-pipeline (no new unsourced claim).

### [FIX-2] — The objectification / exploitation-of-Monroe question: enacted but never named as the live debate it is

The factpack §8.2 and the prompt both flag the exploitation/objectification question as one that must be handled "with proportion — neither moralizing nor ignoring it." The draft does not moralize (good — no editorializing about Warhol's morals), but it currently *enacts* the objectification ("a star turned into a product the week she stopped being a person"; "never reaching past it to anything private"; "the face reduced to its few sellable features") without ever telling the reader that **whether this is a critique of objectification or an act of it is a real and unresolved critical question.** As written, the prose presents the depersonalization as straightforwardly *the work's argument* — which leans, by default, toward the charitable "Warhol is critiquing how society consumed her" reading, without signposting that critics genuinely split on this (some read it as Warhol exposing the machine that ate Monroe; others as Warhol himself coolly cashing in on a fresh death). That asymmetry — naming only the critique reading, by implication, and not the exploitation charge — is the proportion gap.

This is a fairness fix, not a content add: the draft has all the raw material, it just needs one neutral sentence that *names the two-sidedness* rather than resolving it. Suggested text — add to `death` (end of the third paragraph, after "a star turned into a product the week she stopped being a person") OR to `afterlife`:

> Whether that is a cold indictment of how a culture consumed Monroe, or Warhol coolly doing the same thing himself the week she died, is a question critics have never settled — the diptych refuses to tell you which, and that refusal is part of why it still unsettles.

This keeps the no-moralizing posture, removes the implicit lean toward the flattering reading, and gives the exploitation charge its due weight without the gate (or the prose) taking a side.
