## Top 5 comprehension-breakers across the batch

1. **MUST-FIX — Moulin Galette:** "Jeanne Samary (younger sister)" — Estelle is
   the younger sister of actress Jeanne Samary, but Jeanne is not introduced.
   A newcomer reads "the actress Jeanne Samary" and has no idea who that is or
   why the phrasing "younger sister of the actress" is supposed to signal
   something. Estelle needs to stand on her own: "Estelle, a young model
   Renoir knew from the neighborhood."

2. **MUST-FIX — Cradle:** Does the newcomer actually hold the Edma-not-Berthe
   distinction through to the end? The chapter `sister` nails it. But in
   `looking`, the looker pointer says "Not Morisot. Edma. Hold the distinction."
   — good. However, in `show`, the sentence *"she painted her into one of the most
   famous pictures of motherhood in the world"* (the hook) and *"the seated woman
   in *The Cradle* is not Berthe Morisot"* (chapter 1) both prime the reader
   correctly. The risk point is `afterlife`: the phrase *"the sister who quit got
   her likeness into the Louvre"* — at this stage a skim-reader who lost the
   thread might briefly re-confuse "the sister who quit" with Berthe (since Berthe
   is the narrative's protagonist). The sentence is correct but the antecedent
   ("Edma") has not appeared for several hundred words. SHOULD-FIX: re-anchor
   "Edma" by name one more time in the `afterlife` closing lines.

3. **MUST-FIX — Grenouillère:** Édouard Manet is dropped into `afterlife`
   without introduction. He was briefly named in the `break` chapter as part of
   the Baudelaire/modern-life program — but the first chapter (`seine`) never
   introduced him, and the `break` mention is a glancing one. Then `afterlife`
   opens treating him as "Monet's friend and informal patron" as if the newcomer
   already knows who Manet is. A one-line gloss is needed on first real use
   in `afterlife`.

4. **MUST-FIX — Paris Street:** "Société Anonyme" / Impressionist Exhibition
   backstory is told in full in the `show` chapter of *Cradle*, in full again
   in `show` of *Moulin Galette*, and again in `show` of *Paris Street*. That
   repetition is fine as a standalone read, but within the same batch-A session
   a newcomer will have read all three and may be confused that the founding
   story keeps resetting as if they haven't heard it. This is a **reading-order
   design issue**, not a writing error per se — flag for the coordinator to add
   a back-reference ("the First Impressionist Exhibition of 1874 — covered in
   The Cradle read") rather than re-narrating the founding from scratch three
   times.

5. **MUST-FIX — Moulin Galette:** "Cézannes" listed in Caillebotte's collection
   (`show` chapter, para 4) with no introduction of Cézanne. This is a cameo
   name-drop of a completely undefined person. Either define or cut.

#### 4.3 — Cradle: does the newcomer follow that the woman is NOT Morisot?

This is the critical question for this draft.

**Assessment: YES, but with one residual risk.**

The draft does the right things:
- Hook: "Two sisters trained as painters; one… the other painted her into one of the most famous pictures of motherhood in the world" — primes "other sister" as the painter, not the subject.
- Chapter `sister`, explicit callout: "Read that sentence again, because the standard cradle blurb gets it wrong almost every time. The seated woman in *The Cradle* is **not Berthe Morisot.** It is her sister **Edma Pontillon**."
- Chapter `looking`: "(**Not Morisot. Edma.** Hold the distinction; it is the whole picture.)"
- Chapter `show`, para 5: the Julie Manet clarification drives the point home again.

**The residual risk** is in the very last two sentences of the `afterlife` chapter:

> *"The sister who quit got her likeness into the Louvre. The sister who didn't quit put it there."*

These are beautiful closing lines, but: by the time a newcomer reaches them, several hundred words of `afterlife` have passed since "Edma" was last named. The final lines use "the sister who quit" (= Edma) and "the sister who didn't quit" (= Berthe) as the only identifiers. A skim-reader who has lost track of which sister is the painter and which is the subject — and skim-readers definitely exist — could momentarily read this backwards. The echo is intentional and works for an attentive reader, but the antecedents are fully unanchored.

**MUST-FIX:** Add Edma's name to the closing couplet: "Edma — the sister who quit — got her likeness into the Louvre." This doesn't kill the rhetorical rhythm but prevents the final misread.

#### 4.4 Confusing pronouns / sentences requiring re-reads

- **Chapter `making`, para 2 (Degas list):** *"She could not go alone to the working-port docks at La Grenouillère — the Seine-side bathing spot just outside Paris — and paint in the open next to Renoir and Monet the way they painted each other there in 1869."* — SHOULD-FIX: La Grenouillère is introduced earlier in the batch (and in its own deep-read) but a newcomer reading *Cradle* in isolation will stumble on this parenthetical. The description "working-port docks at La Grenouillère" is also slightly incorrect — La Grenouillère was a bathing resort / dance café, not a working port. MINOR but muddying. Simplify: "the riverside dance-café at La Grenouillère, just outside Paris." And remove "working-port docks." (This may also be a factual slip — flag for gate 1/2 as well.)

- **Chapter `sister`, para 7:** *"Her own daughter, **Julie Manet**, wouldn't be born until **1878**, six years after this canvas."* — CLEAR.

- **Chapter `show`, para 2 (800 francs comparison):** *"Monet, the same week, in the same show, sold *Impression, Sunrise* — the picture that was about to lend its title to the entire movement — for the same **800 francs**."* — SHOULD-FIX: "sold" here may confuse a newcomer, because earlier the draft emphasizes the difference between *listing* and *selling*. Did Monet actually *sell* it, or was it only listed? The draft asserts it as a sale, and if that's factually correct it's fine — but the paragraph immediately surrounding it is about Morisot *not* selling at 800 francs, so the contrast with Monet "selling for 800 francs" is load-bearing. If Monet also only listed at 800 francs, this needs correction. If he genuinely sold, fine — but a brief "Monet actually sold his…" would sharpen the contrast.

## Batch-level design note for the coordinator

These four reads will often be encountered together. Three of them (`Galette`, `Paris Street`, `Cradle`) each re-tell the founding of the First Impressionist Exhibition from scratch. Each retelling is fine as a standalone, but in a batched reading session the newcomer will get the founding story three times in full. The coordinator should decide: does each work stand alone (in which case three full retellings are correct), or does a reading session assume a sequence (in which case the second and third appearances should say "the First Impressionist Exhibition of 1874 — described in *The Cradle* / *Galette* chapter")? This is an architecture question, not a writing error — but it is the single most likely source of "I feel like I already read this" in the batch.
