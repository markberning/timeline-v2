# PART A — Movement metadata

## hook (one sentence)
A poet wrote a rulebook for making art with the reasoning mind switched off, and a generation started painting their dreams.

## hookLong (2-4 sentences)
Surrealism was at once a real attempt to map the unconscious and a personality cult with a pope. In Paris in 1924 the poet André Breton published a manifesto declaring Surrealism to be "pure psychic automatism," art made with the rational, editing mind turned off, the hand running free the way Freud's patients free-associated on the couch. Out of that one idea grew two streams that look nothing alike: the automatists (Miró, Masson, Ernst) who let chance and the wandering line do the composing, and the dream-image painters (Dalí, Magritte, Tanguy) who rendered impossible scenes with photographic, hyper-real precision, a clock melting over a branch, a pipe that insists it is not a pipe. Where Dada burned art down with a laugh, Surrealism rebuilt on the ashes with a program, ran itself like a church (Breton expelled Dalí, in the end, primarily for refusing to condemn Hitler and for backing Franco after the Spanish Civil War, with his commercialism a lesser charge), and leaked into film, advertising, and the everyday word "surreal" so completely it never really ended.

## artists row (~7)
1. **André Breton** (1896-1966): the founder and "Pope of Surrealism." Poet and ex-medical-orderly who used Freud's methods on shell-shocked soldiers in WWI; wrote the 1924 manifesto, ran the group like a church (a manifesto, a journal, a research bureau, and serial expulsions), and defined Surrealism as "psychic automatism."
2. **Max Ernst** (1891-1976): the inventor-technician. German painter who bridged Dada to Surrealism and invented frottage (rubbing graphite over paper laid on grainy wood so an image surfaces by chance) and grattage (scraping paint); master of collage; later exiled to New York.
3. **Salvador Dalí** (1904-1989): the showman of the dream image. Spanish painter of the melting clocks; coined his own "paranoiac-critical method"; the waxed mustache and relentless self-promotion; expelled by Breton ("Avida Dollars") primarily for refusing to condemn Hitler and for supporting Franco after the Spanish Civil War, his open commercialism a secondary grievance.
4. **René Magritte** (1898-1967): the deadpan philosopher. Belgian; flat, sign-painter scenes that short-circuit logic (the pipe that says it is not a pipe, the apple over a bowler-hatted face); the quietest and most quoted Surrealist.
5. **Joan Miró** (1893-1983): the automatist poet of shape. Catalan; let chance and the unconscious compose biomorphic, nursery-bright worlds (*Harlequin's Carnival*); pushed steadily toward abstraction.
6. **Leonora Carrington** (1917-2011): the serious woman artist the movement first saw only as a muse. British-Mexican painter and writer; arrived via a relationship with Ernst, then built her own alchemical dream-world (*Self-Portrait, Inn of the Dawn Horse*) and far outgrew the label.
7. **Meret Oppenheim** (1913-1985): the maker of the era's defining object. Swiss-German; the fur-covered teacup (*Object*, 1936) is the single most-cited Surrealist sculpture, and a pointed answer to a movement that wanted women as muses, not makers.

(Optional 8th if room: **André Masson**, 1896-1987, pioneer of automatic drawing; or **Yves Tanguy**, 1900-1955, the deep-space dream-landscapes Breton called the most faithful to Surrealist precepts; or **Frida Kahlo**, 1907-1954, claimed by Breton, who rejected the label.)

## parallels (2-3 "meanwhile")
1. **1922-1939 · Europe**: *The interwar years and the rise of fascism.* Surrealism's whole life runs between the two world wars, against the rise of Mussolini, Hitler, and Franco. The group was militantly left (Breton himself joined the Communist Party in 1927, broke with Stalin, and later co-wrote a manifesto with the exiled Trotsky), which is exactly why Dalí's refusal to condemn Hitler, and his support for Franco, got him expelled. The politics are not background; they fractured the group.
2. **1900s-1930s · Europe**: *Freud and psychoanalysis going mainstream.* Freud's *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900) and his ideas spread across Europe in exactly these decades. Surrealism is the art movement that took Freud most literally, turning a clinical theory (the unconscious, the dream, free association) into a studio program. Freud himself privately called the Surrealists cranks, and only reconsidered, about Dalí specifically, after Dalí came to see him in London in 1938.
3. **1920s-1930s · Northern Europe**: *The drive to pure abstraction.* While the Surrealists chased the dream and the figure, De Stijl (Mondrian) and the Bauhaus were pushing the opposite way: pure geometric form, no story, no unconscious. Two avant-gardes at once, one mining the inner mind, one purging it for pure geometry.

## lineage

**Parents (gave):**
- **Dada** (1916-1924): gave: the engine. The direct parent. Surrealism is Paris Dada's serious wing, keeping Dada's chance, collage, and provocation but aiming them at the unconscious instead of pure negation. (Reference the Dada read for the handoff, do not re-tell it.)
- **Giorgio de Chirico's Metaphysical painting** (c.1909-1919): gave: the visual DNA. The empty arcaded square, the long shadow, the mannequin, dread built from ordinary things. Ernst, Tanguy, Dalí, and Magritte all drank from it.
- **Sigmund Freud and psychoanalysis**: gave: the subject and the method. The unconscious, dreams as the road into it, and free association (the model for automatism). Breton built the whole program on Freud, though Freud himself was skeptical, privately called them cranks, and only partly came round, about Dalí, after meeting him.
- **Symbolism** (late 19th c.): gave: the mood. The dream and the inner vision over the seen world, a deeper ancestor. (Reference, do not re-explain.)

**Children (took):**
- **Abstract Expressionism** (New York, 1940s-1950s): took: automatism, made into gesture. The direct heir. The émigrés' "marks from the unconscious" became Pollock's drip.
- **Magical realism** (literature, mid-20th c.): took: the dream folded into the everyday.
- **Pop-surreal / lowbrow and later dream-painting**: took: the hyper-real impossible image.
- **Film**: took: dream-logic on screen, from Buñuel and Dalí (*Un Chien andalou*, 1929) through a long line after.
- **Advertising and fashion**: took: the surreal juxtaposition, now a default sales grammar. (Dalí himself went commercial, part of why Breton expelled him.)

## influenceSummary (one italic sentence)
*Surrealism took Dada's chance and collage, de Chirico's dreamlit empty square, and Freud's unconscious, made the dream and the accident serious artistic subjects with working methods to match (automatic drawing, frottage, the paranoiac-critical method), and handed automatism forward to Abstract Expressionism and the word "surreal" to everyone else.*

**prose (3 paragraphs):**

1. For all their quarrels, the radical movements before 1924 agreed on one thing: art works the world you can see, or the picture itself, and a wide-awake, deciding mind is in charge of the job. The Impressionists chased light; Cubism broke a visible room into planes; even Dada, in the read right before this one, aimed its insults at public targets, the gallery, the war, the bourgeoisie. Whatever they did, they did it with the conscious, editing mind switched on.

2. Surrealism turned the camera around. It went after the inside, the unconscious, the dream, desire, the irrational, and it deliberately switched the conscious mind off to get there. Breton borrowed the method from Freud's consulting room: free association, where a patient says whatever surfaces with no filter, and the unfiltered flow tells you what the reasoning mind has buried. Make art that way, Breton argued, and you tap a deeper truth than any deliberate composition could reach. He called it "psychic automatism," and it is the whole movement in two words.

3. From that one idea grew two methods that look like opposites. Automatism lets the hand or pen run with no plan and no editing, so chance and the unconscious do the composing: Masson's tangled automatic drawings, Miró's drifting biomorphs, Ernst's rubbed and scraped surfaces. The dream image does the reverse, painting an impossible scene with hyper-real, photographic precision so the irrational looks utterly convincing: Dalí's soft watches, Magritte's pipe that denies being a pipe, Tanguy's deserts of smooth stones. One stream slides toward abstraction, the other toward a too-clear nightmare, but they share a single new subject. Not the seen world. The dreaming mind.

## Chapter 1 — A word, a pope, and a manifesto

### Paris · 1924 · After the bonfire

Dada, the anarchic anti-art movement that came just before, had burned art down, and a fire that consumes everything eventually runs out of things to consume. By 1923 the Paris wing was tearing itself apart, and the loudest argument inside it was about what came next. The Romanian poet Tristan Tzara wanted to keep Dada what it had always been, a pure machine for nonsense and provocation with no destination. A young French poet named André Breton wanted to point all that energy somewhere. Breton was a man of imperious certainty, the kind who states a definition as if it were a command and dares you to disagree, and he won the Paris circle. The read right before this one tells that fight in full, so we will not re-fight it here. What matters for us is the handoff: Surrealism is Dada's serious wing. It kept the chance, the collage, and the appetite for scandal, and it pointed them at a target Dada never had, the unconscious mind.

A word about that word, because the story usually gets it wrong. Breton did not invent the term "surréalisme." The French poet Guillaume Apollinaire (1880-1918) coined it back in 1917, using "surréaliste" in the program note for a ballet and as the subtitle of one of his own plays. Breton took the existing word, gave it his own definition, and tipped his hat to Apollinaire while doing it. So the record is this: Breton founded the movement and wrote its rulebook, but the name is Apollinaire's, seven years older than the movement it would label.

### The manifesto · The rulebook nobody asked for

On 15 October 1924, in Paris, Breton published the *Manifesto of Surrealism* (a manifesto being a public declaration of what a movement stands for, in this case how it intends to make art). It came out through a small press, Éditions du Sagittaire, bound together with one of Breton's own prose-poems. Most movements get a manifesto after they already exist, written to defend work that is already hanging on walls. Surrealism is the rare case where the document came first and named the thing into being. The manifesto is the movement's birth certificate.

The heart of it is a joke that is also dead serious: Breton wrote a mock-dictionary entry, complete with the abbreviation for a masculine noun, defining the new word the way a dictionary would. "SURREALISM, n. m.," it reads, "Pure psychic automatism by means of which one intends to express ... the actual functioning of thought. Dictated by thought, in the absence of any control exercised by reason, free of any aesthetic or moral concern." A few lines later he adds that Surrealism rests "on the belief in the superior reality of certain forms of previously neglected association, in the omnipotence of dream, in the disinterested play of thought." (Those exact words come from one English translation, A. S. Kline's; other translators phrase them slightly differently, which is normal and which the next person to quote them should keep straight.)

Unpack the key phrase, because the whole program lives in it. **Automatism**, in Breton's sense, means making art (writing, drawing, painting, "any other manner") with the conscious, rational, editing mind switched off, so thought flows out unsupervised. He called his version **psychic automatism**; "psychic" here just means "of the mind" (from the Greek *psyche*, mind or soul), nothing supernatural, marking it as a thing of the mind, not the hand. The model was sitting in plain sight in the consulting rooms of the day.

### Freud · The foundation, who called them cranks

That model was Sigmund Freud, the Viennese doctor whose **psychoanalysis** (his method of treating the mind by digging up what it has buried) had spread across Europe in exactly these decades. Two of Freud's ideas became Surrealism's bedrock. The first is **the unconscious**: the part of the mind you do not control or even know about, where desires and fears the waking self has pushed down keep running anyway. The second is **free association**, Freud's clinical technique of having a patient say whatever floats up, with no filter and no shame, on the theory that the unfiltered flow leads straight to the buried material. Breton had seen this work first-hand. During the First World War he served as a medical orderly and used Freud-derived methods on shell-shocked soldiers. He took free association off the couch and turned it into a studio technique: do not compose, do not plan, do not edit, just let it come, and trust that what comes is truer than anything you would have chosen.

One caveat, because the movement liked to claim more than it invented. Automatic writing was not new in 1924. Nineteenth-century spiritualist mediums had scrawled "automatic" messages they claimed came from spirits, and Freud's free association is the direct and acknowledged model. Breton's real contribution was not the idea of switching off the conscious mind. It was making "psychic automatism" the defining method of an organized art movement, with a manifesto, a headquarters, and a membership list.

It got all three, fast, and in fact the headquarters came before the rulebook. On 11 October 1924, four days before the manifesto appeared, the group opened the **Bureau of Surrealist Research** at 15 rue de Grenelle in Paris, a kind of clearing-house and office for the new movement; Breton co-founded it, but it was put under the direction of the poet and actor Antonin Artaud. In December they launched a journal, *La Révolution surréaliste* (*The Surrealist Revolution*). Within weeks Surrealism had a manifesto, an HQ, a magazine, and a man at the center deciding who belonged. Breton would be called the "Pope of Surrealism" before long, and the rest of this read is, in part, the story of what a pope does with that kind of power.

A last orientation before the work begins, because the single idea is about to fork. From Breton's "psychic automatism" grew two streams that look like opposites and run through the next two chapters: the **automatists**, who let chance and the wandering hand compose the picture (Chapter 2), and the **dream-image** painters, who rendered the impossible with photographic precision (Chapter 3). One idea, two methods. Keep the fork in mind.

[MEANWHILE: Vienna, the 1900s-1930s. Freud's *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900) and the rest of his work spread through Europe in exactly the years Surrealism was forming. The Surrealists took Freud more literally than anyone, building a studio program out of his clinical theory. The feeling was not mutual. Freud privately wrote that he was inclined to look on the Surrealists as "absolute cranks." Only after Dalí came to see him in London in July 1938 (Stefan Zweig arranged the meeting; Dalí brought his *Metamorphosis of Narcissus*) did the doctor revise his view upward, and only about that one painter, writing that the young Spaniard's candid, fanatical eyes and undeniable technical mastery had made him reconsider.]

## Chapter 2 — Letting the hand run

### The first stream · Hand off the wheel

Breton had defined the method. Now somebody had to figure out how to actually paint or draw with the reasoning mind switched off, and the first answers came from a handful of artists who took "automatism" at its word. This is the first of Surrealism's two streams, the **automatist** stream, and its rule is simple to state and very hard to do: let the unconscious make the marks. Hand off the wheel. Whatever shows up, keep.

### Masson · A pen with no plan

The purest demonstration came from André Masson, whom Breton brought into the circle in 1924. Masson had been bayoneted and badly wounded in the First World War, and he kept the pen moving even when the line frightened him, which it sometimes did. He made what he called **automatic drawing**: he put a pen to paper and let it run with no subject in mind, no plan, no conscious control, the line tangling and looping across the sheet, and only afterward did he look for the half-figures, the limbs and beaks and torsos, that had surfaced in the snarl. The picture was not designed and then executed. It was let happen and then read. *La Révolution surréaliste* began publishing his automatic drawings in 1925, and they are about as close as visual art ever got to Breton's "actual functioning of thought," a hand moving faster than the mind that would normally edit it.

[FIGURE: Masson, Automatic Drawing (1924) | A pen let loose with no plan, the line tangling across the sheet into half-figures you can only find after the fact. As pure a picture of "psychic automatism" as the movement made: the drawing was let happen, not designed. | Masson, Automatic Drawing, 1924 · MoMA, New York | RIGHTS NOTE: pre-1930 = US-PD, but Masson d.1987, rights-fragile; gate-6 verify the specific file or demote to RestrictedFigure.]

### Miró · A carnival out of hunger

The automatist stream's founding painting belongs to the Catalan painter Joan Miró, and it came, by his own account, out of an empty stomach. *The Harlequin's Carnival*, painted across 1924 and 1925, is a nursery-bright room boiling over with dancing shapes: a winged die, a cat with a guitar, a ladder, a single staring eye, a teeming crowd of little creatures that are not quite animals and not quite toys. Miró said he painted it out of hallucinations brought on by hunger, the visions of a poor young artist who could not afford to eat. The shapes are what get called **biomorphic**, meaning they look like living forms, blobs and cells and limbs, without being any creature you can name. This is the automatist instinct turned playful: let the unconscious populate the room, and do not ask the shapes to mean anything in particular. Miró would keep simplifying and drifting from here, and his stream of Surrealism slides, picture by picture, toward pure abstraction.

[FIGURE: Miró, The Harlequin's Carnival (1924-25) | A teeming, nursery-bright room of dancing biomorphic shapes, a winged die, a guitar-playing cat, a ladder, a staring eye. Miró said he painted it out of hunger hallucinations. The founding picture of the automatist stream. | Miró, The Harlequin's Carnival, 1924-25 · Buffalo AKG Art Museum | RIGHTS NOTE: pre-1930 reproduction = US-PD, but Miró d.1983, RIGHTS-FRAGILE; gate-6 verify the specific file HARD, demote to RestrictedFigure if no clean pre-1930 reproduction confirms.]

### Ernst · Rubbing an image up out of the floor

The most inventive of the automatists was the German painter Max Ernst, who had already crossed over from Dada and who treated chance as a machine to be engineered. His great invention was **frottage**, from the French *frotter*, "to rub." The procedure: lay a sheet of paper over something with a strong grain or texture, a plank floor, a piece of weathered wood, and rub a soft pencil across the top so the texture comes up through the paper as a pattern. Ernst would then look into the random grain the way you look for faces in clouds, and coax out forests, leaves, birds, and beasts that the wood had, in a sense, suggested to him. The composition was half accident, half eye. He collected thirty-four of these into a portfolio he called *Histoire Naturelle* (*Natural History*), made around 1925 and published in 1926, a deadpan field guide to creatures that no naturalist ever catalogued because the grain of a floorboard dreamed them up. He had a companion trick for paint, **grattage**, scraping wet paint off a textured surface underneath so chance shaped what stayed. Frottage is the automatist principle made physical: do not invent the image, let it surface, and take what chance hands you.

[FIGURE: Ernst, plate from Histoire Naturelle (frottage, 1926) | One of thirty-four "natural history" plates conjured by frottage: paper laid over grainy wood and floorboards, rubbed with pencil so leaves, forests, and creatures surface by chance. A field guide to things the grain of a floor dreamed up. | Ernst, Histoire Naturelle, 1926 · MoMA, New York | RIGHTS NOTE: published 1926 = pre-1930 = US-PD for the original plates, but Ernst d.1976; gate-6 verify the specific file.]

Hold all three side by side and the stream comes clear. Masson hands the composition to the running line, Miró to the drifting shape, Ernst to the grain of the wood. None of them sat down to paint a thing they had already pictured. They built methods for getting the conscious mind out of the way, and then read what was left. It is the part of Surrealism that points forward most directly, because twenty years later, in New York, this exact instinct, marks made straight from the unconscious, would become the engine of an entirely American movement.

### The exquisite corpse · Automatism as a party game

Automatism did not stay solitary. From about 1925 Breton's circle turned it into a group game called the **exquisite corpse** (in French *cadavre exquis*): players take a folded sheet of paper and each, in turn, draws or writes one section of a figure (the head, then the torso, then the legs) without seeing what the others did, passing it along folded so the next hand is working blind. Then the paper is unfolded, and an absurd composite stares back, a creature no single mind designed. The game got its name from the very first sentence it ever produced, a string of words assembled the same blind way: "Le cadavre exquis boira le vin nouveau," "The exquisite corpse shall drink the new wine." It is automatism made social, chance composing not one person's picture but the whole room's at once.

### Man Ray · The camera as an unconscious instrument

The automatist instinct ran through the camera too. The American Man Ray, working in Paris, made what he called **rayographs** from 1921: cameraless photographs, made by laying objects directly on light-sensitive paper and exposing them, so the picture forms by chance arrangement rather than by aim. He also **solarized** portraits (a darkroom accident, developed with the photographer Lee Miller around 1929, that throws an eerie silver outline around the figure), turning the camera into something like an unconscious instrument, the photographic cousin of Ernst's frottage. His work is in copyright and lives here in words only.

## Chapter 3 — Painting the dream like a photograph

### The second stream · The too-clear nightmare

The automatists let the picture happen. The other half of Surrealism did the reverse. The **dream-image** painters did not hand anything to chance. (Their other name is the **veristic** stream, from the same root as "verity": "veristic" means truth-to-appearance, and the two terms are interchangeable, the dream-image painted as if it were a verifiable fact.) They planned and executed impossible scenes with the slow, exact, photographic finish of an old master, precisely so that the irrational would look undeniable. The trick is psychological: a melting clock painted as a vague smear is just a smear, but a melting clock painted with every reflection and shadow in place, sitting on a real-looking table on a real-looking shore, forces your eye to believe a thing your mind knows is impossible. They were painting dreams, and the whole point was to make the dream as convincing as a photograph of the waking world.

The visual grandfather of this stream had been working a decade before Surrealism had a name. The Italian painter Giorgio de Chirico, in his **Metaphysical painting** (in Italian *pittura metafisica*, his term for an art of dream-logic and dread built from ordinary things), painted empty Italian city squares with long raking shadows, classical statues, distant trains, and faceless tailor's dummies standing in for people. *The Song of Love* (1914) nails a Greek head and a red surgeon's rubber glove side by side to a wall, with a green ball below and a train on the horizon, an utterly calm picture of an utterly impossible arrangement. Nothing melts and nothing screams; the dread comes from the silence and the wrongness of the light. Ernst, Tanguy, Dalí, and Magritte all drank from de Chirico. He is the literal visual DNA of the movement, painted before the movement existed, which is exactly why his pictures, and not the more famous later ones, are the images you can actually see in this read.

[FIGURE: de Chirico, The Song of Love (1914) | A Greek head and a red surgeon's rubber glove nailed side by side to a wall, a green ball below, a train slipping along the horizon. De Chirico's "metaphysical" dream-logic a full decade before Surrealism had a name, the calm, dreadful empty square the whole dream-image stream grew out of. | de Chirico, The Song of Love, 1914 · MoMA, New York | RIGHTS NOTE: published 1914 = pre-1930 = US-PD; gate-6 verify the specific file (de Chirico d.1978).]

### Dalí · The clocks, and the man who would not stop talking

The showman of the stream, the one whose work became shorthand for the whole movement, was the Spaniard Salvador Dalí, who joined the group around 1929. Dalí painted the single most famous Surrealist image, and here we run into the central problem of this read: the picture cannot be shown. *The Persistence of Memory* (1931) is still under copyright, so you will have to take it in words, and it is worth the slow look.

Set the scene first. The light is hard and clear, a deserted luminous shoreline running back to the gold cliffs of Dalí's own Catalan coast, the sea calm, the sky empty. At the left edge of the picture stands a plain table, its hard square corner jutting into the foreground. Now the watches, four of them, one at a time. One pocket watch drapes limp over that table edge, drooping like something left too long in the sun. A second hangs off the dead branch of a leafless olive tree, melted soft and folded over the wood like wax. A third slumps across a strange pale shape lying on the sand, an eyeless, sleeping-face creature, half a face and half a slug, the watch lying over it like a blanket over a sleeper. The fourth watch, the only firm one, is closed and orange, and it crawls with a swarm of black ants. Now take in the whole: hard light and hard ground, and time itself gone soft, the whole scene held in a dream-stillness where nothing moves and clocks have stopped meaning anything. Everything is painted small (the canvas is about nine inches by thirteen, the size of a sheet of paper) and with a jeweler's precision, so the impossible softness sits inside a world rendered exact. It is a picture about time going limp, dreamed and then painted as fact.

Dalí had a name for his way of working: the **paranoiac-critical method**. The idea was to deliberately induce in himself a paranoid, hallucinatory way of seeing, the kind of mind that reads a hidden second image into everything, a face in a rock formation, a horse in a cluster of figures, and then to paint both images at once so the canvas flickers between them. He claimed it let him tap delirium on demand. As for the melting watches, Dalí said they came to him while he contemplated a runny Camembert cheese at the end of a meal. It is a wonderful anecdote, and it should be filed exactly where it belongs, as Dalí's own story. He was a relentless self-mythologizer who never let a fact get in the way of a better line about himself, so the Camembert is something Dalí said about his painting, not a neutral cause of it.

### Un Chien Andalou · The dream image moves

Before the clocks, Dalí had already helped prove the dream image would work in any medium. With the Spanish filmmaker Luis Buñuel he made *Un Chien Andalou* (*An Andalusian Dog*), a short film premiered in Paris on 6 June 1929, built entirely out of dream-logic with no story to connect its images. Its most notorious shot opens the film: a woman's eye held open, a straight razor drawn across it, the eyeball sliced (the close-up used a dead animal's eye, but the cut lands like an assault on the viewer's own). The point was the same one the painters were chasing, the impossible image rendered so concretely that it grips you, only now it moved and was projected on a wall. It is the dream image applied to cinema, proof that the method was not bound to paint, and it sent dream-logic into film for good.

### Magritte · The pipe that is not a pipe

Where Dalí performed, the Belgian René Magritte sat perfectly still. His paintings are flat, sober, and as plainly painted as a shop sign, which is precisely what makes them so unsettling: he renders the impossible in the bland, reasonable voice of an instruction manual. His most quoted picture is a lesson in one image. *The Treachery of Images* (1929) shows a single brown pipe, carefully painted, floating on a blank background, and beneath it, in tidy cursive, the line "Ceci n'est pas une pipe," "This is not a pipe." The first reaction is to object: of course it is a pipe. But Magritte is right and you are wrong. It is not a pipe. It is a picture of a pipe. You cannot pack it with tobacco or smoke it; the word "pipe," the painted image of a pipe, and an actual pipe you could hold are three different things, and we go through life cheerfully confusing them. It is the cleanest one-line lesson in modern art about the gap between a word, an image, and the thing itself, and Magritte delivers it with the straightest face imaginable. (He kept worrying at the same theme for decades; a much later picture, *The Son of Man* from 1964, shows a man in a bowler hat whose face is blocked by a hovering green apple, a self-portrait that hides the self.)

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Magritte, *The Treachery of Images*, 1929, LACMA (Magritte d.1967; treat as in copyright), and *The Son of Man*, 1964, private collection.]

### Tanguy · A desert of stones

The third dream-image painter worth knowing is the Frenchman Yves Tanguy, who was entirely self-taught and who, the story goes, decided to become a painter on the spot in 1923 after glimpsing a de Chirico (*The Child's Brain*) in the window of Paul Guillaume's gallery on the Rue de la Boétie, spotting it from a passing bus and leaping off to look. He never trained, and he never strayed: Tanguy invented a single landscape and painted it for the rest of his life, one nowhere rendered over and over, a vast, evenly lit plain that recedes to a far, low horizon, the ground littered with smooth, pale, biomorphic shapes that look like bones, pebbles, or organs worn round by some invisible sea. Nothing in it is nameable and nothing is alive, but the light is so consistent and the space so deep that you believe in the place completely, a nowhere rendered with the conviction of a real coast. Breton, who handed out judgments freely, thought Tanguy the most faithful of all of them to Surrealist principles. His mature work is all from the 1930s and 1940s, so, like Dalí's and Magritte's, it is in copyright and lives here in words only.

[IN-COPYRIGHT: no inline image; describe in prose only. Tanguy, e.g. *Indefinite Divisibility*, 1942, Buffalo AKG Art Museum (Tanguy d.1955).]

So the movement that started from a single idea, paint with reason switched off, had split into two streams that share almost nothing on the surface. One wanders toward abstraction; the other tightens toward a too-real nightmare. One trusts the accident; the other trusts the brush. But both are after the same buried country, and neither is painting the world in front of the easel.

## Chapter 4 — Woman as muse, woman as artist

### The contradiction at the center

Surrealism had a problem it never solved and mostly never noticed, and it was not a footnote to the movement but a load-bearing part of how it worked. The Surrealists were obsessed with "woman." She was their great subject and symbol: the muse, the gateway to the marvelous, the embodiment of desire and the irrational, the source of the convulsive beauty they were always chasing. They had a special idol, the **femme-enfant**, the "woman-child," an ideal of a young woman who was supposedly closer to the unconscious precisely because she was naive, unschooled, and not yet hardened into a rational adult. On canvas and in the manifestos, woman was everywhere and exalted.

In the studio and on the membership lists, real women were mostly somewhere else. They were welcomed as lovers, models, and muses, and largely not as colleagues. The men who built the movement were happy to paint women and to sleep with women and to philosophize about Woman, and slow to treat the women in their own circle as serious artists in their own right. That is the contradiction: a movement that put woman at the dead center of its art while keeping actual women out of the room where the art was discussed.

### The artists they kept calling muses

And yet the women were there, and they were good, and several of them ended up making work that outlasted the men's. Most arrived through a relationship with one of the male Surrealists, were introduced to the world as someone's beautiful young companion, and then quietly turned out to be major artists who outgrew the label. Name them.

**Leonora Carrington** came into the orbit through a relationship with Max Ernst, twenty-six years her senior, and was duly treated as the femme-enfant ideal made flesh, a beautiful, willful young Englishwoman. She was also a painter and writer of ferocious originality who built an entire private mythology of alchemy, animals, and dream. Her *Self-Portrait*, also called *Inn of the Dawn Horse*, painted around 1937 to 1938, shows her seated in jodhpurs with a lactating hyena beside her, a white rocking-horse floating behind her head, and a real horse galloping free out the window, a woman painting her own dream-world rather than posing inside a man's. She left Europe, settled in Mexico, and worked for another seventy years.

**Meret Oppenheim**, Swiss-German, made the single most famous Surrealist object of them all, and she made it at twenty-two. The men around her tended to condescend to her as a pretty young protégée; the object answered them. *Object* (1936), often called *Breakfast in Fur*, is a teacup, saucer, and spoon entirely covered in gazelle fur. It started as a café joke in Paris with Picasso and the photographer Dora Maar about how you could cover anything in fur, and it turns the most genteel domestic ritual imaginable, taking tea, into something animal, intimate, and faintly revolting: a cup you cannot drink from without putting fur to your lips. It is the most-cited Surrealist sculpture in existence, made by a woman the men preferred to muse about, and it was the very first work by a woman artist that the Museum of Modern Art ever acquired. The fame of it was so sudden and so total that Oppenheim, overwhelmed, walked away from the Paris scene for years. Like everything else in this chapter the piece is in copyright and lives here in description only.

The list runs on, and each of these deserves better than a name.

**Dorothea Tanning**, the American, painted her own arrival into the movement. Her *Birthday* (1942) is a self-portrait staged like a dream: the artist stands bare-breasted in a narrow corridor where door after door swings open behind her into a receding infinity of further rooms, her skirt dissolving downward into a tangle of writhing roots and tendrils, a small winged lemur-like creature crouched at her feet. She takes the domestic interior and the femme-enfant fantasy and twists them from the inside, the muse turning to paint her own myth.

**Remedios Varo**, the Spanish-Mexican painter, worked in a wholly different key: meticulous, jewel-like interiors that look like pages from an alchemist's manual, hooded figures in narrow towers spinning thread out of moonlight or coaxing music out of machines, every panel painted with the patience of a Renaissance miniature and the logic of a dream.

**Claude Cahun**, the French photographer and writer, turned the self-portrait into a laboratory of identity. Decades before the vocabulary existed, Cahun staged gender as something to be made and unmade, shaving the head bald, posing as a weightlifter, a doll, a masked androgyne, asserting in image after image that the self was a costume you could change.

**Leonor Fini**, the Argentine-Italian painter of sphinxes and commanding women, pointedly refused to formally join Breton's group, keeping the imagery and declining the membership. **Kay Sage**, the American, painted austere architectural dreamscapes, scaffolds and draped towers stranded in empty light, closer to Tanguy's deep space than to anyone's nursery.

### Kahlo, who said no

And then there is Frida Kahlo, the Mexican painter, whom Breton met and promptly tried to claim for Surrealism, declaring her work surrealist as if that settled it. Kahlo declined the honor flatly. "I never painted dreams," she said. "I painted my own reality." It is the cleanest rebuke the movement ever got: the most famous painter Breton tried to absorb looked at the label and refused it, on the grounds that her work was not fantasy at all but the literal facts of her own broken body and divided life. Her great double self-portrait *The Two Fridas* (1939) shows two versions of her seated side by side, hearts exposed and joined by a single vein, an image of a self split in two that she insisted was reportage, not reverie. Include her here not as a Surrealist but as the artist who saw the label clearly and walked away from it.

The summary is the one the men of the movement could never quite make. Surrealism worshipped woman and sidelined women, and the women it sidelined were, several of them, the equals or betters of the men idolizing them.

[MEANWHILE: across the movement's whole life, the women's work was shown, sold, and reviewed as minor or as charming accessories to the men's. The full reckoning came much later: it was the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries before Carrington, Varo, Tanning, Oppenheim, and the rest were widely treated as the major figures they were, and before their prices and museum walls caught up with the men they had been introduced as the companions of.]

## Chapter 5 — The pope and the excommunications

### A movement run like a church

Surrealism was the most organized of the avant-gardes (the avant-garde being the experimental artists out ahead of the mainstream, the ones pushing into new territory before anyone follows), and the price of that organization was a single man deciding who counted. André Breton did not just write the manifesto and edit the journal; he policed the membership, and he was ruthless about it. The nickname he earned, used half in awe and half in mockery, was "the Pope of Surrealism," and the joke had teeth. Breton presided. He convened meetings that functioned as doctrinal courts. And when a member strayed, in art, in politics, or in private life, Breton excommunicated him, the way a church casts out a heretic. There was even a colorful early claim, from one witness, that Breton half-styled himself the pope as early as 1924; take that as period gossip, attributed, not as established fact. What is not in doubt is the pattern of expulsions, which ran for the movement's whole life.

### The trials and the purges

The expulsions started early and never really stopped. Antonin Artaud, the visionary actor and theorist who had directed the movement's own Bureau of Surrealist Research in 1924, was expelled in 1926, along with Philippe Soupault, Breton's own co-author of the foundational automatic-writing experiments. Artaud's case stings the most: he was too extreme even for a movement of extremes, a man who wanted theater to be cruelty and convulsion and who pushed the irrational past where even Breton would follow, and so the man who had run Surrealism's headquarters was read out of Surrealism. The bloodiest purge came with the **Second Manifesto of Surrealism** in 1930, in which Breton settled scores in print and read a long list of members out of the movement. Breton liked to stage these reckonings as collective "trials," group sessions in which the accused answered for his deviation before the assembled faithful, which is exactly the kangaroo-court atmosphere the word "trial" should call up: the verdict was rarely in question. To be a Surrealist was to be one drift, one wrong friendship, one heterodox opinion away from the door.

### The sex sessions, and what they recorded

One episode deserves to be named plainly rather than softened, because it is documented in the movement's own pages. Beginning in January 1928 and continuing on through 1932, the group held a series of recorded discussions about sex, the "Recherches sur la sexualité" ("Researches on Sexuality"), and published them in *La Révolution surréaliste*. They are revealing in a way the participants did not intend. Across the sessions Breton repeatedly and aggressively denounced male homosexuality, shutting down the topic and the men who raised it. A movement that prided itself on liberating desire from bourgeois rules turned out to have firm rules of its own about which desires were acceptable, and its leader's homophobia is there in black and white in the group's own journal. It belongs in the record exactly as it was recorded.

### Breton between Stalin and Trotsky

The purges were never only about art, because Surrealism was always also a political movement, and its politics were the real fault line. Breton joined the **French Communist Party** (the French branch of the international Communist movement, taking its line from Moscow) in 1927, convinced that a revolution of the mind needed a revolution of the state to go with it. It did not go well. The Party wanted art subordinated to its program, propaganda on demand, and Breton would not bend his Surrealism to a Party line; he was expelled in 1933. He then turned hard against Stalin, the Soviet dictator whose show trials and orthodoxy he found monstrous, and in 1938 he traveled to Mexico to meet the exiled Leon Trotsky, Stalin's great rival, then living under Diego Rivera's roof. There the two men drafted a manifesto, "Pour un art révolutionnaire indépendant" ("Manifesto for an Independent Revolutionary Art," 1938), arguing that art must be utterly free of state control; Rivera signed as co-author so Trotsky's name would not sink it, and it ran in the American journal *Partisan Review* that autumn. This is the real political story of Surrealism: not a movement that simply was Communist, but one caught between Stalinism and anti-fascism, whose Marxism turned out to be fiercely anti-Stalinist.

### Dalí, fascism, and "Avida Dollars"

The most famous expulsion, and the ugliest underlying quarrel, was Dalí's, and it turned on exactly that politics rather than on art. The Surrealists took it as given that an artist of conscience stood against fascism as Mussolini, Hitler, and Franco rose across Europe. Dalí would not. He refused to condemn Hitler and was openly fascinated by him, a fascination he kept painting (his *The Enigma of Hitler*, 1939, broods over a photograph of the dictator), and after the Spanish Civil War he supported Franco, the general turned dictator who had just crushed his own country. To his Marxist peers this was unforgivable, and it was compounded by Dalí's frank, gleeful commercialism: he wanted to be rich and famous and was happy to make art for money, which the group regarded as a second betrayal, though the lesser one.

Breton put him on trial and, through the 1930s, drove him out, the break formalized by around 1939. As a parting shot Breton coined an anagram of "Salvador Dalí" that rearranges the letters into "Avida Dollars," which reads in French as *avide à dollars*, "greedy for dollars." Dalí, never one to lose the last word, claimed the nickname had "a certain magical value," because after Breton bestowed it "the rain of dollars has not stopped." And when the group moved against him, Dalí reportedly answered with the line that became his motto, echoing the Sun King's "I am the State": "Le surréalisme, c'est moi," Surrealism, it's me. (Both of those are Dalí's lines and Dalí's self-mythology, not neutral history; he said them, and he meant them to burnish the legend of Dalí.) The fight over Dalí is the clearest case of the politics of the era reaching inside the movement and snapping it.

### What they collected

One more thing belongs in the account. Like many European artists of their generation, the Surrealists avidly collected non-Western art, masks, carvings, and objects from Africa, Oceania, and Native America, which they prized as windows onto "the marvelous," a realm of the irrational and the magical they felt their own over-rational civilization had lost. The admiration was real, but it was admiration filtered through the colonial assumptions of the time, treating whole living cultures as a quarry of exotic raw material for European dreams, with little interest in what the objects actually meant to the people who made them. It was of a piece with the femme-enfant idea: a romance with a projected "primitive" innocence that flattered the romancer more than it honored the thing romanced. Name it for what it was, neither a crime to be screamed about nor a virtue to be celebrated, but the colonial gaze of the age, present in even this self-consciously radical group.

The living example of the tension was the Cuban painter Wifredo Lam, of African, Chinese, and Spanish descent, who was close to Breton and moved inside the Surrealist circle, embraced and exoticized in nearly the same gesture, prized for the very "primitive" otherness the group projected onto him. Lam took the Surrealist vocabulary and turned it the other way, against the colonial gaze rather than in service of it: his great canvas *The Jungle* (1943) crowds the picture with masklike faces and cane-stalk limbs out of Afro-Cuban ritual, claiming the marvelous for the colonized rather than borrowing it from them.

### The big shows · A sect that filled galleries

For all the in-fighting, the movement was reaching a real public, and the proof was the big international exhibitions of the late 1930s. The International Surrealist Exhibition opened at the New Burlington Galleries in London on 11 June 1936 and drew some 23,000 visitors in six weeks, a genuine crowd, and it put British Surrealism on the map more or less overnight. Two years later came the Exposition Internationale du Surréalisme at the Galerie des Beaux-Arts in Paris, opening on 17 January 1938, organized by Breton and the poet Paul Éluard and turned into a piece of theater in its own right: Marcel Duchamp hung the installation, suspending 1,200 coal sacks from the ceiling over a brazier so that visitors moved through a dim, sooty, smelling cavern of a gallery. It was the most theatrical show the movement ever staged, and the clearest measure of how far its reach now ran.

Hold the chapter's two halves together. Surrealism produced genuinely liberating art, and filled galleries doing it, and it also ran itself like a sect, with a pope, heresy trials, excommunications, recorded prejudice, and a tangle with the worst politics of its century. Both are true, and the second does not cancel the first.

## Chapter 6 — Exile, and what leaked out

### The war scatters the group

What finally broke up the Paris group was not Breton's trials but the German army. When France fell in 1940, the Surrealists, many of them Jewish, foreign, leftist, or all three, were in obvious danger, and they scattered. A rescue operation run out of Marseille by Varian Fry (an American journalist who ran an underground network that smuggled thousands of refugees out of Vichy France, the collaborationist French regime in the unoccupied south) helped get endangered artists and intellectuals to safety, and money from the American heiress and collector Peggy Guggenheim, among others, helped pay the passage. Across 1940 and 1941 Breton, Ernst, Masson, Tanguy, the Chilean painter Matta, Kurt Seligmann, Dalí, and more reached New York. The capital of the avant-garde had, for the duration of the war, moved across the Atlantic.

### First Papers, and a mile of string

The émigrés announced themselves with a show in the autumn of 1942 whose title was a grim immigrant joke: "First Papers of Surrealism," named for the first set of documents an immigrant files toward American citizenship. The most memorable thing about it was not a painting but an installation by Marcel Duchamp, the old Dada provocateur, who webbed the galleries with something like a mile of string strung crisscross through the rooms (a piece sometimes called *His Twine*), so that visitors had to peer at the Surrealist paintings through a literal tangle, the art half-trapped, half-protected behind it. It was a fitting welcome: the movement of the irrational, in exile, hanging its dreams behind a maze.

### A movement with no borders

It is worth pausing to register how far Surrealism had already spread by the time the war pushed it overseas, because it makes the New York chapter look less like an accident. By the mid-1930s organized Surrealist groups existed well beyond Paris: in Belgium, where Magritte and Paul Delvaux worked; in Britain, after the 1936 London show; in Czechoslovakia, where a formal Prague group founded in 1934 became one of the most active anywhere; and as far afield as Japan. No other modern movement traveled so wide or lasted so long. Surrealism was the most international and the longest-lived of them all, which is part of why it had a foothold ready when its founders washed up in America.

### The fuse for Abstract Expressionism

The deepest mark the exiles left on America was not their own pictures but their method. The young New York painters circling the émigrés, Jackson Pollock, Arshile Gorky, Robert Motherwell, and the rest, latched onto the one idea that traveled best across the language barrier: **automatism**, making marks straight from the unconscious with the editing mind switched off. They took Masson's running line and Ernst's chance procedures and pushed them past any recognizable image at all, into pure gesture, the loop and splatter and drag of paint as a direct record of the body and the unconscious that drove it. Pollock laying a canvas on the floor and pouring paint across it in long automatic arcs is Breton's "psychic automatism" carried to its limit, the hand running free with no plan, only now the line never resolves into a figure. Surrealism's most forward-looking idea became the engine of the first American movement to lead the world, **Abstract Expressionism**, which is taken up in its own read. Automatism became gesture, and the center of art moved, for good, to New York.

### What leaked out

Breton went home to Paris in 1946 and kept the group going, but its moment as the avant-garde was over; Abstract Expressionism had eclipsed it. And yet, of all the movements in this era, Surrealism may have won the most completely, because it did not stay in the galleries. It leaked. It leaked into **film**, where Buñuel and Dalí's *Un Chien andalou* (1929), with its notorious shot of a sliced eyeball, had already proved that dream-logic could run on a screen, and where the line runs on through David Lynch and a thousand music videos. It leaked into **advertising and fashion**, where the surreal juxtaposition, two things that have no business together, jammed into one image, became and remains a default grammar of selling. It leaked into **magical realism** in literature, the dream folded into the ordinary. And it leaked into the language itself: "surreal" is now an everyday word for anything dreamlike and impossibly strange, used a million times a day by people who have never heard of André Breton.

So measure the movement by what it left behind. Surrealism set out to make the dream, the accident, and the irrational into real artistic subjects, and it won that argument so completely that its instincts are now the common air, in films and ads and the word "surreal" itself. The same movement ran like a church, with a pope and his purges, idolized woman while shutting women out, and tangled with the worst politics of its century. Those are not two stories competing for the verdict; they are the same story, the melting clock and the heresy trial cut from one cloth. Taking the dream seriously turned out to be one of the most consequential ideas in modern art. The people who had it were as human, as petty, and as flawed as the dreams they painted, and the art does not need them to have been otherwise.
