## 2. THE STORY

Yves Tanguy built a single, instantly recognizable world and painted it over and over: a vast, deserted plain receding to a high horizon, scattered with smooth, invented objects that look like bone, stone, or polished metal but resemble nothing in the real world. He had no formal art training — he was **self-taught** — and his turn to painting was a thunderbolt: around 1923 he saw a painting by **Giorgio de Chirico** (*Le Cerveau de l'enfant* / *The Child's Brain*) in a Paris gallery window and decided on the spot to become a painter. **[ArtStory]** He joined André Breton's Surrealist circle in the mid-1920s and became one of its purest practitioners of "dream landscape" painting.

With World War II closing in, Tanguy **emigrated to the United States in 1939**; in **1940 he married the American Surrealist painter Kay Sage** (the wedding was in Reno, Nevada). **[ArtStory]** *Indefinite Divisibility* is an American-period picture, painted in **1942**, the work of an émigré already settled into the wide, empty light of the New World. **[AKG]**

The painting's signature is its **meticulous, illusionistic rendering of things that exist nowhere.** Tanguy paints his invented forms with hard-edged, almost academic precision — convincing weight, convincing shadow, convincing recession into deep space — so that an entirely fictitious object reads as solidly present. At the center stands a **towering "machine" or totem of stacked forms**, a vertical assembly of biomorphic parts that dominates the foreground and **casts a long dark shadow** across the plain (a deep-shadow device that recalls de Chirico). **[AKG]**, **[Artchive]** The museum sums up his project: "Without employing direct references to the real world, Yves Tanguy painted surreal landscapes laden with strange, indeterminate forms." **[AKG]**

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required before/after)

**Before:** A landscape painting depicted a *place* — a real or plausible scene, even when stylized or imagined as a composite of real things. Even de Chirico's dream-piazzas are built from recognizable architecture, statues, arcades. Earlier abstraction (Kandinsky, the Cubists) broke the *finish*: it abandoned smooth illusionistic rendering to flatten, fracture, or simplify the picture.

**The break:** Tanguy keeps the **hard, illusionistic finish** — the deep space, the cast shadows, the modeled solidity of academic painting — and pours it into a **landscape that is wholly invented.** The objects refer to nothing; there is no real-world source to "recognize." It is **abstraction that looks real**: a fully fictitious inner world rendered with the precision of observed fact, so the eye believes a place it cannot name. The break is not a loosening of technique but the opposite — photographic-seeming precision in the service of pure invention, the dream made to look like a documented site rather than a sketch or a smear.

**After:** This "verism" — Surrealist precision-rendering of the impossible (the Tanguy / Dalí / Magritte line, as against the automatist Miró / Masson line) — set the template for illusionistic Surrealism and fed directly into later fantastic, sci-fi, and biomorphic-abstract imagery. The invented-yet-solid object on an empty plain is now a visual cliché *because* Tanguy made it convincing first.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — with caveats)

**Primary quote (on letting the painting surprise him / not planning it):**

> "The painting develops before my eyes, unfolding its surprises as it progresses. It is this which gives me the sense of complete liberty, and for this reason I am incapable of forming a plan or making a sketch beforehand."

- **Attribution:** Yves Tanguy. Widely reproduced and consistently attributed to him; The Art Story cites it in direct connection with *Indefinite Divisibility*. **[ArtStory]**, **[BrainyQuote/AZ]**
- **SOURCE CAVEAT (flag):** The exact original publication is **not reliably pinned down in available web sources.** It is frequently quoted but the period source (commonly said to be a 1940s American art-press statement / questionnaire, e.g. *Art Digest* or *View*) could not be verified online. **Use the quote, attribute it to Tanguy, but do NOT assert a specific publication/date** unless a primary citation is confirmed. The widely circulated text above is stable across sources.

**Companion variant (same idea, planning vs. surprise):**

> "I found that if I planned a picture beforehand, it never surprised me, and surprises are my pleasure in painting."

- **Attribution:** Tanguy; **[ArtStory]**, aggregators. Same source caveat applies — treat as a documented Tanguy statement of method, original publication unverified.
- **Variant note:** The two quotes express one position; some sources paraphrase or run them together. Quote whichever, verbatim, and don't fuse them into a single sentence.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The central stacked totem / "machine."** A tall vertical assembly of smooth forms rises near the middle of the composition — parts piled and balanced like a totem or a piece of invented machinery. It anchors the picture and gives the empty plain a single dominant event. **[AKG]**, **[Artchive]**
2. **The bone / stone biomorphic forms.** Across the plain, smaller objects have the pale, rounded, weathered look of bones, pebbles, or worn stones — organic-seeming but unidentifiable. They are modeled with real volume yet correspond to nothing nameable.
3. **The cast shadows on the empty plain.** Each form throws a long, dark, hard-edged shadow across the floor of the scene — the shadows are what make the invented objects feel physically *there*, and they recall de Chirico's deep raking light. **[AKG]**
4. **The high horizon and deep space.** The ground recedes far back to a horizon set high in the picture, opening an enormous, empty, desert-like distance. "Space appears infinite, like an endless desert." **[AKG]**
5. **The airless, oppressive light.** The atmosphere reads as still and "dense and oppressive," yet is penetrated by a "strong, warming light" — a glow with no visible sun, lighting a windless, lifeless plain. **[AKG]**
6. **The illusionistic finish itself.** Step back and notice the *technique*: smooth, hard, almost academic modeling. The precision is the point — it is what makes a wholly invented scene read as an observed, documented place.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

1. **Title translation.** Use the museum's own pairing: *Divisibilité indéfinie* = *Indefinite Divisibility.* Avoid loose renderings ("Infinite Divisibility," "Endless Divisibility"). The French is *indéfinie* ("indefinite"), not *infinie* ("infinite"). **[AKG]**
2. **Date.** It is **1942**, not c.1940 or 1944. Confirmed by the AKG record. **[AKG]**
3. **Dimensions discrepancy (flag).** The museum lists support as **101.6 × 89 cm** alongside **40 × 35 in.** Note 35 in converts to **88.9 cm**, so the AKG's "89 cm" is a rounding of 88.9. **Cite as 40 × 35 in / 101.6 × 88.9 cm** (precise), or quote the museum's "101.6 × 89 cm" verbatim — do not invent a third figure. Some third-party sites give "101.6 × 88.9 cm"; this is the same painting, just unrounded. **[AKG]**
4. **Over-interpreting the invented forms (key trap).** Do **not** read the objects as specific things — they are **not** machines, bones, organs, robots, or marble-runs in any literal sense. Tanguy's whole method is **non-referential invention**: "without employing direct references to the real world." Pointers may say a form is *bone-like* or *stone-like* (descriptive), but must **not** assert the painting *depicts* a machine, a totem, or any nameable object. Keep the forms invented and indeterminate, per the artist and the museum. The "child's marble-run" / "elaborate piece of machinery" reading appears on some commercial sites (Artchive) — treat as a viewer's analogy, **not** as fact. **[AKG]**, **[Artchive]**
5. **Institution name.** It is now the **Buffalo AKG Art Museum.** "Albright-Knox Art Gallery" is the former name; "Albright Art Gallery" was the name at acquisition (1945). All three refer to the same institution. Don't present them as different museums. **[AKG]**
6. **Quote source.** As in §4, attribute the "develops before my eyes" line to Tanguy but **do not invent a specific publication/date** — the original source is unverified online.
