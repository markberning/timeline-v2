## 2. FRAMING / FAIRNESS

### Scope: Johns is proto-Pop / Neo-Dada, not a card-carrying Pop artist — HANDLED, exemplary.
The draft does not flatly call Johns a Pop artist. The const comment, the `break · after` section ("Johns is not, strictly, a Pop artist… Neo-Dada… proto-Pop… a forerunner rather than a card-carrying member"), and the hook ("The bridge out of Abstract Expressionism") all scope it correctly and consistently. *Flag* is repeatedly framed as **predating** Pop and **making it possible** — the exact safe framing the fact pack §8 prescribes. This is the model handling for a work that sits in a chain it doesn't strictly belong to. **No action.**

### The patriotism / desecration question handled neutrally (Johns left the meaning open) — HANDLED.
The draft consistently refuses to assign a meaning. `break`: "refusing to tell you whether it meant patriotism, irony, or nothing at all." `afterlife`: the "unpatriotic" worry is presented as the *trustees'* reading, in quotation marks, not the draft's own verdict, and the section title even poses it as an open question ("Too patriotic, or not patriotic enough?"). The draft never claims Johns intended desecration nor that he intended homage. **No action.**

### Genius-myth — situated, not mythologized.
The dream origin is the one spot where a genius-myth could creep in ("the most loaded public image in America simply showed up… in his sleep"). The draft defuses it well: it (a) brackets the quote with two explicit honesties about its wandering wording, and (b) grounds the work in laborious, physical making (`making` section) rather than inspiration alone. The "destroyed essentially every piece of art he then owned" claim is his own documented account and is attributed, not asserted as legend. See the [NICE] above re: Rauschenberg for the one place the lone-genius register could be further softened. **No blocking issue.**

### First / only overclaim; proportion.

- **[FIX] The afterlife section asserts a price-record absence that the record contradicts.**
  Draft (`afterlife`, and provenance note for 1958–1973): *"No sale price is part of the story; it changed hands by purchase and then by gift, and the figures aren't in the public record."* / *"No purchase figure survives in the record."*
  This is not right. The 1958 Castelli/MoMA transaction figures **are** documented: at the opening Barr arranged for MoMA to take four Johns works, and the price attached to *Flag* in that deal is on record as **$1,000** (Green Target also $1,000, Target with Four Faces $700, White Numbers $450). The standard account is that *Flag* specifically could not be bought *by MoMA* because of the trustee objection, so **Philip Johnson** stepped in to buy it (at, by the standard telling, that ~$1,000 price) and hold it for the museum. So "the figures aren't in the public record" is an overclaim of absence.
  **Suggested replacement** (keeps the draft's point — that no money passed to MoMA — without the false "no record" claim):
  > "It changed hands twice — Johnson bought it privately (for a sum reported at the time as around a thousand dollars) and then gave it to the museum — so MoMA never paid a cent for the painting it had been too cautious to buy."
  And in the provenance note, replace *"No purchase figure survives in the record."* with: *"Johnson is generally said to have paid on the order of $1,000 (the figure attached to Flag in the 1958 Castelli deal)."* Mark the figure as "reported"/"on the order of" since the exact private-sale terms between Johnson and Castelli are less firmly fixed than the gallery list price. *(This may also be owned by the fact gate; flagging here because it is an asserted-absence fairness/proportion issue.)*

- **[NICE] "the show that made his reputation overnight" / "made him famous almost at once" — supported, keep.** Verified: ARTnews put Johns on its January 1958 cover, Barr stayed three hours and committed MoMA to four works, all but two of eighteen works sold. The "overnight" language is not an overclaim here — it is the standard, well-sourced account. No change needed; noting it because superlatives were on the watch-list and this one clears.

- **[NICE] "It is routinely named one of the hinge pictures of postwar art" — well-calibrated.** Hedged with "routinely named," attributed to consensus rather than asserted in the draft's own voice. Good proportion. Keep.

- **[NICE] "the most loaded public image in America" / "the most impersonal image in American life" — rhetorical superlatives.** These are voice, not factual claims, and read as fair characterization rather than overreach. No action; flagged only for completeness.

- **No "first Pop painting" overclaim** — the draft explicitly disclaims it ("not the first Pop painting, but the one that cleared the ground"). The only "first" asserted is that the MoMA *Flag* is the **first** of Johns's many flag paintings, which is correct (White Flag 1955, Three Flags 1958, etc. all follow). Good.
