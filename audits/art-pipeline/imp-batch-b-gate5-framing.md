## Work 1: The Dance Class

**Overall grade: TILTED**

### MUST-FIX

**M1 — The abonnés economy named then softened (chapter `rehearsal`)**

The draft correctly introduces the abonnés and the exploitation economy. But the sentence that follows the honest accounting immediately softens it in a way that partially erases what was just said:

> "Some of those arrangements became long-term liaisons. Some became marriages. Many were closer to what we would today call exploitation..."

The word "many" is evasive where "most" or even "the structure of it was" would be fairer. More critically, the three-clause structure — liaison, marriage, exploitation — lists them in escalating order, presenting marriage as a *better* outcome along a spectrum. That is the wrong frame. Marriage to a wealthy patron was itself often a transactional exit from poverty, not a freely chosen romantic outcome, for a corps dancer in the 1870s. The ordering implies "some were fine (liaisons), some were better (marriages), many were bad (exploitation)," which understates the coercive structure the paragraph has just correctly described.

**Quoted passage (chapter `rehearsal`):**
> "Some of those arrangements became long-term liaisons. Some became marriages. Many were closer to what we would today call exploitation, made possible by the gap between a corps dancer's wages and a banker's wallet."

**Fairer framing:**
> "Most of those arrangements — whether they became liaisons, informal sponsorships, or eventually marriages — operated from the same power differential: a corps dancer's wages against a wealthy man's wallet. Some dancers chose their situations with clear eyes; many had very little choice. Degas didn't moralize about this. He painted the room it happened in."

This keeps the house voice and the picture-not-moralizing framing while not sorting the outcomes into a false hierarchy.

---

**M2 — Stage mothers framed as "chaperones, in theory" — hedged away from their real function and constraints (chapter `looking`, look-closer pointer 6)**

The prose in chapter `rehearsal` states the mothers were "chaperones — guardians, in theory." The parenthetical "in theory" correctly signals that the backstage-access structure overrode their practical power. But the look-closer pointer 6 repeats the "in theory guarding" phrasing without any follow-through on what "in theory" means. A reader who reads only the pointer (the most likely reader, since look-closer pointers are standalone) gets "stage mothers = in-theory guardians" without understanding why the "in theory" is the whole dark point.

**Quoted passage (look-closer pointer 6):**
> "They were chaperones, in theory guarding their girls against the wealthy male abonnés (season-ticket holders) who had bought the right to walk backstage."

**Fairer framing for the pointer:**
> "They were chaperones — but the abonnés, the wealthy male season-ticket holders, had purchased the right to walk backstage regardless. The mothers could watch; they could not reliably stop what the ticket gave access to. Their presence in the painting is Degas's quiet record of a system that gave working-class women a seat in the room and very little power in it."

---

### SHOULD-FIX

**S1 — "Petits rats" etymology framed as charming rather than dehumanizing**

The prose introduces *petits rats* as "literally 'little rats,' because they scampered through the back corridors." This is the affectionate-nickname framing. The nickname was also, routinely, a diminution — working-class children named after vermin by the same institution that employed and depended on them. A single clause noting the dehumanizing dimension of the name would balance the etymology.

**Quoted passage (chapter `rehearsal`):**
> "Parisians called the youngest ones *petits rats* (literally 'little rats,' because they scampered through the back corridors of the opera house)."

**Fairer framing:** Add after the etymology: "The name was affectionate in the way institution-given nicknames for child workers often are — fond to those who used it, not necessarily to those it named."

---

**S2 — Afterlife chapter's final paragraph ("tote bags and birthday cards")**

The prose in chapter `afterlife` is correct: it names the falsification of popular memory ("pure prettiness — pink tutus, soft light, romantic dream"). But the corrective is soft: "Stand in front of it and the wage labor, the chaperones, the watering can, the bored teenager, and the old man on the stick come back." This lists "wage labor" first in a run of details, which lets the labour/class point appear equal to the watering can and the old man. The framing underweights it.

**SHOULD-FIX suggestion:** Give the labour/class corrective its own sentence before the list: "What tote-bag culture edited out is not just a few details — it is the whole labour argument. The chaperones, the wages, the watering can — all of them are the same claim: that the show cost work, and the workers were right there."

## Work 2: L'Absinthe

**Overall grade: FAIR**

This draft handles the most sensitive axis — Andrée's documented agency, Desboutin's printmaker identity, the staged-not-documentary nature of the picture — with real precision. The factual framing of the 1921 Fénéon interview is correct and proportionate. The refusal of Victorian projection is embedded throughout, not only in the show chapter.

### MUST-FIX

None.

### SHOULD-FIX

**S1 — "Desboutin was the one in real life who actually drank" (chapter `show`)**

The draft correctly cites Andrée's 1921 Fénéon correction. But the summary of that correction in the show chapter tips slightly beyond what Andrée documented:

**Quoted passage (chapter `show`):**
> "Yes, the glass in front of her in the picture held absinthe — Degas put it there for the painting. No, in real life she was not an absinthe drinker. And the man beside her, Desboutin — he was the one in real life who actually drank..."

The fact pack states Andrée said "Desboutin's drinking was the other way around" (i.e., the real drinker was Desboutin, not her), but also notes that popular accounts sometimes over-compress this. The draft's phrasing "he was the one in real life who actually drank" — without qualification — presents as documented fact what the fact pack tags as LIKELY ("Several popular accounts compress this..."). The LIKELY tag is in the fact pack precisely because the 1921 interview record is the surviving source and it is not always quoted in full.

**Fairer framing:** "And the man beside her, Desboutin — the one in real life Andrée implied was the actual drinker — held not absinthe but mazagran, a cold coffee, in the picture. The picture reversed their real habits, or so Andrée said."

This preserves the corrective force while signaling we are working from Andrée's own 1921 account rather than an independent corroboration.

---

**S2 — Andrée's age in chapter `making` ("she was about nineteen")**

Minor. The fact pack gives her birth as 1857, the painting as 1875–76. That makes her 18 or 19 at the sitting, not "about nineteen" as a flat number. This is not distortion — it's a rounding — but given that the draft is precise about Desboutin's biography elsewhere, rounding Andrée's age is the small inequality.

**SHOULD-FIX:** "In 1875–76 she was eighteen or nineteen, at the start of a career..."

## Work 3: Luncheon of the Boating Party

**Overall grade: TILTED**

Three cumulative tilts on the fairness axis, two of which involve the women in the picture being reduced to comparison-objects or labels.

### MUST-FIX

**M1 — "In much better company" comparative framing for Andrée (chapter `looking`, look-closer pointer 3)**

In the looking chapter and in look-closer pointer 3, Andrée is introduced via the Degas comparison:

**Quoted passage (chapter `looking`):**
> "If you've met her before, it's as the seated woman with the absinthe glass in Degas's *L'Absinthe* (1875–76); here she is again, on the other side of the same circle of friends, in **much better company**, with the wine and not the absinthe."

And in look-closer pointer 3:
> "here she's on the other side of the same circle of friends, in much better company, with wine instead of absinthe."

The phrase "much better company" is a framing problem on two axes.

First: it accepts as premise the false Victorian reading of L'Absinthe that the same draft (in the absinthe-draft) correctly refuses — namely, that the Café de la Nouvelle-Athènes in the Degas picture was bad company, a derelict scene. The absinthe draft establishes that Andrée was posing, not drinking, and that the café was the Impressionists' own bar. "Much better company" retroactively re-attaches the moralistic degradation frame to the earlier picture even as that frame is being refused in the work it belongs to.

Second: it describes Renoir's balcony cast — which includes a "former cavalry officer" who is "sometimes described as ex-mayor of colonial Saigon" (Baron Barbier), an Italian journalist, and a civil servant — as inherently better company than the Degas picture's cast of Degas's own friends. This is social hierarchy being smuggled in as comic texture.

**Fairer framing:** Replace "in much better company" with something that describes the actual difference without the comparative moral hierarchy. The real distinction is mood, not worth of company:
> "Here she's on the same circle of friends, in a wholly different key — afternoon sun instead of gaslight, wine instead of absinthe, laughter instead of silence."

---

**M2 — Angèle Legault described only as "a model" with no further personhood (chapter `looking`)**

Every other identified sitter in the picture gets at least one descriptor of their profession, social role, or relationship to Renoir's circle — often two or three. Angèle Legault gets: "**Angèle Legault**, a model." Full stop. Her look-closer pointer does not exist (she is not one of the seven pointers). She is the only woman in the picture aside from Aline who gets no further characterization.

The fairness issue is structural: she is reduced to a functional label ("a model") in a way no male sitter is. Adrien Maggiolo gets "Italian journalist who wrote for the satirical paper *Le Triboulet*." Paul Lhote gets "a writer and journalist friend of Renoir's." Baron Barbier gets "a former cavalry officer." Legault gets a single noun.

**Quoted passage (chapter `looking`):**
> "Seated beside him in a black dress and dark hat is **Angèle Legault**, a model."

The fact pack has no further biographical material on Legault — the framing critic acknowledges this. But the fair answer to sparse documentation is not a single noun; it is to note the documentation gap without using the gap itself as a characterization. The gap matters because "a model" in 1880 Paris carried its own set of assumptions — and pinning someone to that label when every male colleague gets a fuller description compounds rather than neutralizes those assumptions.

**Fairer framing:**
> "Seated beside him in a black dress and dark hat is **Angèle Legault** — Renoir's model, who came to the Fournaise balcony as part of his regular circle. Her biography beyond these sittings hasn't been recovered; she appears in the picture as herself, as all the others do."

---

### SHOULD-FIX

**S1 — "Romantic version / real story" framing for the making chapter**

The draft correctly flags the "one glorious summer afternoon" version as romantic, and explains the assembled-across-months reality. But the structural move is: name the romantic version, call it "mostly" romantic, then pivot to "the real story is more interesting." The word "mostly" in "The romantic version of this picture...is, mostly, a romantic version" introduces unnecessary hedging. Is any part of the romantic version true? The prose doesn't clarify. If the answer is "not really" (he painted across multiple sessions, sitters posed individually), then say so directly.

**SHOULD-FIX:** Drop "mostly" and state plainly what is documented: "Renoir painted the picture across the summer and autumn of 1880 and into 1881, posing each sitter individually."

---

**S2 — Baron Barbier as "ex-mayor of colonial Saigon" — no flag**

The fact pack lists Baron Barbier as "sometimes described as ex-mayor of colonial Saigon." The draft reproduces this in the looking chapter: "a former cavalry officer, sometimes described in the older literature as an ex-mayor of colonial Saigon." The phrase "in the older literature" handles the uncertainty of the Saigon claim adequately, but there is a fairness framing gap here: the colonial-Saigon descriptor, if accurate, is more than a biographical footnote. Barbier's role in French colonial administration of Vietnam would make him a figure of imperial history, not just a "baron in a bowler hat at a balcony lunch." The draft does not flag this dimension at all — neither to explore it nor to note that the "colonial Saigon" identification is unconfirmed.

This is not a MUST-FIX: the identification is flagged as uncertain and the fact pack does not pursue it further. But the fairness critic notes: if the Saigon identification is ever confirmed, it changes the picture's politics, and the draft should at minimum acknowledge this is not a neutral biographical detail.

**SHOULD-FIX:** Add a clause: "If the colonial-Saigon identification holds, Barbier is the one figure in the picture whose presence carries the weight of French imperial history — though the identification in the literature is soft and the draft rightly treats it as uncertain."

## Work 4: Gare Saint-Lazare

**Overall grade: FAIR**

The three mandated refusals (Turner as first-painter-of-railways, Manet 1873 as precursor, Marquet anecdote as embellishment) are all handled correctly and prominently. The series-invention credit is properly qualified. No lone-genius myth; Caillebotte's role is given full weight. No Eurocentric overclaim.

### MUST-FIX

None.

### SHOULD-FIX

**S1 — Zola's *La Bête humaine* as seeded by the Gare Saint-Lazare canvases (chapter `show`)**

The draft states: "He had seen Monet's railway pictures. The seeds are in there." This is a reasonable literary-historical inference, but it is not in the fact pack's documented or LIKELY ledger — it is an author inference. The framing critic notes: the claim implies a direct causal influence (Monet → Zola's novel), which, stated as "The seeds are in there," reads as settled rather than plausible. The draft should flag it as the speculative connection it is.

**SHOULD-FIX:** "Whether Monet's railway pictures directly seeded *La Bête humaine* is a critic's inference rather than a documented connection — but the timing and Zola's documented presence at the 1877 show make it a tempting one."

---

**S2 — Workers framing in the small-figures pointer**

The look-closer pointer 5 describes the platform figures as "porters and passengers and railway crew" and notes that "a porter in a Paris terminus and a fisherman on the Seine, in his hand, are the same kind of small dark vertical mark, and that's the point." This is a correct formal observation. But the framing flattens the railway workers into an atmospheric element at exactly the point in the pointer where the chance exists to note what they represent: the vast industrial workforce behind the modern system Monet is celebrating. The "same kind of mark" observation ends the pointer on a formalist note (both are marks) rather than the social one (both are workers dissolved into atmosphere, which is itself a view of labour). This is an advisory, not a MUST-FIX, given that the broader `station` chapter makes the modernity-of-subject argument well.

## Top 5 findings across the batch

1. **M-DC-1 (MUST-FIX, Dance Class)** — The abonnés economy is introduced correctly but immediately sorted into a false hierarchy (liaison < marriage < exploitation) that reintroduces the softening it was supposed to cut. The structure of exploitation was structural, not gradient.

2. **M-BP-1 (MUST-FIX, Boating Party)** — "In much better company" smuggles the Victorian moral hierarchy back into the Andrée comparison, contradicting what the *L'Absinthe* draft correctly establishes about the Nouvelle-Athènes as the Impressionists' own circle.

3. **M-BP-2 (MUST-FIX, Boating Party)** — Angèle Legault receives a single-noun characterization ("a model") while every male sitter in the same paragraph gets a profession, role, and social relationship. The disparity is structural and repeats across the main prose and the fact ledger.

4. **M-DC-2 (MUST-FIX, Dance Class)** — The look-closer pointer 6 (stage mothers) uses "in theory guarding" without explaining what "in theory" means for a standalone reader. A pointer that omits the mechanism of powerlessness misrepresents what the chaperone's presence in the painting actually documents.

5. **S-AB-1 (SHOULD-FIX, L'Absinthe)** — "He was the one in real life who actually drank" presents as documented fact what the fact pack tags as LIKELY (Andrée's own account, compressed by popular histories). The Fénéon interview should be kept as the source with appropriate weight: what Andrée said, not what has been proved independently.
