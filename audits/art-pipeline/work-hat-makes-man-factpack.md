**The handwritten caption on the work — verbatim German** (from German Wikipedia, transcribing the inscription):

> bedecktsamiger stapel- / mensch nacktsamiger wasserformer / („edelformer") kleidsame nervatur / auch / !umpressnerven! / (c'est le chapeau qui fait l'homme) / (le style c'est le tailleur)

**English rendering** (the widely reproduced translation, e.g. English Wikipedia):

> "seed-covered stacked-up man seedless waterformer ('edelformer') well-fitting nervous system also tightly-fitted nerves! (the hat makes the man) (style is the tailor)"

- ⚠ **Translation caveat / trap.** The German is deliberate pseudo-botanical nonsense. *bedecktsamig* = "angiosperm / covered-seed (plant)"; *nacktsamig* = "gymnosperm / naked-seed." The popular English line renders these as "seed-covered" and "seedless," which loses the botany pun (covered-seed vs. naked-seed plants) but is the translation in common circulation. Treat the German block as authoritative; present the English as "a translation," not "the title." The bracketed French at the bottom — *c'est le chapeau qui fait l'homme* / *le style c'est le tailleur* ("the hat makes the man" / "the style is the tailor") — is part of the inscription and is the source of the work's title.

## 2. THE STORY

Ernst made this in **Cologne in 1920**, the heart of **Cologne Dada** — the local Dada cell he had founded in 1919/1920 with Johannes Theodor Baargeld and (in dialogue with) Jean (Hans) Arp. Cologne Dada shared Dada's general project — mocking bourgeois taste, the war, and "modern man as a conformist automaton" (Wikipedia) — but Ernst's branch was already tilting toward dream-logic, which is why he is later counted a **founder of Surrealism** (the first visual artist to join the movement; LibreTexts/Smarthistory: his early Dada collages were "an important catalyst for the development of Surrealism").

**How it was built.** Ernst worked over a **printed sales-catalogue page of men's hats**. German Wikipedia identifies the catalogue as coming from his father-in-law **Jacob Straus, a Cologne hat manufacturer** — the page showed rows of orderable hat forms. (Note: several English sources loosely call it a "women's hat" catalogue or say he was "minding his father-in-law's hat factory"; the German account — father-in-law's *men's*-hat sample catalogue — is the more specific and is what the inscription's "the hat makes the man" supports. ⚠ flag this minor variance.) Ernst cut and stacked the catalogue hats into **wobbly tower / column "figures,"** then drew and painted **colored tubes, cylinders and cubes (blue, red, green, yellow)** into the gaps so the stacked hats read as standing bodies linked at the joints.

**The joke.** Stack up the commodity — the hat, the off-the-peg uniform of the bourgeois man — and you have "made the man." Identity is just the merchandise piled into a body-shape. The German *c'est le chapeau qui fait l'homme / le style c'est le tailleur* turns the cliché ("clothes make the man") into a pun: the man IS the hat; the "style" is literally the tailor, not the person. The towers also read as phallic, tying the consumer joke to a **Freudian** one: the title is often linked to Freud's *Jokes and Their Relation to the Unconscious* (1905), where the hat is a symbol of (repressed) bourgeois masculine desire. ⚠ The Freud link is interpretive (MoMA Learning / Wikipedia present it as "likely inspired by," not documented by Ernst) — frame as reading, not fact.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (before → after)

**Before:** A picture was something an artist *made* — drew or painted from observation or imagination, with the hand as origin. Collage existed (Cubist *papiers collés*, c. 1912) but mostly as texture/fragment inside a painting.

**After (what this work does that's new):**
- The image is built from **ready-made commercial print** — illustrations lifted whole from a mail-order **catalogue** — recombined, not drawn. The "subject" is mass-produced advertising matter.
- The recombination is **absurd and figure-making**: stacked merchandise becomes a person, glued together with painted "anatomy" and captioned in deadpan pseudo-scientific German nonsense.
- It carries the **readymade logic into the picture plane** and pushes it toward **dream-figures** — chance juxtaposition producing an uncanny body. That is the hinge from **Dada's anti-art collage to Surrealism's collage-as-dream**: the same cut-and-paste, but now read as a window onto the unconscious rather than only as iconoclasm (Smarthistory: for the Surrealists the dislocations of collage were "direct manifestations of unconscious thinking").

Ernst's own statement of the break (overpaintings of 1919–21): he sought "to transform what previously had been merely banal pages of advertising into dramas that contained my most secret desires" (Ernst, quoted in Artforum / Samantha Kavky scholarship — see §4).

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (gated surface — verbatim, with source)

**A. The work's own inscribed caption** (the manifesto-on-the-work). German verbatim + English as in §1; source: German Wikipedia transcription (German) and English Wikipedia (the circulating English translation). Use the German as authoritative.

**B. Ernst defining collage** (use ONE, attributed; flag as a variant set):

> "Collage is … the coupling of two realities, irreconcilable in appearance, upon a plane which apparently does not suit them."
> — Max Ernst. *Source:* the formulation is standard in the Ernst literature and quoted by Smarthistory/LibreTexts ("Ernst described the mechanism of collage … as 'the coupling of two realities, irreconcilable in appearance, upon a plane that apparently does not suit them.'"). It descends from Ernst's writings on collage (collected in *Beyond Painting*, 1948).

⚠ **Variant flag.** This idea circulates in several phrasings and is frequently fused with a paraphrase of Lautréamont's "chance meeting of a sewing machine and an umbrella on a dissecting table" (which Ernst invoked). Common variants: "the systematic exploitation of the … encounter of two or more unrelated realities on an apparently inappropriate plane …" and "He who says collage says the irrational." **Do not present the Lautréamont line as Ernst's own words.** Pick the Smarthistory-quoted form above; cite to *Beyond Painting* if a hard source is required, and verify the exact wording against that book before printing as a direct quote.

**C. Ernst on overpainting catalogue pages** (best for THIS work):

> "[to] transform what previously had been merely banal pages of advertising into dramas that contained my most secret desires."
> — Max Ernst, on the 1919–21 overpaintings. *Source:* quoted in Artforum and in Samantha Kavky, "Max Ernst's Post-World War I Studies in Hysteria" (PDF, Monmouth Univ.). ⚠ Wording varies by translation ("…become a drama revealing our most secret desires"); confirm exact English before quoting verbatim.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **The stacked hats as columns.** The vertical "figures" are nothing but men's hats — bowlers, toppers, soft felts — cut from a catalogue and piled one on another into teetering towers. Read top to bottom and you see merchandise, not anatomy. (German Wikipedia; MoMA Learning.)
2. **The painted connecting tubes / limbs.** Between and around the printed hats, Ernst has hand-drawn and painted smooth cylinders and tubes that act as necks, spines and legs, fusing the cut hats into single standing bodies. (German Wikipedia: colored tubes/cubes fill the spaces between the printed hats.)
3. **The colored vertical "bodies."** The connective passages are washed in clear blue, red, green and yellow gouache, so the grey catalogue hats appear to be wearing — or built from — bright cylindrical torsos. (German Wikipedia.)
4. **The catalogue-cut quality.** Look at the hats themselves: flat, evenly lit, repetitive, with the giveaway uniform rendering of commercial product illustration. The "found" mechanical look is the point — these are advertising images, not drawings. (Smarthistory; Ernst on overpainting, §4C.)
5. **The handwritten caption.** Along the lower edge, in Ernst's hand, the pseudo-botanical German line and the bracketed French proverb — deadpan fake-scientific labeling that turns the figures into specimens and delivers the "hat makes the man" pun. (German + English, §1.)
6. **The phallic / Freudian read.** The stacked, swelling towers are unmistakably bodily and erect — the consumer joke (you are what you buy) doubled as a sexual one (the hat as Freud's symbol of bourgeois male desire). Present as interpretation. (MoMA Learning; Wikipedia.)

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct, with sources)

- **Medium.** Use MoMA's exact line: *"Gouache, pencil, oil, and ink on cut-and-pasted printed paper on paper."* (MoMA.) Common loose versions drop the "oil" or say "on board/cardboard" — German Wikipedia says cardboard ("auf Karton"); **MoMA says "on paper."** Prefer MoMA. ⚠ Minor support discrepancy (paper vs. board) — flag, default to MoMA.
- **Dimensions.** Two figures circulate: **35.2 × 45.1 cm (13⅞ × 17¾ in)** [MoMA-derived + German Wikipedia] vs. **35.6 × 45.7 cm (14 × 18 in)** [English Wikipedia, rounded]. Use **35.2 × 45.1 cm = 13⅞ × 17¾ in** as the authoritative MoMA value; the 35.6 × 45.7 figure is a rounding. ⚠ Confirm against the live MoMA record.
- **Date.** **1920** — consistent across all sources. Not 1919 (Cologne Dada's founding year) and not later.
- **Caption translation.** See §1 — the German is angiosperm/gymnosperm botany nonsense; the circulating English "seed-covered … seedless" is a translation, not the title and not literal. Don't assert the English as inscribed text.
- **The hats: men's vs. women's; factory vs. catalogue.** Authoritative account (German Wikipedia): a **men's-hat sample catalogue** from his father-in-law Jacob Straus, a Cologne hatter — consistent with "the hat makes the *man*." Some English sources say "women's hat catalogue" or "minding the hat factory"; treat those as looser. ⚠ Flag.
- **Freud.** "Likely inspired by" *Jokes and Their Relation to the Unconscious* — interpretive, not documented by Ernst. Don't state as fact.
- **Collage quote vs. Lautréamont.** Don't merge Ernst's collage definition with Lautréamont's umbrella/sewing-machine line (§4 variant flag).
