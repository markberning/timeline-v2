# Gate 2 — STORYTELLING + LOOKING — Post-Impressionism movement draft

**Auditor:** Gate 2 storytelling/looking critic.
**Draft:** `audits/art-pipeline/postimp-draft.md` (8 chapters, ~14,800 words excluding hooks).
**Reference standard:** Cubism, Realism, Impressionism movement chapters in
`src/app/art/[eraId]/[movementId]/s/[sectionId]/movement-narratives.tsx`.

---

## TL;DR — the headline finding

**The draft is 2–3× too long, end to end, against the in-house pacing standard.**
Per-chapter word counts (excluding hooks) measured from the draft:

| Ch | Title | Draft words | In-house band (Cubism reference) |
|---:|---|---:|---|
| 1 | After the moment, the structure | **1,399** | 400–770 |
| 2 | The five who weren't a group | **1,471** | 400–770 |
| 3 | Cézanne | **1,717** | 400–770 |
| 4 | Van Gogh | **2,227** | 400–770 |
| 5 | Gauguin | **1,991** | 400–770 |
| 6 | Seurat + Lautrec | **2,108** | 400–770 |
| 7 | The Nabis and the wider field | **2,190** | 400–770 |
| 8 | Roger Fry / 1910 | **1,684** | 400–770 |
| | **Total** | **14,787** | ~3,200–6,200 |

Cubism's six chapters total roughly 3,100 words. Post-Impressionism is asking
the phone reader to do roughly **the whole Cubism page worth of reading per
chapter**, eight times in a row, on a Modern-era page they have a peer for two
clicks away. That is not "length serving the story." That is six paragraphs
where three would land harder.

**The integrator's flag is right.** The brief said 600–1100. The fact that
Cubism also overran (your note) is true but misleading as cover: the Cubism
overruns sit at 700–850, not 1,500–2,200. This draft is in a different weight
class.

The good news: this is a strong draft of the *facts*, the *voice*, and the
*scene-setting instincts*. The author knows how to open a chapter, how to land
a closing punch, and how to weave biography into stakes. What is missing is the
discipline of *cutting*. Most chapters have one whole section that could be a
single paragraph, one paragraph that could be a single sentence, and one
parenthetical reference-desk aside that could just be deleted.

The grades below assume **every chapter gets a hard compression pass to ~700–900
words** before integration. With that pass, this is a strong page. Without it,
the phone reader bounces around Chapter 4 and never reaches Fry.

No chapter is REWRITE. Two chapters are NEEDS WORK on length alone. Everything
else is FAIR.

## Chapter 1 — "After the moment, the structure" — **FAIR** (length: trim)

**1,399 words across 3 sections.** Target ~700–800.

**What is working.** The opening is the best in the draft — *Picture a long,
hot, awkward upstairs room in Paris* puts the reader on rue Laffitte in one
sentence and earns the second one. The pivot from "the room" to "the picture
stopping every visitor" to "Seurat, age 26" is textbook in-house pacing. The
closing — *In Paris a 26-year-old has finished a vast pointillist canvas… In
Provence a 47-year-old is staring at a mountain* — is a real closing punch, two
poles of the chapter rhymed and resolved. This is how the chapter should feel
throughout.

**Where the air gets in.** The middle section ("The trick the eye couldn't
catch") does work the opening already implies. Once the reader has seen Seurat
quietly bury Impressionism with dots, they already know "the instant is
weightless." Telling them again in a paragraph that begins *But the instant is,
by definition, weightless. It doesn't stay. It doesn't mean anything beyond
itself* is the chapter explaining itself a second time.

The **four-bullet "objection had four parts"** list is the single biggest
pacing problem of the chapter. It is structurally clean but emotionally flat —
a parade of four anchor lines (*Cézanne in Aix / Van Gogh in Arles / Gauguin in
Pont-Aven / Seurat in Paris*) right before Chapter 2 does exactly the same job
at full length. The reader is being briefed on the briefing. Pick **two** of
the four objections and put them in prose; let Chapter 2 do the other two.

### MUST-FIX
- **Compression target: ~700–800 words.** Cut roughly 600 words. The whole
  middle section ("The trick the eye couldn't catch") should shrink to two
  paragraphs, not five.
- The **four-objection bullet list** previews Chapter 2 instead of doing its
  own work. Kill the list. Keep the two strongest as embedded sentences — the
  Cézanne *weight* line and the Van Gogh *wheatfield/feeling* line carry both
  ideas in prose.

### SHOULD-FIX
- The mid-section opens with a meta-tag: *(The previous level of this read
  tells that story in full.)* — that's an admin note, not narrative. Cut.
- The Pissarro/Monet "walked out muttering / refused to show alongside it /
  called a deserter" cluster repeats the same beat three different ways. One
  beat will do — Pissarro "stood up for Seurat and Signac and got called a
  deserter by his own friends" is the one to keep.
- The fifth-painter aside on Toulouse-Lautrec in the bullet section is a
  throat-clear before Chapter 6 — drop it here, it lives in Ch 2 and Ch 6.

### NICE-TO-HAVE
- The Aix section's *"He paints it once. He paints it twice. He paints it, by
  the time he is done in 1906, about thirty times"* is a rhythm worth
  protecting in compression — keep it.

## Chapter 2 — "The five who weren't a group" — **NEEDS WORK** (structurally redundant)

**1,471 words across 7 sections.** Target ~700–900.

**The real problem.** This chapter has a structural twin three sections away.
Chapters 3, 4, 5, and 6 are each a deep dive on one of the five anchors.
Chapter 2 is "here is one paragraph per anchor" — and those paragraphs are
*long*. Cézanne gets 180 words here; he gets 1,700 in Chapter 3. The Chapter 2
paragraph then ends *Chapter 3 is Cézanne's.* The Chapter 4 paragraph ends
*Chapter 4 is Van Gogh's.* And so on for all five.

A phone reader who has just finished Chapter 1 and walks into Chapter 2 reads
roughly six paragraphs of "and tomorrow we will tell you more about him." It is
a table of contents written in good prose. **The reference movement pages do
not have this chapter.** Cubism does not have a "here are the players, more
later" chapter — it walks straight from Demoiselles into Analytic Cubism.

You have a choice: kill Chapter 2 entirely, or rebuild it as a chapter that
does *its own* work — the *room they end up in* idea, the *retrospective label*
idea — without re-stating biographies the next four chapters will deliver in
depth.

**What is working.** The opening — *If you had walked into an art-historical
lecture in Paris in 1905 and asked, "who are the Post-Impressionists?", you
would have got a blank look* — is exactly the angle. The list of more precise
French categories (Néo-Impressionnistes, Synthétistes, Nabis, Symbolistes) is
useful, because it tells the reader something Chapter 1 didn't and Chapters 3–6
won't. The closing pivot to Roger Fry as the man who will hang them in one room
is the right hand-off.

### MUST-FIX
- **Choose: kill or rebuild.** The "one paragraph per anchor" body is a list
  with no scene. Either delete the five anchor paragraphs and turn this chapter
  into a ~500-word "no movement, one label" piece that does *only* the work
  Chapters 3–6 can't do, or scrap the chapter and roll its two genuinely
  unique paragraphs (the French categories, the Cézanne/Gauguin/Van Gogh
  feuds) into Chapter 1's closing or Chapter 3's opening. My recommendation:
  **rebuild, don't delete** — the "five answers, no creed" framing is real
  scaffolding for the reader.
- If you keep it: target **~600 words max**, and each of the five anchor
  mini-paragraphs needs to lose its closing *Chapter N is X's* signpost.

### SHOULD-FIX
- **List-pacing alert.** Seven section headers in 1,471 words = a header
  every 210 words. That is twice the in-house density. Six of those seven
  headers are *each painter, then a final*. Collapse the five painter sections
  into one section with five short anchor paragraphs (or three sentences each)
  under one header, e.g. "Five answers, one room."
- *Cézanne thought Gauguin was "a Chinese painter" and a thief of his ideas* —
  this is a great scrap; surface it earlier (the opening section), not in the
  closing.

### NICE-TO-HAVE
- *plus Van Gogh, who was Dutch and dead, plus Toulouse-Lautrec, who painted
  brothels* is the chapter's best sentence. Preserve.

## Chapter 3 — "Cézanne — the cylinder, the sphere, the cone" — **FAIR** (length: trim, especially section 2)

**1,717 words across 4 sections.** Target ~800–950 (this one is allowed a
little more — it's the foundation chapter for Cubism's existence, and that
costs paragraphs).

**What is working.** The opening on Louis-Auguste the banker is *exactly* the
in-house move — start with the father, because the father is what makes the
painter possible. That is a real scene-anchored opening, not a throat-clear.
The 15 April 1904 letter section is well-paced: it sets up the letter, quotes
it, *and then explicitly hedges what the letter is and what Picasso/Braque
made of it later*. That hedging-in-prose is house voice, done right.

**Where the air gets in.** The second section ("The two great series") is a
**list dressed as prose**. *The Card Players is one. … The Bathers is the
other. And then there is Mont Sainte-Victoire.* Three works, three blocks, each
with dimensions, museum, and a price. The Card Players block ends on the
auction price — a real punch — but the next block (*The Bathers is the
other…*) starts from cold again. Six paragraphs of museum-label data in a row,
without a single moment in front of a single canvas. This is exactly the
pacing failure Cubism's *Analytic* chapter dodges by **picking one Picasso
portrait and one Braque still life** and pinning the whole method to them.

**Closing punch.** The *Picasso, Braque, Matisse, Derain, Vlaminck — they all
walked through that show. They all came out of it changed* triplet is a real
ending and it lands. Keep it.

### MUST-FIX
- **The "two great series" section is the cut.** Pick **Mont Sainte-Victoire
  + one other** (probably the Bathers, because of the Demoiselles handoff to
  Cubism). Lose the full Card Players paragraph or shrink it to two sentences
  (the silence; the auction price). The dimensions-and-museums catalogue
  block — *5 ft 11 in × 4 ft 5 in, five figures, three players plus two
  onlookers* etc. — is reference-desk data, not story. Move dimensions to the
  PaintingFigure credit lines at integration.
- The closing has a hedge paragraph (*It is hard to say one show alone caused
  all of that — the memorial rooms of 1904–06 had been priming the ground for
  three years…*). The hedge is fair but it deflates the punch the paragraph
  before just delivered. Move the hedge **before** the *they all came out
  changed* line so the chapter still closes on the punch, not the asterisk.

### SHOULD-FIX
- *He paints it once. He paints it twice. He paints it, by the time he is
  done in 1906, about thirty times* — this rhythm appears in **Chapter 1** in
  almost identical form. Keep it in Chapter 3 (it's the foundation chapter for
  the mountain); compress in Chapter 1.
- *Cézanne would say it, much later, in a famous letter we'll get to in
  Chapter 3* in Chapter 1, plus *Cézanne accidentally gives modernism its
  catechism* in Chapter 3 — both are signposting the same letter. Cut Ch1's
  forward-reference; Chapter 3 sets it up cold on its own.
- Manuscript-number aside *(the manuscript number is ms-1932-SC-1-1, acquired
  with the Samuel Courtauld bequest of 1932)* is reference-desk filler. Cut.

### NICE-TO-HAVE
- *the patience of a man laying a stone wall* is the chapter's best sentence.
  Move it earlier — currently it's buried in paragraph three.
- *the white of the canvas left showing through in places, the way the
  unstuccoed brick shows through a Roman ruin* — keep, it's a real looking
  moment.

## Chapter 4 — "Van Gogh — the yellow house, the wheat, the gun" — **NEEDS WORK** (length: this is the longest chapter and it shows)

**2,227 words across 5 sections.** Target ~900–1,100 (allowed a little more
because Van Gogh is the marquee biography of the page, but **this is double
that budget**).

**What is working.** The opening *"The hardest fact about Van Gogh, the one
the romance always blurs, is how late he started"* is a beautiful angle and
the right opening — it tells the reader the chapter knows the myth and is
going to undercut it. The ear-cutting paragraph handles the *standard
correction* (lower lobe, not whole ear) cleanly without lecturing. The
Naifeh-Smith suicide-hypothesis hedge is exactly the right tone and length —
it tells the reader the alternative exists and tells them why the chapter is
sticking with the consensus. That is a model in-house hedge.

**Where the chapter sags.** The biographical sweep is overlong, and Section 2
("Paris, then Arles") is the worst offender. The two-year color crash course
in Paris is currently 280 words; it could be 60. Pissarro, Lautrec, Japanese
prints, *200 canvases in those Paris months* — all true, none of them are
moments. The reader is waiting for Arles and the chapter keeps detouring.

The **Theo subplot is sprawling**. Theo is in three different paragraphs in
three different sections: the late-start opening, the Auvers-summer section,
and the closing summary about Jo. Each Theo entry is good but the three of
them overlap. Consolidate: Theo gets one short paragraph in Section 1 (he's
the brother who makes the painter possible), and then one beat at the end
(he dies six months later, Jo builds the legacy). Three Theo paragraphs is
two too many.

The **closing section ("Sales in life — One painting, with a footnote")**
is the chapter's worst pacing decision. The chapter has just landed Van Gogh
in the ground at 37 with the *la tristesse durera toujours* line — a *real*
closing punch. Then it opens a new section to handicap the *one painting in
his lifetime* anecdote and ends on a different note (*He painted around 900
canvases. He sold one for money he could spend. He died face-down in a
field.*). The second ending is good but the first ending was already perfect.
Pick one. My vote: the **last-words ending is the chapter's real ending**;
fold the Red Vineyard / Anna Boch fact into Section 2 as a two-sentence
parenthetical, kill the trailing section.

### MUST-FIX
- **Compression target: ~1,000 words.** Cut roughly half. The closing
  "Sales in life" section in particular: kill it as a section, fold the
  fact into earlier prose. The chapter's real closing is Theo's death and
  Jo's life work — and that beat is currently *inside* the Auvers section
  rather than ending the chapter.
- **Section 2 ("Paris, then Arles") loses half its length.** Pissarro,
  Toulouse-Lautrec, Japanese prints, 200 canvases — pick the one that
  matters most (the color education) and lose the rest. *"He met Pissarro,
  who became a generous teacher. He met Toulouse-Lautrec, who took to him.
  He encountered Japanese woodblock prints in volume."* Three list items
  in a row, none of them a moment.
- **The Yellow House description goes long.** The bedroom dimensions, the
  sunflowers' decorative purpose for Gauguin's room — fold these into one
  paragraph, not three.

### SHOULD-FIX
- The Goupil / Borinage / lay-preacher / "wreck" paragraph in Section 1 is
  three lives in 90 words. It's fine, but it ends *He was, by the standards
  of his bourgeois Dutch family, a wreck.* — that is the punch; everything
  before it can compress to one sentence.
- *"Vincent picks up a brush around 1881"* opens a new paragraph after
  Theo — but Theo's paragraph already had a tense-shift hedge (*(Hold this,
  because the brothers die six months apart.)*). The hedge is good; keep it,
  but move it to the closing where it pays off.
- **Word-repetition flag:** "absinthe" appears in Ch 4 (Gauguin/Van Gogh
  drank absinthe) and Ch 6 (Lautrec drank absinthe). One full parenthetical
  gloss is enough — second use can be plain.

### NICE-TO-HAVE
- *"the most reproduced single picture in Western art after the Mona Lisa,
  and Van Gogh painted it locked up"* — preserve at all costs, it's the
  chapter's best sentence on a canvas.

## Chapter 5 — "Gauguin — Pont-Aven, Tahiti, the colonial question" — **FAIR** (length: trim, but structurally sound)

**1,991 words across 5 sections.** Target ~900–1,000.

**What is working.** This is the most morally serious chapter in the draft
and it earns its length better than Chapter 4 does. The *"There is no
charitable way to tell this part of the story"* paragraph in Section 1 is
the right tone — direct, undeflected, and short. The *"The pictures matter.
The biography is harder."* blurb is one of the best in the draft. The "Part
of the story that doesn't sand down" section is exactly what the framing
gate would want it to be: the adolescent-girls section sits inside the
chapter, not at the end as an asterisk; it is named, it is not laundered,
and the prose ends *"the pictures are the pictures, and they will not be
undone; but a serious account of Gauguin cannot pretend the biography is
just colorful."* That is house voice on a hard subject and it works.

**Where the air gets in.** *Vision after the Sermon* gets 240 words — a real
looking-passage, which is the right call (this is the chapter's looking
anchor). But it is sandwiched between a 200-word setup (Brittany pension
rates, Bernard age 19, Cazalis, cloisonné, the whole vocabulary section) and a
70-word follow-up (Gauguin tries to give it to the parish church). The setup
should be 100 words, not 200. Skip cloisonné as a paragraph noun and let
"like stained glass or Japanese prints" carry it.

The **Arles section is duplicative** of Chapter 4 by design and the chapter
even *says* so (*This is Chapter 4's territory from the other side*). That's
honest, but currently 220 words to do "see Chapter 4." Cut it to 80. The new
information here is: Gauguin took Arles as confirmation that his future was
not in France. That is one paragraph.

The **closing** is strong (*"His pictures and effects were auctioned cheap on
Tahiti the following autumn. Within fifteen years they would be unaffordable
in any market on earth."*) — preserve.

### MUST-FIX
- **Compression target: ~1,000 words.** Cut the Arles section to one
  paragraph (it's Chapter 4's territory; this chapter says so explicitly).
- The Tahiti-as-colony paragraph (Section 4) does its work well but says it
  twice (*Tahiti had been a French protectorate since 1842* / *the real
  Tahiti was a French outpost where French colonial police came round when he
  made trouble*). One beat, not both.

### SHOULD-FIX
- Section 4 opens with *Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We
  Going?* dimensions and BMFA inscription detail. Move dimensions to the
  PaintingFigure credit at integration; keep "long horizontal mural, birth
  on the right, old woman on the left, read it right to left" — that's the
  looking sentence, that's what to keep.
- *His maternal grandmother was Flora Tristan, the socialist writer and
  women's-rights activist (one of the more interesting ancestors any French
  painter ever had)* — the parenthetical aside is funny, but it pulls
  attention from the Lima childhood point. Save it for a margin or drop it.

### NICE-TO-HAVE
- *with the casual ruthlessness that would mark the rest of his life* —
  preserve.
- *He spent the summer of 1888 lobbying — through Theo, who was now
  Gauguin's dealer too — for Gauguin to come down to Arles* — small fact
  worth surfacing into Chapter 4 too (Theo is the connective tissue across
  the two chapters and he's underused as such).

## Chapter 6 — "Seurat's dots, Lautrec's posters" — **NEEDS WORK** (length + structural: this is two chapters welded together)

**2,108 words across 3 sections.** Target ~900–1,000 — but the deeper
problem is the welding.

**The real problem.** This chapter is structurally **two short biographical
sketches joined by a closing paragraph that admits they don't belong
together** (*"Seurat and Lautrec are linked here not because they were
friends (they were barely acquainted) but because they died young, in Paris,
having between them shown two opposite ways out of Impressionism…"*). That
explicit *they were barely acquainted* line is the chapter telling on its own
welding job. The reference movement pages do not do this — they pick a single
through-line per chapter.

A chapter can absolutely hold two painters, but it needs **one center of
gravity** the reader can hold onto. As written, the Seurat half (1,150
words) and the Lautrec half (800 words) each have their own opening, their
own arc, and their own ending; the joining paragraph is admin.

**What is working.** The Seurat half's opening — *Open at the Société des
Artistes Indépendants. In 1884…* — is a clean scene opener and the *they
hung his rejected picture instead. The 24-year-old was Georges Seurat* punch
at the end of Section 1 is one of the best mid-chapter pivots in the draft.

The Lautrec opening (*"The other half of this chapter is the painter who,
by temperament, was Seurat's exact opposite"*) is **the throat-clear**.
That sentence is a structural admission rather than a scene. Lautrec
deserves an opening on a Montmartre night street, not "now the opposite
guy." Compare the Cubism *Analytic* chapter's opening on Picasso and Braque
roped together as mountaineers — that's *what* a paired-painters chapter
opening should feel like: a single image holding both painters at once. This
chapter needs that image.

The Seurat-method section is on the **edge of list-pacing failure**.
Chevreul's 1839 book — Charles Blanc's 1867 *Grammaire* — Rood's 1879
*Modern Chromatics* — these are three citations in one paragraph, each with
date and city. The reader doesn't need three. Pick **Chevreul** (the dyer at
the Gobelins discovering complementary contrast is the *story* here) and let
him stand for all three.

The Seurat painting block is **a worse list than Cézanne's two-series block**:
*Bathers at Asnières — La Grande Jatte — Les Poseuses — Parade de cirque —
The Circus*. Five canvases, each as a bullet, with dimensions and museums.
The phone reader doesn't need a catalogue raisonné. **Pick two:** *Bathers at
Asnières* (the rejected canvas that opened the chapter — closes a loop) and
*La Grande Jatte* (already introduced in Chapter 1; this chapter is where the
*dot* gets explained). *Les Poseuses* and *Parade* and *The Circus* are
reference-desk; the Circus's "unfinished at his death" is the only one with
narrative weight and can be a single sentence in the death paragraph.

The Lautrec half's three-medium structure (*paintings, posters, lithographic
prints*) is also list-shaped. Pick the **poster** (the La Goulue poster is
the chapter's big claim — "the modern poster industry descends from this
single sheet") and the **brothel pictures** (the moral seriousness of *In the
Salon at the Rue des Moulins* is the chapter's tonal counterweight to the
Moulin Rouge glitter). Lose *At the Moulin Rouge* as a separate block, or
fold its May-Milton-face-sliced-off detail into one sentence — that detail
*is* good, just don't give it 180 words.

### MUST-FIX
- **Compression target: ~1,000 words total** across both painters.
- **Throat-clear opening on Lautrec.** *"The other half of this chapter is
  the painter who, by temperament, was Seurat's exact opposite"* must go.
  Open Lautrec on a Montmartre scene — the lurid green gaslight on the
  dancer's face, or his short body climbing a Montmartre stair on his stick.
  Earn the contrast in image, not in label.
- **The Seurat painting block is a five-canvas list.** Cut to two anchor
  canvases (*Bathers at Asnières* + *La Grande Jatte*) with *The Circus*
  surviving only as "his last picture, unfinished at his death" in the
  death paragraph.
- **The Lautrec three-medium block is a list.** Cut to **two** anchors (the
  La Goulue poster + the *Rue des Moulins* brothel waiting room).

### SHOULD-FIX
- Lose the *chromo-luminarisme (a name that did not stick)* aside — it
  doesn't earn its paragraph; the reader gets Néo-Impressionnisme and
  Pointillism cleanly without it.
- *His father was a property speculator and an oddity — he lived
  semi-separately from the rest of the family in a house in the Paris
  suburbs, and visited his wife once a week for dinner.* Funny, but it does
  not pay back. The chapter is already long; drop the eccentric father.
- The Lautrec aristocratic-genealogy paragraph (*Counts of Toulouse,
  Albigensian Crusade, first cousins, Tapié de Celeyran*) is a paragraph
  doing one job — "consanguinity caused his bones." Compress to two
  sentences: *His parents were first cousins. The legs broke at 13 and 14
  and stopped growing.*
- **Word-repetition flag inside paragraphs.** Lautrec section: *"He drank
  himself to death. Heavily. Absinthe mostly, the bitter green wormwood
  liquor that the late-19th-century Paris bohemian drank by the liter."*
  The wormwood gloss already ran in Chapter 4; here it can just be
  *"absinthe."*

### NICE-TO-HAVE
- The *Pointillism / Divisionism / chromo-luminarisme* trio of names is
  exactly the kind of vocabulary-bookkeeping that the reference pages
  resolve in a single in-house gloss — pick one term to do the chapter's
  work and parenthetical-define the other.

## Chapter 7 — "The Nabis and the wider field" — **NEEDS WORK** (length + the entire second half is a parade)

**2,190 words across 4 sections.** Target ~900–1,100.

**The real problem.** Section 4 ("The other tributaries — Signac, Redon,
Rousseau, Cassatt") is a **textbook list-pacing failure**. Four painters,
four 200-word blocks, each opening with the painter's name in bold and
dates in parens. No scene anchors the parade. The chapter even admits this
implicitly — *"The wider cast goes on — Vallotton, Maillol, Maurice
Marinot, the lesser Pont-Aven painters, the late Pissarro who experimented
briefly with pointillism before going back to his own way — but the shape of
the room is now clear."* That sentence is the chapter signaling that it's
out of room. The honest move is: it's out of room **150 words sooner**.

The Sérusier opening (Bois d'Amour, the cigar-box panel, Gauguin's *use the
most beautiful green on your palette*) is the chapter's best moment and it
should carry more of the chapter's weight. The *Le Talisman / the Nabis name
themselves* paragraph is also strong — the Hebrew-for-prophets self-mocking,
the studio called "the Temple," Bonnard as *le nabi très japonard*. This is
the kind of small-detail scene-setting the chapter needs more of.

The Maurice Denis paragraph is **almost a great chapter on its own** — the
*flat surface covered with colors assembled in a certain order* quote +
*"He was 19 years old"* + *"no single sentence pushed European painting
further toward abstraction than that one. By the time Kandinsky paints the
first wholly abstract watercolor in Munich in 1910, Denis's 1890 line has
had twenty years to soak in."* That's a complete arc in 200 words and it
lands.

**What's broken.** After that, the chapter tries to do **two more arcs**
(Bonnard+Vuillard, then four "tributaries") in 1,100 words. Each arc gets a
quarter of the room it needs and the reader leaves Chapter 7 with no
single image to hold onto.

**Closing punch.** *"All they need is somebody to walk into the room and put
a name on the door."* — keep, it's the right hand-off to Chapter 8. But
right now it's preceded by 600 words of *fourth* and *fifth* and *sixth*
painter bios that the reader has stopped tracking.

### MUST-FIX
- **Compression target: ~1,000 words.** This is the biggest cut on the page.
- **Kill the "other tributaries" parade as a list.** Pick **two** of the
  four (Signac + Rousseau, my call — Signac because he's the propagandist
  who codifies the technique, Rousseau because the Banquet Rousseau is the
  best anecdote in this part of the draft) and write them as **scene
  paragraphs**, not as encyclopedia entries with bold-name + dates +
  museum. Redon and Cassatt either survive as one sentence each ("the
  Symbolist Redon, the Japanese-print-debt Cassatt") or move to the
  Movement-level Comprehensiveness pass to be folded into Chapter 2 or
  Chapter 8's wider-cast paragraph.
- **Bonnard + Vuillard collapse into one paragraph each, not two each.**
  The Marthe-de-Méligny biographical paragraph (*she changed her name,
  claiming a noble background she did not have; they would only finally
  marry in 1925*) is fun and not relevant. Cut.

### SHOULD-FIX

## Chapter 8 — "Roger Fry hangs a show" — **STRONG** (length: still trim, but this is the best-paced chapter in the draft)

**1,684 words across 5 sections.** Target ~900–1,100.

**What is working — and it's a lot.** This chapter is the closest the draft
gets to in-house pacing. The opening — *Fry, 44 years old, recently fired
from the Met in a row with J. Pierpont Morgan, looking for work in London,
had been spending his summers in France* — is a real scene with a real
character. The setup section moves crisply through *who he was → what he had
seen → what he proposed → who he rounded up → 228 works in crates → the
title scribbled against a printer's deadline → opens 8 November 1910*. That
is a chapter doing work. The reference Cubism chapters move like this.

The London-press freak-out section is *the* model of how Post-Impressionism
should be writing throughout. *"either an extremely bad joke or a swindle"
— "a widespread plot to destroy the whole fabric of European painting" —
"Pure pornography." "The work of madmen."* Four real quotes, in twenty
words, doing the entire job of "the press lost its mind." That is the
*economy* the longer chapters lack.

The Virginia Woolf line (*"On or about December 1910 human character
changed"*) is the closing punch the section needed and it lands.

The *5 painters dead before the show opened* closing list **earns** its
list-pacing — because the chapter has set up the framing (Fry hanging dead
men's pictures and giving them a name) and the list pays it off as the
chapter's emotional pivot. Lists work when they're the resolution, not the
exposition. This one resolves.

**Where the air gets in.** The **1912 sequel section** and the **1913 Armory
Show section** are both reference-desk inserts of low narrative value
relative to their length (200 + 220 words). The sequel section's only
*moving* fact is "the controversy had cooled by Christmas 1912" — which is
one sentence. The Armory Show is genuinely useful as a hand-off ("the
American collectors went home and started buying"), but the *1,250 works by
about 300 artists, about two-thirds American and one-third European*
inventory paragraph is a registrar's report, not a story.

The closing **One last fact / They were all dead** section is the chapter's
real closing and it's perfect.

### MUST-FIX
- **Compression target: ~1,100 words.** Smallest cut on the page.
- **The 1912 sequel and 1913 Armory Show sections collapse into one short
  section.** *"Two years later, in 1912, a sequel; by then the row had
  cooled. The next year, in New York, the Armory Show carried the same
  pictures across the Atlantic, and American collectors went home and
  started buying."* That's the work those two sections need to do. The
  inventories (5 Cézannes, 19 Matisses, 13 Picassos; 1,250 works, 300
  artists, 32 Redons) go in PaintingFigure credit lines or the integrator
  drops them.

### SHOULD-FIX
- The "five painters all dead" closing list is **strong as written**. Trust
  it. Don't add explanatory prose between the bullets.
- The very final closing paragraph (*"The mountain refused to dissolve. The
  wheatfield kept burning. The Breton women kept seeing things their eyes
  couldn't see. The dots kept building the Sunday lawn. The cabaret lights
  kept catching on the dancers' faces."*) is the **best closing in the
  draft** — five paintings rhymed in five sentences. Preserve. Don't touch
  a word.
- The *no Fauvism / no Die Brücke / no Der Blaue Reiter / no Cubism / no
  abstraction* list right before that paragraph is in danger of being a
  list. It works *because* it's a setup for the five-painting rhyme that
  follows. Keep — but tighten the parenthetical dates (Die Brücke "founded
  in Dresden on 7 June 1905" is a date the reader doesn't need; "founded
  in Dresden in 1905" is enough).

### NICE-TO-HAVE
- *"He stuck the catalogue together with that on the cover."* Final sentence
  of the title-scribbling paragraph — preserve at all costs, it's the
  scene-anchor for the whole improvised-name thesis.

## Cross-chapter findings

### Vague-praise flagging
Sweep run against the list the brief flagged. The draft is **better than
average** on vague-praise filler — the author is mostly disciplined. But there
are still hot spots:

- Ch 4: *"He painted there anyway, and what he painted was extraordinary"* —
  "extraordinary" doing the work a specific canvas should do. Replace with one
  beat from one of the canvases listed two sentences later.
- Ch 5: *"a long horizontal mural Gauguin painted as his testament before
  attempting suicide with arsenic"* — the word *testament* is on the edge of
  vague-praise. The sentence is fine; just make sure the next sentence shows
  the reader *why* it's a testament (birth/life/death across the canvas — and
  it does).
- Ch 6: *"His private drawings, even at the end of his life, are
  extraordinary — soft conté-crayon studies in which a figure emerges out of
  velvety black grain like a photograph slowly developing."* The *extraordinary*
  is propped up by the looking simile that follows, which earns it. Keep as is.
- Ch 7: *"the most direct descendants of Gauguin's Synthetism"* — "most
  direct" is a small claim that hides behind softness. Either commit (*"the
  direct descendants"*) or particularize.

### Word-repetition within paragraphs
- Ch 1: *paint / painted / paint / painting* dense in Section 2. Rotate.
- Ch 4: *"He painted Auvers in a frenzy. He had been working at the rate of
  about a canvas a day for two and a half years; in Auvers he kept it up."* —
  "Auvers" twice, "canvas/canvases" / "painted/painting" cluster. Tighten.
- Ch 6: *"He drank himself to death. Heavily."* / *"He drank again, and added
  syphilis to the bill."* — *drank* twice in the closing paragraph. Vary.
- Ch 7: Section 4 — every fresh painter paragraph opens with *"painter"* in
  the first sentence. *"Paul Signac (1863–1935). Seurat's partner from 1884
  onward, the second great Divisionist."* / *"Odilon Redon (1840–1916). The
  Symbolist tributary."* / *"Henri Rousseau (1844–1910) — known as Le
  Douanier…"* / *"Mary Cassatt (1844–1926). Pittsburgh-born…"* The shape of
  the paragraph is template, and the eye picks it up.

### Repeated transitions
- **"Now the…"** opens four times: *"Now the letter, because this is where…"*
  (Ch 3), *"Now the part of Gauguin's story…"* (Ch 5), *"Now watch what happens
  to him in death"* (Ch 3), *"Now the part of the story that 21st-century
  writing on him cannot skim past"* (Ch 5). Once is a tic, four times is a
  signature. Vary at least two.
- **"End on / End the chapter…"** — Ch 1's *"End the chapter in Provence"* and
  Ch 8's *"End on the strangeness of it."* are both author-stage-directions
  surfacing into the prose. Both read as instructions to a later editor. Lose
  both — they're vestigial author scaffolding.
- **"Now watch / Look at what he does"** — both are house-voice but the draft
  uses them more than the reference does. The reference says it once or twice
  a chapter; this draft says it three or four times per chapter.

### Throat-clearing openings
- Ch 2 Section 1 — fine (the 1905 lecture framing).
- Ch 6 Lautrec opening — **throat-clear** (*"The other half of this
  chapter…"*) — flagged in chapter notes.
- Ch 7 Section 4 — **throat-clear** (*"A movement Roger Fry would in 1910 lump
  under the single umbrella…"*) — flagged.
- Otherwise: chapter openings are mostly scene-first, which is the right
  in-house instinct. The author knows how to open. They struggle with **second
  and third section** openings.

### Closing punches
This is where the draft is **strongest**. Every chapter has a real ending:

- Ch 1 — Paris/Provence pole rhyme — **lands**.
- Ch 2 — Roger Fry hand-off — **lands**.
- Ch 3 — *"the young men in Paris saw it"* — **lands** (move the hedge
  paragraph earlier, then it lands even harder).
- Ch 4 — *la tristesse durera toujours* is the real ending; the chapter then
  *adds another ending* after it, which kills the punch — **fix as MUST-FIX**.
- Ch 5 — *"Within fifteen years they would be unaffordable in any market on
  earth"* — **lands**.
- Ch 6 — *"Neither was at peace"* — fine, but the chapter's whole "linked
  here not because they were friends" qualifier weakens it; the welding fix is
  the bigger one.
- Ch 7 — *"All they need is somebody to walk into the room and put a name on
  the door"* — **lands**, but the chapter has lost the reader by then.
- Ch 8 — the five-paintings rhyme — **best closing in the draft**.

The author **closes** well. The author **structures** chapters well at the
section-header level. The author **opens** chapters well. The author **does
not cut** — and the draft is dying of fat, not of any other defect.
