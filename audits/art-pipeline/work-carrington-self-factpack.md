## 2. THE STORY

Leonora Carrington painted this around age 20, at the very start of her life as a Surrealist. In 1937, as a rebellious daughter of a wealthy Lancashire textile family, she met the German Surrealist **Max Ernst** (then 46, married) in London; they became lovers and she left England to live with him, first in Paris and then in the village of Saint-Martin-d'Ardèche in southern France. She is a participant in Surrealism here, not a bystander — she began the canvas in London and finished it in Paris in 1938.

The picture is a dream-interior. Carrington sits perched on the edge of a **blue armchair** in an enclosed room (two walls meeting at a corner, a tiled floor, a curtained window). She wears **androgynous riding clothes — white jodhpurs/breeches and a jacket** — and her **wild red mane of hair** echoes the animals around her. She extends one hand toward a **spotted/striped hyena with pendulous, engorged (lactating) teats** and oddly human eyes, which mirrors her pose and gaze. Behind and above her, a **white rocking-horse floats on the wall**. Through the curtained window, a **white horse gallops free** into a green landscape.

The reading scholars consistently attach (kept as interpretation, not fact — see §8): the caged domestic room is the constrained aristocratic life she fled; the **female hyena** is a kindred wild, sexually-ambiguous animal-self (a creature Carrington used as her surrogate across her writing too — e.g. her 1937–38 story *The Debutante*, where a hyena takes the débutante's place at a society ball); the **galloping white horse outside** is freedom and escape; the **rocking-horse** ties to childhood and to her own play *Pénélope*, about a girl in love with a rocking horse. The recurrent theme is **female–animal kinship, autonomy, and escape** — a young woman picturing her own psyche through her own bestiary.

The escape the painting forecasts turned literal. When WWII broke out Ernst was interned (as an enemy alien), and Carrington fled to Spain, where she suffered a breakdown and was institutionalized (recounted in her memoir *Down Below*). She left the painting behind with Ernst; he carried it to New York in 1941. Carrington herself reached **Mexico in 1942**, where she became a central figure of Mexican Surrealism.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK *(required)*

**Before:** In mainstream Surrealism, the woman is overwhelmingly the *object* — muse, dreamed-of body, the male artist's symbol of desire and the unconscious (Breton's "convulsive beauty," the women of Ernst, Magritte, Dalí, Man Ray). Women appear *in* Surrealist pictures far more often than they make them.

**After / the break:** Here a young woman Surrealist paints **her own dream-symbolism and her own animal-kin self** — she is the seer and the subject, not the seen object. She gives herself androgynous riding clothes instead of the eroticized nude; she stares back at the viewer; she pairs herself with a *female* animal (the lactating hyena) of her own choosing rather than being arranged as someone else's symbol. The picture asserts authorship and autonomy: the female Surrealist as the dreamer, not the dream. (Carrington's own framing of this stance is the muse refusal in §4.)

## 4. KEY STATEMENT *(verbatim, sourced)*

**Primary quote — refusing the muse role:**
> "I didn't have time to be anyone's muse… I was too busy rebelling against my family and learning to be an artist."

- **Attribution:** Carrington, in response to art historian **Whitney Chadwick** asking about being a Surrealist muse. The Met's own audio-tour text (Katy Hessel, *Museums Without Men*) repeats it, in a fuller form: *"I thought it was bullshit. I didn't have time to be anyone's muse. I was too busy rebelling against my family and learning to be an artist."*
- **Variant flag:** Two forms circulate — the short, widely-quoted version (above) and the longer "I thought it was bullshit…" version. The longer one is the one the Met cites. The line is genuine and well-attested (Chadwick is the scholarly source); treat the *short* form as the safe display quote. It is a general statement on her stance, **not** a statement specifically about this painting.

**Secondary quote — the hyena/animal-self (use with care):**
> "I'm like a hyena, I get into the garbage cans. I have an insatiable curiosity."

- **Attribution:** Carrington, attributed to a **1999 interview** in several secondary sources. Less firmly pinned than the muse quote — if used, attribute loosely ("she once said") and do not tie it directly to the 1937–38 canvas. **Flag as needs-firmer-source.**

*No verbatim quote by Carrington specifically about this painting was found in the research. The muse quote is the key statement; the hyena quote is supporting.*

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6 — prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **The spotted hyena with pendulous teats.** Lower-center, facing Carrington and mirroring her outstretched hand — a striped/spotted female hyena with heavy, engorged (lactating) teats and unsettlingly human eyes. The wild, sexually-ambiguous animal she chose as her self-surrogate.
2. **The white rocking-horse on the wall.** Floating behind and above her head, tailless, weightless — a child's toy gone airborne, tying the dream to childhood (and to her play *Pénélope*).
3. **The galloping white horse outside the window.** Seen through the parted curtain, running free into a green landscape — her symbol of liberty and escape, set deliberately *outside* the enclosed room.
4. **Carrington in jodhpurs / riding dress.** White breeches and jacket — androgynous riding clothes, pointedly not the eroticized female nude of so much Surrealist painting.
5. **Her wild red hair.** A loose, untamed mane that rhymes with the animals' coats — the human and animal selves visually braided together.
6. **The blue armchair.** She sits on the very edge of it, poised and unsettled rather than reposed — a domestic interior she looks ready to leave.

## 6. PROVENANCE

1. **Leonora Carrington / Max Ernst** — Carrington left the painting with Ernst in France at the outbreak of WWII.
2. **New York, 1941** — Ernst brought the painting to New York when he emigrated.
3. **Pierre Matisse** (art dealer, youngest son of Henri Matisse) — Ernst gave it to him **after December 1942**.
4. **Maria-Gaetana Matisse** — inherited on Pierre Matisse's death (Pierre d. 1989).
5. **The Metropolitan Museum of Art — 2002** — given by the **Pierre and Maria-Gaetana Matisse Foundation**; credit line "The Pierre and Maria-Gaetana Matisse Collection, 2002"; accession **2002.456.1**. (The broader Matisse gift was publicly unveiled in Met exhibitions/press 2003–2004.)

*Provenance chain per Wikipedia/Met; the "after December 1942" handoff to Pierre Matisse is the one date worth stating as approximate.*

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS

- **The exact date.** Do not assert a single year. The Met's authoritative dating is **"ca. 1937-38."** "Begun London 1937, completed Paris 1938" is well-attested and fine to state, but the catalogue hedge ("ca.") is the safe display form. Sources variously label it 1937, 1938, or 1937–38; **use ca. 1937–38**.
- **Dimensions.** Use **65 × 81.2 cm / 25 9/16 × 32 in.** One secondary source rounds the width to **81.3 cm**; this is a rounding artifact, not a different object. Convert to **ft/in for display per house rule** (≈ 2 ft 1.6 in × 2 ft 8 in); do not show cm in the reader.
- **The animal symbolism.** The hyena=wild-self, white-horse=freedom, rocking-horse=childhood readings are **scholarly/biographical interpretation**, widely repeated and reasonable — but **frame them as interpretation, not as facts Carrington stated about this canvas.** Avoid over-pinning ("the hyena *means* X"); prefer "scholars read the hyena as…". No verbatim Carrington statement explaining the picture's symbols was found.
- **The Max Ernst relationship.** Factual and relevant (it situates her entry into Surrealism and explains the painting's path to New York), but keep it **proportionate** — this is *her* self-portrait and *her* authorship; do not let Ernst become the headline. The "muse refusal" (§4) is the corrective frame.
- **Title.** "Inn of the Dawn Horse" is a translation/popular subtitle; the Met simply lists "Self-Portrait." Both are correct; lead with the popular full title for the reader, note the Met's plain catalogue title.
- **"First truly Surrealist work."** Often called her first mature/truly-Surrealist painting — attribute as a critical judgment ("often called…"), not as an undisputed fact.
