## 1. Edgar Degas, *The Dance Class* (1874) — Metropolitan Museum, New York

### Hard facts

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Title (EN) | The Dance Class |
| Title (FR) | La Classe de danse |
| Artist | Edgar Degas (1834–1917) |
| Date | 1874 (commissioned 1873, delivered to Faure November 1874) |
| Medium | Oil on canvas |
| Dimensions | 83.2 × 76.8 cm — **2 ft 8.7 in × 2 ft 6.2 in** (reader-facing: **roughly 2 ft 9 in × 2 ft 6 in**) |
| Signature | Signed lower left: "Degas" |
| Museum | The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York |
| Accession no. | 1987.47.1 |
| Credit line | Bequest of Mrs. Harry Payne Bingham, 1986 (accessioned 1987) |
| Current gallery | Gallery 815 (as of late 2023) |

**Discipline note — the Havemeyer trap.** Online aggregators (including some Met search snippets) confuse this picture with Degas's earlier, smaller *The Dancing Class* (acc. 29.100.184, Havemeyer Bequest 1929, ~19.7 × 24.8 cm panel). The 83 × 77 cm *Dance Class* is the Bingham bequest, not the Havemeyer bequest. The author must NOT write "given by the Havemeyers in 1929." Falsehood-to-refuse #1.

### What is literally on the canvas

Setting: a rehearsal room of the **old Paris Opéra (rue Le Peletier)**, which had **burned down on 28–29 October 1873**, the year before the picture's delivery — so the room Degas paints is, by 1874, a memory. The space is rendered with a high, cropped, off-centre floor that drives the eye toward the back wall (Japonisme cropping / diagonal-floor compositional move).

- **Right foreground:** an elderly man in a brown jacket, leaning on a long walking stick — the ballet master **Jules Perrot** (1810–1892), the famous retired dancer-choreographer. Degas painted Perrot over an earlier male figure variously identified as **Louis Mérante** (the Opéra's then-ballet-master); X-radiography confirms the pentimento. (DOCUMENTED — Met conservation; cited in Wikipedia stub.)
- **Centre / mid-ground:** a cluster of ~12–14 dancers in white tutus with coloured sashes (red, blue, green, yellow), some at attention, some adjusting slippers or scratching, some looking away. A girl at left stretches her back.
- **Left foreground:** a **small dog** sniffing or seated on the floor — a deliberate informal note against the corps' formal pose.
- **Far left / back:** a row of "stage mothers" or *abonnés*-adjacent observers in dark dresses, mostly in shadow, on benches against the wall.
- **Back wall:** a printed poster for Rossini's opera ***Guillaume Tell*** — a tribute to **Jean-Baptiste Faure**, the baritone who commissioned the painting and was famous in the *Guillaume Tell* role. (DOCUMENTED.)
- **Floor:** a watering can on the floorboards — used to damp the boards so dancers wouldn't slip; one of the small "factory-floor" details that makes the picture a workplace, not a fantasy.

Total figure count: ~24 dancers plus Perrot plus the chaperones (counts vary slightly with what you count as a figure — the off-canvas-pose dancers behind the column are partial).

### Provenance

1. **Commissioned 1873** by **Jean-Baptiste Faure** (1830–1914), Opéra baritone and major Impressionist patron; **delivered November 1874** for **5,000 francs**. (DOCUMENTED.)
2. **Faure** retained the picture until **4 April 1898**, when he sold it to **Colonel Oliver H. Payne** (1839–1917), New York industrialist, for **$25,000**. (DOCUMENTED.)
3. **Payne** to his nephew **Harry Payne Bingham** (d. 1955) by inheritance, 1917.
4. **Bingham collection / Mrs. Harry Payne Bingham**, 1955 to her death.
5. **Bequest of Mrs. Harry Payne Bingham to the Met**, 1986; **accessioned 1987.47.1**.

### Exhibition history (selected)

- **2nd Impressionist Exhibition, Paris, April 1876** — lent by Faure. (DOCUMENTED — the picture was NOT in the 1st Impressionist Exhibition of 1874; despite the canvas's 1874 date, Faure exhibited it in 1876. Falsehood-to-refuse #2.)
- Subsequent loans from Faure in 1870s–90s; private collection 1898–1986; constantly on view at the Met since 1987 (Gallery 815).

### Relationship to the Musée d'Orsay version

There are **two related canvases**, often confused:

- **Met version**: 83.2 × 76.8 cm, dated 1874, commissioned by Faure, signed lower left.
- **Orsay version** (*La Classe de danse*, RF 1976, **85 × 75 cm**, dated by Lemoisne to 1873–76, by Pickvance to 1874–75): a **separate, related canvas**, similar scene, also with Jules Perrot at right and a watering can. Critics still debate which is the "first" — Pickvance argues the Orsay version is earlier (1873–75), Lemoisne argued the Met's is. The author may NOT call them "two versions of the same painting" — they are two related compositions, both autograph, sharing a vocabulary. Falsehood-to-refuse #3.

### Context the section needs

- **Japonisme cropping**: Degas owned Japanese woodblock prints; the off-axis floor, the figure cropped at the right edge, the column slicing the composition — all are *ukiyo-e* moves transposed to a rehearsal room. The author should name this.
- **The ballet workforce / *petits rats***: the Paris Opéra's dancers were overwhelmingly working-class girls from 7 upward; "stage mothers" chaperoning them in a back corner of the rehearsal room were real and ubiquitous; the wealthy male *abonnés* (subscribers) had access backstage that we would now call exploitative. The mother in dark dress is part of that economy, not an incidental figure.
- **Jules Perrot**: by 1874 a retired sixty-four-year-old former star (he had partnered Marie Taglioni; choreographed *Giselle*'s "Wilis" act with Coralli, 1841); brought back to Paris in his last decade as a teacher. Degas's choice of Perrot rather than a generic *maître de ballet* is a portrait, not a type.
- **No plein air**: indoor Impressionism. The picture is the answer to "what does Impressionism do when it can't go outside?" — modern Paris instant, off-balance, but lit by overhead north light, not sun.

### Falsehoods to refuse

1. "Given to the Met by the Havemeyers in 1929" (confuses two Degas paintings).
2. "Shown at the 1874 First Impressionist Exhibition" (it was the 1876 Second).
3. "The Met and Orsay paintings are the same painting at different stages" — they are two related, separate canvases.
4. "Painted on the spot at the Opéra rehearsal room" — the room had burned the previous October.
5. Any specific stage-mother identification — they are unidentified figures.

### Proposed 5-chapter map

1. **Origin** — Faure's commission (1873), the ballet world Degas already knew, Japonisme arriving in his eye.
2. **Making** — the burned Salle Le Peletier as a remembered room; the Perrot-painted-over-Mérante pentimento; how Degas constructed the scene in the studio.
3. **Looking closer** — the off-balance floor, the watering can, the *Guillaume Tell* poster, the dog, the stage mothers, the cropped-out dancer at the right edge.
4. **The show** — 2nd Impressionist Exhibition, 1876; critics' response; how Degas's "indoor Impressionism" sat with the plein-air pictures.
5. **Afterlife** — Faure → Payne → Bingham → Met (1987); the parallel Orsay canvas; what "the Degas dancer" became in popular memory.

## 2. Edgar Degas, *L'Absinthe* (*Dans un café*) (1875–76) — Musée d'Orsay, Paris

### Hard facts

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Title (EN) | In a Café / The Absinthe Drinker / Absinthe |
| Title (FR) | *Dans un café*, also known as *L'Absinthe* |
| Original (1876) title | *Dans un café* |
| Artist | Edgar Degas |
| Date | 1875–76 |
| Medium | Oil on canvas |
| Dimensions | 92 × 68.5 cm — **3 ft 0.2 in × 2 ft 2.97 in** (reader-facing: **roughly 3 ft × 2 ft 3 in**, portrait orientation) |
| Museum | Musée d'Orsay, Paris |
| Inventory no. | RF 1984 |
| Catalogue raisonné | Lemoisne 393; degas-catalogue.com MS917 |

The current name *L'Absinthe* dates from the painting's **1893 London showing**, not from Degas. DOCUMENTED.

### What is literally on the canvas

The composition is the picture's most modern feature. Almost the entire **lower-left half** of the canvas is **empty**: a **zig-zag of marble café tables**, viewed from above, leads the eye inward to two figures **shoved into the upper-right corner**. The viewer is positioned as if they themselves were a third person at one of the empty tables — which is what makes the picture feel like surveillance, or accidental witness.

- **Right of centre, seated:** a woman in a pale dress, a pinkish-cream jacket, a small black-trimmed hat, hands slack in her lap. Before her on the table, a tall stemmed glass of milky-greenish liquid — **absinthe louche**. She looks down and slightly away, eyes lowered. Sitter: actress **Ellen Andrée** (1857–1925), a friend of Degas, Manet (she sat for *Chez le père Lathuille*, 1879, and *La Prune*, c.1877), Renoir (*Luncheon of the Boating Party*, 1880–81), and the Nouvelle-Athènes circle. (DOCUMENTED.)
- **Right edge, beside her:** a bearded man in a brown jacket and a battered tall hat, holding a clay pipe, looking off to the right, out of frame. A small carafe or glass of pale liquid (often identified as a *mazagran* — cold coffee — NOT absinthe) in front of him. Sitter: artist-engraver **Marcellin Desboutin** (1823–1902), a printmaker friend of Degas. (DOCUMENTED.)
- **Foreground tables:** two or three empty marble café tables, jaggedly arranged, one with a folded newspaper.
- **Behind them:** the warm interior of the **Café de la Nouvelle-Athènes** on the **Place Pigalle** — the Impressionist headquarters café, where Manet, Degas, Renoir, Pissarro, the writers Duranty and Zola, and the irregulars debated. Mirrored back wall, reflected gas-lit room. (DOCUMENTED.)

The two sitters are not interacting. That is the point.

### Provenance (DOCUMENTED — from the catalogue raisonné)

1. **Atelier Degas** → **Charles W. Deschamps**, London dealer, 1876.
2. **Captain Henry Hill** (1812–1882), Brighton — collector and tailor — **1876–1892**. Often called "the Sussex collector." The "Sussex club" / Brighton-club bought-it story (sometimes appearing in popular books) is the **Captain Hill purchase, period** — not a club. LEGEND-CORRECTION: he was a private collector, not a club.
3. **Henry Hill estate sale, Christie's London, 19 February 1892**, lot 209 (sold as *Figures at a Café*).
4. **Alexander Reid**, Glasgow dealer, 1892.
5. **Arthur Kay**, Glasgow, 1893.
6. **Martin et Camentron**, Paris, dealers; then back to **Alexander Reid**, London.
7. **Comte Isaac de Camondo**, Paris, **1893–1911**.
8. **Camondo bequest to the Musée du Louvre**, **1911**.
9. **Jeu de Paume**, Paris, **1947–1986**.
10. **Musée d'Orsay**, **1986** to present.

### Exhibition history

- **2nd Impressionist Exhibition, Paris, April 1876** — as *Dans un café*. Critics already called the figures "ugly and disgusting." (DOCUMENTED.)
- **Brighton, Royal Pavilion Gallery, 1876** (no. 166), lent by Hill. (DOCUMENTED — this is the first English exposure, NOT the famous 1893 scandal.)
- **Grafton Galleries, London, 1893** (no. 258, as part of the Arthur Kay loan group) — the **1893 scandal**. The picture was now titled *L'Absinthe*, and Victorian critics treated it as a temperance tract: "a study in degradation," "uncouth," "a warning." George Moore initially called it that and later recanted ("the picture is merely a work of art"). The scandal made the picture famous and **fixed the title** *L'Absinthe* on it permanently. (DOCUMENTED.)
- Constant exhibition since 1911 (Louvre / Jeu de Paume / Orsay); 40+ international loans in the 20th–21st c.

### Ellen Andrée's later statement (DOCUMENTED — important)

In **1921** — forty-five years after she sat — Andrée gave an interview to the critic **Félix Fénéon** at the **Théâtre Édouard VII** in Paris. She did NOT deny that she had posed. She corrected the *interpretation*: she said she had been the model, that **her glass held absinthe** for the picture's sake, that **Desboutin's glass was something innocuous** (often glossed as *mazagran* — cold coffee), and that in real life **she was not an absinthe drinker** while Desboutin's drinking was the other way around. (Several popular accounts compress this into "Andrée denied she ever drank absinthe and the painting libelled her"; the catalogue-raisonné and serious accounts have the more careful version above.) DOCUMENTED that she clarified the picture in 1921; LIKELY that she had defended herself earlier and the 1921 interview is the surviving record.

### Context the section needs

- **Café de la Nouvelle-Athènes**, Place Pigalle: the actual room where the Impressionist circle met after 1875, when they moved over from the **Café Guerbois** (rue des Batignolles). Author MUST get this right — the picture is set in the Impressionists' own bar. Falsehood-to-refuse: it is NOT the Café Guerbois.
- **Absinthe in 1876**: a popular, cheap, very strong (~70% ABV) anise-fennel-wormwood spirit; a working- and middle-class Paris drink in the 1870s, only later (1880s–1915) demonised. In 1875 it was not yet "the green poison" — Degas's picture is one of the things that *helped* make it that.
- **Why the empty tables matter**: the foreground absence is not a compositional accident. It is the picture's argument — that modern Paris isolates people in the same room. This is the same year (1876) Duranty's pamphlet *La Nouvelle Peinture* argued for unsentimental modern subjects. Degas's two figures are exhibit A.
- **Andrée and Desboutin were friends, not derelicts**: Andrée was a young actress with a career ahead of her (she sat for Manet, Renoir, and others through the 1880s and lived to 1925). Desboutin was a respected printmaker. The "degenerates" reading of the picture is Victorian projection onto two willing portraitists.

### Falsehoods to refuse

1. Locating the scene at the **Café Guerbois** instead of the **Nouvelle-Athènes**.
2. Calling Andrée an "absinthe addict" or a "fallen woman."
3. Crediting the painting to the **1st Impressionist Exhibition 1874** instead of the **2nd, 1876**.
4. The "Sussex club" — it was Captain Henry Hill, an individual.
5. Calling the title *L'Absinthe* Degas's own — the title was fixed in **English**, in **1893**.

### Proposed 5-chapter map

1. **Origin** — the Nouvelle-Athènes circle in 1875; Degas, Duranty, *La Nouvelle Peinture*; why a painter would paint a bar.
2. **Making** — the two sitters (Andrée + Desboutin), the construction of the empty foreground, the decision to push the figures to the corner.
3. **Looking closer** — the louche in Andrée's glass; the slumped postures; the mirrored wall; the watering-down of conversation; the mazagran-not-absinthe in Desboutin's glass.
4. **The 1876 show + the 1893 break** — first reception in Paris (ugly), then the 17-year delayed scandal in London (degeneracy), then Andrée's 1921 correction.
5. **Afterlife** — Hill → Reid → Camondo → Louvre → Orsay; how this single picture helped invent the anti-absinthe campaign and the "Impressionist as modern witness" reading.

## 3. Pierre-Auguste Renoir, *Luncheon of the Boating Party* (1880–81) — The Phillips Collection, Washington DC

### Hard facts

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Title (EN) | Luncheon of the Boating Party |
| Title (FR) | *Le Déjeuner des canotiers* |
| Artist | Pierre-Auguste Renoir (1841–1919) |
| Date | 1880–1881 |
| Medium | Oil on canvas |
| Dimensions | 130.2 × 175.6 cm — **4 ft 3.25 in × 5 ft 9.12 in** (reader-facing: **roughly 4 ft 3 in × 5 ft 9 in**) |
| Museum | The Phillips Collection, Washington, D.C. |
| Object no. | 1637 |
| Acquisition | Purchased by Duncan Phillips from Durand-Ruel, **1923**, for **$125,000** |

### What is literally on the canvas

A wide, sun-dappled balcony at the **Maison Fournaise restaurant** on the **Île de Chatou** (Île des Impressionnistes today), on the Seine just downstream of Paris. A striped red-and-white awning above; the Seine and a railing visible at the back. A long table with the remains of lunch — wine bottles, half-eaten food, grapes, glasses; the whites are still life-grade. Fourteen people across three orderly clusters. The painting is **just under 4½ × 5¾ feet** — among Renoir's largest.

**Identified sitters and their placement** (Phillips Collection canonical list, cross-checked with Wikipedia and Distel/Renoir scholarship):

| Where | Person | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Left foreground**, standing, leaning on the balcony rail in a straw boater and sleeveless white shirt, muscular arms | **Alphonse Fournaise Jr.** | The restaurant owner's son, ran the boat hire |
| **Left, leaning forward on her hands at the rail**, smiling, in a striped straw boater hat | **Louise-Alphonsine Fournaise** | The owner's daughter |
| **Left foreground, seated at the table, in dark hat, holding a small dog (an affenpinscher), cooing at it** | **Aline Charigot** | Renoir's then-girlfriend, future wife (married 1890) |
| **Centre middle distance, with his back to us, in a bowler hat, talking to Alphonsine** | **Baron Raoul Barbier** | Former cavalry officer; sometimes described as ex-mayor of colonial Saigon |
| **Centre, holding a glass to her ear** (or against her cheek, often read as raised to listen / hear a toast) | **Ellen Andrée** | Actress (yes — the same Andrée from *L'Absinthe*) |
| **Beside her, in dark suit**, leaning in | **Eugène-Pierre Lestringuez** | Ministry of the Interior official, friend of Renoir |
| **Right of Andrée, also leaning over her** | **Paul Lhote** | Renoir's writer/journalist friend |
| **Lower right foreground, seated backwards on a chair, in white sleeveless shirt and straw boater, gazing at Aline-and-dog cluster** | **Gustave Caillebotte** | Painter, patron, sailor — the well-known sitter at the corner |
| **Right, beside Caillebotte, seated, in a black dress and dark hat** | **Angèle Legault** | Model |
| **Standing behind Angèle, leaning down to her** | **Adrien Maggiolo** (sometimes "Antonio") | Italian journalist, *Le Triboulet* |
| **Far right background, in tall top hat** | **Charles Ephrussi** | Banker, *Gazette des beaux-arts* editor, critic |
| **In front of Ephrussi, in soft hat, looking up** | **Jules Laforgue** | Poet/critic (Ephrussi's secretary; some sources contest this ID) |
| **Right background, in a hat, hands raised by her ears as if blocking sound** | **Jeanne Samary** | Comédie-Française actress (placement and even ID contested — see note) |
| **Background between figures, partially visible** | Unidentified man, often called simply "a friend of Renoir" | — |

**Notes on contested IDs.** The 14-figure list above is the **Phillips Collection / Distel canonical** identification. Two are softer than the rest: (a) **Jules Laforgue** is sometimes contested — the figure at right under Ephrussi is identified by some scholars as another of Ephrussi's circle; (b) **Jeanne Samary**'s position at the right with raised arms is the Phillips/Wikipedia reading, but some scholarship places her elsewhere or doubts the ID. The author MUST flag these two as contested, not assert them flatly. The other twelve are well-attested.

Light: high-summer late-afternoon, dappled through the awning and trees; Renoir's full chromatic palette — cobalts, vermilions, pale pinks, the new tube colours that made plein air figure painting possible at this scale.

### Provenance (DOCUMENTED)

1. **Renoir studio** → **Paul Durand-Ruel**, Paris, **February 1881** (bought directly from Renoir).
2. Durand-Ruel held it almost continuously for 42 years (some loans, some near-sales that fell through).
3. **Duncan Phillips**, Washington, DC, **1923, $125,000** (Phillips had pursued the picture for roughly a decade).
4. **Phillips Memorial Gallery / The Phillips Collection**, 1923 to present. On permanent view in the gallery built around it.

### Exhibition history (selected)

- **7th Impressionist Exhibition, Paris, March 1882** — three critics named it the show's best work. (DOCUMENTED.)
- **Durand-Ruel exhibitions, Paris and New York, 1880s–1910s**, multiple loans.
- **Phillips Memorial Gallery, 1923** to present; near-continuously displayed.
- Currently on loan (through July 2026) to the Musée d'Orsay's *Impressionism* exhibition; returns to Phillips August 2026.

### Context the section needs

- **Maison Fournaise**: a working **restaurant + boat-rental** on the Île de Chatou, run by Alphonse Fournaise Sr. The family rented rowing skiffs to the Paris middle class taking the train out from Saint-Lazare for a weekend on the Seine. Renoir had been a regular customer for years and the Fournaises were his friends, not paid models. The building still stands and is now a restaurant + museum.
- **Île de Chatou / Île des Impressionnistes**: a long thin Seine island ~10 miles west of central Paris. The Paris–Saint-Germain railway (opened 1837) put it inside a day-trip for Parisians by the 1860s.
- **Boating culture (*canotage*)**: the late-1870s leisure boom — rowing, picnics, weekend escape from the city — was the **new** thing Impressionists painted; this picture is its monument the way Monet's *Bal du moulin de la Galette* (Renoir, 1876) was the dance-hall's.
- **Aline Charigot**: 21 in the painting, seamstress, working class; would marry Renoir in 1890 and they would have three sons. The small dog she's cooing at is a Brussels griffon / affenpinscher type, one of the few literal animals in the picture.
- **Caillebotte the patron**: the seated-backwards figure in lower right was a serious painter himself (the *Floor Scrapers*, 1875) AND a competitive sailor — the Île de Chatou was his boat club. He paid Renoir's bills more than once.
- **Why Veronese keeps being cited**: the composition — banquet table, parted curtain (awning), full chromatic register, modern figures in friezelike arrangement — was Renoir's deliberate echo of *The Wedding at Cana* and *The Feast in the House of Levi*. He'd been at the Louvre studying them. This is documented in his letters.
- **The Phillips story**: Duncan Phillips chased the picture for a decade; he paid more for it than any single painting in his collection. The Phillips Collection's *raison d'être* is essentially this picture plus the works he bought around it.

### Falsehoods to refuse

1. "Renoir married Aline Charigot the next year" — they married in **1890**, nearly a decade later.
2. "All 14 sitters are 100% identified" — Laforgue and Samary are contested; flag both.
3. "Painted in a single sitting / single weekend" — composition was built across 1880–81; figures posed individually.
4. Describing the Maison Fournaise as a "tavern" or "café" — it was a restaurant-with-boat-hire on an island.
5. Locating it on the Seine in Paris — the Île de Chatou is west of Paris, in the suburbs, near Bougival.

### Proposed 5-chapter map

1. **Origin** — Chatou, Maison Fournaise, the boating world Renoir was already in; the Veronese ambition.
2. **Making** — pose-by-pose construction across 1880–81; who Renoir put where and why; the awning, the table, the dog.
3. **Looking closer** — three clusters (left = Fournaise family + Aline + dog; centre = Andrée's listening pose with Lestringuez/Lhote; right = Caillebotte + Angèle/Maggiolo + Ephrussi + Samary); the empty wine bottles; the railing as the picture's horizon line; the awning as Veronese's curtain.
4. **The show** — 7th Impressionist Exhibition, 1882; the "best painting of the show" reception; what critics saw that they didn't see in *L'Absinthe*.
5. **Afterlife** — Durand-Ruel keeps it 42 years; Duncan Phillips's decade-long pursuit; the picture as the Phillips Collection's centre of gravity.

## 4. Claude Monet, *La Gare Saint-Lazare* (1877) — Musée d'Orsay, Paris

### Hard facts

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Title (EN) | The Gare Saint-Lazare |
| Title (FR) | *La Gare Saint-Lazare* |
| Artist | Claude Monet (1840–1926) |

### What is literally on the canvas

The view is from **inside** the **train shed** of the Gare Saint-Lazare, looking down the rails toward the open end of the shed (toward the Pont de l'Europe, just out of frame). The composition is built on three structural layers:

- **Top:** the **iron-and-glass triangular roof** of the shed, its dark girders cutting the sky into facets; pale grey-blue light filters through dirty glass panes.
- **Middle:** **billowing white-grey-blue steam** filling the centre of the canvas, rising from the locomotives below. The steam is the painting's real subject — it dissolves the edges of the engines into pure atmospheric paint.
- **Bottom / foreground:** two black, blocky **locomotives** on parallel tracks, one closer, one farther; small figures (engineers, passengers) at the platform edge, indicated by quick dabs; the rails draw the eye in.
- **Background:** through the steam, the tall pale **Haussmannian apartment buildings** of the rue de Rome, hazy through the haze.

Palette: blues, greys, blacks, with quick warm strokes for shadows under the engines and for figures. The picture argues that the new modern Paris subject — iron, glass, steam, schedule — is paintable by **dissolving its own hard edges into atmosphere**, exactly as Monet had been doing with mist on the Seine.

### The series: which canvas this is

Monet painted **twelve canvases** of the Gare Saint-Lazare during a roughly four-month campaign in **early 1877**. He showed **seven** of them at the **3rd Impressionist Exhibition (April 1877)**.

(There is genuine source variation on the number shown — most scholarship and Wikipedia say **seven**; some popular sources say eight. The catalogue raisonné is the authority; default to **seven** and flag the variation.)

The twelve are now distributed across institutions:

- **Musée d'Orsay, Paris** — RF 2775 (this picture).
- **Musée Marmottan Monet, Paris** — another version.
- **Fogg Art Museum (Harvard Art Museums)**, Cambridge MA.
- **National Gallery, London** — *The Gare St-Lazare*, NG6479.
- **Art Institute of Chicago** — *Arrival of the Normandy Train, Gare Saint-Lazare* (different angle, looking the other way).
- **Pola Museum of Art**, Hakone, Japan.
- **Landesmuseum / Lower Saxony State Museum**, Germany.
- Plus other versions in private and public collections.

This Orsay version is one of the calmer interior views, dominated by the iron-glass roof and the central steam plume.

### The Marquet permission anecdote (DOCUMENTED — important)

In **January 1877** Monet, then thirty-six, decided to paint the Gare Saint-Lazare. He had taken a small studio in the rue Moncey nearby, in the **Nouvelle-Athènes neighbourhood**. He approached the **director of the Compagnie des chemins de fer de l'Ouest** — sometimes named as **Marquet** in popular accounts (the name appears in Monet's own later reminiscences via Gimpel and others; spelt variously and not always traceable in railway records) — and asked permission to set up his easel **inside** the station. Permission was granted: the director (allegedly) had platforms cleared, locomotives stoked specifically to produce steam, and trains held to give Monet his light. (DOCUMENTED at the level of "Monet himself told this story in old age"; the colourful additions — engines stoked on demand, special trains held — are LIKELY embellishments accreted across retellings. Tag the "trains held for him" detail as LIKELY, not DOCUMENTED.)

What IS documented: Monet had formal permission to work inside the station; he made preliminary studies on site and finished the canvases in his rented studio nearby across the spring of 1877.

### Provenance (Orsay version, RF 2775 — DOCUMENTED)

1. **Atelier Monet** → **Ernest Hoschedé**, Paris, by 1877–78. (Hoschedé was the department-store-magnate patron whose bankruptcy auction the next year scattered the great early Impressionist collection.)
2. **Hoschedé bankruptcy sale, Hôtel Drouot, Paris, 5–6 June 1878** — the picture was offered there.
3. **Gustave Caillebotte**, Paris, **10 March 1878** (acquired from Monet directly for **685 francs**; Caillebotte was acquiring in parallel with the Hoschedé sale).
4. **Caillebotte estate / bequest**: Caillebotte died **21 February 1894**; his will left his Impressionist collection to the French State; the State (after fierce negotiation with Léonce Bénédite at the Luxembourg) accepted part of the bequest in **1896**.
5. **Musée du Luxembourg**, Paris, **23 March 1896**.
6. **Musée du Louvre**, Paris, **1929**.
7. **Jeu de Paume** (impressionniste wing), Paris, **1947**.
8. **Musée d'Orsay**, Paris, **1986** (the Orsay opens with the Jeu de Paume's collection at its core).

### Context the section needs

- **The Gare Saint-Lazare was Paris's busiest station** in 1877, the terminus for trains to the western suburbs (Argenteuil, Vétheuil, Chatou, Bougival, Le Havre) — exactly the places Monet and his friends lived and painted. So the station is not a random urban subject for him; it is the doorway in and out of his own life. He had taken the train through it constantly for a decade.
- **The architecture**: cast-iron-and-glass train sheds (built by Eugène Flachat, 1851–53; expanded 1860s) were the great new architectural form of the 1850s–70s — the same vocabulary as London's Crystal Palace (1851), Les Halles (1854–74), and the Gare du Nord (1864). Painting this roof was painting the structural style of the present.
- **Why steam matters**: in 1877, **steam-powered everything** was the visual marker of modernity (Manet's *The Railway*, 1873, hides the railway behind a steam plume). Monet's choice — to make steam the protagonist, the subject the painting actually paints — is the deepest move in the picture.
- **Why this picture in particular**: of the twelve canvases, the Orsay version is the most "indoor," most architectural; others (Chicago) show the arriving train head-on, others (Marmottan) focus on the rail-yard from outside. Monet was using the series structure for the first time — variations on a fixed motif — and what he learns here, he will scale up massively in the Haystacks (1890–91), Rouen Cathedral (1892–94), Houses of Parliament (1900–01), and Water Lilies.
- **Caillebotte's role**: Caillebotte bought it from Monet for 685 francs at a moment Monet was broke (early 1878); the bequest 18 years later put it in the Luxembourg over fierce conservative opposition. Without Caillebotte the picture is in some private collection; with him it is in the Musée d'Orsay.

### Falsehoods to refuse

1. "All twelve canvases hung at the 3rd Impressionist Exhibition" — only **seven**.
2. "Monet painted from life inside the moving station for months" — he had a nearby studio; he finished there.
3. "Monet was the first painter of railways" — Turner 1844, Manet 1873 precede him.
4. Stating "Marquet" as an unqualified historical fact — flag as Monet's own later attribution.
5. Confusing this canvas with the Chicago *Arrival of the Normandy Train* (different angle), the National Gallery *The Gare St-Lazare* (different view), or the Marmottan version.

### Proposed 5-chapter map

1. **Origin** — early 1877, Monet broke, the western-suburbs life that ran through Saint-Lazare daily; why he aimed at the station next.
2. **Making** — the Marquet permission (with the LIKELY tag on the colourful details); the rented studio in rue Moncey; the four-month campaign; the move into series-thinking for the first time.
3. **Looking closer** — the iron-glass triangular roof, the locomotives as black blocks, the steam-as-protagonist, the Haussmann apartments hazy at the back, this canvas vs the Chicago / National Gallery / Marmottan versions.
4. **The show** — 3rd Impressionist Exhibition, April 1877; seven canvases hung together as one of the first true "series" presentations; what critics saw and missed.
5. **Afterlife** — Hoschedé → Caillebotte (685 francs) → 18-year wait → Luxembourg 1896 → Orsay 1986; how this picture seeded Haystacks, Cathedrals, Parliament, Water Lilies — the series logic that defined late Monet.
