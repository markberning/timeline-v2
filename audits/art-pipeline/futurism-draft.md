# PART A — Movement metadata

## hook (one sentence)
A poet who had never picked up a brush bought the front page of a Paris newspaper and announced a painting movement that did not yet have a single painting.

## hookLong (2–4 sentences)
In 1909 Filippo Tommaso Marinetti launched Futurism the way other people launch a product: with a press release. The art came later, when he went and recruited the painters, who then taught Western painting something it had never been able to do, show motion happening over time, the dog's legs blurred into a fan, the building site churning, the body dissolved into lines of force (the painted streaks along which a moving thing's energy seems to radiate). Their subject was the machine age at full throttle, the car and the tram and the electric light and the crowd, and their program, as they themselves announced it, was to burn the museums and worship speed. It is also the movement whose founder glorified war as the world's only hygiene and went on to help write the founding manifesto of Italian Fascism, so the story has to carry both halves at once.

## artists row (~6)
1. **Marinetti** — the impresario; founder, megaphone and money. A poet who launched the movement in print, wrote dozens of manifestos, agitated for war, and co-wrote the Fascist Manifesto (1919). (1876–1944)
2. **Boccioni** — the great painter-sculptor and theorist; wrote the painters' Technical Manifesto, made *The City Rises* and *Unique Forms*. Died young in the war he cheered (1916). (1882–1916)
3. **Balla** — the eldest; taught the others Divisionism, then pushed furthest toward abstraction (the dog on a leash, *Abstract Speed*). (1871–1958)
4. **Carrà** — the painter of crowds and politics (*Funeral of the Anarchist Galli*); later left Futurism for Metaphysical painting. (1881–1966)
5. **Severini** — the Paris man; lived among the Cubists, brought Futurism to France, painted the night club and the train. (1883–1966)
6. **Russolo** — the painter who became a composer; *The Art of Noises* (1913) and the intonarumori (the noise-machine instruments he invented). (1885–1947)

(Optional 7th: **Sant'Elia**, 1888–1916, the architect of the *Città Nuova* drawings, killed in action 1916.)

## parallels (2–3 "meanwhile")
1. **1909–1914 · Paris** — *Cubism in full swing.* While Marinetti was shouting, Picasso and Braque were quietly inventing Cubism a few streets away; the 1911 Paris trip is where the two movements collide. (Reference the Cubism read, do not re-tell.)
2. **1909–1914 · Europe** — *The run-up to the Great War.* Futurism's worship of speed and violence lands in the exact years Europe is arming for the First World War (begins August 1914). The Futurists campaigned for Italy to join, then died in it.
3. **1911 · Munich** — *Der Blaue Reiter.* The same years Kandinsky, Marc and Klee push toward spiritual abstraction. Where the Germans sought the inner and spiritual, the Italians worshipped the outer and mechanical, two opposite roads out of the same modern moment.

## lineage
**Parents (gave):**
- **Italian Divisionism** (Previati, Segantini, Pellizza da Volpedo) — gave: separated strokes of pure color, the Futurists' native technique before Cubism.
- **Cubism** (Picasso & Braque) — gave: fractured, faceted planes, grabbed on the 1911 Paris trip and bent to show motion.
- **The machine age** — gave: the subject (car, train, tram, electric light, factory, the modern city) chosen over the nude and the landscape.
- **Marinetti's politics and temperament** — gave: nationalism, the cult of speed and violence, the engine that pointed the movement at war.

**Children (took):**
- **Vorticism** (London, 1914) — took: machine art and the manifesto, *BLAST*.
- **Russian Cubo-Futurism & Constructivism** — took: the machine, the manifesto, the speed-lines.
- **Dada** (Zurich, 1916) — took: the manifesto as weapon, the provocation, the noise.
- **Noise music / sound art** — took: Russolo's *Art of Noises* and intonarumori, a founding moment for experimental music.

## influenceSummary (one italic sentence)
*Futurism grabbed Cubism's fractured planes and Divisionism's broken color, taught painting to show speed and the machine, and made the manifesto-launch the weapon of the avant-garde (art's experimental front edge), so that nearly every later movement that announced itself with a printed manifesto is downstream of Marinetti's Le Figaro stunt.*

**prose (3 paragraphs):**

1. For as long as there had been painting, a picture froze a single instant. A horse, a dancer, a train were each caught in one fixed pose, in one fixed light, the way a photograph stops a clock. Even the most modern pictures, the bright Impressionist boulevards, the wild Fauve colors, the early Cubist still lifes, showed a world that was standing still, however oddly. And the proper subjects of serious art were still the old ones: the nude, the portrait, the landscape, the myth.

2. Look at Balla's dachshund instead. The little dog trots along a sunlit path at the end of a lady's leash, and Balla paints not one dog but the smear of a dog in motion: the four legs multiplied into a blur of maybe twenty, the swinging leash repeated into a fan of arcs, the lady's own feet stuttered into a row of steps. Nothing is frozen. The picture is trying to show the same legs in many positions at once, time itself laid out across the canvas. That is the change in one glance.

3. Two moves stack to make it. First, the Futurists borrowed Cubism's trick of breaking an object into faceted planes, but used those planes to depict speed and dynamism (the energy of a thing in motion) instead of a static studio object; they thought Cubism, for all its cleverness, stood perfectly still. Second, they threw out the old subjects entirely and painted the machine age, the car, the tram, the electric arc-lamp, the construction site, the rioting crowd, the cabaret, as the only fit subjects for modern art. Motion and the machine become the content. (Fall back to *The City Rises* for the "before/after" if the editors would rather make the "new subject = the machine age" point than the "painted motion" one.)

## Chapter 1 — The press release that started a movement
*Eyebrow: The launch · 1909*

### Paris · 20 February 1909 — A movement with no paintings

Most art movements begin in a studio, and you can read the rest of this era to watch them do it: a few painters quietly working out a new way of seeing, then a critic arriving years later to give the thing a name, usually as an insult. Futurism begins the opposite way. It begins on a newspaper's front page, with a man who could not paint at all.

On 20 February 1909 the readers of *Le Figaro*, the most respectable daily in Paris, opened the most respectable position in their paper, the front page, and found a roar. It was a manifesto, a public declaration of beliefs and intentions, the form a political party or a religious sect uses to announce itself to the world. This one announced an art movement. It was titled the *Founding and Manifesto of Futurism*, and it was signed by an Italian poet named Filippo Tommaso Marinetti, who was thirty-two, wealthy, Paris-educated, born in Alexandria in Egypt in 1876, and who had, as yet, not a single painter behind him and not one Futurist painting in existence. He announced the movement first. He went and found the artists afterward.

That order, words before art, is the whole reason Futurism matters as much for its method as for its pictures. Other movements got named after the fact. This one was declared, in print, before the thing it named existed. It is widely called the first movement launched by manifesto, the prototype of the way the twentieth-century avant-garde (the experimental, rule-breaking front edge of art) would announce itself: not by quietly painting but by issuing a printed declaration of war on the old. Be careful with the superlative: it was not the first manifesto ever written, and the text had in fact already appeared in an Italian paper, the *Gazzetta dell'Emilia* in Bologna, on 5 February, two weeks earlier, and as a leaflet before that. But Paris was the capital of the art world, the place a thing had to happen to become world news, and that is exactly why Marinetti spent his money on the front page of a Paris daily. The Bologna printing was the first ink. The *Le Figaro* front page was the launch that mattered.

### What it demanded — speed, the machine, burn the museums

The manifesto comes in two parts, and both are worth knowing. The first is a swaggering prose preamble, a kind of origin myth. In it, Marinetti and his friends sit up all night arguing under hanging lamps, then storm out at dawn and tear off through Milan in their motorcars, drunk on speed. Marinetti, swerving to miss two cyclists, flips his car into a factory drainage ditch and goes nose-down into the muck. He hauls himself out covered in industrial sludge, and instead of cursing his luck he treats the crash as a baptism: the old, careful world is the ditch, and he has just been reborn fast and modern. Then comes the program proper: eleven numbered points, fired off like rounds.

What they demand is a clean break with everything old and a worship of everything fast. The proper subjects of art, the manifesto says, are no longer the nude and the myth and the saint but the things of modern life at full throttle: the car, the tram, the electric arc-lamp, the factory, the railway station, the great machine-made crowd, and above all speed itself, which the manifesto treats as a new kind of beauty. Point four says it most famously: a roaring racing car, it claims, is more beautiful than the *Winged Victory of Samothrace*, the celebrated ancient Greek statue of a winged goddess that stands at the top of the great staircase in the Louvre. A machine, in other words, beats a goddess. And the past has to go: the museums and libraries and academies are graveyards, the manifesto says, and the right response to them is demolition.

It is genuinely thrilling to read, which is part of the trouble, because the same document carries its rot right out in the open.

### The ugly core — "the world's only hygiene"

You cannot quote the manifesto honestly and quietly drop the ninth point. Point nine is where Marinetti writes that the movement wishes to glorify war, which he calls the sole cleanser of the world (the line is more often quoted in the older English translation as "war, the world's only hygiene"), along with militarism, patriotism, and, in the same breath, scorn for women. Point ten adds that the movement means to fight against feminism. This is not a slip of the pen or a young man's pose that the rest of the story walks back. Marinetti meant it. He spent the next years agitating for Italy to go to war, and ten years after the manifesto he helped write the founding document of Italian Fascism, the violent nationalist movement that would put Italy under the dictator Benito Mussolini in 1922, abolish elections, and become the model Adolf Hitler's Nazis followed in Germany. Marinetti served that regime for the rest of his life.

So Futurism is two things at once from its very first sentence. On one side, it is a real and electrifying revolution in art, the movement that taught Western painting how to show motion. On the other, it is the movement that turned art into a recruiting poster for war and, in its founder's case, for fascism. The chapters that follow keep both in view, and the last one has to put them back together. (The broad "art goes modern, the isms multiply" arc is the era read's job, one level up; this read goes after the specifics.)

## Chapter 2 — Finding the painters
*Eyebrow: Recruitment · 1910*

### Milan → Rome — Marinetti gathers a cause its artists

A manifesto with no art is a stunt. To become a movement, Futurism needed people who could actually make things, and within a year Marinetti had them. He gathered a handful of young painters, mostly in Milan, some in Rome, and signed them up to his cause. What Marinetti gave them was not a technique, which he did not have, but a banner, a publicity machine, and permission to be as loud and modern as they wanted.

They were not interchangeable, and it is worth telling them apart, because the rest of the story keeps splitting along their differences. **Umberto Boccioni** (born 1882) was the engine of the group, an intense, theoretical man who argued in print as hard as he painted and who wanted, as he put it, to show that everything moves and runs and turns at top speed; he would push furthest, all the way into sculpture. **Giacomo Balla** (born 1871) was the odd one out by a decade, the eldest, the teacher, a patient experimenter who had taught the younger men their craft and would end up chasing the painted blur further toward pure abstraction than anyone. **Carlo Carrà** (born 1881) was the political one, drawn to crowds, riots and funerals, the painter of the mob. **Gino Severini** (born 1883) was the cosmopolitan, the one who actually lived in Paris and knew everybody. And **Luigi Russolo** (born 1885) was the youngest and the strangest, a painter who would soon get bored of paint and walk off to invent a new kind of music. Five very different men, one borrowed banner.

In 1910 they paid Marinetti back in the only currency the movement traded in: manifestos. On 11 February they issued the *Manifesto of the Futurist Painters*, signed by all five, and in April the *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting* (Milan, 11 April 1910), largely written by Boccioni. The April document is the one that actually says how to paint. It demands the painting of what it calls the dynamic sensation, the felt energy of things in motion, and a "universal dynamism" in which nothing is ever truly at rest. It rejects the nude. It mocks the dark, tarry "bituminous tints" of the old masters. It is, for the first time, an aesthetic program and not just a slogan, the painters working out what the poet's noise was supposed to look like on canvas.

### The Italian starting point — Divisionism

Here is the thing the textbooks often skip: at this moment, in 1910, these men were not yet doing anything that looks like the Futurism you have seen. They were not Cubists, they had not yet been to Paris, and their canvases shimmer with an entirely Italian technique called **Divisionism**.

Divisionism is the Italian cousin of the French pointillism of Georges Seurat: instead of mixing colors on the palette and laying down a smooth field, you paint in many small separated dots and strokes of pure unmixed color and let the viewer's eye blend them, which makes the surface vibrate with a peculiar inner light. The Italians had their own line of it through the painters Gaetano Previati, Giovanni Segantini, and Pellizza da Volpedo, and Balla had learned the method and taught it to the younger men. So early Futurist pictures shimmer with little broken strokes of bright color before they ever learn to fracture into planes. The light comes first. The motion comes later.

There was a second pull on them too, this one from a machine. In the years just before Futurism, the photographers Étienne-Jules Marey in France and Eadweard Muybridge in America had been making what are called chronophotographs, sequences that froze a galloping horse or a running man into a row of separate instants on one plate, the body caught in a dozen positions at once. Those motion studies were famous and widely reproduced, and they prefigure the most recognizable Futurist trick of all, the multiplied limb, the single body shown in many positions across one image. The camera had already chopped motion into frames. The Futurists would paint the frames back into a blur.

So by 1910 the painters had their shimmering color and a head full of motion studies, but they still had no way to make a canvas actually move. That tool was about to be handed to them, by their rivals, on a train trip to Paris.

## Chapter 3 — Paris, 1911: borrowing the broken plane
*Eyebrow: The Cubist debt · 1911–1912*

### Severini's invitation — the trip that changed the look

The single most important journey in Futurist history is a train ride to Paris in the autumn of 1911. **Severini** was the group's man in France; he actually lived in Paris, among the painters, and he understood that his Milan friends, for all their noise, had not yet seen the most advanced painting in Europe. So he brought **Boccioni**, **Carrà**, and **Russolo** to the city and introduced them to Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque, the two painters whose work the Cubism read covers in full, and to **Cubism**.

The encounter rewired them. Cubism, which the Cubism read covers in full, had found a way to break an object into faceted, overlapping planes and show it from several angles at once, dissolving the old illusion that a painting was a window onto a single fixed view. The Futurists looked at that fractured surface and saw exactly the tool they had been missing. Several of them went home and reworked canvases they had already started, breaking their shimmering Divisionist surfaces into hard Cubist planes. The look of mature Futurism, the thing you picture when you hear the word, is born in the months after this trip.

[FIGURE: Braque, The Portuguese, 1911 (cross-reference, from the Cubism read) | This is the tool the Futurists took to Paris to borrow: a Cubist canvas broken into faceted brown and grey planes, the object turned and studied from several angles at once. What the Italians added was time, the planes set in motion. (Image already in the app's set for the Cubism read; no new rights clearance needed.) | Braque, *The Portuguese*, 1911 · Kunstmuseum Basel]

### "Cubism stands still" — the one big difference

But they did not simply copy. The Futurists picked a fight with their own teachers, and the fight is the key to the whole movement. Cubism, they complained, was motionless: it took its broken planes and aimed them at a guitar, a bottle, a newspaper, a person sitting still in a studio, a static object turned and studied. The Futurists wanted to aim the same broken planes at the opposite target. They wanted to use fracture not to anatomize a still thing but to depict **motion**, the rush and blur of a thing moving through space and time. Same tool, opposite job. The way the Futurists told it, with the swagger they told everything, Cubism had invented a marvelous machine and then wasted it painting a fruit bowl.

So the debt is real and it is large, the biggest single visual debt Futurism owes anyone, and the read should say so plainly rather than pretend the Italians invented the fractured plane. What the Italians added was time. They took Cubism's still, faceted space and set it spinning.

### Bernheim-Jeune, February 1912 — Futurism goes international

They announced the result with another piece of theater. In February 1912 the *First Exhibition of Futurist Painting* opened at the **Bernheim-Jeune gallery in Paris**, one of the city's leading dealers in modern art, then toured to London, Berlin, Brussels and beyond. It put Futurism in front of the whole international avant-garde at once, and it provoked the obvious charge, which was half fair: that the Italians were just Cubist imitators who had arrived late and turned up the volume. The Futurists' answer was the same one they always gave. Yes, we took your planes. No, we are not doing your painting. You froze the world. We are going to make it move.

The tour did real work. The London leg, in particular, is where Futurism lit a fuse under the English avant-garde: a young painter named Wyndham Lewis saw the show and the noise around it, and within two years he had launched his own hard-edged, machine-worshipping British movement, Vorticism, with a magazine called *BLAST* (1914). It is the clearest case of Futurism's most durable export, which was never really a style but a method, the manifesto, the scandal, the touring provocation, copied movement by movement across Europe.

## Chapter 4 — Painting speed
*Eyebrow: The masterpieces · 1910–1914*

### The machine city — Boccioni's "The City Rises"

Start with the building site. Boccioni's *The City Rises* (1910) is his first big Futurist canvas, almost ten feet wide, and it shows the modern city under construction, which is to say it shows the city literally rising. In the foreground a huge **red dray-horse**, an enormous working draft animal, strains and rears, and around it men haul and heave, and the whole surface, horse and men and scaffolding and dust, dissolves into one churning, swirling surge of labor and energy. You do not read it as a horse and some workers. You read it as force. The subject is not a scene; the subject is the energy of a city being built.

### Emotion as motion — the "States of Mind" triptych

Then the railway station. Boccioni's *States of Mind* (1911) is a set of three panels about a parting at a train station, and it tries to do something almost impossible: to paint feelings as movement. Stand in front of the first panel, *The Farewells*, and you are inside the goodbye: a steam-choked platform, embracing couples half-dissolved in the churn, and floating through the fog a giant locomotive number stenciled on the side of the engine, a banal stamped numeral turned into the one solid thing in a swimming world. The second panel, *Those Who Go*, drives the departing passengers into hard slanting streaks, faces and window frames smeared sideways by the speed of the carriage, the visual feel of being pulled away. The third, *Those Who Stay*, drops into long, sagging verticals, like a downpour of grief, the drooping posture of the people left standing on the platform. Three kinds of motion for three states of feeling. Emotion painted as direction.

### The textbook of motion — Balla's dog

Now the famous one, and the easiest to read on a phone. Balla's *Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash* (1912), painted while he was staying with a student out in the Tuscan countryside, shows a little dachshund being walked along a sunlit path, and it is the single clearest demonstration of what painted motion even means. Balla does not paint one dog; he paints the blur of a trotting dog, the four short legs smeared into a fan of maybe twenty, the swinging leash repeated into a stack of arcs, the lady's walking feet stuttered into a little row of steps. It is what your eye actually does when something moves too fast to fix, multiplied and laid out on the canvas. A camera could freeze that dog in one crisp instant; what a camera cannot do, and what Balla is after, is to hold all the instants at once, the same legs in a dozen places in a single still image. No glossing needed: you simply see the dog moving. If you only remember one Futurist image, this is the one to remember, because it makes the whole idea legible in a single glance.

### The riot — Carrà's "Funeral of the Anarchist Galli"

If Boccioni took the machine city and Balla took the trotting dog, **Carrà** took the mob. His *Funeral of the Anarchist Galli* (1910–11) paints a real event he had witnessed: the funeral of Angelo Galli, an anarchist worker, which the police tried to break up, so that the mourners' procession turned into a brawl. Carrà gives you the clash itself, a storm of red flags and black-clad bodies and raised poles, everything tilted and slashing, the violence rendered as so many lines of force crossing and colliding. It is the third of the Futurists' chosen modern subjects, alongside labor and travel: political violence in the street, painted with the same churning energy as the building site.

### The line of force — the cyclist

Push the idea further and the subject starts to disappear into pure energy. Boccioni's *Dynamism of a Cyclist* (1913) takes a man on a bicycle and abstracts him almost out of existence. There is no recognizable face, no clear wheel, barely a bicycle: just a tight knot of slashing blue and ochre wedges leaning hard to one side, the whole body, the machine and the rushing air fused into one diagonal streak of effort. What you are looking at is a picture made almost entirely of what the Futurists called **lines of force**, the imaginary lines along which a moving thing's energy seems to travel and bend the space around it. The man has become his own speed. By the time Balla paints *Abstract Speed + Sound* (1913–14), even the cyclist's body is gone; there is no car and no rider left at all, just the swoosh of speed and the wedge of noise it leaves behind, Futurism arriving at near-total abstraction.

### Sculpture as wind made solid — "Unique Forms of Continuity in Space"

And then the single most famous Futurist object, which is not a painting at all. Boccioni, never content to stay in one medium, had written a *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Sculpture* in 1912, arguing that statues too should be opened up and set in motion instead of standing there like polished idols, and *Unique Forms of Continuity in Space* (1913) is that argument made solid. It is a striding human figure remade as flame and wind made solid, the muscles peeled back and pulled out into wings and ridges of motion, as if the figure were leaving streaks of its own movement in the air as it walked. Italy put it on the twenty-cent euro coin. One honest footnote that matters for a careful reader and matters even more for the people choosing which photo to show: Boccioni made the work in 1913, but he made it in **plaster**, and that original plaster survives in São Paulo, Brazil. It was never cast in bronze in his lifetime. Every famous bronze you have seen, the one at the Museum of Modern Art and the others, is a later, posthumous cast, the earliest from 1931, fifteen years after Boccioni was dead. So when people say "Boccioni's 1913 bronze," they are using shorthand: the sculpture is 1913, the bronze is posthumous.

## Chapter 5 — A total program, and a woman answers back
*Eyebrow: The total program · 1912–1914*

### Not just painting — a way of living

Futurism never stayed inside the picture frame, because it was never really about painting alone. Marinetti's idea was a total program, a wholesale remaking of modern life, and so the manifestos kept coming, for sculpture, for photography, for theatre, for cooking, even for clothing. The point was to drag every corner of life into the machine age.

It even had a live arm. The Futurists staged what they called *serate*, "evenings," rowdy stage events at theatres in Milan, Turin, Rome and beyond. A serata was not a performance so much as a scripted ambush: the Futurists would take the stage in front of a paying crowd, read incendiary manifestos and free-word poems, insult the audience and the city's most sacred local heroes, and wait for the room to turn. It usually did, into jeering, then thrown vegetables and coins, then outright brawls and the occasional arrest. That was the goal; the riot was the art. The serata was Futurism's most direct invention, the artistic event staged to provoke, and it became the template for the noisy public provocations of Dada a few years later. Several of the spin-offs below also began as things shouted from a serata stage.

### The noise machines — Russolo and "The Art of Noises"

The first is sound. **Russolo**, who had started as a painter (his *Dynamism of an Automobile*, 1912–13, drives a car into a wedge of overlapping blue arcs, pure speed turned into geometry), put down his brushes and effectively invented noise music. In 1913 he wrote a manifesto called *The Art of Noises*, which argued that the modern ear, raised on factories and engines and trams, was bored by the polite sounds of the orchestra and craved the actual noise of the machine age. Then he built the instruments to make it. He called them **intonarumori**, which means "noise intoners," a family of boxes with cranks and levers and horns that produced rumbles, hisses, roars and shrieks on command, a machine-age orchestra of pure noise. It sounds like a joke and it was a foundation: Russolo's noise machines are one of the starting points of twentieth-century experimental and electronic music.

### The machine city — Sant'Elia's "New City"

The second is architecture, and the drawings are extraordinary objects in their own right. **Antonio Sant'Elia**, a young architect, drew a visionary metropolis he called the *Città Nuova*, the "New City," and wrote a *Manifesto of Futurist Architecture* (1914) to go with it. Look at one of the *Città Nuova* sheets and you see a single colossal building drawn at a steep upward angle, like a tower photographed from the gutter: a stepped, set-back power station or apartment block, sheer and windowed and many storeys tall, its banks of elevators run up the outside of the wall in glass and steel shafts instead of being hidden inside, and below it the streets, the tramlines and the railways stacked in open layers one above another, traffic threaded through the base of the building. No columns, no domes, no ornament, no nod to a single thing built before. It is a city designed for speed and electricity rather than for monuments, and it looks like nothing that existed in 1914. Almost none of it was built, and Sant'Elia did not live to build any of it, but the drawings shaped how the whole twentieth century pictured the city of the future.

### Words gone wild — Marinetti and "parole in libertà"

Marinetti, for his own part, kept writing, but he attacked the page itself. He pushed a style he called *parole in libertà*, "words in freedom": poems with the grammar torn out, the words scattered across the page at different sizes and angles and typefaces, sometimes mixed with mathematical signs and onomatopoeia, the layout doing the work that sentences used to do. His showpiece was *Zang Tumb Tumb* (1914), a book-length sound-poem about a battle he had reported on in the Balkans, in which the title itself, *zang tumb tumb*, is the noise of the artillery, and the typography on the page lurches and explodes to mime the bombardment. It reads less like a poem than like a page trying to be an engine. This is the same instinct that ran through the noise machines and the serate: language, like everything else, was to be retooled for the machine age.

### A woman answers back — Valentine de Saint-Point

Now the part the movement's admirers tend to skip. The manifesto's scorn for women was not an accident, and it did not go unanswered. In 1912 a French writer and performer named **Valentine de Saint-Point** published a *Manifesto of the Futurist Woman*, and she pointedly subtitled it "An Answer to F.T. Marinetti." What makes her interesting is that she did not simply scold him. She out-radicalized him: she rejected the sentimental, soft, muse-like idea of woman just as fiercely as he did, and demanded a fierce, strong, even violent womanhood to match the violent age, and the next year she pushed further still with a scandalous *Futurist Manifesto of Lust* (1913). She took Futurism's own logic and turned it back on its misogyny.

Marinetti himself later offered a defense, which is worth reporting precisely so it can be measured. He claimed that "scorn for woman" had been aimed at the old sentimental-muse cliché, the soft romantic ideal of woman, and not at actual women. That is his after-the-fact gloss, his own spin, and it does not survive contact with point ten of the founding manifesto, which calls in plain words to "fight against feminism." You do not get to scorn the cliché and outlaw the movement for women's rights in the same breath and then say you only meant the cliché. And the movement leaned on women even as it scorned them in print: Benedetta Cappa, a painter who would become one of the leading figures of Futurism's aerial-painting second phase, was a serious artist in her own right and was also Marinetti's wife. Futurism scorned women on the page and depended on them in fact.

## Chapter 6 — The war it wanted
*Eyebrow: The reckoning · 1914–1944*

### Interventionism — begging for the war

Point nine was not a metaphor. When the First World War broke out in the summer of 1914, the Futurists did exactly what the manifesto had promised: they campaigned, loudly, for Italy to abandon its neutrality and join the fighting. This is the movement called **interventionism**, the push to get Italy into the war, and the Futurists threw their whole publicity machine behind it. Carrà made the case in paint with his *Interventionist Demonstration* (1914), a spinning collage of torn newspaper, slogans and shouting words (the Futurists called this words-in-freedom mode *parole in libertà*, "free words," text scattered and sized for noise rather than sense), a picture that is literally war propaganda made of headlines. Many Futurists did not just agitate. They volunteered.

### 1916 — the war kills the movement

And then the war they had begged for destroyed them. In 1916 it killed two of the most important men in the movement in a single year.

**Boccioni**, the best painter and the sharpest mind Futurism had, died on 17 August 1916, near Verona, and the manner of it is a cruelty almost too neat to believe. He had volunteered for the war he had spent years demanding. He was not killed in combat. He was on a cavalry exercise, a training ride, when his horse bolted; he was thrown and trampled, and he died the next day, aged thirty-three. The "died gloriously in battle" version that sometimes attaches to him is simply wrong, and the truth is worse: a training accident, not a heroic charge. And one detail makes it unbearable. He had named the horse Vermiglia, after the red dray-horse churning up the building site in *The City Rises*, the painting from Chapter 4. The red horse that announced the movement threw and killed the man who painted it.

The same year, **Sant'Elia**, the architect of the New City, was killed in action at Monte Ermada. The heroic group did not survive the peace. Carrà drifted away into a still, dreamlike, classicizing manner called Metaphysical painting; Severini moved toward a calmer, more classical Cubism; the first and greatest phase of Futurism, the painters' Futurism, was effectively over by 1918, gutted by the very catastrophe it had cheered on.

### The rot — Marinetti and fascism

That is the tragedy. Here is the disgrace, and the read does not get to skip it. Marinetti did not stop. In 1919, with Alceste De Ambris, he co-wrote the **Fascist Manifesto**, the founding platform of Benito Mussolini's new movement, the *Fasci Italiani di Combattimento*, published in Mussolini's own paper on 6 June 1919. Marinetti had been there at the founding meeting in Milan that March. He was a genuine, founding-era fascist, and Futurism's whole worldview, its romance with violence, with the machine, with national rebirth and the cleansing power of war, fed straight into the aesthetics of the regime. In the 1920s and 1930s he rebuilt a "second Futurism," chiefly a style called *aeropittura*, aerial painting: pictures made from the dizzying viewpoint of an airplane, the ground tilting and rushing up at the viewer, the cockpit and the propeller and the diving horizon all swept into one vertiginous swoop, a whole genre built to glorify flight and the air force. Marinetti called it the daughter of fascist aviation and Italian Futurism, which tells you exactly whom it was painted for, and he bound the whole enterprise ever tighter to Mussolini. He took a seat in the Royal Academy, the regime's official body of honored intellectuals, in 1929. He died in 1944 still serving the regime.

Hold the contradiction, because flattening it in either direction is a lie. Do not pretend Marinetti was anything other than a fascist who helped build fascism; the framing is not optional and the man earned it. But do not run the disgrace backward over everything, either. Not every Futurist was a fascist; some of them had been anarchists or socialists, and some drifted away from both the movement and the politics. The masterpieces of Chapter 4, the churning city and the blurred dog and the striding bronze, were painted between 1910 and 1913, years before the Fascist party existed. Even Marinetti's own arc has a complication worth keeping: in 1920 he actually walked out on the fascists, calling them reactionary, before reconciling and serving them for the rest of his life. The dog on the leash is not a fascist object, and saying so is not laundering anything. It is just refusing to let the founder's later crimes erase what the painters actually did.

So the verdict is double and it has to stay double. Futurism taught Western art to show time and motion, the blur, the multiplied limb, the line of force, and it made the manifesto the avant-garde's weapon, art launched by media campaign, by scandal and touring provocation, so that Dada and Surrealism and Constructivism and Vorticism, every later movement that announced itself with a printed program, are downstream of one poet's stunt on the front page of *Le Figaro*. And it is the movement that made art a recruiting poster for war, and whose founder helped write fascism into being. Both of those are true at the same time, and the reason Futurism is worth this much trouble is that you are not allowed to keep only the half you like.
