## STORYTELLING

[NICE] **The opening drop into HatSalon is excellent** — "thirty-five years old, broke,
the father of three, and not remotely famous" is exactly the *L'Absinthe* cold-open
register (cf. "Degas, who was forty-one and prickly and rich"). No notes; keep it.

[FIX] **One meta-narration tic to scrub — the recurring "the picture we are about to
look at."** It appears in HatSalon:
> "Matisse decided to hang the picture we are about to look at, a portrait of his wife."
and again
> "He showed it anyway."
The *L'Absinthe* reference uses "the picture we're about to look at" too, so it is not
banned house-wide — but the project voice memo (`feedback_no_meta_narration`) is explicit:
"tell the story, never narrate what the chapter is about / how it links." One instance is
fine as a hinge; here it is the *only* forward-reference and reads slightly tour-guide.
Suggested fix: "Matisse decided to hang a portrait of his wife." Let the next section
do the looking without the pre-announcement.

[FIX] **HatScandal paragraph 4 leans on a borrowed phrase instead of showing.**
> "What undid them was precisely the thing we have been looking at: colors that did not
> correspond to anything they could see, smeared across a human face. A green nose, a
> mauve cheek, no shadow where shadow belonged."
This is good, but "the thing we have been looking at" is a back-reference that does the
work a concrete re-image would do better. The *L'Absinthe* scandal section never says
"the thing we looked at"; it re-states the visual fact fresh. Suggested fix: drop "the
thing we have been looking at" and lead straight with the image — "What undid them was
the green nose, the mauve cheek, the shadow painted the wrong color — paint that refused
to match anything a viewer could see."

[NICE] **The Vauxcelles Cubism aside ("He had a real talent for it") is perfect dry wit**
and is the kind of illuminating cross-reference the house voice prizes. Keep verbatim.

[FIX] **HatAfterlife paragraph 4 ("Whatever the figure, the sale mattered enormously")
is the one passage that drifts toward flat summary.**
> "They would go on to buy Matisse and Picasso by the wall-full, and their
> Saturday-evening apartment became the room where the future of painting got introduced
> to itself."
The second clause is vivid and earns its place; the first clause and the topic-sentence
"the sale mattered enormously" are encyclopedia connective tissue. Not a blocker, but
tighten: cut "the sale mattered enormously" (telling, not showing) and open on the
concrete — "The sale put money in the pocket of a demoralized painter at the lowest
moment of his career, and it launched the Steins as collectors."

[NICE] **The price-dispute paragraph (HatAfterlife) handles a messy fact beautifully** —
"Each party, you will notice, remembered the haggle in the way that flattered them" turns
a provenance footnote into a human beat. Exactly the fact-pack flag handled as story.

## LOOKING

[NICE] **HatLooking is the best section in the draft and needs no structural change.**
The "step back / now walk into the face / lift your eye to the hat / drop back down to
her hands / finally notice the background" choreography is precisely guided looking, and
every claim is *located* ("a vertical streak along the bridge," "just under the hat brim,"
"the top half of the canvas"). The green-nose / green-forehead / NOT-the-Green-Stripe
distinction is made by making the reader SEE both, which is the right way to disarm the
single most-confused fact. This is the gold standard for the axis.

[FIX] **The brushwork is under-seen relative to the color.** The brief asks specifically
for the reader to see "the brushwork." The draft tells us the paint is "loose, visible
strokes" (HatMaking) and that there is "bare or thinly painted canvas showing through"
(HatLooking) — good — but unlike *L'Absinthe* ("painted in dense strokes," "the whole
picture is a sketch held to the size of a finished painting"), it never makes the *handling*
itself visible in the looking section. The face is described as color-patches but not as
*paint laid down* — no sense of speed, thickness, or where strokes are dragged vs. dabbed.
Suggested fix: in the face paragraph add one concrete brushwork beat, e.g. "and you can
see the speed of it — the strokes are dragged on wet and left, not blended, so the cheek
is a few swipes of color sitting side by side rather than a modeled surface." This both
serves the LOOKING brief and reinforces the "finished-in-a-rush" thread from HatMaking.

[NICE] **The fan is genuinely seen** — "thick splashes of white and peach flecked with
violet and green" is concrete and located. The brief flags the fan specifically; it is
handled.

[FIX] **Eye-line / gaze is missing from the looking pass.** HatLooking opens "turned
slightly, looking out at you" — but the figure's gaze, expression, what her face is
*doing* emotionally, never returns. *L'Absinthe*'s looking section spends a whole beat on
Andrée's "stare into the middle distance." For *Woman with a Hat* the face is the
battleground, so a single sentence on what the eyes/mouth read as (composed? blank?
masklike under the color?) would deepen the looking without adding art-history. Optional
but recommended. Suggested fix: after the face-as-color paragraph, one line — "For all
the violence of the color, the face itself is composed, even serene: she looks steadily
out, mouth set, as if entirely unbothered that she has been painted in green."

## CLARITY

[NICE] **Arbitrary color vs. local color is glossed exemplarily** (HatMaking) — defined,
contrasted, and motivated ("For four centuries European painters had treated local color
as the floor under their feet"). Modeling is glossed twice (annotation + HatLooking),
correctly, since it recurs across sections that may be read out of order.

[FIX] **"bourgeois" appears unglossed.** HatLooking:
> "This is a half-length portrait of a fashionable bourgeois woman"
A reader with zero prior knowledge may not have the word. It is glossed-by-context two
sentences later ("the kind a banker might commission of his wife"), which mostly covers
it, but the term lands before the gloss. Minor. Suggested fix: "a fashionable
middle-class (bourgeois) woman" — or simply "fashionable well-to-do woman" and drop the
French.

[FIX] **"milliner" gloss placement.** HatMaking glosses it correctly —
> "a milliner (a maker and seller of women's hats)"
— but the section's own *title* ("The woman who made hats, painted with one") and the
HatMaking blurb use the concept before the body gloss. That is fine for the title (it
self-glosses by paraphrase). No change needed; noting it was checked.

[NICE] **All French is pronounced AND translated on first use** — Collioure ("koll-YOOR"),
Salon d'Automne ("sa-LOHN doh-TUN," "the Autumn Salon"), Vauxcelles ("voh-SELL"),
"Donatello chez les fauves" / "les fauves" / "la cage aux fauves" all translated inline.
This matches the *L'Absinthe* standard exactly.

[FIX] **"Donatello was a great Renaissance sculptor" — glossed, good, but "Renaissance"
itself is undefined.** HatScandal:
> "(Donatello was a great Renaissance sculptor; the gentle bust stood in for him.)"
For a true zero-knowledge reader "Renaissance" is jargon. The era is named elsewhere
("a polite Renaissance manner") also unglossed. Low severity — Renaissance is closer to
general knowledge than "louche" — but the house bar is zero prior knowledge. Suggested
fix (light touch): "Donatello was a celebrated sculptor of the Italian Renaissance, the
15th-century rebirth of classical art." One clause, once, covers both uses.

[NICE] **"engraver" / printmaking is NOT a problem here** (that gloss lives in the
*L'Absinthe* file, not this one) — checked because the figure-list mentions a sculptor
and a critic, both glossed in-body ("an unrelated sculptor," "writing in the newspaper
*Gil Blas*"). No unglossed roles.

## CROSS-CHECKS (fact-flag fidelity, since these are reader-facing surfaces too)

Stripe* (Copenhagen). This is the single biggest factual trap on this painting and the
draft disarms it without overloading the reader. Consistent "siblings, not twins" framing.

[NICE] **Disputed price** given as a range with both partisan accounts, never a settled
number — matches the flag. **"Black, of course"** correctly framed as Purrmann's reported
studio anecdote ("hold it loosely"). **"First Fauve painting"** correctly refused in favor
of "the face of the scandal." All fact-pack flags honored in the prose, not just the ledger.

header dateLabel and stats use ′ ″; body prose uses "two and a half feet").
