## 2. THE STORY — FROTTAGE

**Frottage** (French *frotter*, "to rub") is Ernst's signature technique: lay a sheet of paper over a **grainy, textured surface** — weathered floorboards, wood grain, leaves, sacking, a breadcrust — and **rub a soft pencil (graphite / "black lead") across it** so the surface's texture surfaces as a pattern on the paper. Ernst then **read images into the resulting marks** — forests, leaves, eyes, feathers, beasts, the sea, the rain — and worked them up into the "creatures" of a fictional natural history. The artist is not inventing the forms; he is **reading them out of chance texture**.

**The discovery (verify — confirmed):** On **10 August 1925**, in a **seaside inn at Pornic** (Atlantic coast of France), on a **rainy evening**, Ernst was transfixed by the **scrubbed, deeply grooved wooden floorboards** of his room. He laid paper on them and rubbed — and the rubbings, in his account, "lost the character of the wood and assumed the aspect of unbelievably clear images." He linked it to a childhood obsession with a panel of **false mahogany** by his bed that had triggered half-sleep visions. He then extended the method beyond floorboards to any textured surface, and assembled a selection of the resulting drawings as *Histoire Naturelle*.

**The "natural history" conceit:** the portfolio presents the frottages as the plates of a mock scientific natural-history atlas — leaves, forests, feathers, eyes, insects, landscapes, and invented organisms with deadpan taxonomic-sounding titles (e.g. *The Habit of Leaves / Les Moeurs des feuilles*, *Little Tables around the Earth*, *Fields of Honor, Floods, Seismic Plants*, *The Wheel of Light*, *The Fugitive*, *The Idol*). The wood grain becomes nature itself.

**Frottage as the visual cousin of automatism:** by letting chance texture supply the image and the eye supply the reading, Ernst made a *visual* analogue of **Surrealist automatism** — the automatic writing André Breton's group used to bypass conscious control. Ernst's own phrase for what frottage does is intensifying the artist's "hallucinatory faculties."

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required — before / after)

**Before:** A drawing or print is something the artist **invents and executes** — the marks originate in the hand and the will. Even Dada collage, Ernst's own prior mode, still *assembles chosen* fragments. The image-source is the artist.

**After (the break):** In frottage / *Histoire Naturelle*, the **image-source is chance texture in the world**, and the artist's job shifts to **reading accident, not inventing form**. Ernst recast himself from author to **"spectator"** of his own pictures — the marks come from the floorboards; the picture comes from what his eye finds in them. This:
- makes **chance + found texture** a legitimate generative engine for fine art (a line that runs forward to grattage, décalcomania, and later automatist abstraction);
- gives Surrealism a **repeatable visual method** for tapping the unconscious, parallel to automatic writing — the bridge from Breton's literary automatism into a graphic practice;
- and reframes "drawing skill": there is **no conventional draftsmanship** on the page — no modeling, no observed line — yet legible creatures and landscapes appear. The accident does the drawing.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — the manifesto-analog)

Ernst's own account of the discovery, from **"Beyond Painting" / "Au-delà de la peinture"** (essay first published 1936 in *Cahiers d'Art*; English in *Beyond Painting, and Other Writings*, ed. Robert Motherwell, New York: Wittenborn, Schultz, 1948):

> "Beginning with a memory of childhood … in the course of which a panel of false mahogany, situated in front of my bed, had played the role of optical provocateur of a vision of half-sleep, and finding myself one rainy evening in a seaside inn, I was struck by the obsession that showed to my excited gaze the floor-boards upon which a thousand scrubbings had deepened the grooves. I decided then to investigate the symbolism of this obsession, and, in order to aid my meditative and hallucinatory faculties, I made from the boards a series of drawings by placing on them, at random, sheets of paper which I undertook to rub with black lead."

And on the result:

> "The drawings thus obtained steadily lost … the character of the material being studied — wood — and assumed the aspect of unbelievably clear images."

- The date **10 August 1925** is Ernst's own and is repeated by MoMA, Moderna Museet, Britannica, et al. — safe to assert.
- The inn was at **Pornic** — corroborated by Moderna Museet and Britannica. (Some older texts say only "a seaside inn"; "Pornic" is the standard identification.)

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6 — prose pointers, no coordinates)

*(Written generically because no single plate is wired; if a plate is later chosen, re-point to what is actually visible in it.)*

1. **The wood grain is right there on the surface.** What reads as ridged hills, striated forests, or feathered fans is the literal rubbing of grained wood — follow the parallel striations and you are looking at floorboard, not landscape.
2. **No conventional drawing.** There is no outline, no shading-by-hand, no observed contour — the forms are built entirely from rubbed texture and then nudged into legibility. The "drawing" is found, not laid down.
3. **The natural-history motifs.** Look for the recurring vocabulary of the portfolio: **leaves and stalks, feathers, single staring eyes, forests, the horizon line of sea-and-sky**, and invented beast-or-plant organisms — all conjured from grain.
4. **Figure-from-ground ambiguity.** The eye flips between "this is texture" and "this is a creature." That oscillation — making you do the seeing — is the point; it is the visual equivalent of reading shapes in clouds.
5. **The deadpan caption does work.** The mock-scientific title (e.g. *The Habit of Leaves*, *Seismic Plants*) tells you to read the marks as a specimen in an atlas; the humour and the uncanny both live in that gap between taxonomy and accident.
6. **It's a reproduction, and it shows.** As a **collotype** the plate has the even, slightly velvety tonality of a photomechanical print, not the bite of an intaglio line or the tooth of an original graphite rubbing — a reminder that *Histoire Naturelle* publishes the frottages, it is not the rubbings.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct, with sources)

1. **Frottage ≠ grattage.** **Frottage** (1925) = *rubbing* a pencil over paper laid on a textured surface (a drawing technique; the basis of *Histoire Naturelle*). **Grattage** (developed 1927) = *scraping* wet paint off a canvas laid over a textured object with a palette knife (a *painting* technique — the oil-paint cousin of frottage). *Histoire Naturelle* is **frottage**, not grattage. Don't blur them. *(Moderna Museet; Britannica.)*
2. **Discovery date is 10 August 1925** (Pornic seaside inn) — Ernst's own date. The portfolio is **1926**. Frottages 1925 / portfolio 1926 — keep the two dates distinct; don't collapse to "1925" or "1926" alone.
3. **The plates are reproductions, not the rubbings.** *Histoire Naturelle* is a **collotype (phototypie) portfolio** that *reproduces* Ernst's frottage drawings. The original frottages are separate works; the 34 plates are photomechanical prints. Don't call the portfolio "34 frottages" as if the sheets were the rubbings — they are reproductions *after* frottage. *(MoMA: "portfolio of thirty-four collotypes after frottage.")*
4. **Plate count = 34** (numbered I–XXXIV). Verified across MoMA, the gallery, and auction records. Don't write 33 or 36.
5. **Wrong-work image trap.** Searching "Histoire naturelle" surfaces **18th-century Buffon-era natural-history book engravings** (e.g. *Histoire naturelle des oiseaux*, 1772) on Commons — **unrelated** to Ernst. Also: *The Blue Forest* and *The Forest* are Ernst **frottage-derived paintings**, not *Histoire Naturelle* plates — don't substitute them for a plate.
6. **"Lithographs" mislabel.** Some secondary sources loosely call the plates "lithographs." The correct medium is **collotype / phototypie**. Use collotype.
7. **Arp wrote the preface** (Hans/Jean Arp), not Breton. Don't attribute the preface to Breton or to Éluard.
