## 2. THE STORY

Pollock made *Autumn Rhythm* in his East Hampton (Springs, Long Island) studio
in 1950, at the peak of his "drip"/"pour" period (roughly 1947–1950). He
**unrolled a length of raw, unprimed canvas onto the studio floor** and worked
over it from **all four sides**, never stretching or priming it first.

He did **not** touch brush to canvas in the conventional sense. He poured and
flung liquid **commercial enamel (house paint)** straight from the can, and
trailed it off **sticks, dried/hardened brushes, basting syringes, and trowels**,
letting the fluid paint fall, drip, pool, and spatter onto the surface. The paint
records the **motion of his whole body** moving around and over the canvas —
walking, crouching, flicking, dripping — rather than the motion of a hand at an
easel. There is **no depicted subject, no figure, no central image**: just an
**allover web** of black, white, brown, and tan skeins laid in rhythmic, looping
layers across the entire surface, edge to edge.

The work was made over **several months in 1950** and partly documented by
photographer **Hans Namuth**, who shot **500+ photographs** plus **two films**
(a black-and-white film and the famous color film *Jackson Pollock 51*, shot
through a sheet of glass from below) of Pollock at work that year. Namuth's
photographs let scholars reconstruct the **sequence** of *Autumn Rhythm*: per art
historian Monica Bohm-Duchen, Pollock began at the **right third**, laying down a
skein of thin black lines, then worked methodically across.

**The title came later.** Pollock exhibited it as ***Number 30*** at the Betty
Parsons Gallery (Nov.–Dec. 1950) and at MoMA's *15 Americans* (1952). He
numbered rather than named his pictures from 1947 so titles wouldn't impose
meaning. Only by the **Sidney Janis Gallery show (1955)** did it carry the title
***Autumn Rhythm*** (art historian T.J. Clark argues the lyrical title was
Pollock's own choice). [Flag: exact circumstances of the retitling are
attributed to scholars, not a Pollock document — say "later retitled," not
"Pollock titled it in 1955."]

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (REQUIRED — before / after)

**Before:** Western painting since the Renaissance was built on a window — a
**figure/ground** relationship with a **focal point** and a hierarchy of
important vs. background areas, where paint described *something* (a body, a
landscape, an object) sitting in illusionistic space. Even most earlier
abstraction kept composed centers, edges, and a sense of "depicted" form.

**After:** *Autumn Rhythm* dissolves all of that:

- **Allover composition, no focal point.** Every square inch carries roughly
  equal weight; there is no center, no top-bottom or near-far hierarchy. The eye
  has nowhere to "land" and nowhere to rest. The composition has no climax.
- **No figure/ground.** Foreground and background collapse into one continuous
  woven surface. Nothing sits "in front of" anything else.
- **No depicted object.** The picture is not *of* anything. There is no subject
  to read — the paint refers only to itself and to the act that made it.
- **The act of painting becomes the subject.** The canvas is, in Harold
  Rosenberg's later phrase, "an arena in which to act." The painting is the
  **record of an event** — the body's movement through time and space — rather
  than an image of a thing.
- **Paint as gesture / trace of the body.** Each line is the frozen track of a
  pour, a fling, a step. The work is, in effect, the physical residue of Pollock
  moving around the canvas: painting as recorded performance.

That combination — allover field + no figure/ground + the painted *act* as the
content — is the break, and it became foundational for postwar American
abstraction (field painting, and later Minimalism's "no relational composition").

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — flag variants)

**Primary, ship this one** — from Pollock's statement "My Painting,"
*Possibilities I*, no. 1 (Winter 1947–48), p. 79:

> "My painting does not come from the easel. I hardly ever stretch my canvas
> before painting. I prefer to tack the unstretched canvas to the hard wall or
> the floor… **On the floor I am more at ease. I feel nearer, more a part of the
> painting, since this way I can walk around it, work from the four sides and
> literally be in the painting.**"

Continuation of the same statement (also *Possibilities*, 1947–48):

> "When I am *in* my painting, I'm not aware of what I'm doing… the painting has
> a life of its own. I try to let it come through. It is only when I lose contact
> with the painting that the result is a mess. Otherwise there is pure harmony,
> an easy give and take, and the painting comes out well."

**The "no accident" line** — from the **William Wright interview, summer 1950**
(recorded for a Sag Harbor radio station; widely reprinted). Exact wording
varies by source; the most-quoted compressed form is:

> "I can control the flow of paint: there is no accident."

The fuller transcript reads: "With experience it seems to be possible to control
the flow of paint, to a great extent… I don't use the accident — 'cause I deny
the accident." **Flag:** the punchy "I can control the flow of paint: there is no
accident" is the canonical short quote (used by Smarthistory) but is a
**condensation** of the longer Wright-interview passage — fine to quote, but
present it as Pollock's statement on control, not a single verbatim sentence if
precision matters.

**"I am nature"** — attributed to Pollock (reportedly to Hans Hofmann, ~1942,
via Lee Krasner's later recollection). **Flag: anecdotal/secondhand**, not a
documented written statement. Do NOT present as a sourced quote; mention only as
attributed.

> **Recommendation:** lead with the "On the floor I am more at ease…" passage
> (cleanest provenance, *Possibilities* 1947–48). Use "there is no accident" as a
> supporting line to kill the randomness myth (§8), attributed to the 1950
> Wright interview.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6 — prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **The skeins of color.** The whole surface is a tangle of looping lines in a
   tight palette: **black, white, brown, and tan/buff**. No bright hues — the
   autumnal browns and creams are where the title "Autumn" lands. Trace one black
   line and watch it dive under and over others.

2. **The allover rhythm with no center.** Scan the surface for a focal point —
   there isn't one. Every region is as busy and as "finished" as every other.
   The looping lines set up a continuous, even pulse across the entire field;
   your eye keeps moving and never settles.

3. **No object, nothing depicted.** Look for a figure, a horizon, a thing — there
   is none. Nothing is "in front of" anything else; the picture is not a window
   onto a scene. It refers only to itself.

4. **The drips, pools, and spatters.** You can read the *physics* of how it was
   made: thin trailed lines where paint streamed off a stick, fatter propulsive
   arcs where it was flung, little **pools** where it puddled, and fine
   **spatters** flicked off the edge of a loaded brush. Each mark is the track of
   a movement.

5. **The raw, unprimed canvas showing through.** The bare tan canvas is left
   exposed in the gaps between the skeins — it isn't a painted background, it's
   the **untouched cloth itself**, doing the work of the "ground." That exposed
   raw fabric is part of the picture, not under it.

6. **The scale you stand inside.** At roughly **8¾ feet tall and over 17 feet
   wide**, it fills your field of vision when you stand close — you don't look
   *at* it so much as stand *within* it, the way Pollock stood within it on the
   floor. The size is doing argumentative work: it puts your body where his was.

  Art, New York — George A. Hearn Fund, 1957 (57.92).**

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

1. **"Action painting" was NOT Pollock's term.** It was coined by critic
   **Harold Rosenberg** in **"The American Action Painters," *ARTnews*, December
   1952** — two years after *Autumn Rhythm* was made. Rosenberg framed the canvas
   as "an arena in which to act." Don't put the phrase in Pollock's mouth or
   imply it existed when he painted this. (Source: ARTnews 1952; Tate / Courtauld
   on Rosenberg.)

2. **It is NOT random or accidental.** This is the central misreading. Pollock
   **controlled** the pour — "I can control the flow of paint: there is no
   accident" (William Wright interview, 1950) — and Namuth's photo/film sequence
   shows a **deliberate, methodical** build-up (right third first, layer by
   layer). Call it controlled / choreographed, never "splattered at random."
   (Source: Smarthistory; Wright interview; Bohm-Duchen via Namuth photos.)

3. **Enamel / house paint, NOT oil.** The medium is **commercial enamel (house
   paint)**, per the Met ("Enamel on canvas"). Do not write "oil on canvas."

4. **The title was added later.** It was made and first shown as **Number 30**
   (1950–52); the lyrical title **Autumn Rhythm** attached only by the 1955
   Sidney Janis show. Don't imply Pollock painted "an autumn scene" — the title
   is post-hoc and the painting depicts nothing. (Source: Wikipedia / Met.)

5. **Dimensions trap.** Ship **266.7 × 525.8 cm / 8 ft 9 in × 17 ft 3 in** (Met
   primary). The larger 269.9 × 528.3 × 6.7 cm figure is the *same painting*
   measured with depth/stretcher — not a different work. Don't average them or
   present them as conflicting facts.

6. **Hans Namuth: photos AND films (and they're not "of" Autumn Rhythm being
   finished on glass).** Namuth shot 500+ stills plus two films in 1950; the
   famous **color film** *Jackson Pollock 51* shows Pollock painting **on glass**
   (a separate setup), not *Autumn Rhythm* itself. *Autumn Rhythm*'s creation is
   documented mainly by the **photographs**. Don't conflate the glass-painting
   film footage with this canvas.
