## 2. THE STORY

**Automatism** is the central Surrealist method: you let the hand (or the writing
hand) move *without conscious control* so that whatever surfaces comes from below
deliberate thought — the unconscious. André Breton built Surrealism around it; he had
arrived at it first as **automatic writing** (the 1919 experiment *Les Champs
magnétiques* with Philippe Soupault — racing the pen to outrun the editing mind), and
his 1924 *Manifesto* defined Surrealism itself as "psychic automatism in its pure
state" (see §4). The wager: reason, taste, and morality are filters laid over a truer
mental life, and if you switch the filters off, the truer thing leaks onto the page.

**Masson is the figure who carried automatism over from words into line.** Starting in
the **winter of 1923**, he was among the first Surrealists to try drawing this way
(Hans/Jean Arp had made earlier "automatic" drawings, c.1916–18, by other means). The
method: pen to paper with **no subject and no composition decided in advance**, the
hand moving **fast** across the sheet "like a medium channeling a spirit" (MoMA's own
description). Out of the resulting lacelike tangle, **fragments of bodies and things
half-emerge** — Masson "soon found hints of images… emerging from the abstract,
lacelike web of pen marks." Across his automatic drawings these recurrent fragments
are hands, birds, fish, breasts, and other body parts: the line keeps *almost*
condensing into a figure and then dissolving again.

**Trance / hunger / drugs — verify, don't overstate.** This part is real but should be
stated carefully. Masson said he worked in a **state of semi-trance**, letting the
hand move freely (he is widely quoted to that effect). Biographical accounts
(Wikipedia's Surrealist-automatism and Masson entries, drawing on the standard
literature) report that he would **force himself to work under strict conditions —
after long stretches without food or sleep, or under the influence of drugs** — to
loosen conscious control, and that he ran these altered-state experiments alongside
neighbors and friends in Paris (Antonin Artaud, Michel Leiris, Joan Miró, Georges
Bataille, Georges Malkine). He was also a **badly wounded WWI veteran with what we'd
now call PTSD**, and that trauma is regularly tied to why automatism appealed to him —
a generative way to make images without composing them. **Caution:** keep this as
"he deprived himself of food/sleep and used drugs to reach an altered state," which
the sources support; do NOT escalate it into a precise claim that *this specific sheet
(38201)* was made in a drug- or hunger-trance — no source ties a named condition to
this individual drawing.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required — before / after)

**Before:** A drawing is a record of something *seen or imagined and then composed* —
the artist decides on a subject, sets it in a space, and the marks describe it. Even
the wildest pre-Surrealist drawing is the hand *executing* a picture the mind has
already chosen. The skill on display is depiction and control.

**After (the break):** Here the drawing is a record of the **unconscious hand itself**
— **process over depiction**. There is no prior subject; the line is laid down faster
than judgment can intervene, and the "image" is whatever the viewer (and, after the
fact, the artist) can find *in* the marks. The picture is no longer a window onto a
chosen scene; it's a **trace of an act**, evidence of mark-making rather than a
description of a thing. That inversion — letting the mark generate the subject instead
of the subject dictating the mark — is what makes Masson's automatic drawing a break,
and it's the line that runs forward to Abstract Expressionism's gesture (Pollock
explicitly admired Masson's automatism).

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced)

**Primary (recommended) — Breton's definition of Surrealism, 1924 *Manifesto*:**

> "SURREALISM, *n.* Psychic automatism in its pure state, by which one proposes to
> express — verbally, by means of the written word, or in any other manner — the actual
> functioning of thought."

> "ENCYCLOPEDIA. *Philos.* Surrealism is based on the belief in the superior reality
> of certain forms of previously neglected associations, in the omnipotence of dream,
> in the disinterested play of thought. It tends to ruin once and for all all other
> psychic mechanisms and to substitute itself for them in solving all the principal
> problems of life."

— André Breton, *Manifesto of Surrealism* (1924). **Translation flagged:** this is the
**Richard Seaver & Helen R. Lane** English, from *Manifestoes of Surrealism* (Univ. of
Michigan Press, 1969) — the standard English text. Source: *Manifesto of Surrealism*,
The Anarchist Library (full Seaver/Lane text). Use the dictionary sentence on its own
if a single short pull-quote is wanted; the Encyclopedia sentence is the optional
second beat.

**Variant flag:** other English editions phrase the dash clauses slightly differently
("verbally, in writing, or by other means"); the original French reads "*Automatisme
psychique pur par lequel on se propose d'exprimer… le fonctionnement réel de la
pensée.*" Quote **one** translation and name it; don't blend.

**Secondary (a Masson quote on his own method), if a maker's voice is preferred:**

> "The wandering line is probably a key characteristic of my work. But it wasn't the
> line that was wandering, it was me."

— André Masson, quoted in Hyperallergic, "André Masson's Tortured and Sensuous
Automatic Drawings." **Confidence MEDIUM:** verified in that article but the original
publication context isn't pinned here; if used, attribute as "Masson, quoted in
Hyperallergic" rather than implying a primary source. The Breton manifesto quote is
the safer, fully-sourced choice.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — NO coordinates)

1. **One continuous, unbroken line.** Follow the ink and you can travel most of the
   sheet without lifting off — a single nervous skein that loops back on itself, the
   physical trace of a hand that wasn't stopping to plan.
2. **Body parts that half-emerge and dissolve.** Look for the line *almost* resolving
   into a hand, a breast, a bird, a fish — fragments surfacing out of the tangle and
   sinking back, never settling into a whole figure. (Name only the fragments you can
   actually see in the wired image — verify at integration before asserting "a fish.")
3. **No fixed ground or horizon.** Nothing tells you which way is up or where the
   "floor" is; the marks float in an unanchored, all-over field rather than sitting in
   a described space.
4. **Speed you can read in the mark.** The line's whip and overshoot — where it
   races past a turn — shows the pace; this is a *fast* drawing, and the velocity is
   part of what you're looking at.
5. **Density that thickens and thins.** Knots where the hand circled and worried the
   same patch, against open areas where it shot across — the rhythm of an unplanned
   pass, not an even hatching.
6. **The faint after-touch.** Where the web tips slightly toward a recognizable form,
   you're seeing Masson's small *conscious* nudges — the bits he let stand or lightly
   coaxed once a shape suggested itself (see §8: it isn't "pure" all the way down).

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (with corrections)

1. **WHICH 1924 drawing (the big one).** "Masson, *Automatic Drawing*, 1924" is
   ambiguous — **multiple** exist. Hyperallergic alone names three 1924 sheets:
   *Automatic Drawing*, *À Louis Aragon*, and *À Paul Éluard*; MoMA also holds
   *Birth of Birds* (c.1925) and the 1926 sand painting *Battle of Fishes* (often
   confused with the drawings). **Correction:** our work is the untitled-style
   *Automatic Drawing*, **MoMA object 38201, 23.5 × 20.6 cm, ink on paper** —
   confirm the wired image is that exact sheet by eye, not by the shared title.
2. **The Breton-manifesto quote attribution.** Easy to mis-cite. It is **Breton**, in
   the **1924 *Manifesto of Surrealism***, in the **Seaver/Lane** translation — name
   the translator, since wordings differ across editions, and don't attribute the
   *definition of Surrealism* to Masson. **Correction:** quote one named translation;
   the French original is *"Automatisme psychique pur…"* (see §4).
3. **"Pure," unplanned automatism — don't over-claim.** The romantic story is a pure
   trance with zero conscious input. The sources are explicit that Masson **let the
   automatic marks stand but then consciously elaborated** — "at times he elaborated
   on these with conscious changes or additions" (MoMA-derived description); for the
   related sand paintings he "consciously enhanced them with pencil to create figures"
   (Smarthistory). Hyperallergic notes the work "revealed a certain amount of
   reflection and artistic strategy." **Correction:** present the drawing as
   *largely* automatic with **acknowledged conscious shaping after the fact** — not
   "purely" unplanned. The "pure state" is Breton's *ideal/definition*, not a verified
   description of how this sheet was finished.
4. **Trance/hunger/drugs — real but don't pin to this sheet.** Supported in general
   (semi-trance method; deprivation of food/sleep; drug use; WWI trauma). **Not**
   supported: that *this specific drawing* was produced under a named condition.
   **Correction:** keep altered-states detail at the level of Masson's *practice*, not
   this object's making.
5. **Dimensions / orientation.** Don't transpose. **23.5 cm is the HEIGHT, 20.6 cm the
   WIDTH** (9¼ × 8⅛ in) — a **portrait/upright** sheet, taller than wide. If the wired
   image is landscape, it's the wrong work (or rotated) — flag it.
6. **Medium.** It's **ink on paper** (a drawing) — not a painting, not the sand-and-
   gesso technique of *Battle of Fishes*. Keep the medium exact.
7. **Date format.** MoMA writes the date in parentheses "(1924)" = institution-
   attributed, not an inscribed date; fine to print "1924," just don't claim it's
   signed/dated on the sheet.
