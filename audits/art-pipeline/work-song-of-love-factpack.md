## 2. THE STORY

Before there was a Surrealist movement, there was de Chirico's **Metaphysical
painting** (*pittura metafisica*) — the style he developed c. 1910–1918, partly
with the painter Carlo Carrà. Its signature: silent, sun-raked, deserted
Italian-style **piazzas and arcades**, long shadows, a flat hard light, and
incongruous objects set down together with the calm logic of a dream. De Chirico
wanted the *enigma* — the feeling that an ordinary square, at a strange hour,
conceals something it will not say.

*The Song of Love*, painted in Paris in mid-1914, is the most famous single image
of that project and the one most often called **proto-Surrealist** — it was made
a full decade before André Breton "founded" Surrealism with the 1924 *Manifesto*.
On a low wall in an open architectural setting de Chirico nails together things
that have no business being together: a **plaster head of a classical
statue** (widely identified as the **Apollo Belvedere**), an oversized
**red/pink rubber surgeon's glove**, and, on the ground below, a **green ball**.
Behind the wall, framed by an arcade, a small **locomotive** trails **smoke** on
the horizon under a hard blue sky. Nothing connects the objects by ordinary
logic; they connect only as a dream connects things.

**The Surrealist debt.** This juxtaposition is exactly what Breton and the
Surrealists later seized on as a model. The most-cited proof is **René Magritte**:
in 1922 the poet **Marcel Lecomte showed Magritte a reproduction** of *The Song
of Love*, and Magritte said the encounter was *"one of the most moving moments of
my life."* It helped turn him toward Surrealism. (Exact wording + the
reproduction caveat in §4 and §8.)

The **locomotive/smoke** motif recurs across de Chirico's early work and is
usually read autobiographically: his father, **Evaristo de Chirico, was a railway
engineer** who worked on rail lines in Greece, where the artist was born. (Secondary
/ interpretive — present as a common reading, not fact.)

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK *(REQUIRED — before/after)*

**Before:** A painting's objects were expected to belong to one coherent world —
a still life's fruit and jug share a table; a landscape's elements share a place.
Even Cubism, the radical style next door in 1914 Paris, fractured *how* a single
subject was seen; it did not throw unrelated subjects into one silent square and
let them stand there unexplained.

**The break:** *The Song of Love* fuses objects by **dream-logic juxtaposition**
rather than by subject, place, or visual analysis. An antique god's head, a
modern surgical glove, a child's ball, and a distant train coexist in a still,
over-lit piazza, producing **metaphysical disquiet** — the sense that the scene
*means* something withheld. The picture is legible and serene on the surface and
deeply uneasy underneath.

**After:** That move — incongruous objects rendered with deadpan clarity to
generate unease — is the operating principle Surrealism would build on a decade
later. De Chirico **fed Surrealism its central image ten years early**: Breton's
circle treated him as a forerunner, and Magritte's whole career of plainly painted
impossible juxtapositions traces back to seeing *this* picture. The "break" here
is unusual in that it predates the movement it belongs to — a bridge built before
there was a road on the other side.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced) + variants

Two candidate "key passages." Use the **Magritte** quote as the primary key
statement (it directly concerns *this painting*); the de Chirico **enigma** motto
is the secondary key passage on the metaphysical project.

### A. René Magritte on first seeing *The Song of Love* (PRIMARY)
> "When I saw [the reproduction of] *The Song of Love* … it was **one of the most
> moving moments of my life: my eyes saw thought for the first time**."

- **Most-cited short form (Wikipedia, NYRB):** "one of the most moving moments of
  my life: my eyes *saw* thought for the first time."
- **Variant:** some sources give "my eyes saw **the mind** for the first time"
  (translation variant of *la pensée*). Prefer **"thought."**
- **Context to get right:** 1922; shown a **reproduction** by poet Marcel
  Lecomte; not the original canvas. Confidence: HIGH on substance, MEDIUM on the
  single canonical English wording (it is a translated quote with circulating
  variants).

### B. De Chirico's enigma motto (SECONDARY — the metaphysical credo)
> Latin: **"Et quid amabo nisi quod aenigma est?"**
> English: **"And what shall I love if not the enigma?"**

- **Where it appears:** inscribed by de Chirico on an early **self-portrait
  (1911)** — not on *The Song of Love* itself. (One source mis-dates the
  self-portrait to 1920; the **1911** dating is the standard one — flag.)
- **Lineage:** the phrase echoes **Nietzsche**, whose thought saturates de
  Chirico's early metaphysical period (the self-portrait poses him in a
  Nietzsche-derived attitude). Treat the Nietzsche link as **well-attested but
  interpretive**, not as a verbatim Nietzsche citation.
- Confidence: HIGH on the Latin/English and the 1911 self-portrait location.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6 — prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **The rubber glove.** A large, limp **surgeon's rubber glove** is tacked flat
   to the wall like a specimen. Sources describe it as a hollow **mold of a hand
   that implies the absence of a human presence**. Apollinaire noted in July 1914
   that de Chirico had bought a **pink rubber glove** precisely for the uncanny
   charge it would lend the pictures. (Often called "red"; see §8 on color.)
2. **The marble/plaster head.** Beside the glove, a **classical sculpted head** —
   widely identified as the **Apollo Belvedere** — fixed to the same wall: a relic
   of an idealized antiquity, the calm of vanished classical order set against the
   modern glove.
3. **The green ball.** A single **green sphere** sits on the ground below the wall
   — a plain, almost toy-like object whose ordinariness deepens the strangeness of
   its company; nothing explains why it is there.
4. **The wall and the arcade.** The objects hang on a **low wall**; behind and
   around it an **arcaded building** frames the space — the colonnade/arcade is a
   recurring de Chirico stage-set, lit by a hard, sourceless daylight that flattens
   everything and lengthens shadows.
5. **The distant train + smoke.** Over the wall, on the horizon, a small
   **locomotive** trails a plume of **smoke** under a bright sky — the recurring
   railway motif read as a nod to the artist's railway-engineer father; it pulls a
   modern, moving world into the still tableau.
6. **The overall stillness / scale.** Everything is rendered with deadpan, almost
   diagrammatic clarity at small scale (under 2½ ft tall); the **calm legibility is
   the trap** — the eeriness comes from how *reasonably* impossible things are
   shown, not from any visual distortion.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

1. **The Magritte "moving moment" quote.**
   - Correct wording: **"one of the most moving moments of my life: my eyes saw
     thought for the first time."** Beware the **"saw the mind"** variant
     (translation of *la pensée*). Use **"thought."**
   - Correct the *object*: Magritte saw a **reproduction** (shown by Marcel
     Lecomte, **1922**), **not the original canvas**. Do not write that he stood
     before the painting.
2. **Glove color/type.** Commonly described as a **red rubber surgical/surgeon's
   glove**; Apollinaire's 1914 note and several sources call it **pink**. Safe
   phrasing: a **rubber surgeon's glove** (note it reads pink/red). Don't assert a
   single definitive color as documented fact.
3. **The head's identity.** Secondary scholarship overwhelmingly calls it the
   **Apollo Belvedere**; MoMA's own short description says generically a "classical
   Greek statue / sculpted head." Safe phrasing: a classical head, **commonly
   identified as the Apollo Belvedere** — don't present the specific ID as MoMA's
   wall text.
4. **Date.** It is **1914** (MoMA: Paris, June–July 1914) — **not** a later
   1920s/Surrealist-era work. Its whole significance is that it **predates**
   Surrealism (founded 1924).
5. **Dimensions.** **73 × 59.1 cm / 28¾ × 23⅜ in** — a small panel. Don't inflate;
   reproductions make it feel monumental.
6. **Enigma motto location.** "Et quid amabo nisi quod aenigma est" is on de
   Chirico's **1911 self-portrait**, **not** on *The Song of Love*. One source
   mis-dates it 1920 — use **1911**.
7. **"Founded Surrealism."** De Chirico did **not** found Surrealism and was not a
   Surrealist; he was a **forerunner** the Surrealists admired. He had largely
   turned against avant-garde modernism by the time the movement formed.
