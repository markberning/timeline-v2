## 2. THE STORY

By 1953 Rothko had been painting in his mature, "classic" idiom for about four to five years. After the war he had passed through Surrealist-derived "multiforms" (the late-1940s phase: clusters of soft, fuzzy, biomorphic blots floating against a single-hued ground) and then pared them down. By 1949–50 the blots had resolved into the format he would repeat, with endless variation, for the rest of his life: **two or three soft-edged rectangles of luminous color, stacked vertically, hovering against a colored ground** that shows through and around them. *No. 61 (Rust and Blue)* is a textbook instance — a maroon/rust rectangle above, a paler band, and a deep blue-violet block, all breathing against and within a blue field.

The making is the point of the look. Rothko built these surfaces from **thin, scrubbed-on washes of heavily thinned oil**, layer over layer, so the under-colors glow up through the top ones and no edge is ever hard. He let the rectangles bleed into the ground rather than sit on it, which is why the forms seem to advance, recede, and pulse rather than stay put. He sometimes called this effect "inner light." There is no figure, no horizon, no object — and yet the scale (over nine feet tall) and the breathing color are meant to do what a figure once did: confront the viewer at human, even bodily, scale.

That scale was deliberate and stated. Rothko: *"I paint very large pictures… precisely because I want to be very intimate and human. To paint a small picture is to place yourself outside your experience… However if you paint the larger picture, you are in it"* (statement in *Interiors*, May 1951). He wanted the works seen **close** — he reportedly favored a viewing distance of about 18 inches — so the field fills your vision and you stand *inside* the color, not in front of a decorative panel across a room.

And he was adamant — to the point of irritation — that this was **not** about color. He rejected "abstractionist," rejected "colorist," and insisted the rectangles were vehicles for raw human feeling: *tragedy, ecstasy, doom*. The famous Selden Rodman exchange (§4) is the canonical statement. The people he most wanted to reach were the ones who broke down weeping in front of the paintings; the ones who admired the "color relationships," he said, missed the point entirely.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required: before / after)

**Before.** In Western painting, color had been the servant of something else: it described an object, modeled a body, filled a drawn composition, set a mood for a scene. Even the first wave of abstraction (Kandinsky, Mondrian) tended to keep *structure* — line, geometry, balanced composition — as the load-bearing element, with color as an attribute of the shapes. Emotion in a picture was carried by what was depicted: a grieving face, a storm, a crucifixion.

**The break.** Rothko removes the object, the line, the drawn composition, and the horizon, and asks **pure color, at enveloping scale, to carry the emotional content by itself.** Three soft rectangles dissolving into a field are not a "picture of" anything. The composition isn't *balanced* so much as *dissolved* — edges feather, planes float, the ground pushes through. What's left is an encounter: you stand close, the color surrounds you, and the work is engineered to produce a felt state (awe, grief, elevation) rather than to be read or admired.

**After.** This reframes abstraction as a **spiritual / emotional event rather than a formal exercise.** It is the high statement of Color Field painting and feeds directly into the next generation — the stained, soaked fields of Helen Frankenthaler and Morris Louis, the immersive environments that culminate in the Rothko Chapel (Houston, 1971). The lasting move is the claim that a field of color, with nothing in it, can do the emotional work that figuration used to do — and can be **felt before it is understood.**

**One-line break:** *He takes the object out of the painting and hands its whole emotional job to color — felt close, felt bodily, felt before it's understood.*

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced)

**Primary quote (the canonical one):**

> "I am not an abstractionist… I am not interested in the relationship of color or form or anything else. I'm interested only in expressing basic human emotions — tragedy, ecstasy, doom and so on — and the fact that a lot of people break down and cry when confronted with my pictures shows that I communicate those basic human emotions… The people who weep before my pictures are having the same religious experience I had when I painted them. And if you, as you say, are moved only by their color relationships, then you miss the point."

**Source (primary):** Mark Rothko, quoted in **Selden Rodman, *Conversations with Artists* (New York: Devin-Adair, 1957), p. 93** — from a conversation Rodman recorded in **1956**. Reprinted as "Notes from a conversation with Selden Rodman, 1956" in *Writings on Art: Mark Rothko*, ed. Miguel López-Remiro (Yale Univ. Press, 2006), p. 119.

**VARIANT FLAGS — use the Rodman text above, watch for these:**
- Many web sources drop "and so on" or punctuate with em-dashes / commas differently ("tragedy, ecstasy, and so on"). The Rodman text is the one to anchor to.
- The opening is often quoted as "the relationship of color **to** form." The widely reprinted Rodman wording is "the relationship**s** of color or form **or anything else**." Prefer "or form."
- The "people who weep… religious experience" passage is sometimes quoted as a *separate* Rothko quote; it is the same Rodman conversation. Fine to use either the short core ("…tragedy, ecstasy, doom") or the longer passage, but cite Rodman 1957 either way.
- **Do NOT** attribute the line to "a 1947 essay" or "his MoMA wall text" — that's wrong; it's the Rodman interview.

**Secondary (scale, optional second pull):**
> "I paint very large pictures… precisely because I want to be very intimate and human… if you paint the larger picture, you are in it."
> — Rothko, statement in *Interiors* (May 1951).

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The stack.** Find the major blocks reading top to bottom — a warm rust/maroon rectangle up high, a paler bridging band, and a deep blue-violet block below, all set against a blue ground. There are no objects: the whole "subject" is these few soft slabs of color and the room they make.
2. **The soft, feathered edges.** No rectangle has a ruled border. Each one frays and breathes into the color around it, as if the forms were exhaled onto the canvas rather than drawn. That softness is what keeps them from looking like flat panels.
3. **Color that advances and recedes.** Look for a few seconds and the warm block seems to float forward while the blue drops back — then it reverses. Nothing on the canvas moves, but the planes won't hold still; they pulse.
4. **The glow from underneath.** The surface is built from many thin, scrubbed washes of thinned paint, so lower layers shine up through the top ones. The color looks lit from within rather than painted on — what Rothko called "inner light." Hunt for the spots where one hue bleeds up through another.
5. **The ground showing through.** Notice where the blue field seeps around and between the rectangles. The blocks don't sit on top of a background; they dissolve into it, which is why the picture reads as one continuous breathing field, not shapes-on-a-backdrop.
6. **The scale.** It's over nine feet tall — taller than a person. Rothko wanted it seen up close (he favored roughly 18 inches away) so the color fills your whole field of vision and you stand *inside* it. Imagine it not as a picture on a wall but as a wall of color you're meant to step into.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS

1. **The id slug is misleading.** `orange-yellow` ≠ this painting. The wired MOCA work is *No. 61 (Rust and Blue)* (rust/brown on blue). *Orange and Yellow* (1956) is a **different** Rothko in the **Buffalo AKG (Albright-Knox)**. Build the read on Rust and Blue; don't import orange/yellow language. **CORRECTED.**
2. **Title variants are both legitimate descriptors, neither is "the" title.** MOCA's full label is *No. 61 (Rust and Blue) [Brown, Blue, Brown on Blue]*. Rothko favored numbers, not names; the parenthetical color tags were added to tell his many untitled/numbered canvases apart. Lead with **No. 61 (Rust and Blue)** and note *Brown, Blue, Brown on Blue* as an alternate. Don't present them as two different paintings.
3. **There is more than one Rothko "No. 61."** Rothko numbered works year by year, so "No. 61" alone is ambiguous. The MOCA / Panza Collection canvas is specifically *No. 61 (Rust and Blue)*, 1953, 292.7 × 233.7 cm — confirmed by the MOCA record. The wired image is this one.
4. **Don't call him a "colorist" or "abstractionist" in the prose voice.** Rothko explicitly rejected both labels (§4). "Color Field" is the accepted art-historical *movement* tag and is fine as a category — but frame his **intent** as emotional/spiritual, with color as the *vehicle*, not the subject. Saying the painting is "about color" reproduces exactly the reading he said missed the point.
5. **Don't over-mystify.** The plain mechanics — thinned washes, layered glazes, feathered edges, big scale, close viewing — are documented and concrete. Explain *how* the glow happens; don't reach for vague "transcendence" filler. The feeling is the goal, but the means are physical paint.
6. **Dimensions:** use **292.7 × 233.7 cm / ~9 ft 7 in × 7 ft 8 in** (face). The 1¾ in / 4.45 cm third figure is stretcher **depth**, not a face dimension — don't print it as height/width.
7. **Early-exhibition claims are shaky.** The 1961 MoMA showing (his retrospective) is solid; the occasionally-cited "1955 Sidney Janis Gallery" first showing is **unverified** — flag as uncertain rather than assert.
8. **Date is firm at 1953** (MOCA). Some merchandise/print listings hedge "(date unknown)" — ignore them; MOCA dates it 1953.
