# Fact pack — POST-IMPRESSIONISM (kind: MOVEMENT, Modern era, c.1886–1905, France/UK)

Coordinator-built ground truth for the art content pipeline. The author drafts
the **movement-level chaptered narrative** ONLY from this. Every concrete claim
traces to a sourced item here or is flagged `[VERIFY]`. (Web-checked
2026-05-25; fact-checker gate re-verifies independently.)

Dimensions are given in cm from the museum record AND converted to
**feet/inches** (this app uses imperial only, never cm —
`feedback_art_dimensions_imperial`).

**Tone note for the writer.** Post-Impressionism is a retrospective label — no
manifesto, no founding meeting, no self-identifying group. The five "anchors"
worked in different cities, often in disagreement with each other, and would
mostly have rejected the bin. The honest framing is: *a critic in London in
1910 needed a name for the painters who came after Impressionism but were not
yet Fauves or Cubists, and the name stuck.* The story is not "the
Post-Impressionists did X" — it is "five very different artists each
answered Impressionism in their own way; later we grouped them."

## 0. THE LEGEND LEDGER — documented vs myth (READ FIRST)

| Claim | Verdict | The real fact |
|---|---|---|
| "Post-Impressionism" was a self-named movement | **[LEGEND / false]** | The term is **retrospective**, coined by **Roger Fry** for his 1910 London show. Cézanne died in 1906 having never heard it; Van Gogh and Seurat were already dead. None of the five "anchors" signed a manifesto, attended a founding meeting, or used the word about themselves. [W-PI][W-Fry] |
| Roger Fry "invented" the term in 1910 | **[DOCUMENTED, with nuance]** | The show titled *Manet and the Post-Impressionists* opened **8 November 1910**; the catalogue and reviews fixed the word. But Fry later told *The Nation* (1911) he picked the name on the fly because he needed an umbrella for the show and rejected alternatives like "expressionists." Frame it: he gave it the name that *stuck*, in a slightly improvised way. [W-Fry][Berkowitz] |
| Van Gogh's *Wheatfield with Crows* was his last painting | **[LEGEND / contested]** | Popularized by the 1956 film *Lust for Life*. The Van Gogh Museum and scholars (Walther & Metzger; Jan Hulsker) note there is **no evidence** for this; the Museum's current view is that *Tree Roots* (Auvers, July 1890) is more likely his last. [W-Wheatfield] |
| Van Gogh shot himself in a wheatfield, 29 July 1890 | **[MOSTLY DOCUMENTED — exact spot contested, date of death vs date of wounding]** | He was shot/shot himself on **27 July 1890** and died of the wound in his room at the Auberge Ravoux, Auvers, in the early hours of **29 July 1890**. The "wheatfield" location is traditional but never proven; Naifeh & Smith (2011) argue for a different spot and even a different shooter (16-yr-old René Secrétan). Default safe wording: "shot in a field near Auvers, 27 July; died two days later." [W-VG][W-DeathVG] |
| Van Gogh sold only one painting in his life — *The Red Vineyard* to Anna Boch | **[DOCUMENTED, with nuance]** | True for the "marquee sale": *The Red Vineyard* sold to **Anna Boch** at **Les XX, Brussels, in March/April 1890** for **400 francs (~£16)**. But the Van Gogh Museum lists at least a handful of other minor sales/exchanges in life (a self-portrait to Sulley, a few studies via Theo). Honest wording: "the only painting he is known to have sold for a real price" or "the only one he certainly sold in life." [W-RV][VGM] |
| Cézanne's "cylinder, sphere, cone" letter is the founding theoretical text of modern art | **[DOCUMENTED, but routinely OVER-CITED]** | The letter to Émile Bernard, **15 April 1904**, exists (the autograph is at the **Courtauld Gallery**, ms-1932-SC-1-1). But it is **one sentence in a long correspondence** advising a younger painter, not a manifesto. Don't claim Cézanne intended it as theory. Frame it as: the line Picasso, Braque and Bernard himself quoted back at the world. [Courtauld][W-Cz] |
| Seurat died of "diphtheria" at 31 | **[CONTESTED]** | Wikipedia: "variously attributed to a form of meningitis, pneumonia, infectious angina, and diphtheria." Hedge — never assert a single cause. Death date: **29 March 1891**, age 31. [W-Seurat] |
| Toulouse-Lautrec was a dwarf because his parents were cousins | **[PARTIAL / hedge]** | His parents were **first cousins** (documented). The specific genetic disorder is debated; **pycnodysostosis** is the most cited candidate (so-called "Toulouse-Lautrec syndrome") but is still inferred, not proven. Phrase as "an inherited disorder, most often identified as pycnodysostosis." [W-TL] |
| Gauguin "discovered" Tahiti | **[LEGEND / false]** | Tahiti had been a French protectorate since 1842 and a colony since 1880 — Gauguin arrived as a colonial subject, not an explorer, and lived among a French-administered population that already had cathedrals, gendarmes, and a port. Romance him as an exile, never a discoverer. [W-Gauguin] |
| Picasso "saw the 1907 Cézanne retrospective and invented Cubism" | **[OVER-SIMPLIFIED]** | The Salon d'Automne held memorial Cézanne rooms in **1904, 1905, and 1906** while he was still alive, and a much larger **retrospective in 1907** (the year after his death). All of these mattered. *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* was painted in 1907. Causation is real but lumpy — don't say "the 1907 show alone." [W-Cz][W-Cubism] |

## 1. THE NAME — Roger Fry and the 1910 Grafton show

### The exhibition

- **Title:** *Manet and the Post-Impressionists* (the standard short form).
  Catalogue subtitle and revised running title: *Manet and the
  Post-Impressionists, Nov. 8th to Jan. 15th, 1910–11*. [DOCUMENTED]
  [W-PI][Grafton-Cat]
- **Venue:** **Grafton Galleries, London** (a commercial gallery on Grafton
  Street, Mayfair). [W-PI][Berkowitz]
- **Dates:** **8 November 1910 – 15 January 1911.** (Wikipedia's
  *Post-Impressionism* article and the catalogue title both give 8 November as
  the opening; the *original brief* from Fry mentioned 5 November but the
  show in fact opened on the 8th — **use 8 November**.) [Grafton-Cat][V&A]
- **Number of works:** **~228 — 206 paintings and drawings plus 22 bronzes and
  ceramics** (catalogue figures, widely repeated; some sources say ~220–250
  because items were added/withdrawn during the run). [Berkowitz][TheCollector]
- **Visitors:** **~25,000 over two months**; gate receipts and sales
  net ~**£4,600** (a commercial success). [Berkowitz][TheCollector]
- **Organizers:** **Roger Fry** with **Desmond MacCarthy** (secretary) and
  **Clive Bell**. Lady Ottoline Morrell was a patron. [W-Fry]

### The artists in the 1910 show (selection — not exhaustive)

Manet (the borrowed "anchor"), Cézanne, Van Gogh, Gauguin, Matisse, Picasso,
Derain, Vlaminck, Maurice Denis, Redon, Seurat, Signac, Sérusier, Vallotton.
(Fry's net was very wide; "Post-Impressionist" in 1910 meant roughly "after
Manet, before us" — it included artists we now call Fauves and proto-Cubists.)
[W-PI][TheCollector]

### The public reaction

The reception is real, not embroidered. Examples that are **documented**:

- **Wilfrid Scawen Blunt** (diary, 15 Nov 1910): the show was "either an
  extremely bad joke or a swindle." [Berkowitz]
- **Robert Ross**, *Morning Post*: called it "a widespread plot to destroy the
  whole fabric of European painting." [Berkowitz]
- Many reviews used variants of "the work of a lunatic" / "infantile" /
  "outrage." [Berkowitz][TheCollector]
- **Virginia Woolf**, much later (1924, "Mr. Bennett and Mrs. Brown"): **"on
  or about December 1910 human character changed."** This is the famous
  retrospective epitaph — true that she wrote it, **not** true that anyone
  said it at the time. Quote her as the *later* witness. [W-Fry]

**Born-verified quotable for the manifesto block:** the Blunt diary line is
the cleanest contemporaneous reaction; the Woolf line is the cleanest
retrospective one. Pair them.

### Fry's introduction — gate the wording

The catalogue introduction is reproduced in **Roger Fry's *Vision and Design***
(1920) and the catalogue itself is in the **Internet Archive**
(`manetpostimpress00graf`). One **safe-to-quote** passage frequently appears
across academic sources (Berkowitz; Bullen, *Post-Impressionists in England*,
1988):

> "The pictures collected together in the present Exhibition are the work of
> a group of artists who cannot be defined by any single term. … They have
> aimed not at illusion but at reality."

Use this only with the source citation. If the writer wants a different line,
re-verify against the Internet Archive catalogue PDF before quoting.

### The 1912 sequel — the *Second Post-Impressionist Exhibition*

- **Title:** *Second Post-Impressionist Exhibition. British, French and
  Russian Artists.* [DOCUMENTED] [INHA][V&A]
- **Dates:** **5 October – 31 December 1912**, Grafton Galleries. [INHA]
- **Organizers:** Roger Fry; with the new Russian section curated by **Boris
  Anrep** and the British section selected by Fry + Clive Bell. Poster
  designed by **Vanessa Bell** and **Duncan Grant**. [V&A]
- **Scope:** **~250 works** (French + British + Russian sections combined).
  French room dominated by **Cézanne (~5)**, **Matisse (~19)**, **Picasso
  (~13 in total across both arrangements — counts vary)**, plus Derain,
  Vlaminck, Marchand, Lhote, Bonnard, Marquet. British section: Roger Fry
  himself, Duncan Grant, Vanessa Bell, Wyndham Lewis, Eric Gill, Stanley
  Spencer. Russian section: Goncharova, Larionov, Roerich. [V&A][Frick]
- **Significance:** the show that consolidated the term and pushed it from
  "Cézanne / Van Gogh / Gauguin" to include living Fauves and proto-Cubists.

### Fry's later theoretical scaffolding

- **"An Essay in Aesthetics"** — first published *New Quarterly* April 1909,
  reprinted as the opening chapter of *Vision and Design* (Chatto & Windus,
  London, 1920). [W-Fry]
- *Vision and Design* (1920) collects Fry's essays from ~1900–1920 — the
  theoretical home of "significant form" (a phrase more associated with
  Clive Bell's *Art*, 1914, but a shared Bloomsbury vocabulary).

## 2. THE TERM AND ITS SCOPE

### Date range — hedged

- **Conventional range: c.1886 – c.1905** (Wikipedia, Tate, NGA glossaries).
  [W-PI]
- **Why 1886:** the **8th and last Impressionist Exhibition** (Paris,
  **15 May – 15 June 1886**, above Maison Dorée, rue Laffitte) was where
  **Seurat showed *A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte*** —
  the painting that made Impressionism look suddenly past-tense.
  [W-Seurat][W-Grande-Jatte]
- **Why ~1905:** the **Salon d'Automne of 1905** is where Matisse, Derain
  and the others were dubbed **les Fauves** ("the wild beasts") by critic
  Louis Vauxcelles — the next break. Some scholars push Post-Impressionism
  to 1914 to keep the late Cézanne effect in the bin. [W-PI]
- **Hedge both endpoints.** "Roughly between the last Impressionist show
  (1886) and the rise of Fauvism (1905)" is the safe phrasing.

### Why the category is contested

- **No manifesto, no founding meeting, no membership list.**
- **The five "anchors" didn't form a group.** Cézanne worked alone in
  Provence; Van Gogh in Arles/Saint-Rémy/Auvers; Gauguin in Brittany then
  Polynesia; Seurat in Paris; Toulouse-Lautrec in Montmartre. They mostly
  never met each other in any meaningful way (Van Gogh + Gauguin in Arles
  Oct–Dec 1888 is the famous exception).
- **The label is a London import.** It is a *British* critical
  construction, applied to *French* painters, retroactively. French art
  history at the time used more granular categories: **Néo-Impressionnistes**
  (Seurat, Signac), **Synthétistes / École de Pont-Aven** (Gauguin, Bernard),
  **Nabis** (Denis, Bonnard, Vuillard, Sérusier), **Symbolistes** (Redon).
- **Honest framing for the writer:** "Post-Impressionism is less a movement
  than a *room* art history puts five very different artists into, so we
  have somewhere to put them."

## 3. THE BREAK — "after the eye, the structure"

The argument the writer needs to make: **Impressionism dissolved the world in
light; Post-Impressionism put it back together — five different ways.**

### One-clause answer per anchor

- **Cézanne** — Impressionism is "only an eye" (his own phrase, reported by
  Bernard: *"Monet n'est qu'un œil, mais quel œil!"* — "Monet is only an
  eye, but what an eye!"). Rebuild the world as **structure**: planes,
  weight, geometry. **Cylinder, sphere, cone.** [W-Cz][MoMA-Letters]
- **Van Gogh** — color is **emotion**, not optics. *"Instead of trying to
  reproduce exactly what I have before my eyes, I use color more arbitrarily
  in order to express myself forcibly"* (letter 520 to Theo, Aug 1888,
  Arles). [W-VG]
- **Gauguin** — flat color + outline + symbol. **Synthetism** (Pont-Aven,
  with Émile Bernard, 1888): the picture is an arrangement of color areas,
  not a window. Then **primitivism** in Tahiti (1891, 1895).
- **Seurat** — color science. **Divisionism / pointillism**: tiny strokes
  of pure pigment that mix in the eye, not on the palette. Built on
  **Chevreul's *De la loi du contraste simultané des couleurs*** (1839) and
  **Ogden Rood's *Modern Chromatics*** (1879). [W-Seurat]
- **Toulouse-Lautrec** — flat color and silhouette borrowed from **Japanese
  prints (ukiyo-e)**, applied to modern Parisian life. Lithography and the
  modern poster as a fine-art medium. [W-TL]

### Cézanne's "cylinder, sphere, cone" — the canonical passage

**Source:** Cézanne to Émile Bernard, **15 April 1904**, autograph manuscript
held at the **Courtauld Gallery, London** (accession ms-1932-SC-1-1; 4 pages;
acquired by the Courtauld in 1932 with the Samuel Courtauld bequest).
[Courtauld]

**English translation (the standard one, John Rewald ed., *Cézanne: Letters*,
1937; and Alex Danchev, *The Letters of Paul Cézanne*, Thames & Hudson 2013,
on which MoMA's online edition is based):**

> "Allow me to repeat what I said when you were here: treat nature by means
> of the cylinder, the sphere, the cone, the whole put into perspective, so
> that each side of an object or a plane is directed towards a central
> point. Lines parallel to the horizon give breadth, that is to say a section
> of nature, or, if you prefer, of the spectacle which the *Pater Omnipotens
> Aeterne Deus* spreads before our eyes. Lines perpendicular to that horizon
> give depth. But nature for us men is more depth than surface, whence the
> necessity of introducing in our vibrations of light, represented by reds
> and yellows, a sufficient quantity of bluish tones, to give the feel of
> air."

**Original French (Rewald):**

> "Permettez-moi de vous répéter ce que je vous disais ici : traiter la
> nature par le cylindre, la sphère, le cône, le tout mis en perspective,
> soit que chaque côté d'un objet, d'un plan, se dirige vers un point
> central…"

[MoMA-Letters][Courtauld][CFA-Cezanne]

## 4. THE MANIFESTO — ABSENT (the silence to tell)

Post-Impressionism, like Cubism, **has no founding manifesto.** That silence
is the story. Two surrogate "founding documents" frame the void:

### Surrogate 1 — Roger Fry's 1910 catalogue

- **Document:** Catalogue of *Manet and the Post-Impressionists*, Grafton
  Galleries, London, 8 Nov 1910 – 15 Jan 1911.
- **Verifiable public link:** **Internet Archive,
  `manetpostimpress00graf`** (full PDF + OCR text;
  `archive.org/details/manetpostimpress00graf`). The V&A also holds a
  poster (`O578995`). [IA-Cat][V&A]
- **What it does:** **named** the movement. Fry wrote the catalogue's
  introduction (the unsigned "Editorial" is by Desmond MacCarthy, drafted
  with Fry's input — the authorship has been parsed by Berkowitz 2017).
- **Caveat for the writer:** the prose was hammered out under deadline as a
  defense of unfamiliar pictures; treating it as a doctrinal text would
  flatter it.

### Surrogate 2 — Cézanne to Émile Bernard, 15 April 1904

- **Document:** the *cylinder, sphere, cone* letter (see §3 above).
- **Why it functions as a manifesto:** it is the **one sentence** Picasso,
  Braque, Bernard, and a century of art-historical surveys quote when they
  need to make Cézanne the father of modern art. The letter itself is
  private and tactical (advice to a younger painter); the *quotation* is
  the founding text.
- **Verifiable public source:** **Courtauld Gallery online collection,
  ms-1932-SC-1-1** (full digitization, 4 pages); transcript in Danchev,
  *The Letters of Paul Cézanne* (Thames & Hudson, 2013). [Courtauld]

### How to write the absence

The manifesto block does **not** claim Post-Impressionism had a manifesto.
It tells the *silence* — "Cézanne wrote a letter, Fry wrote a catalogue, and
between them they made a movement that the five anchors would have refused to
call a movement." This is the same template the Cubism page uses (Picasso
& Braque issued no statement; Gleizes & Metzinger's *Du «Cubisme»* 1912 is
the surrogate).

## 5. THE FIVE ANCHORS — biographical fact ledgers

Brevity = good. Each ledger is what the writer needs in front of them.

### Paul Cézanne (1839–1906)

- **Born:** 19 January 1839, Aix-en-Provence. **Died:** 22 October 1906,
  Aix-en-Provence (caught in a rainstorm while painting, pneumonia). [W-Cz]
- **National origin:** French (Provençal).
- **Father:** **Louis-Auguste Cézanne**, banker (co-founded Banque
  Cézanne-Cabassol) — the inheritance funded Cézanne's life-long refusal of
  the Paris market. [W-Cz]
- **Training:** École spéciale de dessin, Aix; arrived Paris 1861; rejected
  repeatedly by the École des Beaux-Arts and the Salon (his Salon
  rejections are the longest of any major Impressionist-era painter).
- **Key signature works:** the **Card Players series** (1890–1895, five
  versions); the **Mont Sainte-Victoire series** (~30+ oils + many
  watercolours from 1880s to 1906); **Large Bathers** (three versions,
  1894–1905). [W-Cz]
- **The defining move:** building the picture out of small, parallel
  brushstrokes that read as **planes** rather than illusion. "I want to make
  of Impressionism something solid and durable, like the art of the
  museums." (Quoted in Bernard's recollections; treat as Cézanne's
  paraphrased aim, not a verbatim quote.) [W-Cz]
- **The humanizing fact:** he lived most of his life in Aix, increasingly
  reclusive, and was nearly forgotten by the Paris art world until **Ambroise
  Vollard**'s solo show, **1895** (Galerie Vollard, Paris, ~150 works) made
  him visible again — and even then mostly to other painters. [W-Cz]
- **The afterlife event:** the **1907 Salon d'Automne Cézanne
  retrospective**, **1 October – 22 October 1907**, **~56 works**. The
  Salon had also dedicated memorial rooms in 1904, 1905, and 1906 — so the
  effect was cumulative, not one show. Picasso, Braque, Matisse, Léger all
  said in later years that the **late Cézannes were the trigger** for what
  became Cubism and Fauvism. [W-Cz][W-Cubism]

### Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890)

- **Born:** 30 March 1853, Zundert, Netherlands. **Died:** 29 July 1890,
  Auvers-sur-Oise (gunshot wound, 27 July; died 1:30 a.m. 29 July).
  [W-VG][W-DeathVG]
- **National origin:** Dutch.
- **Career arc (key dates):**
  - 1869–76: dealer (Goupil & Cie, The Hague / London / Paris).
  - 1879: lay preacher among miners, the Borinage, Belgium.
  - 1881: starts drawing seriously (age 28).
  - 1886–87: Paris — meets Impressionists, encounters Japanese prints.
  - **Feb 1888:** moves to **Arles**.
  - **May 1888:** rents the **Yellow House**, 2 place Lamartine.
  - **23 Oct 1888:** **Gauguin arrives** in Arles to share the Yellow House.
  - **23 Dec 1888:** the **ear-cutting** — Van Gogh threatens Gauguin with
    a razor, later that evening cuts off part of his left ear and delivers
    it to a brothel worker named Rachel. [W-Bandaged]
  - **8 May 1889 – May 1890:** voluntary admission to **Saint-Paul-de-Mausole
    asylum, Saint-Rémy-de-Provence**. *Starry Night* painted there, June 1889.
  - **20 May – 29 July 1890:** **Auvers-sur-Oise**, under Dr Paul Gachet.
  - **27 July 1890:** shot in chest/abdomen in a field near Auvers;
    staggers back to Auberge Ravoux.
  - **29 July 1890, 1:30 a.m.:** dies, Theo at his bedside. Last words
    (Theo's report to their mother): *"La tristesse durera toujours"* — "the
    sadness will last forever."
- **Theo:** Vincent's younger brother, art dealer (Boussod, Valadon & Cie),
  Vincent's lifelong financial and emotional support. Theo dies **25 January
  1891**, six months after Vincent (syphilis-related complications, age 33).
- **Sales in life:** **one painting certainly sold for money** — ***The Red
  Vineyard*** to **Anna Boch** at **Les XX, Brussels** (the avant-garde
  Belgian exhibiting society's annual show, **18 January – 23 February
  1890**, though the sale was completed in **March/April 1890** before the
  show closed/after Van Gogh shipped). Price **400 francs (~£16)**. Anna
  Boch was the sister of Eugène Boch, a painter Van Gogh portrayed. The
  Vineyard is now at the **Pushkin State Museum, Moscow** (Shchukin
  collection nationalized). [W-RV][VGM]
- **The Helene Kröller-Müller collection** — the first systematic Van Gogh
  collector. From ~1908 onward Helene Kröller-Müller (wife of Dutch
  industrialist Anton Kröller) bought hundreds of Van Goghs; the collection
  became the **Kröller-Müller Museum**, Otterlo, NL (opened 1938), which
  holds ~90 Van Gogh paintings — the second-largest holding after the Van
  Gogh Museum, Amsterdam. [W-VG]
- **The modern record:** ***Portrait of Dr Gachet***, sold **Christie's New
  York, 15 May 1990, lot 21, $82.5 million** to **Ryoei Saito** (honorary
  chairman, Daishowa Paper Manufacturing). Held the auction record for
  Impressionist/Post-Impressionist work for years. [W-Gachet]

### Paul Gauguin (1848–1903)

- **Born:** 7 June 1848, Paris. **Died:** 8 May 1903, **Atuona, Hiva Oa,
  Marquesas Islands** (French Polynesia). [W-Gauguin]
- **National origin:** French (with Peruvian maternal grandmother — Flora
  Tristan, the socialist writer; he spent ages 1–7 in Lima).
- **Career arc:**
  - 1865–71: merchant marine, then French Navy.
  - **1871:** stockbroker, Paris (Galichon Bourdon).
  - **1873:** marries **Mette-Sophie Gad** (1850–1920), Danish; five
    children.
  - **1882–83 stock-market crash:** quits the bourse; commits to painting.
  - **1886:** first summer at **Pont-Aven**, Brittany.
  - **1887:** Panama and Martinique trip.
  - **1888:** *Vision after the Sermon* (Pont-Aven, with Bernard).
  - **23 Oct – 23 Dec 1888:** Arles with Van Gogh.
  - **1 April 1891:** sails for **Tahiti**, arrives Papeete 9 June 1891.
  - **1893–95:** brief return to France.
  - **1895:** sails again for Tahiti, never returns.
  - **1897–98:** paints ***Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are
    We Going?*** and reportedly attempts suicide by arsenic afterward.
  - **1901:** moves to Hiva Oa (Marquesas), builds the "Maison du Jouir."
  - **8 May 1903:** dies on Hiva Oa, age 54. [W-Gauguin]
- **The defining move:** **Synthetism** — flat color areas separated by
  dark contours (*cloisonnisme*), pictorial symbol over optical record.
  Worked out at Pont-Aven 1888 with **Émile Bernard**; the credit for who
  invented what is one of the long-running 19th-century art quarrels.
- **The Vollard exhibition:** **Galerie Vollard, Paris, November 1898 — ~9
  Tahitian paintings and 1 sculpture**. The show that began Western
  critical interest in Gauguin. [W-Gauguin]
- **The estate sale on Tahiti, 1903:** after his death, his pictures sold
  cheap — most were dispersed for a fraction of what they would fetch a
  decade later. **Specific prices:** hedge, don't cite figures without
  re-verification.

### Georges Seurat (1859–1891)

- **Born:** 2 December 1859, Paris. **Died:** 29 March 1891, Paris, age 31.
  [W-Seurat]
- **National origin:** French.
- **Cause of death:** **uncertain** — Wikipedia: "variously attributed to a
  form of meningitis, pneumonia, infectious angina, and diphtheria." His
  infant son **Pierre Georges** also died days later of the same illness.
  Frame as "a sudden infection — diphtheria or meningitis, the record is
  unclear." [W-Seurat]
- **Training:** École municipale de sculpture et de dessin, Paris;
  **École des Beaux-Arts, 1878** under **Henri Lehmann** (a pupil of
  Ingres). Then voracious self-study of color theory: **Chevreul**,
  **Charles Blanc** (*Grammaire des arts du dessin*, 1867), **Ogden Rood**
  (*Modern Chromatics*, 1879 — translated 1881 as *Théorie scientifique des
  couleurs*). [W-Seurat]
- **Founded the Société des Artistes Indépendants**, **1884**, with Signac,
  Redon, Henri Edmond Cross, and others — to mount a **jury-free annual
  Salon des Indépendants** in opposition to the official Salon system.
  [W-Seurat]
- **Key signature works:** *Bathers at Asnières* (1884, NG London);
  *La Grande Jatte* (1884–86, AIC); *Models* (1886–88, Barnes
  Foundation); *Parade de cirque* (1887–88, Met); *The Circus*
  (1890–91, Orsay — unfinished at his death). [W-Seurat]
- **The defining move:** **Divisionism / Pointillism** — color theory
  translated into discrete touches of pure pigment, optically combined by
  the viewer's eye. The technical name *chromo-luminarisme* never stuck;
  *Néo-Impressionnisme* (coined by critic Félix Fénéon in 1886) did.
- **Where he showed *La Grande Jatte*:** the **8th Impressionist
  Exhibition**, **15 May – 15 June 1886**, above Maison Dorée, rue
  Laffitte, Paris. Catalogue No. 175 (verify). [W-Seurat]

### Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec (1864–1901)

- **Born:** 24 November 1864, Albi, France. **Died:** 9 September 1901,
  Château Malromé, Gironde, age 36. [W-TL]
- **National origin:** French aristocrat — **Henri Marie Raymond de
  Toulouse-Lautrec-Monfa**, descendant of the medieval Counts of Toulouse.
- **Parents:** first cousins (his parents' consanguinity is documented and
  is widely cited as the cause of the inherited disorder).
- **The disorder:** broke both femurs (left in 1878 aged 13; right in 1879
  aged 14); the legs failed to grow normally afterward. **Modern best
  guess: pycnodysostosis** (sometimes called "Toulouse-Lautrec syndrome"),
  but this is an inference, not a confirmed diagnosis. Final adult
  height: **~1.52 m / ~5 ft 0 in**. [W-TL]
- **Career arc:** moves to **Montmartre** in 1884; resident at 7 rue
  Tourlaque from 1887; lives among and depicts the dance halls, cafés-concerts,
  cabarets, and brothels.
- **The poster move:** **1891**, his first poster — ***Moulin Rouge: La
  Goulue*** — appears on Paris hoardings and *makes* the modern art
  poster. He produces ~31 posters and ~370 lithographs.
- **Key signature works:** ***At the Moulin Rouge*** (1892–95, AIC);
  ***In the Salon at the Rue des Moulins*** (1894, Musée Toulouse-Lautrec,
  Albi); ***Jane Avril*** (poster, 1893).
- **Death:** complications of **alcoholism and syphilis**, in his mother's
  château. He had been institutionalized for alcoholism in 1899. [W-TL]
- **The museum:** the **Musée Toulouse-Lautrec, Albi**, opened 1922 in the
  Palais de la Berbie, holds the largest single collection of his work
  (~1,000 pieces), donated by his mother and the Comtesse Adèle de
  Toulouse-Lautrec.

## 6. THE WIDER CAST — one-line ledgers

### Émile Bernard (1868–1941)

French; meets Gauguin at Pont-Aven, 1888; co-developer of *cloisonnisme* /
**Synthetism** (the credit-quarrel with Gauguin lasted the rest of their
lives). The friend Cézanne wrote to in the famous 1904 letter; published
Cézanne's correspondence and conversations after Cézanne's death (his
*Souvenirs sur Paul Cézanne*, *Mercure de France*, 1907). [W-Bernard]

### Paul Sérusier (1864–1927)

French; painted ***Le Talisman / The Talisman***, October 1888, Pont-Aven —
a small panel (~**27 × 21.5 cm / ~10½ × 8½ in**, **Musée d'Orsay, RF 1985-13**)
painted on the lid of a cigar box under Gauguin's direct instruction (Gauguin
told him to use pure color, no naturalism). Sérusier carried it back to Paris
and showed it to his fellow students at the Académie Julian — they reacted
to it like a religious icon, and named themselves the **Nabis** (Hebrew for
"prophets," chosen by Henri Cazalis). [W-Talisman][W-Nabis]

### Pierre Bonnard (1867–1947)

French; Nabi; intimate domestic interiors and bathers; lifelong companion/wife
**Marthe (Maria Boursin)**. *Dining Room in the Country* (1913, Minneapolis);
*Nude in the Bath* series (1925–46). [W-Bonnard]

### Édouard Vuillard (1868–1940)

French; Nabi; small-format, densely patterned bourgeois interiors. The
**Natanson** household (founders of *La Revue blanche*, Paris) were his
patrons. *Mother and Sister of the Artist* (c.1893, MoMA). [W-Vuillard]

### Maurice Denis (1870–1943)

French; Nabi; the theoretician of the group. **The famous line:** *"Se
rappeler qu'un tableau, avant d'être un cheval de bataille, une femme nue,
ou une quelconque anecdote, est essentiellement une surface plane recouverte
de couleurs en un certain ordre assemblées"* — "Remember that a picture,
before being a battle horse, a nude woman, or any other anecdote, is
essentially a flat surface covered with colors assembled in a certain order."

- **Where:** essay ***Définition du néo-traditionnisme***, **published
  August 1890** in the periodical ***Art et Critique***, signed "Pierre
  Louis" (his pseudonym at the time).
- **Caveat:** Denis was **19** when he wrote this. Wikipedia notes the idea
  was not original (compare Hippolyte Taine's *Philosophie de l'art*, 1865)
  — but Denis's wording became the catechism. [W-Denis]

### Paul Signac (1863–1935)

French; Seurat's pointillist partner from 1884; after Seurat's death (1891)
became the propagandist for **Néo-Impressionnisme**. Wrote ***D'Eugène
Delacroix au néo-impressionnisme***, **Paris, Éditions de la Revue blanche,
1899** — the book that codified Divisionism as a theory. President of the
Société des Artistes Indépendants from 1908. [W-Signac]

### Odilon Redon (1840–1916)

French; the **Symbolist** tributary. Two careers in one: the early *noirs*
(charcoal drawings and lithographs of monsters, eyes, and dreams, ~1870–95),
then a late-life conversion to luminous **pastel and oil** in saturated
color (~1895 onward). Showed at the **Salon d'Automne** and in the **1910
Grafton show**. [W-Redon]

### Henri Rousseau (1844–1910)

**Le Douanier ("the customs officer")** — French; tollkeeper at the Paris
city gates; self-taught; **the naïve outsider**. Late tropical jungle
pictures (he never left France — the jungles came from the **Jardin des
Plantes** in Paris). ***The Dream*** (1910, MoMA, 204.5 × 298.5 cm /
**~6 ft 8½ in × 9 ft 9½ in**, his largest jungle picture, exhibited at the
**Salon des Indépendants, 18 March – 1 May 1910**, months before he died on
**2 September 1910**). Picasso bought a Rousseau cheap from a junk dealer
and held the famous **"Banquet Rousseau"** in his Bateau-Lavoir studio in
**November 1908** — half tribute, half joke, attended by Apollinaire,
Gertrude Stein, Braque, Marie Laurencin. **Hedge his placement** — Rousseau
is **adjacent** to Post-Impressionism, not central; he's better called an
*outsider whom the avant-garde elected as a forerunner.* [W-Rousseau][W-Dream]

### Mary Cassatt (1844–1926)

American (Pittsburgh-born; lived in France from 1874); the only American in
the inner Impressionist circle; close friend of **Degas**, who pulled her
into the **8th Impressionist Exhibition (1886)** and beyond. Her late work,
after a major **1890 exhibition of Japanese woodblock prints at the École
des Beaux-Arts**, turned overtly to **Japonisme** — *The Child's Bath*
(1893, AIC) is her signature piece. [W-Cassatt]

## 7. PUBLIC RECEPTION — beyond the 1910 show

### 1910 Grafton show — reactions

- **Critics:** Robert Ross (*Morning Post*) — "a widespread plot to destroy
  the whole fabric of European painting"; Charles Ricketts dismissed it;
  *The Times* called it "the rejection of all that civilization has done,
  the good and the bad." [Berkowitz]
- **Public:** ~25,000 visitors; many came to laugh. The Grafton's
  visitors' book reportedly recorded comments like "Pure pornography" and
  "the work of madmen." [Berkowitz]
- **Defenders:** Roger Fry himself; Desmond MacCarthy (catalogue); Clive
  Bell; Vanessa Bell; Virginia Woolf (in retrospect). The **Bloomsbury
  Group** in effect formed around the defense of these pictures.

### 1912 Grafton show — softer reception

By **5 October 1912**, the controversy was dulled — Fry could mount a
second show with more recent French art (Matisse, Picasso, Derain) plus a
British and Russian wing, and the press treated it as a known quantity. Some
sources note Roger Fry himself called the public response "less violent."
[Frick]

### American reception — the 1913 Armory Show

- **Title:** *International Exhibition of Modern Art*.
- **Dates and venue:** **17 February – 15 March 1913**, **69th Regiment
  Armory, Lexington Avenue, New York**. Then travelled to Chicago
  (24 Mar – 16 Apr) and Boston (28 Apr – 19 May). [W-Armory]
- **Scope:** ~**1,250 works** by ~**300 artists** (numbers vary by source —
  the often-cited figure is **1,090 works by 306 artists**, depending on
  whether prints/sketches are counted). About **2/3 American, 1/3
  European**.
- **Post-Impressionists present:** **Cézanne (~13 works), Van Gogh (~18),
  Gauguin (~12), Seurat, Redon (largest European retrospective in the
  show, ~32 works), Matisse, Picasso, Duchamp**. Duchamp's *Nude Descending
  a Staircase* was the succès de scandale. [W-Armory]
- **Significance:** the show that introduced American audiences to the
  whole Post-Impressionist + early modern range at once; remembered as the
  birth event of American modernism.

- **Commons filename candidate:** `Paul_Gauguin_-_Vision_after_the_Sermon.jpg`

### 6. Gauguin — *Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?* (1897–98)

- **Museum:** Museum of Fine Arts, Boston (Tompkins Collection — Arthur
  Gordon Tompkins Fund, **36.270**).
- **Dimensions:** 139.1 × 374.6 cm = **~4 ft 6½ in × 12 ft 3½ in**.
- **Medium:** oil on canvas.
- **Blurb fact:** Painted as his testament before an arsenic suicide attempt
  in December 1897 (Gauguin survived). The title is inscribed in the upper
  left corner; he intended the picture to be read right-to-left — birth,
  life, death.
- **Commons filename candidate:** `Paul_Gauguin_-_D'ou_venons-nous.jpg`
  (verify).

### 7. Cézanne — *The Card Players* (1894–95) — choose the **Musée d'Orsay** version

- **Museum:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris (RF 1969-30; bequest Isaac de Camondo).
- **Dimensions:** 47.5 × 57 cm = **~1 ft 6¾ in × 1 ft 10½ in**.
- **Medium:** oil on canvas.
- **Blurb fact:** The smallest of the **five versions** and the most
  reproduced. Two Aix farm laborers, a bottle between them, total silence.
  (The Met version, 1890–92, 65.4 × 81.9 cm, is the second-most reproduced;
  the largest is at the **Barnes Foundation**, Philadelphia, 134.6 × 180.3
  cm — five figures, three players plus two onlookers. A fifth version sold
  privately in 2011 to Qatar for a price variously reported at
  **$250–320 million** — at the time the highest price ever paid for a
  painting.) [W-Cards]
- **Commons filename candidate:** `Paul_Cézanne_-_Les_joueurs_de_cartes,_1892-95.jpg`
  (verify).

### 8. Cézanne — *Mont Sainte-Victoire seen from Les Lauves* (1902–06)

- **Best canonical version for the movement page:** **Philadelphia Museum of
  Art** (The George W. Elkins Collection, **E1936-1-1**), 1902–04.
- **Dimensions:** 73 × 91.9 cm = **~2 ft 4¾ in × 3 ft 0¼ in**.
- **Medium:** oil on canvas.
- **Blurb fact:** Cézanne painted this view ~11 times in oil and many more
  in watercolor from his Les Lauves studio (built 1902) just north of Aix.
  The mountain becomes architecture — the canvas reads as planes of color
  before it reads as landscape. Picasso and Braque called Cézanne *"the
  father of us all"* on the basis of these late views. [W-MtSV][PhilaMuseum]
- **Other major versions** (for the writer's options):
  - Kunstmuseum Basel, 1904–06 (the most abstract/all-planes one).
  - Kunsthaus Zürich, 1902–06.
  - Pushkin Museum, Moscow, 1904–06.
  - Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art, Kansas City.
- **Commons filename candidate:** `Paul_Cézanne_-_Mont_Sainte-Victoire_-_Philadelphia.jpg`
  (verify).

### 9. Toulouse-Lautrec — *At the Moulin Rouge* (1892–95)

- **Museum:** Art Institute of Chicago (Helen Birch Bartlett Memorial
  Collection, **1928.610**).
- **Dimensions:** 123 × 140.5 cm = **~4 ft 0½ in × 4 ft 7¼ in**.
- **Medium:** oil on canvas.
- **Blurb fact:** Lautrec inserted himself in the background (the short man
  in the top hat behind the dancer La Goulue). The lurid green face in the
  right foreground is dancer **May Milton**, sliced off by an off-square
  cropping that reads as a snapshot — a Japanese-print device. The picture
  was reframed shortly after Lautrec's death and the right strip was
  removed for decades; AIC reattached it in 1959.
- **Commons filename candidate:** `Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec_-_At_the_Moulin_Rouge.jpg`
  (verify).

## 9. INFLUENCE LINEAGE — for the lineage block

### Grew out of

- **Impressionism** (1874–86) — the direct parent.
- **Édouard Manet** (1832–83) — the older father-figure both Impressionism
  and Post-Impressionism look back to.
- **Japonisme** — Japan's forced opening (1854) and the flood of *ukiyo-e*
  woodblock prints into Paris from the 1860s. Hokusai, Hiroshige, Utamaro.
  The single biggest non-Western influence on European painting between
  1860 and 1900.
- **Color theory** — **Michel Eugène Chevreul**, ***De la loi du contraste
  simultané des couleurs***, Paris, 1839; **Ogden Rood**, ***Modern
  Chromatics***, New York, 1879 (French translation 1881); **Charles
  Blanc**, ***Grammaire des arts du dessin***, 1867.
- **The 8th and last Impressionist Exhibition**, **15 May – 15 June 1886** —
  where Seurat showed *La Grande Jatte*, where the Impressionist project
  begins to break open.

### Led to

- **Fauvism** (1905) — Matisse, Derain, Vlaminck. Lit fuse: the late Van
  Gogh's emotive color.
- **German Expressionism** —
  - **Die Brücke** ("The Bridge"), founded **Dresden, 7 June 1905** by
    Kirchner, Heckel, Schmidt-Rottluff, Bleyl. Lit fuse: Van Gogh and
    Gauguin.
  - **Der Blaue Reiter** ("The Blue Rider"), founded **Munich, December
    1911** by Kandinsky and Marc. Lit fuse: Gauguin's symbolism + color
    abstraction.
- **Cubism** (Picasso & Braque, Paris, 1907–14) — direct via **Cézanne's
  late landscapes** and the 1907 retrospective. Picasso: *"Cézanne was my
  one and only master."*
- **The Nabis** themselves (Paris, 1888–c.1900) — Bonnard, Vuillard, Denis,
  Sérusier, Ranson, Maillol — direct descendants of Gauguin's Pont-Aven
  Synthetism.
- **Abstraction** — the longest-term consequence. Kandinsky in *Concerning
  the Spiritual in Art* (1911) cites Cézanne directly. Mondrian's pre-1914
  trees come straight out of Cézanne and the Nabis.

## Notes for the writer

1. **Five anchors, not one movement.** Structure the narrative by the five
   answers to Impressionism, not by one "Post-Impressionist" story.
2. **The label is a London import.** Use Fry's 1910 show as the framing
   device for the *naming*, never as the framing device for the *making* —
   the pictures were made in Aix, Arles, Pont-Aven, Tahiti, Paris, between
   1884 and 1906.
3. **The five never met as a group.** The only documented overlap is **Van
   Gogh + Gauguin in Arles, 23 Oct – 23 Dec 1888**. Mention it; use it as
   the dramatic point where two of the five collide and produce nothing
   together but a severed ear.
4. **Hedge the manifesto.** There is no manifesto. The Fry catalogue and
   the Cézanne letter are surrogate documents — say so.
5. **Don't say "Post-Impressionists believed X."** Say "Cézanne wanted X /
   Gauguin wanted Y / Seurat wanted Z." The label is a room, not a creed.
