## 2. THE STORY

In late 1963 and early 1964 Warhol turned his studio (the Factory) into a small box factory. He hired **carpenters to build plywood boxes** to the exact dimensions of supermarket shipping cartons, then he and assistants **Gerard Malanga and Billy Linich (Billy Name)** house-painted them and **silkscreened the brand graphics** onto the sides — not just Brillo, but Mott's Apple Juice, Kellogg's Corn Flakes, Del Monte Peach Halves, Campbell's Tomato Juice, and Heinz Tomato Ketchup.

He unveiled them at his second solo show at the **Stable Gallery, New York, opening April 21, 1964**, filling both rooms with the boxes **stacked in rows several feet high like a grocery warehouse / stockroom** (estimates run to ~400 boxes). A shopper walking in saw what looked like a delivery bay of merchandise — except the "cartons" were hand-built wood, painted and printed to *look* like cardboard.

That gap — object indistinguishable from non-art object — is exactly what stopped the philosopher **Arthur C. Danto**. He encountered the show and later called it transformative; it turned him into a philosopher of art. In his essay **"The Artworld," published in *The Journal of Philosophy*, October 15, 1964**, he used Warhol's box to ask: when a thing that is *art* looks identical to a thing that is *not* art, what makes the difference? His answer — that the difference is not visible, but a matter of **theory and context ("the artworld")** — became foundational, and he later developed it into his **"end of art"** thesis (art's history, as a search for its own definition, had reached its philosophical terminus).

**The authorship irony:** the very design Warhol "appropriated" had itself been made by an artist. **James Harvey**, an Abstract Expressionist painter, had designed the commercial Brillo carton as a day-job package job. So the "found" commercial graphic was already someone's artwork-adjacent labor — and Harvey, watching a Pop artist get the credit and the money for *his* box, complained publicly (see §4 / §8).

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK *(required — before / after)*

**Before:** A sculpture was something you judged by *looking* — its form, surface, handling, composition told you it was art and told you it was good. Even Pop's painted soup cans were still pictures *of* products, hung on a wall as obvious paintings. The art object and the everyday object stayed visibly distinct.

**The break:** *Brillo Box* makes the art object **visually indistinguishable from the commercial object it copies.** Standing in the gallery, you cannot tell the Warhol from a real stockroom carton by eye. So the question that decides whether it's art **shifts off the retina** — away from how it looks — and onto **theory, intention, and context**: who made it, why, where it stands, what idea it carries. Warhol weaponized *sameness* instead of difference.

**After:** This is a founding move of **Conceptual art** and the institutional/contextual theories of art that followed — the idea that art is constituted by an idea and an "artworld," not by a special appearance. It is the literal object Danto built his "Artworld" / "end of art" philosophy on. After the Brillo Box, "but is it art?" is permanently a question about *framework*, not *craft*.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT *(verbatim, sourced; variants flagged)*

**Primary (Danto's framing of the Brillo Box question):**

> "What in the end makes the difference between a Brillo box and a work of art consisting of a Brillo box is a certain theory of art."
> — **Arthur C. Danto, "The Artworld," *The Journal of Philosophy*, Vol. 61, No. 19 (Oct. 15, 1964), p. 581.**

- **Variant / continuation (verify exact wording before quoting the tail).** The sentence is frequently extended in secondary sources with "…It is the theory that takes it up into the world of art, and keeps it from collapsing into the real object which it is." That continuation is widely attributed to the same essay but I did **not** confirm it word-for-word against the primary text in this pass — **flag: quote the first sentence only unless the continuation is verified.**
- **Danto, later (1994), on the same idea** (verify edition): "Something can be an artwork which looks just like a real thing. What makes the one art may be something quite invisible, perhaps how it arrived in the world and what someone intended it to be." — attributed to Danto, "The Philosopher as Andy Warhol" (Warhol Museum inaugural publication, 1994).

**Alternative key statement (Warhol's own voice, if a Warhol quote is preferred over Danto):**

> "The reason I'm painting this way is that I want to be a machine, and I feel that whatever I do and do machine-like is what I want to do."
> — **Andy Warhol, interview with Gene Swenson, "What Is Pop Art?", *ARTnews*, November 1963.**

- Note: the "machine" quote is from **1963**, the year *before* the Brillo show — use it as context for his mass-production stance, not as a statement *about* the boxes specifically. The Danto quote is the on-the-nose key statement for this work; recommend leading with Danto.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The red-white-and-blue Brillo design.** White ground (literally white house paint), with the word **BRILLO** printed so the consonants read navy blue and the vowels cherry red, plus a **swooping red wave** arcing across the top of the logo — a deliberately patriotic, all-American supermarket palette.
2. **The "24 GIANT SIZE PKGS" line.** Across the top of the side panel, blue capitals advertise the carton's contents — "24 GIANT SIZE PKGS." It's wholesale-shipping language, not gallery language: a reminder you're looking at a *bulk carton*, not a single retail box.
3. **"SHINES ALUMINUM FAST" / "NEW!"** Along the bottom runs the product promise "SHINES ALUMINUM FAST"; a white **"New!"** with an exclamation point sits in the upper corner. Advertising copy, reproduced deadpan as the surface of a sculpture.
4. **The silkscreened logo *on wood*.** The graphics aren't printed cardboard — they're **silkscreen ink laid onto a solid wooden box.** Look for the matte, slightly flattened ink sitting on a hard painted surface rather than the sheen and give of a real cardboard carton.
5. **Handmade imperfections vs. a real carton.** Because each box was hand-painted and hand-pulled through a screen, no two are perfectly identical, and the registration/edges are subtly *off* from a machine-printed shipping carton. The near-perfection is the point — and the tiny imperfections are where the human hand leaks back in.
6. **The box-as-sculpture, and the stack.** A single box is a freestanding **cube on the floor** — a sculpture you walk around, not a picture on a wall. En masse at the Stable Gallery they were **stacked in rows like a warehouse**, so the "art" arrived as inventory: quantity, repetition, and retail display became the form.

## 6. PROVENANCE / EDITIONS

- **Not a unique object.** Warhol's studio produced **hundreds** of these boxes across multiple campaigns and several carton designs; *Brillo Box* exists in many examples spread across major museums and private collections (see §1 locations). The **wired image is a representative original**, anchored to MoMA's 1964 box.
- **1964 Stable Gallery campaign** — the original, canonical boxes (the ones Danto saw).
- **1968 Stockholm (Moderna Museet) campaign** — a batch made in connection with Warhol's 1968 retrospective; sometimes called "Stockholm type" / "1968 type" boxes.
- **Later-editions controversy (proportionate).** In **1990 — three years after Warhol's 1987 death — Moderna Museet's founding director Pontus Hultén commissioned ~105 Brillo boxes from a carpenter in Malmö, Sweden**, using a 1968-era box as the model. He later represented them as 1968 "originals" made to Warhol's instructions and sold many into the market with that provenance. Exposed by the Swedish press (*Expressen*, 2007). In **2010 the Andy Warhol Art Authentication Board classified them as "copies"** — distinguishing the 1968 "Stockholm" boxes (exhibition-related copies) from the 1990 "Malmö" boxes (exhibition copies / not authentic Warhols). One Malmö box had sold for over $200,000 at Christie's in 2006. **This affects only the posthumous Malmö batch — it does not impugn the genuine 1964 originals.**

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS *(corrected, with sources)*

- **TRAP — "Warhol designed the Brillo look."** No. The carton's red-white-blue graphic was designed by **James Harvey**, a commercial artist who was *also* an Abstract Expressionist painter. Warhol copied an existing package. Harvey's gallery issued a press release on his behalf — "Andy is running away with my box" — over Warhol getting the credit (and sales) for Harvey's design. **The authorship irony is real and VERIFIED.** (Wikipedia: James Harvey; *ARTnews*.)
- **UNCERTAINTY — what year Harvey designed the carton.** Sources disagree: commonly cited as **1961**, but the James Harvey biography sources also say **1959** (designed at Stuart and Gunn). **Flag this; if a year is needed, say "around 1960 (sources give 1959–1961)" rather than asserting one.**
- **TRAP — "they're cardboard boxes" / "found objects."** They are **not** cardboard and **not** readymades. Each is a **carpenter-built plywood/wood box**, house-painted and **silkscreened** to imitate cardboard. The whole point is that it is hand-fabricated to look mass-produced — closer to *simulation* than to a Duchamp readymade. (MoMA; Whitney.)
- **TRAP — treating all "Brillo Boxes" as interchangeable originals.** The **1990 Malmö boxes are posthumous copies** ruled "copies" by the Warhol Authentication Board in 2010; don't present a Malmö box as a 1964 original. Keep this **proportionate** — it's a provenance footnote, not the work's headline. (*ARTnews*; *The Art Newspaper*; Art & Object.)
- **DIMENSIONS — pin to the source.** Use **MoMA's 17 1/8 × 17 × 14 in / 43.3 × 43.2 × 36.5 cm** for the cited box; "~17 × 17 × 14 in" is fine as a round figure. Don't invent a single "official" dimension for the whole series — examples vary slightly.
- **QUOTE TRAP — the Danto continuation.** The famous first sentence (§4) is solid. The "…It is the theory that takes it up into the world of art…" tail is widely quoted but **not verified word-for-word here** — don't append it unless confirmed against the 1964 *Journal of Philosophy* text.
