## 2. HOOK MATERIAL

Candidate hook (author to polish): a nine-foot-wide building site where a giant red workhorse, lunging out of a haze of dust and feathered color, drags the men trying to hold it — Boccioni's first all-out Futurist canvas, painting *labour and the modern city* with the violence the academy saved for battle scenes. [facts all DOCUMENTED below]

Why it's a real hook, not hype: this is genuinely **his first major Futurist painting** — the canvas where the Futurist theory (manifestos, below) first became a monumental picture. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia, Smarthistory/Hypercritic, MoMA all call it his first major/first Futurist work]

## 3. NARRATIVE BEATS (~5)

### Beat A — The manifestos: a movement before its paintings
- **Marinetti's founding *Manifesto of Futurism***: written by Filippo Tommaso Marinetti; first appeared in Italy (preface to a poetry volume, Milan, Jan 1909; then the *Gazzetta dell'Emilia*, Bologna, 5 Feb 1909), and famously published in French on the **front page of *Le Figaro*, Paris, 20 February 1909**. [DOCUMENTED — EBSCO, Britannica, DailyArt]
  - The brief the user supplied says "Le Figaro, 20 Feb 1909" — CORRECT, but note for accuracy: it was *not* its first-ever appearance, just its famous one. Author may say "splashed across the front page of *Le Figaro*" without claiming it was the debut.
- **The painters then organized.** *Manifesto of the Futurist Painters* (*Manifesto dei pittori futuristi*), **11 February 1910**, signed by Boccioni, Carlo Carrà, Luigi Russolo, Giacomo Balla, Gino Severini. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia]
  - First read publicly at the Politeama Chiarella, **Turin, 8 March 1910**. [DOCUMENTED — search]
- **Boccioni's *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting*** (*Manifesto tecnico della pittura futurista*), **11 April 1910**, same five signatories (Boccioni, Balla, Carrà, Russolo, Severini). This is the "how to paint it" document — universal dynamism, the spectator placed *inside* the picture, "lines of force." [DOCUMENTED — search; Wikipedia]
  - CAUTION: a separate document, the *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Sculpture*, is **April 1912** and is Boccioni's alone — do NOT mix it up with the 1910 painting manifesto.

### Beat B — Boccioni paints the theory: the building site
- Subject: a **building site / urban construction in a growing suburb of Milan**, men and horses straining at heavy work amid scaffolding, smokestacks/chimneys, and (per several readings) trams in the background. Boccioni takes *modern labour and the rising industrial city* as a heroic subject. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia, Smarthistory, Hypercritic]
- The exhibition title *Il lavoro* ("The Work"/"Labour") at the 1911 Mostra d'arte libera underlines that the painting is, at its core, about **work and the construction of the modern city**. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia]

### Beat C — The great red horse
- The dominant figure is a **huge red workhorse** surging forward; the small human workers cling to and are dragged/knocked about as they try to control it. The horse reads as the raw energy, violence and speed of the city's growth. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia: "domesticated but straining against its tethers," "violence, energy, and speed of progress"]
- FRAME-IT note: the common line that the horse "knocks the workers to the ground" is a reading of the action — supportable but describe what's literally on the canvas (men leaning back, hauling, the horse lunging) and keep the symbolic reading as a reading. [DOCUMENTED action / interpretive symbolism]

### Beat D — Divisionism meets Futurist dynamism
- Boccioni's technique here is still **Divisionism** — the Italian descendant of Seurat's pointillism: color laid in small separate strokes that mix in the eye for a vibrating, luminous surface. Boccioni had come up in this tradition. [DOCUMENTED — Smarthistory: "His technique still owes much to Divisionism, a movement to which he had until then belonged, together with Gaetano Previati and Giovanni Segantini."]
- KEY for the "break": in *The City Rises* the Divisionist stroke is stretched into **long, feathered, directional brushstrokes** that follow the motion — so the old optical-color method is bent to serve the NEW Futurist goal of *dynamism* (movement, force, energy). Divisionist surface + Futurist subject/motion = the hinge of the picture. [DOCUMENTED — Smarthistory; Hypercritic; multiple]
- Context names for the clarity gate: **Gaetano Previati** and **Giovanni Segantini** = the leading Italian Divisionists Boccioni came out of (Previati especially as a theorist of the technique). **Georges Seurat** = the French pointillist source. [DOCUMENTED]

### Beat E — The first major Futurist painting (and its afterlife)
- Repeatedly described as **Boccioni's first major Futurist work / first Futurist painting** — the turning point from his Divisionist period into Futurism proper. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia, Hypercritic, MoMA wall text framing]
- It became a **headline/touring work** for Futurism's travelling European exhibitions of 1912 (Paris, London, Berlin, etc.), where it was bought (provenance below). [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia, Boccioni bio]
- Boccioni's later, more radically fractured Futurist work (e.g. the *States of Mind* series, 1911; the sculpture *Unique Forms of Continuity in Space*, 1913) goes further — *The City Rises* is the doorway, still half in Divisionism. [DOCUMENTED — context]

## 4. SIX "LOOK CLOSER" ANNOTATIONS

Each is `{label, where, detail}` — a verifiable claim about what is literally on the canvas, checked against the Commons full-res image (2583 × 1715 px). `where` is a written location phrase; NO crop coordinates. Author should re-eyeball against the image at integration; these are drafted from the high-res file + sources.

1. **The great red horse** — *where:* center / center-left, the massive animal lunging forward and to the left, filling the heart of the canvas. *detail:* The painting's engine: a huge red-brown draft horse, far larger than any worker, caught mid-surge. It is not a real horse-color so much as a charge of energy; everything in the composition is dragged into its motion. Read it as the city's growth itself — power that the men can barely hold. (Symbolic reading is interpretation; the lunging red horse is verifiably there.) [DOCUMENTED]

2. **The straining workers** — *where:* clustered around and below the horse, foreground, leaning hard against it. *detail:* Small human figures grip harness and ropes and lean their whole weight back, hauling against the animal. Boccioni shrinks the men and enlarges the beast so the *labour* reads as a struggle of force against force — the dignity (and danger) of modern work made heroic. [DOCUMENTED]

3. **The construction scaffolding** — *where:* background, right and upper-right, behind the dust. *detail:* Thin scaffolds, the skeleton of buildings going up, plus factory chimneys/smokestacks. This is the literal "city rising": a suburb under construction. The architecture is faint and hazed compared with the horse, kept deliberately secondary to the energy in front of it. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia/Smarthistory cite scaffolding, chimneys, industry under construction]

4. **The blurred motion** — *where:* throughout, but strongest around the horse's body and the workers' limbs. *detail:* Edges dissolve; the figures smear into the air around them. Boccioni paints not a frozen instant but the *passage* of movement — the Futurist idea (from his Technical Manifesto) that a body in motion multiplies and merges with its surroundings, so "things in motion" are shown as a haze of force, not a snapshot. [DOCUMENTED — technique + manifesto]

5. **The Divisionist strokes** — *where:* anywhere across the surface; clearest in the open ground and the horse's flank. *detail:* The whole picture is built from countless small, separate strokes of bright, often complementary color (Divisionism — the Italian heir of Seurat's dot). Up close it's a flicker of distinct touches; step back and they fuse into a vibrating glow. Here the strokes are pulled long and directional so the optical-color method also carries the *motion*. [DOCUMENTED]

6. **The swirling composition** — *where:* the overall design — figures, dust, and color wheeling around the central horse. *detail:* Rather than a stable horizontal scene, everything spirals inward toward the lunging horse: diagonals of rope, hauling bodies, and feathered strokes all curve into one centrifugal whirl. The composition itself enacts dynamism — there's no calm place for the eye to rest. [DOCUMENTED action / interpretive read of the design]

> Annotation honesty note for the fact-checker: #1 and #6 carry interpretive symbolism ("the city's energy," "enacts dynamism"). Keep the literal-on-canvas claim (red horse / spiral design) as the spine and frame the meaning as reading. None of the six asserts a disputed visual fact.

## 6. KEY FIGURES / CAST

- **Umberto Boccioni (1882–1916)** — the painter. Born 19 Oct 1882, Reggio Calabria. The leading painter-theorist of Italian Futurism. Trained in Rome under **Giacomo Balla**, who introduced him to Divisionism. Signed both 1910 painting manifestos; wrote the *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting* (1910) and the *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Sculpture* (1912). **Died 17 August 1916, age 33**, at a Verona military hospital, the day after he was **thrown from his horse and trampled during a cavalry training exercise** while serving in the Italian Army in WWI (he had volunteered; stationed near Verona, Sorte di Chievo). [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia, Met, multiple]
  - IRONY note [DOCUMENTED as fact; the "prefiguring" framing is interpretive]: Boccioni — who put a violent horse at the center of *The City Rises* and again in *Charge of the Lancers* (1915) — was killed by a horse. State the death plainly; if the author wants the resonance, frame it as the kind of irony history hands out, not as if the paintings foretold it.
- **Filippo Tommaso Marinetti (1876–1944)** — poet, founder and impresario of Futurism; wrote the founding *Manifesto of Futurism* (*Le Figaro*, 20 Feb 1909). The movement's spokesman and financier; not the painter of this work. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Giacomo Balla (1871–1958)** — Boccioni's **teacher**, who introduced him (and Severini) to Divisionism; co-signer of the 1910 painters' manifestos. The bridge between Divisionism and Futurism. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Gaetano Previati (1852–1920)** — leading Italian Divisionist and theorist of the technique; named (with Segantini) as the Divisionist tradition Boccioni came out of. [DOCUMENTED — Smarthistory]
- **Giovanni Segantini (1858–1899)** — the other major Italian Divisionist Boccioni is grouped with. [DOCUMENTED — Smarthistory]
- (Context, optional cast) **Ferruccio Busoni (1866–1924)** — composer/pianist; first private owner, bought it London 1912. [DOCUMENTED]

## 8. LEGEND / DISPUTED / FRAME-IT LEDGER (the apocrypha catch)

- 🟡 **"First major Futurist painting"** — well-supported and repeated by MoMA/Smarthistory/Wikipedia, but it's a "first" claim, the exact trap the pipeline warns about. SAFE to state, but keep it Western/Futurism-scoped ("his first major Futurist painting" / "the first monumental Futurist canvas"), not a universal "first modern X." [DOCUMENTED but scope it]
- 🟡 **Le Figaro, 20 Feb 1909** — TRUE for the famous *Le Figaro* front-page printing, but it was not the manifesto's first-ever appearance (Italian printings preceded it in Jan–Feb 1909). Don't write "first published in *Le Figaro*"; write "published / splashed across the front page of *Le Figaro*." [DISPUTED-as-usually-told]
- 🟡 **"The horse knocks the workers to the ground"** — a common, defensible *reading* of the action; describe the literal hauling/lunging and keep the knock-down as interpretation. [interpretive]
- 🟡 **The death "prefigured" by his horse paintings** — the death (trampled by a horse, 1916) is DOCUMENTED fact; the "his paintings foretold it" framing is a romantic gloss. State the fact; frame the resonance as irony, not prophecy. [LEGEND framing on a real fact]
- ⚠️ **1912 Busoni purchase price** — NO figure found in sources. Leave `price: null`; do not invent. [UNKNOWN]
- ⚠️ **Date "1910–11"** — MoMA dates the painting **1910**; the *1911* is the *Il lavoro* exhibition, not a second painting campaign. Author should say "1910" for the work and treat 1911 as the exhibition year, to avoid implying a long 1910–11 execution. [precision]
- ⚠️ **Don't conflate the manifestos:** *Manifesto of the Futurist Painters* (11 Feb 1910) ≠ *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting* (11 April 1910) ≠ *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Sculpture* (April 1912). All three get mixed up online. [precision]
- ⚠️ **Separate objects:** MoMA also owns a small *Study for The City Rises* (1910, MoMA 36544); the Estorick Collection (London) holds another study. The work-read is the big 199 × 301 cm canvas (507.1951) only. [precision]
