## 1. CORE FACTS

- **Primary title (MoMA):** ***Object*** — that is MoMA's catalog title, plain. The work is
  cataloged/described as *Object (Fur-covered cup, saucer, and spoon)*; the parenthetical is the
  medium pressed into service as a descriptor, widely used as a working title.
  [MoMA via Smarthistory; Khan Academy]
- **French / coined title:** ***Le Déjeuner en fourrure*** — usually rendered **"Luncheon in
  Fur"** or **"Lunch in Fur."** This was **not Oppenheim's title** — it was coined by **André
  Breton**, the Surrealist leader, as a pun on Manet's *Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe* (and glancing at
  Sacher-Masoch's *Venus in Furs*). Oppenheim's own working title was prosaic: roughly *"Cup,
  saucer and spoon covered with fur."* English variants in circulation: *Breakfast in Fur*, *Fur
  Breakfast*, *Object in Fur*. [Wikipedia *Le Déjeuner en fourrure*; Smarthistory; NPR]
- **Artist:** **Méret Oppenheim** (German-born Swiss Surrealist; b. Berlin 1913 — d. Basel 1985).
  [Britannica; Wikipedia]
- **Date:** **1936**, Paris. [MoMA; all sources]
- **Medium:** **Fur-covered cup, saucer, and spoon** — an ordinary porcelain/white teacup, saucer,
  and metal spoon (bought at a Paris department store, variously Monoprix; the Schiaparelli/Uniprix
  retellings differ — **[VERIFY @ MoMA]**), wrapped/covered in the speckled tan fur of a **Chinese
  gazelle**. [MoMA medium line via Smarthistory/Khan; Wikipedia]
- **Dimensions (MoMA, both systems):**
  - Cup: **4 3/8 in / 10.9 cm** diameter
  - Saucer: **9 3/8 in / 23.7 cm** diameter
  - Spoon: **8 in / 20.2 cm** long
  - Overall height (assembled): **2 7/8 in / 7.3 cm**
  [Smarthistory / Khan Academy quoting MoMA] — **[VERIFY @ MoMA]** the overall-height figure.
- **Current location:** **The Museum of Modern Art, New York.** Credit line: **Purchase.**
  Acquisition year tied to **1946** (see §6 for the study-collection vs. permanent-collection
  nuance). [MoMA; Artsy; Wikipedia]

## 2. THE STORY

- **The café spark.** The standard account: in 1936 Oppenheim was at a Paris café (the
  retellings name **Café de Flore**) with **Pablo Picasso** and his partner **Dora Maar**.
  Oppenheim was wearing a **fur-covered metal-tube bracelet** of her own design (a pattern she
  had sold to the couturière **Elsa Schiaparelli**). Picasso admired it and joked that you could
  cover anything in fur; Oppenheim shot back **"Even this cup and saucer?"** — and, as her tea
  went cold, reportedly asked the waiter for **"un peu plus de fourrure"** ("a little more fur").
  She made the object shortly after. Present this as the **standard account**, not gospel — see
  §8. [NPR; Wikipedia; Artnet; Artsy]
- **The Surrealist OBJECT as a category.** *Object* debuted at the **"Exposition surréaliste
  d'objets"** (Charles Ratton gallery, Paris, May 1936), Breton's exhibition that formalized the
  *Surrealist object* — the everyday thing displaced into art, charged with dream-logic and
  desire. *Object* became the type-specimen of that whole category. [Smarthistory; Wikipedia]
- **The tactile shock.** The work's force is bodily: fur belongs against skin, porcelain against
  lips. To imagine drinking from a fur-lined cup is to feel hair on the tongue and lips — pleasure
  and revulsion at once. The disgust is the point; the object refuses its own function. [Smarthistory; Khan Academy]
- **Gendered / sexual readings (keep proportionate).** Critics later read the fur as bodily,
  erotic, even explicitly sexual; the work has been a touchstone for feminist art history. Hold
  this proportionate: these readings came **after**, and Oppenheim resisted being reduced to the
  fur cup or to a "woman Surrealist." Note both: the readings exist and are real; they are not
  established as *her* stated intent. [Artnet; Smarthistory; Getty podcast transcript]
- **The MoMA sensation.** Alfred H. Barr Jr. saw it (it traveled to the 1936 London International
  Surrealist Exhibition) and brought it into MoMA's **"Fantastic Art, Dada, Surrealism"** (winter
  1936–37), where visitors voted it the **"quintessential Surrealist object."** It made the
  23-year-old Oppenheim instantly famous — fame she grew ambivalent about, since it eclipsed the
  rest of a long career. [Wikipedia; Artsy; Britannica]

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required — before / after)

- **Before:** Sculpture meant a thing *made* — carved, modeled, cast — and prized objects (fine
  porcelain, fur) signaled luxury and decorum. A teacup was a teacup.
- **The break:** *Object* is a **found-object assemblage**: nothing is carved or cast. Oppenheim
  *buys* a cup and a pelt and joins them. The Surrealist object becomes **sculpture** — the
  artwork is the *idea and the displacement*, not the craft. And its content is a deliberate
  **collision of comfort and revulsion**: it fuses the cozy ritual of tea with the gag reflex of
  fur in the mouth, so a domestic comfort-object turns uncanny and faintly obscene.
- **After:** It became the canonical Surrealist object and a permanent reference point for
  found-object and assemblage art, for the uncanny everyday, and for feminist readings of the
  body in the household object. [Smarthistory; Khan Academy; Wikipedia]

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced)

**Best-sourced verbatim Oppenheim quote** — from her acceptance speech for the **Art Award of the
City of Basel (Basler Kunstpreis), 16 January 1975**, on being a woman artist:

> "Freedom is not given to you — you have to take it."

Fuller passage from the same speech (women artists / taboos), as widely quoted:

> "Yes, I'd even go so far as to say that, as a woman, one is obliged to prove via one's lifestyle
> that one no longer regards as valid the taboos that have been used to keep women in a state of
> subjugation for thousands of years. Freedom is not given; one has to take it."

- **Source:** 1975 Basel Kunstpreis acceptance speech. Quote circulates in two near-identical
  forms — *"Freedom is not given to you, you have to take it"* and *"Freedom is not given; one has
  to take it"* — both from the **same speech** (translation variant, not two sayings). **Flag the
  variant; do not present as two quotes.** [NMWA artist profile; Wikipedia *Méret Oppenheim*;
  Getty transcript] **[VERIFY]** the exact English wording against a print catalog if the read uses
  it verbatim.
- **On the café/title origin** — if the read wants a quote tied directly to *Object*, the reliably
  attributed line is the café riposte **"Even this cup and saucer?"** (to Picasso), but this is a
  *reported* exchange, not a documented verbatim Oppenheim statement — treat as anecdote (§8), not
  as a sourced quotation.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The fur swallows the set.** Cup, saucer, *and* spoon are all sheathed in the same speckled
   tan gazelle fur — not one furred object but a whole tea service gone shaggy.
2. **The rim is the shock.** Look at the lip of the cup, the edge you'd drink from: that's where
   fur meets mouth, and where the whole queasy idea lives.
3. **The furred spoon.** The spoon's shape is still legible under the pelt — bowl and handle — but
   a fur-coated spoon is the most useless object of the three; you can't stir, you can't sip.
4. **Use is denied.** The fur cancels every function: a cup that can't hold tea without soaking,
   a saucer that can't catch a drip, a spoon that can't scoop. It looks like a tea service and
   works like nothing.
5. **Comfort vs. revulsion in one glance.** The fur reads as soft, strokable, luxurious — then
   the brain registers *in the mouth* and the softness curdles. Both responses fire at once.
6. **It's small and ordinary.** Note the modest scale (a normal teacup) and the store-bought
   plainness underneath the fur — the unsettling charge comes from cheapness made strange, not
   from grandeur.

## 6. PROVENANCE

- Made Paris, 1936; shown at the Charles Ratton Surrealist-objects show (Paris, 1936) and the
  London International Surrealist Exhibition (1936); then MoMA's *Fantastic Art, Dada, Surrealism*
  (NY, winter 1936–37).
- **To MoMA:** credit line **"Purchase," acquisition dated 1946.** [MoMA via Artsy/Wikipedia]
- **Nuance worth carrying (do not flatten):** several sources note Alfred H. Barr Jr. initially
  bought it (reportedly with his own funds, conservative trustees balking); it entered MoMA's
  **study collection in 1946** and was formally accessioned into the **permanent collection in
  1963.** MoMA's public credit line still reads **Purchase**. So "Purchase, 1946" is correct as the
  catalog line, but the "first work by a woman in MoMA's collection" claim hangs on the 1946
  study-collection date. State the 1946 line; if the read makes the "first woman" claim, footnote
  the study-collection-vs-1963 wrinkle. **[VERIFY @ MoMA]** [Wikipedia; Artsy]

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS

- **The Picasso / Dora Maar café anecdote.** Present as the **standard, well-attested account**
  (café, fur bracelet, "Even this cup and saucer?", "a little more fur"). It is repeated by
  reputable sources (NPR, Artnet, Artsy, Wikipedia) and is broadly consistent — but it is a
  *reported* exchange with small variants (which café; exact wording; whether the "more fur"
  line happened that day). Frame as "the story goes," not documented fact. Don't invent
  dialogue beyond the attested lines. [NPR; Wikipedia; Artnet]
- **Title confusion — the biggest trap.** Three names float around and they are NOT
  interchangeable in provenance:
  - ***Object*** = MoMA's plain catalog title (often expanded to *Object (Fur-covered cup,
    saucer, and spoon)*).
  - ***Le Déjeuner en fourrure*** ("Luncheon in Fur" / "Lunch in Fur") = **Breton's coinage**,
    not Oppenheim's, punning on Manet.
  - ***Breakfast in Fur*** / *Fur Breakfast* = loose English translations of Breton's title; common
    but informal. Use *Object* as the primary title; introduce the French as Breton's nickname.
  - Don't credit the French title to Oppenheim. [Wikipedia; Smarthistory]
- **The medium / dimensions.** Use MoMA's line (**fur-covered cup, saucer, and spoon**; Chinese
  gazelle fur; cup 10.9 cm / 4 3/8 in dia.; saucer 23.7 cm / 9 3/8 in dia.; spoon 20.2 cm / 8 in;
  overall H 7.3 cm / 2 7/8 in). Beware sources that give only one dimension or only inches.
  **[VERIFY @ MoMA]** before shipping any number. [Smarthistory/Khan quoting MoMA]
- **The purchase year.** "**Purchase, 1946**" is the catalog line. The often-seen claim that Barr
  bought it "the year it was made (1936)" conflates his *first seeing/acquiring impulse* with the
  formal 1946 acquisition; permanent-collection accession was 1963. Don't write "MoMA bought it in
  1936." [Artsy; Wikipedia]
- **Which department store / which fur.** Retellings vary (Monoprix vs. other Paris stores); the
  fur is consistently **Chinese gazelle**. Use "a Paris department store" if unsure of the store.
  **[VERIFY]**
- **Gendered readings as intent.** Don't assert that Oppenheim *intended* the sexual/feminist
  reading. It's a later critical layer she neither fully endorsed nor controlled; she was wary of
  being boxed as the "fur-cup woman." Keep it as reception, not authorial statement. [Artnet; Getty]
