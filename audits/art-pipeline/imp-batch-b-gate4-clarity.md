# Gate 4 — Newcomer / Clarity Critic
## Impressionism Batch B: Dance Class · Absinthe · Boating Party · Gare Saint-Lazare

Read cold. The critic is a sharp 15-year-old who has never been to a museum.
Five flag types: (1) undefined terms, (2) unintroduced people, (3) unexplained
leaps, (4) confusing pronouns / re-reads, (5) stray editorial tags.

Grade scale: CLEAR / NEEDS-GLOSS / LOST  
Severity: **MUST-FIX** (comprehension breaks) · SHOULD-FIX (friction)

---

## 1. THE DANCE CLASS

**Overall grade: NEEDS-GLOSS**

The draft is the strongest of the four — definitions arrive early and in the
right order. The chapter that earns the most flags is `making` (technical art
terms cluster there). Nothing collapses into LOST.

---

### 1.1 Undefined terms on first use

**MUST-FIX**

**`X-radiograph`** — chapter `making`, first used as:
> "the **X-radiograph**, an X-ray taken of a painting, which shows you the dense
> paint layers underneath the surface like an architectural plan of the artist's
> changes."

The inline gloss *is* there, in the same sentence. **Flag retracted — correctly
defined.** No fix needed.

**`pentimento`** — the draft never uses this word, so no flag.

**`choreographer`** — chapter `making`:
> "the **choreographer** (the person who invents and arranges the steps)"

Correctly defined inline. No fix needed.

**`ballet master`** — chapter `making`:
> "Calling him the *ballet master*, by the way, means the senior man in charge of
> training the corps and running rehearsals — the head of the dance school inside
> the opera house."

Correct, but the parenthetical arrives *after* the word has already been used
twice in the same paragraph without a gloss (it appears on page 1 in `rehearsal`
without any definition there). SHOULD-FIX: add a one-phrase gloss to the first
use in `rehearsal` — something like "(the senior teacher who ran the class)."

**`plein-air`** — chapter `show`:
> "they worked outdoors, in front of the actual light, chasing the sun across
> grain fields and harbors and gardens."

The concept is explained correctly, but the French term itself — **plein-air**
(printed *plein-air* in the prose) — is never explicitly called out as French or
defined by name. A 15-year-old will trip on it. SHOULD-FIX: "(French for 'open
air,' meaning painting outdoors on the spot in front of the real light)"

**`Salon`** — chapter `show`:
> "the Salon — the official annual state-run art exhibition in Paris, which was
> effectively the only door into a career."

Well defined. No fix needed.

**`provenance`** — chapter `afterlife`:
> "**provenance**, which means the documented chain of who owned a work of art,
> from the artist's hand to wherever it sits now. Provenance is a painting's
> paper trail."

Clear inline definition. No fix needed.

**`petits rats`** — chapter `rehearsal`:
> "Parisians called the youngest ones *petits rats* (literally 'little rats,'
> because they scampered through the back corridors of the opera house)"

Correct inline definition. No fix needed.

**`abonnés`** — chapter `rehearsal`:
> "the ***abonnés*** (pronounced 'ah-buh-NAY'), the wealthy male season-ticket
> holders who had bought, along with their seats, the right to walk *backstage*"

Well defined. No fix needed.

**`Japonisme` / `ukiyo-e`** — chapter `looking`:
> "***ukiyo-e*** (pronounced 'oo-kee-yo-eh,' literally 'pictures of the floating
> world,' the cheap mass-produced color woodblock prints that flooded Paris when
> Japan reopened to Western trade)"
> "***Japonisme*** (pronounced 'jah-poh-NEEZM,' the late-1800s French taste for
> Japanese visual art)"

Both defined inline, with pronunciation guides. Solid. No fix needed.

**`corps`** — chapter `rehearsal`:
> "The Opéra ballet was a working corps of around 150 dancers"

*Corps* (pronounced "core") is a French military/dance term a 15-year-old will
not automatically know. SHOULD-FIX: add "(pronounced 'core' — the full company
of dancers in the ballet)" on first use.

## 2. L'ABSINTHE (DANS UN CAFÉ)

**Overall grade: NEEDS-GLOSS**

The strongest chapter is `looking` — it earns no flags. The weakest is `show`,
where `scandal` is deployed before it's defined (the draft does eventually
define it, but one sentence too late), and `Grafton Gallery` is named only once
without context. The `cafe` chapter is dense with proper names the first-time
reader can't yet rank.

---

**MUST-FIX**

**`louche`** — chapter `cafe`:

The louche *ritual* is described before the term arrives. Clear enough in
order. No fix needed.

**`mazagran`** — chapter `making`:

**`demi-monde`** — this term from the prompt checklist does NOT appear in the

**`Nouvelle-Athènes`** — chapter `cafe`:

Correctly defined. No fix needed.

**`Grafton Gallery`** — chapter `show`:

The definition is there, but it comes *after* the word "scandal" has been
used in section headings and repeatedly in the prose for about three paragraphs

substantive use in the `show` chapter body (not the heading).

### 2.2 People introduced without a one-liner

**MUST-FIX**

**George Moore** — chapter `show`:
> "The novelist **George Moore**, an Anglo-Irish writer who had actually lived
> in Paris and known the Nouvelle-Athènes circle..."

"Anglo-Irish writer" is thin. He is named as a novelist and as someone who
recants the temperance reading — that's enough context for comprehension.
Marginal; SHOULD-FIX to add "playwright and art critic" for completeness,

**Félix Fénéon** — chapter `show`:
> "the **1921 interview** with the critic **Félix Fénéon** in the **Théâtre
> Édouard VII** in Paris"

Fénéon is called "the critic" but gets no dates or further context. For a
15-year-old, "a Paris art critic who in 1921 interviewed Andrée for a Paris
theatre journal" would be cleaner. SHOULD-FIX.

**Manet, Pissarro, Renoir, Cézanne, Sisley** — chapter `cafe` mentions them
in passing as "the loose group of young painters now called the Impressionists."
As a group mention without individual glosses, this is fine — the reader learns
these are painters. No flag.

---

### 2.3 Unexplained leaps

**MUST-FIX**

Chapter `show` opens:
> "Off it went into private hands, and there it sat. Now skip seventeen years."

"Off it went into private hands" — which hands? The previous paragraph mentions
the 1876 Paris reception and says the picture "was sold." The buyer is not
named until the next chapter (`afterlife`). A 15-year-old reading in chapter
order will be confused: who bought it in 1876? MUST-FIX: either name Hill here
("...and went to an English dealer and then a Brighton collector named Captain
Hill") or add a bridging clause ("...and would not resurface publicly for
seventeen years").

---

### 2.4 Confusing pronouns / re-reads

"Attached to a noun in the title" is compact and slightly cryptic. The noun is
"Absinthe." A 15-year-old will get it with a half-second delay, but "attached
to one word — *Absinthe* — in the title" would remove the friction entirely.

## 3. LUNCHEON OF THE BOATING PARTY

**Overall grade: NEEDS-GLOSS**

The `chatou` and `making` chapters are clean. The `looking` chapter — the
longest — carries a pile-up of fourteen proper names in rapid succession that
a 15-year-old cold reader will not be able to track; the draft is aware of
this and provides a cluster-by-cluster structure, but the sheer density of
contested IDs at the end of the cluster tour is fatiguing. The `afterlife`
chapter introduces an unexplained term (`Caillebotte bequest`) and two
unexplained people (Phillips's family members, Jean Renoir) without glosses.

---

### 3.1 Undefined terms on first use

**MUST-FIX**

**`canotage`** — chapter `chatou`:
> "*Canotage*, the French called it (roughly 'boating'; *canot* is a small rowing
> boat, and *canotage* is the whole subculture of rowing, picnics, and
> river-loafing built around it)."

Correctly defined inline. No fix needed.

**`straw boater`** — chapter `chatou`:
> "men in **straw boaters** (a hard, flat-brimmed straw hat with a black ribbon,
> originally worn by boat crews — hence the name)"

Defined inline. No fix needed.

**`bowler hat`** — chapter `looking`:
> "in a bowler hat (the round, hard-felt hat that became urban office-uniform in
> the late nineteenth century)"

Defined inline. No fix needed.

**`top hat`** — chapter `looking`:
> "in a tall **top hat** (the formal black silk cylinder that was still the
> gentleman's hat for a man dressing up in 1880)"

Defined inline. No fix needed.

**`Salon`** — chapter `show`:
> "the official **Salon** (the state-sponsored annual exhibition that had been
> the only respectable way for a painter to be seen in Paris before this whole
> crowd decided to go around it)."

Defined inline. No fix needed.

**`Caillebotte bequest`** — chapter `afterlife` (and mentioned in `show`):
> "(He shows up in two of these batch-B paintings, by the way — sitting here on

**`Gazette des beaux-arts`** — chapter `looking`:

Defined inline. No fix needed.

**`awning`** — chapter `chatou`:
> "a striped red-and-white awning running overhead"

Not defined. An `awning` is a cloth canopy — a common household word, but the
draft later explains it in full in the `looking` chapter Look Closer pointer

summer sun"). SHOULD-FIX: a quick gloss on first use — "(the cloth canopy
shading the balcony from the sun)."

**`plein-air`** — not used in this draft. No flag.

**MUST-FIX**

## 4. LA GARE SAINT-LAZARE

**Overall grade: NEEDS-GLOSS**

Cleanest draft of the four on the definitions. The `station` chapter does an
excellent job of explaining what a *gare* is, what a train shed is, what a
locomotive is — all textbook newcomer handling. The flags cluster in `making`
(one significant definitional slip) and `afterlife` (a compound proper-name
cluster the reader can't unpack without help).

---

### 4.1 Undefined terms on first use

**MUST-FIX**

**`plein air`** — chapter `making`:
> "**Plein air** (French for 'open air') is the practice of painting outdoors,
> on the spot, in front of the real light, which was the Impressionists'
> signature method and Monet's specialty."

Correctly and fully defined inline. No fix needed.

**`train shed`** — chapter `station`:
> "the **iron-and-glass train shed**. A *train shed* is the giant roofed hall
> a station throws over the tracks where the engines pull up — the room you walk
> into when you step off the platform and look up."

Correctly defined. No fix needed.

**`locomotive`** — chapter `station`:
> "(A *locomotive* is the engine car of a train, the part that does the pulling.)"

Defined. No fix needed.

**`terminus`** — chapter `station`:
> "the **terminus**, meaning the end-of-the-line stop, for trains running west"

Defined inline. No fix needed.

**`Caillebotte bequest`** — chapter `afterlife`:
> "That bequest — what specialists now call the **Caillebotte bequest** —
> triggered one of the loudest art fights in nineteenth-century France."

"Bequest" (a gift left in a will) is clear from context. "Caillebotte bequest"
is used as an art-historical shorthand. Works for the context given the
surrounding explanation. No fix needed.

**`non-fini`** — chapter `show`:
> "The familiar charge of *non-fini* — 'unfinished,' the academy's polite way of

Defined inline with quotation marks and translation. No fix needed.

**`series`** (as an art term) — chapter `making`:
> "a coordinated group of paintings of *the same motif* under varying conditions,

Defined in context. No fix needed.

**`Haussmannian`** — used in Look Closer pointer 4 and in `looking` chapter:
> "the **Haussmannian apartment buildings** of the rue de Rome"
> Look Closer pointer defines it: "the kind of regular, pale-stone, six-story
> apartment block that the city planner Baron Haussmann had pushed across central
> Paris in the 1850s and 60s"

The Look Closer pointer defines it, but the word also appears earlier in the
`looking` chapter body without a gloss. SHOULD-FIX: add a parenthetical at the
first use in the `looking` chapter body itself — "(the regular six-story stone
apartment blocks that defined mid-century Paris after Baron Haussmann's
rebuilding of the city)."

**Georges Rivière** — chapter `show`:

**Émile Zola** — chapter `show`:
