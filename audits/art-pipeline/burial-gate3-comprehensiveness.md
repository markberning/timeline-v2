### M1 — The Stone Breakers: the twin Salon bombshell (Chapter 3 — The bomb in the Salon)

**What it is.** At the same Salon of 1850–51, Courbet also exhibited *The Stone
Breakers* (1849–50), a monumental canvas of two road-workers that he had painted
immediately before the Burial. Critics and the public received the two pictures as
a deliberate one-two punch — the same radical program (heroic scale, common
working-class subjects, no idealization) delivered twice in the same hall. The
Burial is most fully understood as the more *extreme* half of a pair, not as a
solitary outrage. The Stone Breakers was later lost: it was destroyed during the
Allied bombing of Dresden in 1945.

**Why it's a must-add.** Every standard survey account (MET, Wikipedia, Khan
Academy, Stone Breakers Wikipedia article) pairs the two works in the same
breath. The combined effect on critics was part of the scandal the draft is
analyzing in chapter 3. Omitting the twin makes the Salon reception read as if
the Burial landed in a vacuum. One sentence is enough; the draft need not
re-narrate the other painting, only acknowledge the pairing and the loss.

**Verified facts.**
- Both exhibited at the Salon of 1850–51 (MET essay confirms: "including The
  Stonebreakers (1849–50; now lost) and A Burial at Ornans (1849–50; Musée
  d'Orsay, Paris) challenged convention by rendering scenes from daily life on
  the large scale previously reserved for history painting").
- The Stone Breakers destroyed 1945 during the bombing of Dresden (Wikipedia,
  The Stone Breakers; The Daily Beast/Hyperallergic; also: a second reversed
  version survives in the Oskar Reinhart Collection, Winterthur).

**Where to add.** Chapter 3, Offense 2 or the "frame around the offense" section.
One to two sentences: "The Burial did not arrive alone — in the same Salon,
Courbet also showed *The Stone Breakers* (1849–50), another monumental canvas of
road laborers at heroic scale, and critics encountered the two pictures as a
coordinated assault. (The Stone Breakers was lost in 1945 when the bombing of
Dresden destroyed the cart carrying it out of the city.)"

**Source:** MET essay "Gustave Courbet (1819–1877)" at metmuseum.org; Wikipedia
"The Stone Breakers"; The Daily Beast "Gustave Courbet's The Stonebreakers."

### M2 — Named sitters: the veterans in Revolutionary-era dress (Chapter 2 — Fifty neighbors)

**What it is.** Among the ~50 identified sitters are two elderly men wearing
costumes dating from 1793 — the later stages of the French Revolution. These are
identified as François Pillot-Secrétan and Jean Baptiste Cardey, friends of
Courbet's grandfather. Their antique dress is conspicuous in a crowd otherwise
in 1840s–50s mourning black, and it is an *intentional* time-stamp: these men
lived through the Revolution, and Courbet put them in period dress, making the
frieze span generations and tying the painting to France's republican memory.

**Why it's a must-add.** The draft mentions the mayor and justice of the peace
in passing but never names specific sitters or describes the veteran detail, which
is one of the most discussed compositional choices in the scholarship. It deepens
the "democracy of death" reading already implicit in the draft and adds texture
to the claim that these are "real, specific people." Wikipedia, Smarthistory, and
multiple secondary sources treat the veterans as a key descriptive detail.

**Verified facts.**
- Two veterans of the Revolution of '93 appear in period costume (Wikipedia "A
  Burial at Ornans"; search result confirms "their costumes come from 1793, the
  later stages of the French Revolution").
- Identified as François Pillot-Secrétan and Jean Baptiste Cardey, Courbet's
  grandfather's friends (art-facts.com; Smarthistory).
- Courbet's three sisters also identifiable: Juliette (covering her mouth), Zoé
  (face fully covered), Zélie (far right, near the dog) (DailyArt Magazine;
  MutualArt).

**Where to add.** Chapter 2, "The frieze — Reading the crowd left to right"
section, after the existing sentence about the mayor and justice of the peace.
Two to three sentences naming the veterans and noting the 1793 costume detail.

**Source:** Wikipedia "A Burial at Ornans" (sitters section); art-facts.com "Top
8 Facts"; DailyArt Magazine; MutualArt.

### M3 — Champfleury: the critic who named Realism here (Chapter 3 or 4)

**What it is.** The writer and critic **Champfleury** (Jules Husson, 1821–1889)
was the Burial's most prominent public defender after the Salon scandal and is
widely credited as the first person to use the term "Realism" in the context of
art criticism. He wrote in direct response to the attacks on the painting: "there
is not a trace of socialism in A Burial at Ornans... It has pleased the painter to
show us the domestic life of a small town... As to the alleged ugliness of the
townspeople, there is nothing exaggerated about it." He later wrote the catalogue
for Courbet's Pavilion of Realism in 1855.

**Why it's a must-add.** The draft's chapter 3 describes the hostile critics but
names no defenders. Champfleury's response is the primary counter-voice in the
historical record and his defense is the origin of the word "Realism" in its
art-critical sense. Leaving him out makes the reception look one-sided. More
importantly, he is the bridge from the chapter 3 Salon scandal to chapter 4's
claim that the Burial is "Realism's manifesto before the manifesto" — he is
literally the person who coined the term while defending this specific painting.

**Verified facts.**
- Champfleury was recognized as the first to use "realism" in art criticism and
  hailed the Burial as a landmark (EBSCO Research Starters; MutualArt).
- His exact quoted defense of the painting survives and is widely cited (multiple
  sources reproduce the "not a trace of socialism" passage).
- He also wrote the 1855 Pavilion of Realism catalogue (Caesura Magazine).

**Where to add.** Chapter 3, after the three offenses, or as a transitional beat
into Chapter 4. One short paragraph naming him and quoting or paraphrasing his
defense. The 1855 Pavilion connection can be noted in Chapter 4 as the payoff.

**Source:** MutualArt "The Social Sublime: Decoding Courbet's Burial at Ornans";
EBSCO Research Starters "Courbet Establishes Realist Art Movement"; multiple
secondary sources quoting the Champfleury passage.

### M4 — Dutch civic-guard portrait tradition (Chapter 2 or 4)

**What it is.** Courbet directly drew the Burial's compositional structure —
the long horizontal frieze of named individuals at equal eye level — from the
Dutch 17th-century civic-guard group portrait tradition (think Rembrandt's *The
Night Watch* and Frans Hals's militia portraits). This is confirmed by documented
biographical fact: Courbet visited Belgium and the Netherlands in 1846–47 and the
trip was transformative for his ideas about group composition and unflinching
portraiture. The Musée d'Orsay's own page on the painting cites the Dutch group
portrait as the direct formal source for the frieze.

**Why it's a must-add.** The draft presents the horizontal frieze as if Courbet
invented it wholesale as an anti-hierarchical gesture, missing that he was
consciously adapting a known art-historical model and then radicalizing it. This
is meaningful: the frieze IS anti-hierarchical, but it works partly because a
viewer with any art knowledge recognized the civic-guard format and felt the
subversion — ordinary French villagers where the Amsterdam guilds used to be.
This is a substantive art-historical point that several major sources (Musée
d'Orsay, art-facts.com, DailyArt) treat as important.

**Verified facts.**
- "A Burial at Ornans is nonetheless inspired by old models, in particular the
  large Dutch group portraits that Courbet saw while on a visit to the
  Netherlands. More specifically, Courbet took his inspiration from group
  portraits of Dutch civic guards in the 17th century" (art-facts.com; confirmed
  by multiple sources citing the 1846–47 Netherlands visit).
- "The sumptuous blacks recall Spanish art" (same source — Courbet's debt to
  Velázquez and Zurbarán for his black palette is a secondary point, SHOULD-ADD
  level; see below).
- Musée d'Orsay page directly cites the Dutch group portrait as formal precursor.

**Where to add.** Chapter 2, "The frieze" section. One sentence: "The format
had a precedent — the Dutch 17th-century civic-guard group portrait (Rembrandt's
era), which Courbet had studied on a trip to the Netherlands in 1846–47 — but
the precedent makes the move more pointed, not less: he slotted provincial French
villagers into the compositional slot the Amsterdam guilds used to occupy."

**Source:** art-facts.com "A Burial at Ornans by Gustave Courbet — Top 8 Facts";
Google Arts & Culture (Musée d'Orsay page); DailyArt Magazine.

## SHOULD-ADD

These are genuine enrichments that a stronger draft would include, but their
omission does not leave a "hole" in the reader's picture.

### S1 — "Democracy of death" / death-as-equalizer reading (Chapter 2 or 3)

The draft gestures at the no-hierarchy reading via the frieze description but
never states the "democracy of death" interpretation flatly. Multiple scholarly
accounts (including Albert Boime's quoted interpretation: "the funeral, like the
Sunday dress, equalizes its participants") treat this as the central political
reading. One explicit sentence — "Critics and later scholars have called it a
'democracy of death': in the grave, the mayor stands no taller than the
gravedigger" — would sharpen what the draft already implies.

**Source:** Wikipedia "A Burial at Ornans" (Boime quote); artchive.com; multiple
secondary sources.

---

### S2 — The dog: symbol of fidelity vs. deliberate anti-sentiment (Chapter 2)

The draft handles the dog well as a Realist gesture but the secondary literature
offers a richer contested reading: some scholars read the dog as a traditional
symbol of loyalty accompanying the dead (a well-established iconographic role for
dogs in funerary imagery), while others see it as Courbet deliberately undermining
sentiment. Naming the tension — loyalty symbol vs. anti-symbol — in one clause
would add depth without adding length.

**Source:** humanalens.com; MutualArt; Wikipedia "A Burial at Ornans."

---

### S3 — Courbet's father Régis appears in the painting (Chapter 2)

In addition to the three sisters, Courbet's father Régis Courbet is identifiable
among the male mourners. This tightens the point that it is a genuine family
portrait embedded in a civic one — Courbet painted his own family into the
frieze, which is a different emotional register from painting strangers.

**Source:** DailyArt Magazine; Smarthistory.

---

### S4 — Spanish blacks: Velázquez/Zurbarán palette influence (Chapter 2)

The extraordinary range of blacks in the painting — funeral black clothing, dark
sky, earth tones — is widely noted as recalling Spanish Golden Age masters,
particularly Velázquez and Zurbarán, whose work Courbet also studied. The Louvre
held significant Spanish holdings in this period. This is a SHOULD-ADD rather
than MUST because the draft does not discuss palette as a separate axis; if it
did, the Spanish influence is the required addition.

**Source:** art-facts.com; DailyArt Magazine.

## Summary

The draft is structurally sound and the throughline (humble subject × monumental
scale = the scandal and the door) is well executed. Four genuinely important beats
are missing. The Stone Breakers pairing (M1) changes the reception story in Ch 3:
the Burial was not a solo outrage but half of a coordinated Salon ambush, and the
twin's later destruction is a poignant footnote. The veterans in Revolutionary
dress (M2) are the most discussed compositional detail the draft skips and they
deepen both the sitter-identification thread and the 1848-resonance argument. The
omission of Champfleury (M3) leaves Ch 3's reception one-sided and severs the
natural bridge to Ch 4's "manifesto" argument — he is literally the person who
coined "Realism" while defending this painting. The Dutch civic-guard tradition
(M4) is the formal source the Musée d'Orsay itself names; without it, the frieze
reads as invented rather than pointedly adapted. None of the four additions
requires more than a sentence or two; together they would lift the draft to a
complete account.
