### [FIX-A] Magritte's commercial-art career is described as a *look*, never a *biography*

The draft repeatedly likens the pipe to "a catalogue illustration," "a department-store ad," "a smoker's mail-order catalogue" — good looking-language. But it never states that Magritte *actually worked as a commercial artist*: wallpaper-factory draughtsman (1922–23), then a poster/advertising and sheet-music-cover designer in Art Deco style until the 1926 Galerie Le Centaure contract let him paint full-time. That fact is the single strongest defense against the genius-myth, because the flat illustrational finish is a *learned trade*, not a philosopher's affectation. Its absence also leaves the repeated "like an ad" similes unexplained — the reader doesn't know the resemblance is autobiographical.

Suggested insert in `TrcLooking`, after the sentence "That choice is deliberate." (the pipe-rendering paragraph):

> And it is no accident that he could paint like an advertisement: before he painted full-time, Magritte earned his living as a commercial artist in Brussels, designing posters, advertisements, and sheet-music covers in the crisp Art Deco style of the 1920s, until a 1926 gallery contract freed him to paint. The flat, even, ad-man's finish was a trade he already owned. He turned it on the pipe on purpose.

(Source: René Magritte biography, Wikipedia; Open Culture, "Magritte's Early Art Deco Advertising Posters, 1924–1927." All born-verifiable.)

### [FIX-B] The word-image idea reads as sui generis; the Dada precedent is unnamed

`TrcMaking` opens "The pipe did not arrive out of nowhere" and then locates its origins entirely *inside Magritte's own* two-year run of word-image pictures. True but incomplete: Magritte was not the first to put a contradicting word on a depicted object. Francis Picabia's mechanical drawings for *391* (c. 1917–24) already labeled objects with the wrong name (a propeller captioned *âne*, "donkey"). Naming a precedent is exactly the fairness move the brief asks for, and the fact pack already half-concedes it by calling the LACMA canvas "art-historically *first*" only in the narrow sense of "first of Magritte's own pipe versions." One clause prevents the read from implying he invented word-image painting.

Suggested insert in `TrcMaking`, after "set image and text side by side so they argued." (end of the second paragraph):

> The raw move — a word that fights the object it labels — was not invented here; the Dadaists had already mislabeled things to jam the machinery of sense, as when Francis Picabia drew a propeller and captioned it *âne* ("donkey"). What Magritte added was rigor and calm: he narrowed the prank into a single clean proposition about representation and painted it with a straight face.

(Source: Wikipedia, *The Treachery of Images*; Contemporary Art Issue, "The History of Text-Based Art." Picabia/*391* born-verifiable.)

## 2. FRAMING / FAIRNESS

### First/only overclaim — CLEAN (no FIX needed, one [NICE] guard)

The draft does **not** claim "first conceptual painting" or "invented word-image painting." Influence language is properly hedged: "the conceptual seed that later movements would harvest directly," "run back, **in part**, to a man in Brussels." The "purest thing he ever made in that vein" and "the cleanest and most ruthless" claims are about *his own* series, not a global first — acceptable.

- **[NICE]** `TrcBreak` says later movements "run back, in part, to" the pipe — keep the "in part." If [FIX-B] lands, this is fully covered. One residual phrase to watch: "the one thing pictures had been carefully not saying for five hundred years" (`TrcAfterlife` close) is rhetorically strong but reads as a near-absolute "first ever to say it." It survives because [FIX-B] will have already named Picabia upstream; without [FIX-B] it tips into overclaim. Recommend landing [FIX-B] *or* softening the close to "the thing realistic pictures had spent five hundred years quietly not saying."

### Genius-myth — [FIX-A] is the fix

Without [FIX-A], the read presents Magritte almost purely as a philosopher-in-paint ("a man who had decided, very deliberately, to put the thing and the picture and the word in front of you"). He *is* situated in Surrealism (Paris 1924, the journal, Mesens, the movement's launch) — good — but his trade as a working ad-man is the missing leg. Land [FIX-A] and the genius-myth risk is resolved: he becomes a commercial artist who turned his day-job craft into an argument, which is both truer and a better story.

- **[NICE]** `TrcProposition` calls Surrealism a Paris-1924 movement and correctly notes Magritte "did the opposite" of the melting-dream Surrealists. Consider one clause naming that Magritte was the *Brussels* Surrealist wing (distinct from Breton's Paris circle), so he isn't read as a Paris insider. Optional.

### Proportion — keep it a painting, not a philosophy lecture — [NICE]

The read leans heavily didactic: `TrcProposition`, `TrcMaking`, and `TrcBreak` are largely argument; `TrcLooking` is the only section that is mostly *about the canvas*. That is defensible for this particular work (the painting genuinely *is* a proposition), and the looking section + the six annotations carry real material presence. But two light touches keep it a painting:

- **[NICE]** Add one concrete physical note to `TrcLooking` to ground the object: the canvas is **oil on canvas** (the fact pack flags an "it's a drawing/lithograph" trap — worth one sentence asserting the medium, since the illustrational look invites that error). E.g. after the size sentence: "For all that it looks printed, it is oil on canvas — the brushed, hand-painted thing, not a poster or a print."
- **[NICE]** The afterlife's icon paragraph ("parodied, T-shirted, and quoted") is good proportion-correction — it returns the work to the world. No change needed.
