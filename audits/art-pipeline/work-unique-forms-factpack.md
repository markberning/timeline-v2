## 3. HOOK (one line, house voice — for the author to sharpen)

A bronze man caught mid-stride, his body torn open into flame-shaped flanges by his own speed — built in plaster in 1913 by an artist who died three years later and never saw it cast in metal, and now small enough to ride in your pocket on the Italian 20-cent coin.

## 4. NARRATIVE BEATS (~5)

**Beat 1 — Futurism's drive to sculpt motion itself.** Futurism, founded by the poet **Filippo Tommaso Marinetti** with the **Founding Manifesto of Futurism**, published on the front page of the Paris paper *Le Figaro* on **20 February 1909** [DOCUMENTED], worshipped speed, the machine, and the modern city. The manifesto's most famous line declares that "a roaring car… is more beautiful than the Victory of Samothrace [the *Nike*]" [DOCUMENTED, quote verified]. The Futurists wanted art that captured not a frozen pose but **movement, force, and the rush of modern life.** Boccioni was the movement's leading visual artist and its main sculptor.

**Beat 2 — Boccioni's *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Sculpture* (1912).** Before he made the figure, Boccioni wrote the theory. His **Technical Manifesto of Futurist Sculpture, published 11 April 1912** [DOCUMENTED], attacked the traditional statue — solid, closed, sitting on a pedestal, sealed off from the world. He called for **"the complete abolition of the finished line and the closed [self-contained] statue"** [DOCUMENTED — quote verified against the manifesto text], and to **"open up the figure like a window and enclose within it the environment in which it lives"** [DOCUMENTED — quote verified]. He wanted to **"model the atmosphere"** around things [DOCUMENTED — quote verified]. A sculpture should not stop at its own skin; it should grab the space it moves through.

**Beat 3 — The striding figure with no fixed contour.** *Unique Forms* is that theory made metal: a single human figure in mid-stride, but with **no fixed outline** — its surfaces peel back into wing- or flame-like flanges, as if the body is being reshaped by the air it pushes through. Boccioni stripped away the **arms** entirely. The pose and the wind-swept forms are very close to the ancient Greek **Winged Victory (Nike) of Samothrace** — pointed comparison, given Marinetti had just dismissed that very statue — and recall **Rodin's *Walking Man*** [DOCUMENTED that critics/scholars draw both comparisons; the *intent* to "homage" them is interpretation → frame as "scholars read it against," not as Boccioni's stated aim].

**Beat 4 — "Plastic dynamism."** Boccioni's word for what he was after was **dynamism** — *plastic dynamism*: not a snapshot of a moving thing, but the **fusion of a body with the motion and the space it generates.** As he put it, the continuity he wanted was **NOT** made by repeating "legs, arms and faces," but by an "intuitive search for the *unique form which gives continuity in space*" [DOCUMENTED — quote verified, and it is literally the source of the title]. The flanges are the *wake* of the stride, motion made solid.

**Beat 5 — The posthumous bronzes + the euro-coin afterlife.** Boccioni **died in 1916**, at **33**, of injuries after being **thrown from a horse during a military training exercise** in WWI [DOCUMENTED — Verona; framed as the standard account]. The work existed only as **plaster** in his lifetime; he never cast it in bronze. The **first bronzes were cast in 1931** — fifteen years after his death — and more followed (1949, 1972…). The bronze is now the version the world knows. Italy put it on the **20-cent euro coin** (designed/issued from 2002; selected in the late 1990s) [DOCUMENTED], so the most reproduced Futurist sculpture rides in millions of pockets. There is a real **irony/tension** here worth a beat: a movement that prized the new, the unique, and the ephemeral is famous through **mechanical bronze multiples** the artist never authorized in his lifetime.

## 5. "LOOK CLOSER" ANNOTATIONS (6) — PROSE pointers, no crops/coordinates

Per the pipeline: `{label, where, detail}` only; `where` is a written location phrase; the page shows the whole figure (tap to zoom). These describe the standard ¾ striding view. Every visual claim verified against the work's documented description.

1. **`label:` The striding muscular legs**
   `where:` Lower half — the two thick legs, one driving forward, one trailing.
   `detail:` The legs are the engine of the whole figure: heavy, muscular, frankly powerful, mid-stride with the weight thrown forward. Everything above them is being dragged and reshaped by that motion. This is where the sculpture is most clearly a *body walking* rather than an abstract shape. [DOCUMENTED — figure is a striding human form]

2. **`label:` The flame- and wind-like flanges**
   `where:` The torso, back, and thighs — the curved fins and blades of metal peeling off the body's surface.
   `detail:` The figure's "skin" doesn't close. It splits into wing- or flame-shaped flanges that stream backward, as if the air is carving the man as he moves. This is the literal form Boccioni's manifesto demanded — no "finished line," no sealed surface; the body opened into the space around it. [DOCUMENTED — the manifesto's "abolition of the finished line"; the flanges are the work's signature feature]

3. **`label:` The arms that aren't there**
   `where:` The shoulders and sides — where arms would hang, there are none.
   `detail:` Boccioni left the figure **armless**. The flanges flare from the torso and thighs instead. Scholars read the missing arms against two famous precedents — the armless ancient **Nike of Samothrace** and **Rodin's *Walking Man*** — though that's a reading, not a documented statement of intent. The effect is to turn a man into pure forward force. [DOCUMENTED no arms; comparison is scholarly reading → "is often read against," not asserted as Boccioni's aim]

4. **`label:` The wake of motion**
   `where:` Behind and around the figure — the trailing curves that seem to belong to the air, not the man.
   `detail:` These backward-streaming forms are not clothing or anatomy. They are the *trail* of the stride made solid — Boccioni's "plastic dynamism," the idea that a moving body and the space it disturbs are one continuous form. The title's "continuity in space" is exactly this. [DOCUMENTED — plastic dynamism / the title's own logic]

5. **`label:` The aerodynamic torso**
   `where:` The chest and midsection, swept and streamlined like a thing built to move through air.
   `detail:` The torso reads less like a ribcage than like a hull or a fuselage — streamlined, faceted, pushed by speed. Futurism loved the machine, and the body here is half-man, half-engine, which the shiny bronze only sharpens. [DOCUMENTED — Futurist man/machine fusion; the "mechanized quality" of the bronze noted in sources → frame as the bronze's effect]

6. **`label:` The (almost) pedestal-less stride**
   `where:` The base — the figure rises on two block-like footings rather than a traditional plinth.
   `detail:` Boccioni's manifesto attacked the figure sealed on its pedestal. Here the figure strides off two small block supports, not a grand plinth, so it reads as *going somewhere* rather than posed for display. [DOCUMENTED that the manifesto rejected the pedestal/closed statue; the two block footings are visible on the casts. NOTE for author: it is NOT literally floating/baseless — it has integral footings; phrase as "minimal blocks, not a grand plinth," not "no base."] [DISPUTED-if-overclaimed]

## 6. PROVENANCE / CASTING HISTORY (gated provenance surface) — THE PLASTER-VS-BRONZE STORY

**This is the heart of the verification ask. Report precisely:**

- **1913 — the original is PLASTER.** Boccioni modeled *Unique Forms* in **plaster** in 1913. (One stray source said "wax" — **[DISPUTED]**: the strong consensus, including Wikipedia and the museum accounts, is **plaster**; the surviving original is the **plaster** in São Paulo. Author: say plaster.) He exhibited his Futurist sculptures in 1913 (Paris/Galerie La Boétie) [DOCUMENTED that he showed sculpture in 1913; treat the exact venue lightly unless re-verified]. **No bronze was cast in his lifetime.** [DOCUMENTED]
- **1916 — Boccioni dies.** Thrown from a horse in a military training exercise near Verona, dies of his injuries, age **33**. [DOCUMENTED, framed as standard account]
- **The original plaster** survives and is at the **Museu de Arte Contemporânea da Universidade de São Paulo (MAC USP), Brazil.** [DOCUMENTED]
- **1931 — first bronzes (posthumous, 15 years after his death).** **Two** bronze casts made; **one is at the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York.** MoMA dates its cast **"1913 (cast 1931 or 1934)"** — note MoMA's own hedge on the exact cast year. [DOCUMENTED — MoMA object page]
- **1949 — two more bronzes.** One at the **Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York**; another at the **Museo del Novecento / "Museum of the Twentieth Century," Milan.** [DOCUMENTED]
- **1972 — further bronzes.** Additional casts (sources say ~eight more), several made **not from the plaster original but from a 1949 bronze** (i.e., a cast-of-a-cast). The **Tate (London)** cast is associated with the 1972 casting. [DOCUMENTED, with the cast-from-a-cast caveat — flag as a real authenticity wrinkle]
- **Other casts:** **Kröller-Müller Museum (Otterlo, Netherlands)**; one given to the **National Gallery of Cosenza (2014)**. [DOCUMENTED]
- **The euro coin:** *Unique Forms* was chosen (selection in the late 1990s, ~1998) as the design on the Italian **20-cent euro coin**, in circulation from 2002. [DOCUMENTED]

**Authenticity wrinkle to surface honestly (framing-relevant):** every bronze is **posthumous** — none was cast, finished, or approved by Boccioni. Some are casts taken from *other bronzes*, not the plaster. So "the bronze in the museum" is at one or two removes from the artist's own hand. This is exactly the documented-vs-legend / honest-provenance terrain the pipeline cares about; do NOT let the prose imply Boccioni cast it in bronze.
