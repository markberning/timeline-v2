### SHOULD-CONSIDER (non-blocking, would improve the read)

**SC-1 — Courbet's reputation before Stone Breakers.**
The draft introduces him as "the public face of Realism" and references the Burial, but doesn't specify that Stone Breakers was his *second* large Realism salvo (after the Burial, which was painted slightly earlier in 1849–50 and shown at the same Salon). The chain context is there, but a sentence acknowledging which canvas Courbet finished *first* — the Burial — would clarify that Stone Breakers was the deliberate follow-through, not the opening move. Source: Wikipedia (Stone Breakers) — both were shown together at the 1850–51 Salon; the Burial was also completed 1849–50.

**SC-2 — The phrase "about seventy" for the old man.**
Ch2 uses "about seventy" (sourced from Courbet's Wey letter). The annotations repeat it. Good. A tiny expansion that the prose doesn't quite make: Courbet specifically said the pairing showed "beginning and end" — the old man "too old for this work, the boy too young." The narrative *implies* this but never states it as Courbet's own frame. Worth making explicit since it's documented in the letter. Source: Courbet's letter to Wey (Wikipedia).

**SC-3 — No mention of the influence trail beyond "Social Realism."**
The lineage children list "Social Realism" and "Millet's peasants" but the narrative itself never says a word about who the painting influenced downstream. Given that Van Gogh's Sower chapter (two entries along this chain) explicitly names Millet, and that the Gleaners chapter names Van Gogh directly — the Stone Breakers' own influence on later documentary/labor art is quietly absent. This is not a blocking gap (the chain handles cross-influence), but a line in the afterlife chapter noting its downstream impact would give the work its due.

**Verdict: PASS.** This is the strongest of the four drafts. Every essential is covered; the three should-considers are refinements, not gaps.

**MA-1 — Proudhon's portrait was worked from a photograph.**
The fact pack flags this as load-bearing (fact #11 in the fact ledger) and it IS a remarkable, very Realist detail — working a portrait of France's most famous radical from a photograph in 1855 is the kind of fact that rewards attention. The draft mentions that Proudhon "wasn't available to sit" and that Courbet "worked his likeness up from a *photograph*" (Ch4), so it IS present. Re-checking... yes, this is covered in Ch4. **MA-1 (REVISED) — The lay figure / mannequin on the left side.**

A reader who has heard of this painting will have likely encountered the mannequin; its absence is a comprehensiveness gap.

Verifiable fact: A mannequin (lay figure) is visible in the painting, on the left side, posed in a manner often read as a crucified figure / the death of academic painting. Source: Wikipedia *The Painter's Studio*; Orsay documentation; fact-pack claim #14.

### MUST-ADD (essential, blocking)

**MA-1 — Millet's specific personal connection to sowing / the authentication of his biography.**
The Sower is argued, throughout the draft, as credible because Millet was a real peasant's son who did the work. This is the narrative's central claim ("almost every painter who ever pointed a brush at a peasant did it from the outside"). But the draft never lands the one specific that makes the claim concrete: Millet grew up *doing field work*, including sowing. The fact pack supports this ("He grew up doing field work, which is load-bearing for the authenticity") but the narrative's Ch1 only says he was "born into a real farming family and grew up doing the actual work" — in the general sense, before immediately pivoting to his artistic training. There is no beat where the reader is shown that *this specific gesture* (hand-sowing) was one Millet's own hands had performed. The claim is there; the specificity that makes it viscerally true is absent.

This is a THIN rather than MISSING gap — the concept is present but the grounding is weak. It matters enough that the prose should land it once: Millet knew what a sower's throw felt like from the inside, not just from observation. Without that anchor, the "outsider vs insider" argument in Ch1 (the entire first chapter's thesis) floats.

Verifiable fact: Millet came from a Normandy farming family that worked the land; field labor including sowing would have been part of the agricultural childhood his biographers document. Source: Encyclopædia Britannica, *Jean-François Millet*; fact-pack item — "He grew up doing field work, which is load-bearing for the authenticity."

**Action needed:** One sentence in Ch1 or Ch2 grounding the sowing arm specifically in Millet's own experience. E.g., that he knew from childhood exactly what the follow-through of a throw looks like from the inside, not from a hired model posing.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER (non-blocking)

**SC-1 — The Barbizon School's relationship to Realism could be one notch more explicit.**
The draft says Barbizon is "one short step from painting the real people in front of you." This is correct but slightly undersells the hand-off. A sentence noting that the Barbizon painters remained primarily *landscape* painters — it was Millet specifically who added the human figure at the center — would make Millet's contribution crisper. The Sower is not just Barbizon; it is the moment Barbizon gains a human heart.

**SC-2 — The accession number and exact MFA credit line.**
The content file includes the correct MFA credit ("Gift of Quincy Adams Shaw through Quincy Adams Shaw, Jr. and Mrs. Marian Shaw Haughton, 1917"). The narrative doesn't quote it (not expected), but a confirmation in the afterlife chapter that the painting has been on continuous view at the MFA since 1917 would close the loop for readers who want to go see it.

**SC-3 — The "which Sower hung at the Salon" question is handled correctly.**
The draft (Ch5) handles the Boston-vs-Yamanashi ambiguity honestly and non-dogmatically. No gap here.

**Verdict: CONDITIONAL PASS.** One MUST-ADD (ground the sowing-from-experience claim more concretely in Ch1 or Ch2 — currently present but thin). Everything else is covered; Van Gogh section is exemplary.

### MUST-ADD (essential, blocking)

**MA-1 — No date for *when* the painting was actually made / executed.**
The fact pack states unambiguously: "Date: 1857. Completed and shown the same year." The content file correctly carries `year: 1857`. But the narrative never clearly states *when Millet made this specific canvas*. Chapter 1 says "he circled it in drawings for years before he committed it to this large canvas" — but does not say he made the final canvas in 1857. Chapter 3 opens "In 1857 Millet sent the painting to the Salon" — which is the first hard date in the prose for the work's existence. The reader must infer that it was made in 1857, which they can do, but a work-read this thorough should state the date of execution directly. It is the single most basic identification fact for a painting, and it is never plainly said.

Verifiable fact: *The Gleaners* was painted and completed in 1857 (the same year it was first exhibited at the Salon). Source: Wikipedia, *The Gleaners*; Encyclopædia Britannica; Musée d'Orsay collection records.

**Action needed:** Add one explicit sentence — in Ch1's "The man who painted it" section, or at the opening of Ch2 — giving the date of execution: 1857, the culmination of about seven years of studies on the gleaning theme.

**MA-2 — The 7-year gestation is under-used.**
The fact pack notes that "Millet had circled the gleaning theme in drawings/studies for roughly seven years before this large painting." The draft mentions this in Ch1 ("circled it in drawings for years") but passes over it quickly. This is a genuinely load-bearing fact: the painting that looks like a snapshot of a single afternoon in a field is actually the end-product of nearly a decade of looking. That gap between the apparent immediacy and the long germination is part of what distinguishes this from reportage — it is not a quick painting of peasants; it is the most refined possible statement of a subject Millet had been trying to say for seven years. The draft is THIN on this, not MISSING, but a deeper treatment would strengthen the meaning chapter.

Verifiable fact: Millet worked toward the gleaning subject in drawings for approximately seven years before the 1857 canvas. Source: Encyclopædia Britannica (*Jean-François Millet*); Bates summaries; fact-pack ledger item #15.

**Action needed:** Either in the opening or meaning chapters, give this gestation its full weight — the seven-year slow approach, the accumulation of understanding — as the context that makes the final canvas so finished and unimprovable.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER (non-blocking)

**SC-1 — The women's anonymity as Millet's point.**
The draft correctly refrains from naming the three women (none reliably identified). But there is a brief opportunity, in the meaning chapter, to make this a positive observation: the anonymity is *itself* the argument — not three named models, but three types, or even one body shown three times through a cycle. The draft's Ch2 glances at this ("Three women, or one woman's whole exhausting day shown three times at once") but doesn't develop it as a deliberate artistic choice comparable to Courbet's hidden faces in Stone Breakers. Both artists made anonymity central; that parallel would reward a sentence.

**SC-2 — The primary-colors-as-dignity argument in Ch2.**
The observation that blue/red/yellow caps echo the colors reserved for kings' and cardinals' robes is excellent and original — one of the best readings in the four drafts. However, the draft presents this as straightforward observation, where it would be slightly more accurate to frame it as a powerful interpretive reading (Millet did not sign a note saying "I gave the peasants primary colors on purpose as a dignity claim"). The move is widely recognized in scholarship — fine to make it as a strong reading, just worth one hedging word ("as if the loudest color in the room belongs to the lowest people in it") so it doesn't harden into an asserted fact about Millet's intent.

**SC-3 — Millet's relationship to the Gleaners vs The Angelus.**
The draft mentions *The Angelus* (1857–59) both in Ch1 and Ch4 as a sibling work. This is correct and well-placed. One small gap: the draft doesn't note that The Angelus was *also completed in 1857* (begun 1857, finished 1859 — sometimes listed as 1857–59). Mentioning that Millet was working on *both* at roughly the same time, one facing poverty squarely (Gleaners) and one offering religious consolation (Angelus), would make the contrast between the two works richer. Not blocking, but textured.

**Verdict: CONDITIONAL PASS (two MUST-ADDs).** MA-1 (state when the painting was made — 1857) is a basic identification gap that should not survive into the final draft. MA-2 (the 7-year gestation is thin) is an essential framing fact that the current prose under-serves.
