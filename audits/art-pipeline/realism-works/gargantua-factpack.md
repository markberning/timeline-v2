# Fact Pack — Daumier, *Gargantua* (1831)

**Work:** *Gargantua*
**Artist:** Honoré Daumier (1808–1879)
**Date:** drawn / dated **1831**; the impression was put out into Gabriel Aubert's shop
window on or about **15–16 December 1831**.
**Medium:** **lithograph** (a print made by drawing in greasy crayon/ink on a flat block
of limestone, then chemically treating the stone so ink sticks only to the drawn marks
and is repelled elsewhere; the inked stone is pressed onto paper. Invented c. 1796 by
Alois Senefelder; it let an artist's own hand be mass-reproduced cheaply — the medium
that made cheap, daily political imagery possible).
**Subject:** King **Louis-Philippe I** (the "Citizen King," r. 1830–1848) drawn as
**Gargantua**, the gluttonous giant of François Rabelais's 16th-century satire.
**Dimensions:** image ≈ **8.5 × 12 in (≈ 21.4 × 30.5 cm)**, **landscape** (wider than
tall). Catalogue records vary slightly by impression: Yale gives the stone as
9¼ × 12¹⁄₁₆ in (23.5 × 30.6 cm). Use **8.5 × 12 in** for stats; orientation is landscape
(confirmed by the composition + the image file aspect ≈ 1.44).
**Now at:** **Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF), Paris**, with surviving impressions
also at Yale, the Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco, and others. Few impressions survive
(police destroyed the stone and remaining proofs).
**Rights:** public domain (artist d. 1879; work 1831).
**heroAspect:** 1136 × 790 px image file ⇒ **1.44** (w/h).

## The political world (DEFER the full politics to the Realism overview — terse here)

- **The July Monarchy / the Citizen King.** The **July Revolution of 1830** (three days
  of Paris street-fighting, "les Trois Glorieuses") toppled the reactionary Bourbon king
  Charles X. The deputies installed his cousin **Louis-Philippe** of the house of Orléans
  as a constitutional "**King of the French**" (not "King of France") — branded the
  **roi citoyen, the "Citizen King,"** a bourgeois monarch in a top hat carrying an
  umbrella, ruling for the propertied middle class. His regime (1830–1848) is the
  **July Monarchy**. The early hope curdled fast: it governed for bankers and the rich,
  and the press turned on it. (The larger 1830/1848 politics belongs to the Realism
  overview — keep it terse here.)

- **Charles Philipon and the satirical press.** **Charles Philipon** (1800–1862),
  a fearless caricaturist-entrepreneur, founded the weekly satirical journal
  **La Caricature** in **1831** (later also the daily **Le Charivari**, 1832).
  Philipon ran the most aggressive anti-Louis-Philippe campaign in print and was
  Daumier's employer/ringleader. Philipon's own famous gag: at his **November 1831**
  trial he sketched the king's head **metamorphosing in four stages into a pear**
  ("**la poire**" — French slang for "fool / fathead"). The pear became the regime's
  inescapable mocking emblem. Philipon was nonetheless convicted of insulting the king,
  fined 2,000 francs, and imprisoned. **In Daumier's print the king's head is the
  pear-head** — round-cheeked, tapering to a tuft on top — Philipon's gag made giant.

- **The publisher and printer.** Drawings for La Caricature were sold as separate prints
  through **Gabriel Aubert's** caricature shop in the **Galerie Véro-Dodat** (a glass-
  roofed Paris shopping arcade). The lithographic printer was **Hippolyte Delaporte**.
  *Gargantua* was put in Aubert's shop window / offered as a separate sheet — **it was
  drawn for / associated with La Caricature but was seized before it could circulate
  widely; scholars (Childs) stress it was sold in the shop window, not run in the
  journal's pages.** Frame this honestly: intended for the La Caricature world, suppressed
  on appearance.

- **Censorship / the press law.** Daumier, Aubert, and Delaporte were charged under the
  **press law of November 1830** with "arousing hatred and contempt of the king's
  government" and **offending the king's person** — the latter a treason-class offense,
  **lèse-majesté** (injuring the dignity of the sovereign). (The July Monarchy's tougher
  **September Laws of 1835** would later ban political caricature outright; that's after
  this print — note as aftermath, don't overclaim it as the cause here.)

## Gargantua / Rabelais (define)

**Gargantua** is the enormous, insatiable giant of **François Rabelais's** comic satire
*Gargantua* (1534) and *Pantagruel* — a figure of vast appetite and bodily excess (the
source of the English word *gargantuan*). Daumier's reader in 1831 knew the name as
shorthand for monstrous greedy consumption. Daumier turns the king into that giant.

## The image — what is actually in the print (verified vs. the file)

A **bloated giant king** sits in a low armchair/throne set on a platform, legs splayed,
belly enormous, **pear-shaped head** tipped back, **mouth gaping open**. A long **plank /
gangway (a ramp)** runs from the ground at lower-right steeply **up to the king's open
mouth**. Up this plank, a file of **tiny laborers carry baskets and sacks of coins /
tribute** — at the foot of the plank one bends over a large basket/hopper, loading the
king's food; a crowd of **ragged, destitute common people** is massed at the right,
the source of the squeezed wealth. Beneath / beside the king's seat, **what he excretes
is not waste but documents — patents, commissions, honors, decorations, ribbons** — and
**well-dressed officials / cronies scramble** for them and carry them off (toward a
government building — read as the Chamber of Deputies / the bureaucracy) at lower left.
The reading: **the people's taxes go up the ramp into the king; out the other end come
jobs, medals, and favors for the privileged.** Trickle-*up*. Signed "**h. Daumier**"
lower left.

## Daumier the man (DEFER career detail to Realism overview)

- **1808–1879.** Born Marseille, worked in Paris. A **prolific caricaturist** — roughly
  **4,000 lithographs** over his life, plus paintings, drawings, sculptures, wood
  engravings. The premier visual satirist of 19th-c. France.
- **Gargantua (1831)** is his early breakthrough and the print that made his name —
  and cost him **six months in prison**.
- Later, **as a painter**, Daumier turned the same unsentimental eye on the modern city's
  poor: **The Third-Class Carriage** (c. 1862–64, oil, Metropolitan Museum) — covered as a
  Realism work / in the Realism overview. He is one of the four Realists in the overview
  (Courbet, Millet, Daumier, Bonheur): the **Realist of the modern city**, where Courbet
  and Millet are the Realists of the province and the field.

## The prosecution & prison — the careful, accurate version

1. *Gargantua* appears (Aubert's window), **~15 Dec 1831**; **immediately seized/banned**;
   police order the **lithographic stone destroyed** and remaining proofs confiscated.
2. **Trial 23 February 1832** (some sources 22 Feb): Daumier, Aubert, Delaporte tried.
   Daumier convicted; sentenced to **six months in prison + a 500-franc fine**.
3. **The sentence was initially SUSPENDED.** Daumier kept working and kept needling the
   regime. After a later lithograph, he was **arrested at his parents' apartment in
   August 1832** and the suspended sentence activated.
4. He **served from 30 August 1832 at Sainte-Pélagie prison** (a Paris political/debtors'
   prison) and was **released 14 February 1833.** He drew while inside.
   → So "six months in prison for Gargantua" is true but with a real twist: the term was
   suspended at trial and only triggered months later. Tell it straight.
