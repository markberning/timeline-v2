## The painting at a glance

- **Title:** The Sower (French: *Le Semeur*)
- **Artist:** Jean-François Millet (1814–1875)
- **Date:** 1850
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 101.6 × 82.6 cm = **40 × 32½ in** = **3 ft 4 in (tall) × 2 ft 8½ in (wide)** — PORTRAIT orientation. heroAspect = 82.6/101.6 = **0.813**.
- **Location:** Museum of Fine Arts, Boston (MFA Boston)
- **Accession:** 17.1485
- **Credit line (MFA, exact):** "Gift of Quincy Adams Shaw through Quincy Adams Shaw, Jr. and Mrs. Marian Shaw Haughton" (1917)
- **Shown at:** the Paris Salon of 1850–51 — the SAME Salon that hung Courbet's *A Burial at Ornans* and *The Stone Breakers*.

## The version question (HANDLE HONESTLY)

There are multiple Sowers by Millet:
- **MFA Boston, 1850** — the famous one; the version reproduced by the Google Art Project (the Wikimedia file `Jean-François_Millet_-_The_Sower_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg` = the MFA Boston canvas). **ART_IMG credit "Museum of Fine Arts, Boston" is CONFIRMED CORRECT.**
- **Yamanashi Prefectural Museum, Kofu, Japan, 1850** — a nearly identical second version painted the same year.
- Earlier/related: a version at the National Museum Wales, Cardiff (c. 1847–48, higher horizon, less monumental figure); an oil at the Carnegie Museum of Art, Pittsburgh; pastels at the Clark (Williamstown) and the Walters (Baltimore).

**Which canvas actually hung at the 1850–51 Salon is genuinely ambiguous in the
sources.** The MFA describes its painting as "generally acknowledged as the one
shown at the Paris Salon of 1850–51." Other reputable sources state the Yamanashi
version was the one exhibited. → In the narrative, frame the Boston picture as the
famous, standard "original" version while flagging the which-canvas-hung debate as
debate, NOT settled fact. Marked **[VERIFY]** below.

## Provenance (well-documented)

- 1850 — painted by Millet at Barbizon.
- c. 1851/1852 — sold by the artist to **William Morris Hunt** (1824–1879), the American painter; Hunt had seen *The Sower* at the Salon (the Salon ran 1850–51, with Hunt usually said to have seen it in 1851) and it sent him to Barbizon to study with Millet for roughly two years. Hunt became the key early American champion of Millet.
- 1874 — Hunt sold it through Doll and Richards, Boston, to **Quincy Adams Shaw** (1825–1908), the Boston collector.
- 1917 (accessioned March 29) — given to the MFA by Shaw's heirs (Quincy Adams Shaw, Jr. and Mrs. Marian Shaw Haughton). On view ever since.

## Jean-François Millet (1814–1875)

- Born October 4, 1814, in **Gruchy**, a hamlet near Gréville on the Cotentin peninsula in **Normandy** (northwest France). A real farming family — he grew up doing field work, which is load-bearing for the authenticity of the peasant pictures.
- Trained in Cherbourg, then Paris from 1837; studied in the studio of **Paul Delaroche** (1797–1856), a successful academic history painter.
- Early career: portraits, some mythological/pastoral nudes, struggle and poverty.
- **1849:** fled a **cholera** epidemic in Paris and settled in **Barbizon**, a village at the edge of the Forest of Fontainebleau, ~30 miles (50 km) south-southeast of Paris.
- Died January 20, 1875, in Barbizon.

## Barbizon School (DEFINE inline)

- The **Barbizon School** = a loose group of French landscape and rural-life painters (Théodore Rousseau, Camille Corot, Charles-François Daubigny, Millet, others) who, from the 1830s–1860s, based themselves in and around the village of **Barbizon** to paint directly from the real countryside rather than from studio formulas or idealized Italianate scenery. Named after the village. A parent of Realism (the Realism overview lists "Barbizon landscape" as a parent). Millet is its peasant-figure wing; the others are mostly pure landscape.

## The Salon, the academy, the hierarchy of genres (DEFINE; DEFER detail upward)

- **The Salon** = the official annual (later biennial) State art exhibition in Paris, run by the **Academy of Fine Arts** (the State institution that set the rules of painting). It was the one show where a French career was made or broken.
- **Hierarchy of genres** = the official ranking of subject matter — history/myth/scripture at the top, ordinary modern life at the bottom; SCALE and seriousness were reserved for the top. (Full treatment is in the Realism overview and the Burial read; here, terse.)

## The reception (1850–51 Salon)

- *The Sower* drew "much attention and criticism." It was praised by some ("an energetic study full of movement") and dismissed by others (a critic on the rough surface: "trowel scrapings").
- The deeper alarm: a lone, monumental, almost menacing peasant — heroic scale for a field laborer — appearing only **two years after the 1848 Revolution** (which toppled King Louis-Philippe and briefly put workers/peasants at the center of French politics; the Realism overview tells 1848 in full). With the rural poor restless and "socialism threatening bourgeois society," a giant striding sower read to frightened viewers as a class threat. (MFA: viewers "were shocked by Millet's heroic treatment of a lowly peasant.")
- Cross-reference: at the SAME Salon, Courbet hung *A Burial at Ornans* (a village funeral at the scale of kings) and *The Stone Breakers* (two laborers, life-size). The two painters are the two halves of Realism's public arrival — Courbet the loud Paris firebrand, Millet the quiet Barbizon peasant-painter.

## Millet's politics (HANDLE CAREFULLY)

- Millet was **repeatedly charged with being a socialist** because he kept painting peasants, and he **resisted/rejected the label**. Defenders of the period stressed the "fundamentally classical" nature of his art. He saw himself as painting the dignity and biblical timelessness of rural labor, not making propaganda. → Frame: the menace was largely in the eye of frightened viewers; Millet's own stated aims were closer to the eternal/biblical than the revolutionary. Do not assert he WAS or WASN'T secretly political — give both the charge and his denial.

## The biblical / monumental reading

- A sower scattering seed is one of the most loaded images in Western culture (the Parable of the Sower; "as ye sow, so shall ye reap"). Millet gives a single field-worker the size and gravity of a religious figure — peasant as monument. Frame the biblical echo as a widely-noted resonance, not a documented statement of intent unless attributed.

## Afterlife / influence

- **Van Gogh's obsession:** Vincent van Gogh revered Millet above almost any painter, made **dozens of drawings/copies** after Millet, and across his career produced **30+ works on the sower theme**, most famously the blazing-color *Sower* canvases at Arles in 1888 (e.g. the *Sower at Sunset*) — deliberately swapping Millet's dark, gray, earthy palette for radiant orange/violet and a sun as a halo. A famous Van Gogh *Sower* is at the Kröller-Müller Museum, Otterlo. (Frame: Van Gogh transformed rather than mimicked.)
- The image has had a very long life as a symbol of labor, sowing, and rural dignity (reproduced widely; used in religious and political contexts).

## Look-closer pointers (verified against `img/sower.jpg` — PROSE, no crops)

A single peasant strides DIAGONALLY DOWN a dark hillside at dusk:
1. **The striding figure** — large, dark, nearly a silhouette, filling the canvas; mid-stride, left leg forward, weight pitched downhill toward the viewer; he reads as monumental and slightly menacing.
2. **The sowing arm** — his RIGHT arm is flung across his body to his left, hand open, just past the moment of the throw; the seed scatters; the LEFT hand holds the seed-bag (a sack/apron of grain) at his hip.
3. **The shadowed face** — a soft cloth/floppy hat is pulled low; the face beneath it is in shadow, features barely legible — he is almost faceless, which adds to the looming, anonymous force of the figure.
4. **Legs and feet** — blue trousers, a rust/brown jacket; the lower legs and feet are bound in straw/cloth wrappings, caked with the same dark earth he walks on.
5. **The ploughman + oxen + birds on the hill** — far upper RIGHT, tiny: a man driving a team of oxen finishes ploughing the slope, with the warm last light of the **dusk** sky and a sunlit haystack/bank behind him. A scatter of **birds** rises at the upper LEFT (after the seed). These tiny background figures set the giant foreground sower's scale.
6. **The dusk light** — the sky is a cold dusk gray-blue above, warming to a band of pale orange at the right horizon; the field and figure are sunk in deep gloom. It's the end of the working day.
