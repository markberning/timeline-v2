## The setting (what is literally depicted) — verified vs image + sources
- The **Paris horse market**, held on the tree-lined **Boulevard de l'Hôpital**, in the 13th arrondissement on the Left Bank, near the **Salpêtrière** (a vast former hospital/asylum). [Wikipedia; National Gallery London]
- **At the far left, in the distance: the dark grey domed roof and turret of the Salpêtrière** ("the Asylum of la Salpêtrière," NG London description). VERIFIED in the image — a faint pale dome low on the left horizon.
- A **line of plane trees** runs across the background behind the horses (the boulevard's avenue). VERIFIED in image (the green/brown canopy behind, denser at right).
- The horses are **draft horses** — heavy working breeds — explicitly including **Percherons** (the powerful grey/dappled French draft breed from the Perche region). The big dappled-grey rearing horses at centre read as Percherons. [Wikipedia]
- VERIFIED in image: a churning **left-to-right diagonal** of horses and handlers down the avenue; **grey/dappled draft horses rearing at centre**; a **dark (near-black) horse rearing** left-of-centre; a **chestnut/brown horse** mid-left; **handlers on foot and mounted** straining to control them (blue smocks, shirtsleeves); **clouds of pale dust** kicked up at the horses' feet; a crowd of small figures at right under the trees (buyers/the line of the market).
- Bonheur signed/dated at lower right ("Rosa Bonheur 1853" visible).

## How she made it — research method (documented)
- Bonheur **sketched at the horse market for about eighteen months, roughly twice a week** (sources: "for a year and a half … twice a week"; "summer 1850 to end 1851"). [Wikipedia]
- She **obtained official police permission to wear men's clothing** — a *permission de travestissement* (permit to cross-dress) — required of any woman wearing trousers in Paris under an 1800 ordonnance, **renewable** (every six months). She wore men's clothes (trousers, smock, short hair) to **sketch at the horse market, slaughterhouses, abattoirs, farms, riding schools and stockyards** without drawing a crowd. [Wikipedia; JSTOR Daily; Gardner Museum; Van Slyke / ncfs]
  - FRAMING (locked): the trousers were a **practical work permit**, not a stunt or eccentricity — the official justification was that men's clothes let her move through markets and abattoirs to study animals. State plainly; do not sensationalize.
- Her **anatomical study** was rigorous: she dissected animals and studied carcasses at the abattoirs to get the muscle right.

## The artist — who she was (framing-rich, documented)
- **The most celebrated / most famous woman painter of the 19th century** (Wikipedia: "widely considered to be the most famous female painter of the nineteenth century"). A working professional, not a "lady painter."
- An **animalier** (an *animal painter* — a recognised specialty of painters who took animals as their primary subject; *animalière* in the feminine). Animal painting sat low in the academic hierarchy of genres, below history and portrait — Bonheur made it pay like history painting.
- Already **established before the Horse Fair**: *Ploughing in the Nivernais* (1849), a French state commission (~3,000 francs), won a **First Medal at the Salon of 1849** (now Musée d'Orsay). So in 1853 she arrived at the Salon already a known, decorated name — the Horse Fair confirmed and globalised the fame, it did not create it. [Wikipedia; Musée d'Orsay]
- **Légion d'honneur (the French state's top order of merit, founded by Napoleon 1802):**
  - **1865 — decorated Chevalier (knight) of the Légion d'honneur**, the medal brought/pinned by **Empress Eugénie** (wife of Napoleon III), who reportedly said genius has no sex. **First woman artist to receive it.** [Wikipedia]
  - **1894 — promoted to Officier (Officer)** of the order, the first woman so promoted. [Wikipedia]
  - ⚠️ **DO NOT claim "grand cross."** The brief's "first woman awarded the grand cross of the Légion d'honneur" is NOT supported by Wikipedia (which says Chevalier 1865 → Officer 1894). Several popular sites loosely say "Grand Cross 1865," but that conflicts with the authoritative chronology and the order's grade structure. SAFE CLAIM: **first woman artist decorated (Chevalier, 1865); promoted Officer (1894).** [Wikipedia Rosa Bonheur] — see ledger flag.
- **Financial independence:** colossally successful commercially. With the Horse Fair money she bought the **Château de By** near Fontainebleau in **1859** — reportedly the **first woman to buy property in her own name** off her own earnings — and ran her own large household and menagerie (sheep, horses, even lions). [chateau-rosa-bonheur.fr; France Today; Smithsonian]
- **Companions (state plainly, honest record, not sensationalized):**
  - **Nathalie Micas** — Bonheur's lifelong companion; they lived together **over 40 years** until Micas's death (1889); they are buried together at Père Lachaise. Micas also obtained a *permission de travestissement*. Micas helped paint the **half-size NG-London replica**. [Wikipedia; Nathalie Micas Wikipedia]
  - **Anna Klumpke** — the American painter who became Bonheur's companion in her last years and her **sole heir**. [Wikipedia]

## The dealer & the painting's career (documented)
- **Ernest Gambart** — the Belgian-born, London-based art dealer who was Bonheur's dealer/promoter. He **bought the Horse Fair in 1854 for 40,000 French francs** and **toured it through Britain (c.1855–57)**, exhibiting it as a money-making attraction; he was a pioneer of **subsidiary/reproduction rights** and had the image **engraved** for mass sale. [Wikipedia; Ernest Gambart Wikipedia]
- **The engraving:** **Thomas Landseer** made the famous engraving (an *engraving* = a print pulled from an incised metal plate, the 19th-c. way to mass-distribute an image), based on a **half-size replica**. The print sold widely and made the composition famous across Britain and America before most people had seen the original. [Wikipedia]
- Queen Victoria reportedly had the painting brought to Buckingham Palace / Windsor for a private viewing during the British tour. [common in the literature — FRAME as reported]

## Provenance (verified order; FT/IN n/a — prices in francs/dollars)
1. **Rosa Bonheur** (the artist), painted 1852–55.
2. **1854 — Ernest Gambart** (dealer, London), bought from the artist for **FF 40,000**; toured it through Britain and had it engraved. [Wikipedia]
3. **1857 — William Parkinson Wright** (a collector). [Wikipedia]
4. **1866 — Alexander Turney Stewart** (the New York department-store magnate). [Wikipedia]
5. **March 1887 — Cornelius Vanderbilt II**, bought at the estate auction of Stewart's widow for **$53,000**, and **immediately gave it to the Metropolitan Museum of Art**. [Wikipedia]
6. **1887 – present — The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.** Gift of Cornelius Vanderbilt, 1887; accession 87.25; on view (gallery 812). [Met API]

## Other versions (documented — for the afterlife chapter)
- **National Gallery, London (NG621):** a Bonheur **half-size replica, ~120 × 254.6 cm (≈ 4 ft × 8 ft 4 in)**, c.1855, painted by Bonheur **with help from Nathalie Micas** for Gambart so Landseer could engrave it; donated to the NG in **1859 from the estate of Jacob Bell**. [Wikipedia; National Gallery London]
- Two further half-size replicas exist (McConnel family / Jack Wheeler; the Hill-Lowe one sold Sotheby's 1978). [Wikipedia] — mention only if useful; keep tight.

## Salon 1853 reception (documented)
- First shown at the **Salon of 1853** (the *Salon* = the official annual state art exhibition in Paris, the one show that made or broke a career; run by the Academy). [Wikipedia]
- **Praised by the critics** on debut; commentators noted the **"masculine" character/vigour** of the work (a back-handed compliment of the era — "good, *for a woman*"). [Wikipedia]
- The **scale** is the structural shock: an eight-by-sixteen-foot canvas — history-painting acreage — spent on horse-dealers and draught animals at a market. (Same hierarchy-of-genres argument the Realism overview makes; here applied to the *animalier* category.)
- *The Horse Fair* became one of the most reproduced/popular paintings of the 19th century, especially in Britain and America via the engraving.
