# Framing / Fairness Gate — Realism Batch A
Critic: FRAMING / FAIRNESS gate
Date: 2026-05-25
Works audited: stone-breakers · studio · sower · gleaners

Grades: FAIR / TILTED / DISTORTED
Must-fix = distortions that block ship. Should-fix = tilts that weaken the read.

---

## 1. The Stone Breakers (Courbet, 1849)

**Grade: FAIR**

This is the strongest framing of the four. The draft does the hardest thing well: it
holds Courbet's own "I saw them" account in genuine tension with Proudhon's socialist
reading rather than collapsing the two. The hidden-faces analysis is honest about
the painting's refusal of pity vs. propaganda. The Proudhon paragraph gives real
biographical context (Ornans connection, the "property is theft" lineage) without
turning him into a mere label-dispenser. The Francis Wey letter is sourced by name.
The romantic-genius risk is low because the draft consistently shows Courbet's
choices as calculated artistic decisions, not solitary genius inspiration.

**No MUST-FIX items.**

**SHOULD-FIX (two items):**

S1. Courbet's circle — present but thin.
The draft names Proudhon and Wey but does not name Champfleury (who coined the word
"Realism" and was Courbet's most important intellectual collaborator in shaping the
movement's self-understanding) or Max Buchon (Courbet's childhood friend from Ornans
who wrote on his behalf and helped secure Bruyas's patronage). Both appear in the
Studio draft. The gap is not distorting — the Stone Breakers draft is a work-level
read that correctly defers movement context to the overview — but a single sentence
somewhere in the Salon chapter naming the wider circle would foreclose the lone-genius
reading. Suggested addition: one clause in the "The painting did not arrive alone"
section, e.g., "…part of a small Ornans circle — the critic Champfleury, the poet Max
Buchon, the philosopher Proudhon — who were arguing Realism into a movement that same
year." (Sourced fact: Champfleury wrote the 1857 essay "Le Réalisme"; Buchon's
friendship with Courbet documented in Rubin, *Realism and Social Vision in Courbet &
Proudhon*, Princeton, 1980.)

S2. The "first socialist painting" framing.
The chapter title is '"The first socialist painting"' (in quotes, interrogative). The
draft then immediately undercuts the claim and correctly restores the Proudhon/Courbet
tension. The quotes-plus-interrogation do the job. This is FAIR. No fix required —
noting here only because the phrase lives on the surface and could scan as overclaim
to a reader who doesn't get past the heading.

**Verdict:** Honest, nuanced, handles the Courbet/Proudhon tension well. SHIP with
the optional circle sentence added.

## 2. The Painter's Studio (Courbet, 1854–55)

**Grade: TILTED — one MUST-FIX, two SHOULD-FIX**

The draft does excellent work on the allegory structure, the Proudhon/Baudelaire/
Champfleury identifications, and the Napoleon III caveat (correctly flagged as
interpretation, not fact). The problem is a consistent romantic-genius tilt that
inflates Courbet's singular agency and erases the collaborative intellectual labor
that produced both the painting and the Realism movement it summarizes.

**MUST-FIX:**

M1. Bruyas reduced to "money-man"; his intellectual role erased.
The draft introduces Alfred Bruyas only as the patron "who bankrolled him." In
fact Bruyas was a serious collector and thinker about modern art who engaged Courbet
in an extended correspondence about the nature of realism and self-portraiture; he
contributed to shaping the conceptual program Courbet called his "real allegory."
Their relationship produced the famous *The Meeting* (Bonjour M. Courbet, 1854) the
same year Studio was being planned. Reducing him to a wallet is factually thin and
structurally tilts the read toward lone genius.
Exact phrase: "With money from his patron Alfred Bruyas (a wealthy collector from the
south of France who bankrolled him)…"
Fairer framing: "With financial backing — and intellectual encouragement — from his
patron Alfred Bruyas (a collector from Montpellier who had corresponded with Courbet
for years about art, self-portraiture, and the project of making painting honest), he
put up a building of his own…"
Source: Michael Fried, *Courbet's Realism* (Chicago, 1990), ch. 3; the Courbet–Bruyas
correspondence is in the Musée Fabre, Montpellier.

**SHOULD-FIX:**

S1. Champfleury's role understated in the "Seven years" chapter.
The draft names Champfleury as "the critic credited with first using the word
'Realism' for the new art." That framing is passive-credit. Champfleury did not
merely coin a term: he wrote the theoretical defense of Realism that gave it
intellectual coherence and public standing; he organized critical response; and the
letter Courbet wrote explaining the Studio is addressed *to Champfleury* — i.e.,
Courbet needed to explain his own painting to his closest theoretical ally. The draft
correctly quotes Champfleury's letter as the source for what the painting means, then
undersells him. A short amplification — "the critic who had built Realism's
theoretical case in print" — would correct the imbalance without turning this into a
biography of Champfleury. Source: Jules-Antoine Castagnary's *Salons* and Jules
Champfleury, *Le Réalisme* (Paris, 1857).

S2. The model's agency: absent entirely.
The "nude with nowhere to be" analysis is conceptually sharp (retired academic nude,
old subject watching new art get made). But who the model was, whether she was a
professional who participated knowingly in this programmatic joke, is simply passed
over. The draft treats her purely as a sign, which reproduces the original erasure.
This is a SHOULD-FIX not a MUST-FIX because the identity of Studio's nude model is
not definitively established; the art-historical literature debates it. The fix is
not "name her" (she may be unnameable) but one clause acknowledging the person behind
the sign: "a real model whose identity is now uncertain, positioned by Courbet to play
the role of the displaced academic tradition." That is both more honest and more
interesting.

**Verdict:** Courbet's network is present but the collaborative intellectual
infrastructure — Bruyas's engagement, Champfleury's theoretical authorship — is
downplayed in a way that tilts toward lone-genius. The Bruyas fix is MUST-FIX; the
others improve the read. TILTED — do not ship until M1 is addressed.

## 3. The Sower (Millet, 1850)

**Grade: FAIR**

The Millet-as-actual-peasant framing is one of the best anti-romantic-genius moves in
all four drafts, and the draft does it in the first sentence. The "both things are
true — Millet meant reverence, Paris read revolt" framing in the Meaning chapter is
exactly right on the disputed-politics front: it neither sanitizes the politics into
mere "scenes of rural life" nor overclaims a settled socialist intent. The Barbizon
School paragraph correctly gives real agency to Rousseau, Corot, Daubigny, and others
as collaborators in the landscape-painting project before Millet joined.

The Van Gogh afterlife section correctly identifies influence vs. imitation and names
the Kröller-Müller canvas, though the "more than thirty works on the sowing theme"
claim should be confirmed (see S1 below).

**No MUST-FIX items.**

**SHOULD-FIX (two items):**

S1. "More than thirty" Van Gogh sower works — verify before shipping.
The draft states Van Gogh made "more than thirty works on the sowing theme." This
claim lives in the shipped prose and is specific enough to embarrass if wrong.
The commonly cited figure is approximately 30 works referencing The Sower across Van
Gogh's career; some counts are higher or lower depending on whether drawings, letters
with sketches, and copies after Millet are all counted. Before shipping, confirm
against the Van Gogh Museum catalog or Ronald Pickvance, *Van Gogh in Arles* (MMA,
1984). If uncertain, hedge: "dozens of drawings and more than twenty finished works."

S2. The "quiet, rural half vs. Courbet the loud city-facing half" binary.
The draft sets up Courbet (loud / city / manifesto) against Millet (quiet / village /
no jokes). That binary is productively clarifying but slightly oversimplified — it
implies Millet was not politically legible in Paris, when the Salon reaction described
in Ch. 3 makes clear he very much was. The Meaning chapter corrects this (the
"nervous Paris saw revolt" line is good), but the introduction in Ch. 1 should add
one beat noting that the "quiet" version was still read as dangerous, so the contrast
is in method, not in political effect. This is a structural coherence note more than
a fairness note, but it protects against the "sanitized into mere scenes of rural
life" failure mode.

**Verdict:** The Millet-as-peasant framing is genuine and sustained. The
disputed-politics tension is honestly held. The Barbizon peers are present and
credited. FAIR — ship after verifying the Van Gogh count (S1).

## 4. The Gleaners (Millet, 1857)

**Grade: FAIR — with one factual flag that needs resolution before ship**

This is the strongest framing work on the question of female subjects / subjects with
agency. The three gleaners are not aestheticized or sentimentalized — the draft
explicitly refuses prettiness, names "dignity without sentimentality" as the central
move, and the Looking chapter treats the women's physical postures, the meagreness of
their yield, and the social geography of the field (wealthy harvest behind, poverty
bent in front) with genuine analytical respect. The bodies are not props; the work is
the subject.

The Millet-politics question is handled with appropriate care — the draft twice marks
its own uncertainty with in-text [VERIFY] flags, which is honest self-notation but
also a sign that those passages need resolution before ship (see S1 and S2 below).

The Pommery afterlife is well-handled: she is named fully (Jeanne-Alexandrine Louise
Pommery), identified by her role (head of the champagne house), and the provenance
note correctly distinguishes the 1889 auction purchase from the 1891 bequest.

**No MUST-FIX items — but the two in-text [VERIFY] flags must be resolved.**

**SHOULD-FIX (three items, one requiring pre-ship resolution):**

S1. [VERIFY] on Millet's politics — must be resolved, not left in shipped prose.
The draft's Meaning chapter contains two bracketed self-verification flags:
"[VERIFY: Millet's politics are genuinely disputed… the section below presents the
dispute, asserts neither.]" and "[VERIFY: this reflects the commonly reported
stance… should be checked against a primary or scholarly source before shipping.]"
These are the right instincts, and they flag exactly the contested claim: Millet
repeatedly distancing himself from socialist readings.
This IS well-documented. Primary source: Millet's letters to Alfred Sensier, his
biographer, consistently express that his intent was moral/spiritual rather than
political; e.g., his famous statement that he wanted "to paint nothing that was not
the expression of a real truth" (reported in Alfred Sensier, *La Vie et l'oeuvre de
J.-F. Millet*, Paris, 1881). Secondary: Robert Herbert, *Jean-François Millet*
(exhibition catalog, Hayward Gallery / Grand Palais, 1975–76) is the standard
scholarly source and confirms Millet's resistance to the socialist label while
documenting the reception gap. The [VERIFY] flags should be replaced with the
sourced claim before ship; they are not blockers to the framing, just loose ends.

S2. [VERIFY] on the "no biblical consolation" reading — also needs resolution.
The second [VERIFY] flag appears at the end of the Meaning chapter: "[VERIFY: the
'no biblical consolation in this canvas' reading is one strong art-historical
line; others stress the quietly sacred tone Millet brings to peasant labor — present
as a live tension, not a verdict.]" 
This is actually correct as written — the tension IS live and the draft presents it
honestly. The flag can be removed and the sentence left as-is, since the draft
already signals it as a contested reading ("one strong art-historical line"). Source
for both sides: Robert Herbert (above); T.J. Clark, *The Absolute Bourgeois* (Thames
and Hudson, 1973), ch. 3, for the secular/political reading.

S3. Pommery's gender as a framing asset — underused.
Jeanne-Alexandrine Louise Pommery was not just the head of a champagne house; she was
one of the most powerful businesswomen in mid-19th-century France, who took over the
company after her husband's death and built it into an empire. The afterlife chapter
correctly names her but does not note the irony that the painting of the rural
female poor was purchased and bequeathed to the nation by a woman of significant
independent wealth and agency — the furthest possible extreme from the gleaners
themselves, yet a woman nonetheless. This is not a MUST-FIX; it is an optional
enrichment note that, if added, would make the afterlife richer and more honest about
whose choices shaped the painting's survival. One sentence is enough.
Source: Kolleen Guy, *When Champagne Became French* (Johns Hopkins, 2003), ch. 2,
on Pommery's role in the house.

**Verdict:** Strongest treatment of female subjects in the batch. The gleaners are
treated with genuine respect for their agency within the subject matter (they are not
romanticized, not anonymized into pure symbol). The two [VERIFY] flags need to be
closed with sources before ship but do not represent distortions — they are the
author's honest uncertainty about contested claims that happen to be resolvable. FAIR
— do not ship with open [VERIFY] flags; close them using the sources above.

## Summary

| Work | Grade | Ship? |
|---|---|---|
| The Stone Breakers | FAIR | Yes — optional circle sentence recommended |
| The Painter's Studio | TILTED | Hold — resolve M1 (Bruyas) before shipping |
| The Sower | FAIR | Yes — verify Van Gogh count before shipping |
| The Gleaners | FAIR | Yes — close the two [VERIFY] flags with sources |

**Pattern across the batch:** The romantic-genius risk is mostly managed well. The
one real tilt (Studio / Bruyas) is a specific, fixable under-description. The Millet
works do something genuinely harder than the Courbet works — sustaining the
"Millet resisted the socialist label but the radical effect was real" tension — and
they do it honestly across both the Sower and Gleaners without collapsing into
either "harmless pastoral" or "deliberate manifesto." The Gleaners' treatment of the
three women as people with bodies, labor, and diminishing rights (rather than as an
aesthetic composition) is the batch's strongest piece of anti-erasure framing.

**The one universal gap:** None of the four drafts discusses who the actual models
were — by name, or even by acknowledged anonymity. For the Courbet works the two
stone-breakers are confirmed as unnamed road workers from Maisières; for the Millet
works the gleaners' identities are unrecorded. Acknowledging that we don't know who
these specific people were — and that the historical record did not care to preserve
their names — would be a small act of honesty that reinforces the "dignity not pity"
argument the drafts are already making. Not a MUST-FIX, but a note for the
coordinator to consider as a cross-work consistency edit.
