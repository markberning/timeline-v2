# Fact pack — Gustave Courbet, *The Stone Breakers* (1849)

**Kind:** WORK (deep read, 5 chapters). Chain: Works of Realism, index 2 of 9 (after *A Burial at Ornans*, before the rest of the Realism work-reads).
**Author's note to the critics:** the original is **destroyed**; only reproductions survive, and the one we ship is **desaturated / near-monochrome** because the surviving reproductions of the lost original are not reliable color records. ## Core identification
- **Artist:** Gustave Courbet (1819–1877), French. [VERIFY — well established]
- **Title:** *The Stone Breakers* (Fr. *Les Casseurs de pierres*). [VERIFY]
- **Date:** 1849 (painted late 1849; sometimes given 1849–50). [VERIFY]
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas. [VERIFY]
- **Dimensions:** **165 × 257 cm** (height × width) = **5 ft 5 in × 8 ft 5 in**.
  - 165 cm ÷ 2.54 = 64.96 in → 5 ft 5 in. 257 cm ÷ 2.54 = 101.18 in → 8 ft 5 in.
  - heroAspect (W/H) = 257 / 165 = **1.558 → 1.56**. [VERIFY — Wikipedia rounds to "1.5 m × 2.6 m"; 165×257 cm is the standard catalogue figure across multiple sources.]
  - **Life-size:** the figures are at, or close to, human scale on a canvas over eight feet wide. [VERIFY — widely stated; "life-size" is the standard characterization.]
- **Formerly:** Gemäldegalerie (Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister), Dresden. [VERIFY]
- **Status:** **Destroyed / "missing," presumed destroyed, February 1945.** [VERIFY — see "Destruction" below; circumstances contested.]

## The two figures (verified against image + Courbet's own letter)
- **An old man, kneeling** on one knee (right of center), swinging a long-handled hammer/sledgehammer down onto stone; face hidden under a **straw hat**; white shirt, striped/buttoned waistcoat, baggy patched trousers, **wooden sabots (clogs)**. [VERIFY — Courbet's letter; visible in image.]
- **A young man / boy, standing** (left), seen from behind, **straining to carry a basket of broken stones**; we see his back and the nape of his neck, face turned away; torn white shirt (a rip at the shoulder), a **suspender/leather strap**, ragged trousers, worn/mismatched footwear. [VERIFY — Courbet's letter + Dupays; visible in image.]
- **Both faces are hidden / averted** — the standing youth turns his back (we see his nape); the old man's face is under the hat brim. Contemporary critic **A.J. Dupays** complained Courbet had "suppressed the two heads." [VERIFY — quoted in 19thcenturyart-facos.com; load-bearing for the "refusal of pathos" reading. I paraphrase, not quote at length.]
- **Tools & gear, scattered around them:** a pick/mattock, a basket (at far left), broken stone in heaps; a **small dark cooking pot / pail and a bit of bread + spoon at the right edge** — their meal. [The pot/bread is VISIBLE IN THE IMAGE; I did NOT find it described in the sources I read, so the prose frames it as "off to the side sits a little black cooking pot… presumably their lunch," i.e. visible-fact + hedged reading, NOT asserted as documented.]
- **Background:** a steep dark bank / hillside fills almost the whole top of the canvas; only a thin sliver of pale sky at the upper right. The hill presses the two figures forward; there is almost no open space, no horizon to escape into. [VERIFY — visible in image; consistent with standard descriptions of the compressed, airless setting.]
- **Signature:** "G. Courbet," lower left. [Visible in image.]

## Origin — Courbet's own account (the "I have seen them" letter)
- In **late November 1849** Courbet wrote to the historian **Francis Wey** (and his wife Marie Wey) describing how, near **Maisières** (a village near Ornans, on the way to the Château of Saint-Denis where he meant to paint a landscape), he stopped to look at **two men breaking stones on the road**, was struck by "the most complete expression of poverty," and arranged for them to come pose at his studio the next morning. [VERIFY — Wikipedia quotes the letter; this is the documented genesis. ATTRIBUTION NOTE: one secondary source (artincontext) attributes a similar account to Champfleury + Wey; the primary recipient per Wikipedia is **Wey**. I attribute it to Wey and frame it as "by his own account, in a letter."]
- Courbet's letter also describes the figures in detail (the old man of seventy with the straw hat, patched trousers and cracked sabots; the young man with the tattered shirt, leather suspender, holed boots). [VERIFY — quoted on Wikipedia. I paraphrase his description rather than quote the full passage.]
- **Courbet's own framing was anti-allegorical:** for him it began as something he *saw* — two real men by a real road — not a thesis. [VERIFY — standard reading, e.g. "for Courbet it was simply a memory of something he had seen."]

## Reception — the Salon, the Burial's twin
- First shown at the **Salon of 1850–1851** (the Salon ran across that winter). [VERIFY]
- Hung in the **same Salon as *A Burial at Ornans*** — the two monumental Courbets arrived as a coordinated pair (same program: heroic scale, common laborers, zero idealization). [VERIFY — established; matches our Burial work-read, which already names this pairing. CROSS-REF the Burial read; DEFER the full Salon-scandal mechanics to the Realism overview + Burial read.]
- **Wooden clogs satirized:** the press caricatured the figures, exaggerating the size of the sabots. [VERIFY — stated in secondary sources.]

## Meaning — Proudhon, dignity vs pity
- **Pierre-Joseph Proudhon** (1809–1865), the radical philosopher (an Ornans man, Courbet's friend), read the painting as social truth-telling — a "**socialist painting**," "a masterpiece in its genre," an icon of the laboring poor / a condemnation of a society that grinds people between childhood and old age. He read the young worker as "the proletariat." [VERIFY — Proudhon's posthumous *Du principe de l'art et de sa destination sociale* (1865) is the source of the extended reading; widely cited. The famous "first socialist picture"-type label is associated with Proudhon's reading; I frame it as Proudhon's READING, not a fact about the painting, and not a flat "the first socialist painting" claim.]
- **The honest tension to keep:** Courbet *saw* it; Proudhon *theorized* it. The painting itself refuses sentimentality — no pleading eyes, no pathos, no uplift — which is exactly what lets it read as dignity (they are monumental, serious, real) rather than pity (poster-child victims). [VERIFY — standard art-historical reading; the hidden faces are the formal mechanism, per Dupays.]
- **[LEGEND]/hedge:** the neat phrase "the first socialist painting" is a label attached to the picture in hindsight (via Proudhon's reading); it is not a documented self-description by Courbet and not an uncontested fact. FRAME as a reading.

## Afterlife — Dresden, 1945, a painting that survives only in reproduction
- The original entered the **Gemäldegalerie, Dresden** (it had passed into German collections; the Dresden picture gallery held it by the 20th century). [VERIFY — "formerly Gemäldegalerie, Dresden" is the standard collection line.]
- **February 1945:** lost during the end of WWII. The **standard account**: it was on a **transport moving pictures out of Dresden toward Königstein Fortress** when the convoy was bombed (Feb 1945), destroyed along with **~154 other paintings** (figures of "153 other paintings" / "154 pictures" both appear). [VERIFY — widely repeated.]
- **CONTESTED:** **Richard Raskin (1988)** argued the painting was **not** on the doomed transport but had **gone missing in 1944** after being moved; the museum officially lists it as **"missing."** [VERIFY — cited on Wikipedia. The prose FRAMES the destruction as the standard account AND flags that the exact circumstances are contested / the museum status is "missing," presumed destroyed. Do NOT assert the bombing as settled fact.]
- **Only reproductions survive.** The reproductions are unreliable as a color record (the picture we show is desaturated / near-monochrome) — so we treat its muted tone as a record of a lost work, not as Courbet's palette. [VERIFY — this exact framing was locked in the Realism movement proof-run (run 7): "color reproductions exist; reframed to 'survives only in reproductions'; shown desaturated as an honest record." We do NOT claim "only black-and-white reproductions exist" — that was the overclaim the movement gate caught.]
- **A second, smaller, reversed version** by Courbet survives in the **Oskar Reinhart Collection, Winterthur** (Switzerland) — ~56 × 85 cm, a mirror image, darker. [VERIFY — Wikipedia. Mentioned briefly as a survivor; NOT the work we read.]
