**Overall verdict:** CLEAR. One stray bracket must be removed before ship; the Louvre gloss is a tidy improvement but not blocking.

## 2. Gargantua (Daumier, 1831)

### Chapter grades

| Chapter | Grade | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| king | CLEAR | Louis-Philippe, July Monarchy, roi citoyen, La Caricature, caricature, and lithography are all glossed in-chapter. Rabelais/Gargantua introduced in looking, not here — fine. |
| looking | CLEAR | All visual elements explained; Rabelais/Gargantua gloss is present and correct |

| meaning | CLEAR | Argument well-structured; Realism connection explained |
| afterlife | CLEAR | September Laws cross-ref is self-consistent; Sainte-Pélagie flagged in trial |

### MUST-FIX (comprehension blockers)

**Ch. trial — "Galerie Véro-Dodat" used without gloss:**
> "the Galerie Véro-Dodat, one of the glass-roofed shopping arcades of central Paris"

The inline gloss ("glass-roofed shopping arcade") is actually present and sufficient. Not a blocker. *Downgrading to no action.*

**Ch. trial — "proofs" used without gloss (first use in this chapter):**
> "ordered the lithographic stone destroyed and the remaining proofs confiscated"

"Proofs" (printed copies pulled from the stone) is undefined here and the word has a different everyday meaning (evidence). A zero-knowledge reader will likely think it means "evidence" rather than "printed copies."
- Suggested gloss: "the remaining **proofs** (printed copies pulled from the stone) confiscated"

**Ch. king — "La Caricature" named, then "caricature" glossed, but in the WRONG order:**
> "he founded a satirical weekly called **La Caricature** — a journal whose entire purpose was to mock the powerful in print (the word 'caricature' means a drawing that exaggerates someone's features or character to ridicule them)."

This is actually well-handled — the gloss for "caricature" the word appears in the same sentence that introduces the journal name. No issue.

### SHOULD-FIX

**Ch. king — "the revolution of 1789" mentioned with no prior gloss in this work:**
> "trying to wind the clock back to before the great Revolution of 1789"

A 15-year-old who has not read the Realism overview may not know what 1789 refers to. One-phrase gloss would help.
- Suggested: "the great French Revolution of 1789, which had overthrown the monarchy and shaken Europe"

**Ch. king — "roi citoyen" glossed but the English is given inside quotes inside the bold:**
> "the **roi citoyen**, the '**Citizen King**'"

Fine as is; both terms are adjacent. No action needed.

**Ch. trial — "Sainte-Pélagie" given no explanation:**
> "the doors of **Sainte-Pélagie** — a Paris prison that held debtors and political offenders — closed behind him"

The inline dash-gloss is present. Fine.

**Ch. afterlife — "September Laws of 1835" cross-referenced from trial chapter (ch. 3) but the full explanation only lives there:**
> "when the September Laws of 1835 finally banned political caricature outright (Chapter 3)"

The chapter cross-ref is included, but a reader who starts at the afterlife chapter won't know what it was. A one-line reminder would help:
- Suggested: "the September Laws of 1835, which banned political caricature outright (Chapter 3 covers them in full)"  
This is already almost exactly what the text says — actually fine as written. No action needed.

**Overall verdict:** CLEAR with one MUST-FIX (gloss "proofs" as printed copies). Clean prose throughout; no dangling cross-refs; Rabelais handled well.

## 3. The Third-Class Carriage (Daumier, c.1862–64)

### Chapter grades

| Chapter | Grade | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| rail | CLEAR | Railway class tiers defined clearly; lithograph re-glossed (reader may not have read Gargantua first) |
| looking | CLEAR | All visual elements described in plain language; "squaring for transfer" not in this chapter — fine, it's in unfinished |
| unfinished | CLEAR | "Squaring up / squaring for transfer" gets a full plain-language explanation; underdrawing, X-ray all explained |
| meaning | CLEAR | Realism framing explained from scratch; no jargon assumed |
| afterlife | NEEDS-GLOSS | One term and one unexplained person (see below) |

### MUST-FIX (comprehension blockers)

**Ch. afterlife — "bequest" used, then glossed, but the gloss comes AFTER the word in a parenthetical and the sequence is slightly confusing:**
> "the painting came to the **Metropolitan Museum of Art** in New York as a **bequest** — that is, a gift left in her will"

Actually this is correct and clear — the gloss follows immediately after an em-dash. Fine. *Downgrading to no action.*

**Ch. afterlife — "Mary Cassatt" introduced with no who-she-is:**
> "guided for years by her friend, the American painter **Mary Cassatt**"

A zero-knowledge 15-year-old has no reason to know who Mary Cassatt is. She needs a one-line identification.
- Suggested: "guided for years by her friend **Mary Cassatt**, an American painter who championed French Impressionism in the United States"

**Ch. afterlife — "H. O. Havemeyer Collection" — who is H. O. Havemeyer?**
> "part of the celebrated H. O. Havemeyer Collection"

The chapter introduces Louisine Havemeyer and mentions "her husband" but never names or identifies him as "H. O. Havemeyer." A first-time reader won't connect the two.
- Suggested: either spell out "Henry Osborne Havemeyer" on first use and then use H. O. naturally, or replace "the celebrated H. O. Havemeyer Collection" with "her collection (known as the H. O. Havemeyer Collection, after her late husband)."

### SHOULD-FIX

**Ch. rail — "Les Chemins de fer" given in French with no English gloss:**
> "a whole lithograph series on **the railways** (*Les Chemins de fer*)"

The English "the railways" appears immediately before the French title, so this is readable — but adding a parenthetical "(literally: The Railways)" or simply dropping the French title (it's not needed for comprehension) would be cleaner.

**Ch. meaning — "Impressionists" mentioned with no gloss:**
> "The Impressionists, a few years later, would fill their canvases with exactly these anonymous urban crowds"

A zero-knowledge reader may not know who the Impressionists are. Since this is a forward-looking comparison at the end of a chapter (not load-bearing to the argument), a brief gloss suffices.
- Suggested: "The Impressionists — the movement that followed Realism in the 1870s–80s — would fill their canvases…"

**Overall verdict:** NEEDS-GLOSS (minor). Two MUST-FIX items in the afterlife chapter (Cassatt ID; H. O. Havemeyer disambiguation). Rest of the work is clear.

### MUST-FIX (comprehension blockers)

**Ch. bonheur — "Empress Eugénie" introduced with no who-she-is:**
> "the **Empress Eugénie** — the wife of Napoleon III, then ruling France"

The text actually does supply "the wife of Napoleon III, then ruling France" in the same sentence. Fine as is. *Downgrading to no action.*

**Ch. bonheur — "Légion d'honneur" named; "founded by the first Napoleon" but no plain-language gloss of what it actually is:**
> "the cross of the **Légion d'honneur** (the French state's highest order of merit, founded by the first Napoleon)"

The parenthetical gloss is present. Fine.

**Ch. bonheur — "Père Lachaise cemetery" used with no gloss:**
> "the two are buried together at Père Lachaise cemetery in Paris"

A 15-year-old reader outside France will not know this is a famous, historic cemetery. The name is non-load-bearing to the argument; either add a brief gloss or just say "a Paris cemetery." The current phrasing implies it's significant (it's one of the most famous cemeteries in the world) without saying why.
- Suggested: "Père-Lachaise, the famous historic cemetery in Paris where many of France's great artists and writers are buried"

**Ch. afterlife — "Ernest Gambart" introduced as "a Belgian-born art dealer" — sufficient:**
The text identifies him. Fine.

**Ch. afterlife — "engraving" used without a gloss:**
> "He had *The Horse Fair* turned into an **engraving** (a print made from an image cut into a metal plate, the era's technology for mass-producing a picture)"

The parenthetical gloss is present. Fine.

**Ch. afterlife — "Thomas Landseer" named as "the engraver" — no further ID:**
> "the work done by the engraver **Thomas Landseer**"

A zero-knowledge reader doesn't need to know who Landseer is — the role ("the engraver") is sufficient. But since his famous brother Edwin Landseer (animal painter, also Royal Academy) is a well-known name, and might create a false connection, it would be cleaner to omit the personal name entirely or add a brief parenthetical.
- Suggested: omit "Thomas Landseer" (the name adds nothing to the story) OR add "(brother of the celebrated animal painter Edwin Landseer)."  
Low priority.

**Ch. afterlife — "William Parkinson Wright" and "Alexander Turney Stewart" introduced by name only:**
> "passed to the English collector **William Parkinson Wright** in 1857, and then…into the collection of **Alexander Turney Stewart**, the New York department-store magnate"

Stewart gets a profession gloss ("New York department-store magnate"). Wright gets nothing.
- Suggested: add a brief gloss for Wright: "the English collector **William Parkinson Wright**" → "the English art collector **William Parkinson Wright**" (minimal; the word "collector" itself is the gloss needed here, and it is present — fine).

**Ch. afterlife — "Cornelius Vanderbilt II" named as "of the railroad dynasty" — but "of the railroad dynasty" is the only gloss:**
A 15-year-old outside the US may not know the Vanderbilts. The gloss "of the railroad dynasty" is present but spare. Low priority — the name is not load-bearing.

### SHOULD-FIX

**Ch. market — "abattoirs" used with no English equivalent:**
> "she studied dead ones, dissecting carcasses and working in the abattoirs (the slaughterhouses)"

The inline gloss "(the slaughterhouses)" is present. Fine.

**Ch. market — "Ploughing in the Nivernais" referenced without explaining what Nivernais is:**
> "**Ploughing in the Nivernais** (1849), a great frieze of oxen dragging a plough through heavy earth"

"Nivernais" is a French region. A zero-knowledge reader doesn't need to know where it is, but the word is unexplained and slightly opaque. Low priority; the description of the work ("oxen dragging a plough through heavy earth") is enough.

**Ch. salon — "First Medal" at the Salon mentioned (in market chapter) but "First Medal" is an internal academy ranking not defined:**
> "had won a First Medal at the Salon"

A very brief gloss would help: "a First Medal (the Salon's top prize)."

**Ch. bonheur — "Anna Klumpke" introduced as "the American painter" with no further ID:**
> "Bonheur's companion in her final years was the American painter **Anna Klumpke**, whom she made her sole heir"

No gloss beyond "American painter" is needed here; the role in the story is clear. Fine.

**Overall verdict:** NEEDS-GLOSS (minor). The main MUST-FIX is the Père-Lachaise reference (explain what it is) and the "First Medal" ranking. All other issues are low-priority polish.
