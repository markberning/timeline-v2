### SHOULD-CONSIDER (non-blocking, would improve the read)

**SC-1 — The gap between Appleton's commission and Millet selling it is not precisely dated.**
Ch3 describes the slow climb in reputation "across the 1860s" but doesn't say when Millet actually sold the reworked painting after Appleton declined it, or who the first buyer in the ownership chain was. The provenance node says "a chain of private collectors" through the 1860s–1880s. The narrative is honest about not knowing the intermediate owners but a reader might want to know: how long did Millet actually hold the canvas? The fact pack leaves this undated too, so this is not a draft failure — but if the author has access to a more precise date for Millet's first sale after the rework, a single sentence grounding the timeline would strengthen Ch3.

**SC-2 — The Chauchard-as-department-store-magnate gloss is present but the building irony could be sharper.**
The narrative correctly explains that Chauchard co-founded the "Grands Magasins du Louvre" (a department store, no relation to the museum). What it does not quite land is the deeper irony: the painting France couldn't find public money to save from America was eventually bought back by a private merchant at a higher price and *given* to the nation — the exact outcome the government's failure at auction had looked certain to prevent. The narrative touches this ("given, free, to the nation that had failed to buy it across the table twenty-one years before") but could make the irony even more explicit. Not blocking; the passage is already strong.

**SC-3 — No mention of the droit de suite consequence landing anywhere in the artist's family.**
The draft correctly notes the droit de suite connection as "a lasting footnote." For a zero-knowledge reader this would land harder if given one concrete note: Millet had died in 1875, fourteen years before the bidding war, so his widow and children saw none of the record prices. That is the specific human cost that made the legal argument stick. Source: general; Millet died 1875, well before the 1889–90 boom.

**Verdict: PASS.** The best-structured and most comprehensive of the four batch-B drafts. All four brief-mandated essentials are fully covered. No blocking gaps.

### MUST-ADD (essential, blocking)

**MA-1 — The print's dimensions and landscape orientation are not stated in the narrative.**
The content object has `dimensions: '8.5 in × 12 in'` (landscape). The narratives never mention the physical size. For a work where the format matters — a cheap, hand-holdable, landscape sheet, not a large canvas — the dimensions are a comprehensiveness essential. The looking chapter (Ch2) calls it "a black-and-white printed sheet about eight and a half inches tall and a foot wide," which is correct, but it gets the orientation backwards: the print is **landscape** (wider than tall; 8.5 in tall × 12 in wide is consistent with landscape; "eight and a half inches tall and a foot wide" actually describes a portrait if taken literally as height × width). Check the phrasing: the sheet should be described as approximately 8.5 inches tall and 12 inches wide, but make clear it is a horizontal/landscape orientation (the giant sits at the left, the crowd at the right; the composition reads left to right across the wide dimension). This is a verifiable physical fact: the print is landscape format. Source: Yale catalogue (9¼ × 12¹⁄₁₆ in, stone dimensions), confirmed by the composition and the fact pack's heroAspect of 1.44 (wider than tall).

**Action needed:** Revise the one sentence in Ch2 that describes the dimensions to make the landscape orientation explicit: e.g., "a black-and-white printed sheet about eight and a half inches tall and **twelve inches wide**" (not "a foot wide" — 12 inches is correct but "a foot wide" makes it sound square or portrait at the sizes named). Verify the exact numbers against the Yale or BnF catalogue entry before writing into the final draft.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER (non-blocking)

**SC-1 — Philipon's own conviction and fine at his November 1831 trial.**
The narrative mentions Philipon's pear-gag trial but does not note that Philipon was himself convicted and fined 2,000 francs. The fact pack has this (fact ledger #8). A single sentence noting that Philipon paid personally for the pear would sharpen the portrait of the two-front war — the publisher and the artist both paying for the campaign, at roughly the same time. Not blocking; the narrative's portrait of Philipon is already vivid.

**SC-2 — Le Charivari is not mentioned in the narrative (only La Caricature).**
The fact pack notes Philipon also founded Le Charivari (1832), and that Daumier's main long-term home was Le Charivari (where the *Les Chemins de Fer* railway lithograph series ran). The Carriage chapter mentions both papers, but the Gargantua chapter refers only to La Caricature. A brief note in Ch5 (the long career section) that Daumier moved his output to Le Charivari after the September Laws curtailed political caricature would give the reader the full arc. Factual source: Wikipedia (Philipon / Le Charivari); fact pack.

**SC-3 — The title "Gargantua" is used but Rabelais's novel title is never given.**
The narrative says "François Rabelais's sixteenth-century comic satire" (no title) and "Rabelais's 1534 giant." The novel is titled *Gargantua* (1534); it is part of the *Gargantua and Pantagruel* cycle. A reader looking it up benefits from having the full title. Not blocking, but the first mention of the source work should include its title. Verifiable: Wikipedia (*Gargantua and Pantagruel*).

**Verdict: CONDITIONAL PASS.** One MUST-ADD: clarify the landscape orientation of the print in Ch2 (the current phrasing ambiguously implies portrait). Small fix; everything else is covered.

### MUST-ADD (essential, blocking)

**MA-1 — The self-portrait-among-the-handlers tradition is not mentioned anywhere in the narrative.**
The fact pack explicitly flags this (ledger item #118) as a "popular suggestion" and "legend" — the National Gallery London plays a "spot the self-portrait" game, and Wikipedia notes it as suggested but not definitively identified. This is one of the most widely repeated facts about *The Horse Fair* in public art education: the idea that Bonheur placed herself among the handlers, disguised in her own working clothes. Whether or not it is verifiable, it is a famously-told anecdote about this specific painting, and a five-chapter deep read of the work should acknowledge and frame it correctly. The fact pack already provides the correct framing ("popular suggestion / suggested, never definitively identified") and the source (National Gallery; Wikipedia). A zero-knowledge reader who has heard of this painting will quite likely have encountered this claim. Not including it at all is a comprehensiveness gap.

**Action needed:** Add a brief passage — a short paragraph in Ch4 (the Bonheur biography chapter) or in Ch2 (the looking chapter, as a "look for it yourself" invitation) — noting that one of the riders or handlers is often identified as a self-portrait of Bonheur in her working clothes, while acknowledging that no figure has been definitively confirmed as hers. Frame it as an engaging tradition/legend, not a fact. Verifiable that the claim exists and is common: National Gallery London page for NG621; Wikipedia, *The Horse Fair*.

### SHOULD-CONSIDER (non-blocking)

**SC-1 — The specific Salon date is given as "1853" throughout but "Salon of 1853" is correct (it ran May–August 1853).**
The draft is accurate. This is a note of confirmation, not a gap.

**SC-2 — A.T. Stewart's identity could use one more word of context.**
The provenance identifies him as "the New York department-store magnate." What the narrative doesn't say is that Stewart was then one of the richest men in the United States — the richest of his time by some accounts — and his collection of European art was famous. A single clause establishing him as the most prominent private collector in America at the time would make the transatlantic journey of the painting feel more like the cultural event it was. The fact pack identifies him as "one of the wealthiest men in America" — the narrative already has "one of the richest men in America." This is covered. On reflection, not a gap.

**SC-3 — The lineage parents (Stubbs, Géricault, Parthenon frieze) are in the chips but never in the narrative.**
None of these antecedents — Géricault's charging horses, Stubbs's anatomical precision, the Elgin Marbles frieze — appear in any of the five chapters. For a painting so overtly in dialogue with the tradition of horse painting, a sentence in Ch2 or Ch4 naming Bonheur's predecessors would strengthen the art-historical placement. Particularly Géricault (whose 1821 Derby paintings and horse studies are the immediate French precursor to Bonheur's scale and energy) is a natural comparison. Not blocking, but the absence of any predecessor in the prose, while the lineage chips list three, is a small gap between the chips and the narrative.

**SC-4 — The second A.T. Stewart sale detail: it was his widow's estate, not his own.**
The narrative says "the painting fetched $53,000 — and the buyer was Cornelius Vanderbilt II" and the provenance entry says "bought at the estate auction of Stewart's widow." The narrative chapter prose says Vanderbilt bought it "at the estate auction of Stewart's widow" (correct, matching the fact pack). COVERED — this checked out on re-read. No gap.

**Verdict: CONDITIONAL PASS.** One MUST-ADD: include the self-portrait-among-the-handlers tradition in the narrative (with correct legend framing). Everything else — including all four brief-mandated essentials (Bonheur's trousers permit, the painting's scale, Gambart's British tour and engraving, and the Vanderbilt gift to the Met) — is fully and accurately covered.
