# Clarity Gate — Batch A (Realism works)
Critic: NEWCOMER / CLARITY  
Date: 2026-05-25  
Reader model: sharp 15-year-old, zero museum background, first time through.

Grades: **CLEAR** (reads without a stumble) · **NEEDS-GLOSS** (one or more terms/people need a one-liner, but the chapter stays followable) · **LOST** (a concept or leap leaves the reader genuinely unable to follow)

---

## 1. The Stone Breakers (Courbet, 1849)

### Ch 1 — Two men on a road
**Grade: CLEAR**

No comprehension blockers. "Hierarchy of genres" and "history painting" are introduced with inline plain-language glosses before any argument rests on them. "Realism" is named and one-lined. "Francis Wey" is called "the historian" — thin but not blocking.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"the historian Francis Wey"` — reader doesn't know why a random historian receives a painter's letters; one more word ("his friend the historian") is already there, which is fine, but swapping "historian" for "writer and friend" removes a faint speed-bump.

---

### Ch 2 — A boy, an old man, and a road
**Grade: CLEAR**

Very strong visual walk-through. "Sabots" is glossed inline ("the heavy clogs French peasants wore") — model behavior.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"the sound of an absence"` (para 1) — mildly purple for a 15-year-old; not a blocker, just a phrase they might re-read.

---

### Ch 3 — The painting did not arrive alone
**Grade: CLEAR**

"The Salon" is introduced with a tight inline gloss: "the official annual State exhibition, the single show in France where an artistic career was made or buried." First-use gloss is present. "The academy" is mentioned parenthetically ("the academy ran it") without a standalone gloss, but the reader already has enough from "official State exhibition" to follow.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"the academy ran it"` — "the academy" appears without a prior standalone definition; it only appears in the parenthetical after the Salon gloss. A reader leaning on this chapter alone (the overview may not be read first) benefits from "the academy (the State institution that controlled who got to exhibit)." Not blocking since the Salon is well defined.
- `"1848 — the revolution that toppled the king"` — single appositive, borderline thin; the Realism overview covers it, but a reader not reading in order gets only one clause. Minimal blocker.

---

### Ch 4 — "The first socialist painting"
**Grade: CLEAR**

Proudhon is introduced with a full who-he-is sentence ("France's most famous radical thinker … 'property is theft' … first writer to call himself an anarchist"). "Anarchist" is immediately glossed in the next clause. "The proletariat" is glossed inline ("the class of people who own nothing and live by selling their labor"). All clean.

No must-fix or should-fix items.

---

### Ch 5 — The painting that ended up in Germany
**Grade: CLEAR**

"Gemäldegalerie in Dresden" is described as "the great picture gallery of that city, one of the major museums of Europe." "Königstein Fortress" is named without a gloss, but since the function (moving treasures out of the city) is immediately explained, the reader can follow. Richard Raskin is identified as "one scholar" with a date — appropriate caution.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"Oskar Reinhart Collection in Winterthur, Switzerland"` — named with no context. A half-clause ("a private museum in Winterthur, Switzerland") would smooth this for a zero-knowledge reader.

---

**STONE-BREAKERS VERDICT:** All five chapters CLEAR. Lightest work in the batch. Inline glosses are present at every jargon point; named sources get their credentials. The SHOULD-FIX items are polish, not blockers. No MUST-FIX.

## 2. The Painter's Studio (Courbet, 1854–55)

### Ch 1 — The biggest show on Earth says no
**Grade: CLEAR**

"Exposition Universelle" is immediately glossed: "a world's fair, the kind of vast international exhibition where every nation hauls in its machines and its manufactures and its art to show off in one place." "Realism" and Courbet are introduced with a one-liner. "Alfred Bruyas" is introduced as "his patron … a wealthy collector from the south of France who bankrolled him" — exactly right. "Pavilion of Realism" named and described.

SHOULD-FIX
- The title's `"seven years of my artistic and moral life"` is quoted but the significance of "seven years" is deferred to Ch 2. A tiny forward-pointer ("we'll come back to those seven years") would remove the faint confusion here, though the chapter itself answers it quickly enough.

---

### Ch 2 — Seven years, summed up
**Grade: CLEAR**

"Champfleury" introduced as "Jules Husson, 1821–1889, the critic credited with first using the word 'Realism' for the new art." Perfect.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"the Loue (pronounced 'loo'), the river of his home country near the town of Ornans"` — good pronunciation guide, but "his home country" is a mildly ambiguous phrase (sounds like it might mean France). "The river that ran through his hometown of Ornans" is clearer.

---

### Ch 3 — Walking across the room
**Grade: NEEDS-GLOSS**

One must-fix:

MUST-FIX
- `"the academic nude, the idealized unclothed figure that the official art schools drilled their students to draw, over and over, as the foundation of all serious art"` — this is introduced in Ch 2, not Ch 3. In Ch 3 ("The Looking") the nude is referenced again without the gloss, relying on the reader having retained it from Ch 2. Since Ch 3 repeats the visual walk-through, and a reader jumping straight here would hit "the academic nude" unglossed, a brief parenthetical recap ("the unclothed model academic training used as the foundation of serious art") would make the chapter self-standing.
- `"the Burial one chapter back"` — this is a cross-reference to a different work in the chain ("A Burial at Ornans"), not a chapter of this work. The text says "you have met this animal's cousin" and "the small indifferent dog Courbet dropped into a solemn funeral." A reader who has not read the Burial chapter gets the dog reference but will briefly wonder what funeral and what app chapter this points to. A tiny clarification: "the Burial at Ornans (the Courbet work read just before this one in this app)" — not blocking, but momentarily confusing.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"he called them 'the shareholders'"` — the Courbet-in-a-letter context is clear, but the paragraph just before doesn't tell the reader which letter (the Champfleury letter introduced in Ch 2). Minor.
- `"a fashionably dressed couple of art collectors"` — in Ch 4 these same people are described as "a woman of the world and her husband." The phrasing shift between chapters is fine but slightly inconsistent; aligning helps.

---

### Ch 4 — Putting names to the faces
**Grade: NEEDS-GLOSS**

Two must-fix items:

MUST-FIX
- `"Promayet — Alphonse Promayet (1822–1872), a violinist and a childhood friend"` — clean. But `"Urbain Cuenot"` and `"Max Buchon (1818–1869), a poet and a friend"` are listed in quick succession with minimal IDs. Buchon gets "a poet and a friend"; Cuenot gets nothing beyond "another old friend from home." In a chapter that explicitly says "let's be introduced," leaving Cuenot without even a noun ("old friend from Ornans" is fine) creates a small gap.
- `"Proudhon … worked his likeness up from a photograph, which in 1855 was still a young and slightly disreputable technology"` — the "disreputable" framing is interesting but the reader needs a half-clause of why photography was disreputable (e.g., "considered too mechanical to count as a 'real' artistic source"). Without it, "slightly disreputable" hangs unexplained.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"Les Fleurs du Mal ('The Flowers of Evil') … prosecuted for it"` — Baudelaire is introduced very well (name, dates, title translation, prosecution). The only thing missing is what he was prosecuted for (obscenity/immorality), which would complete the picture for a reader who doesn't know. Adds one clause; not blocking since "prosecuted" is itself explained as a fact.
- `"the poacher was reworked and added relatively late, not part of the original plan"` — "X-rays that let conservators see the layers underneath" is a nice plain-language explanation of technical study. Good.

---

### Ch 5 — A masterpiece nobody could house
**Grade: NEEDS-GLOSS**

One must-fix:

MUST-FIX
- `"the Paris Commune of 1871 (the brief, violently suppressed revolutionary government that briefly ruled Paris, which Courbet publicly backed)"` — the gloss is present but uses "briefly" twice in the same clause ("brief … that briefly ruled Paris"). Grammatical slip that a young reader will notice, not a comprehension failure, but the double word undermines the gloss's clarity. Tighten to: "the brief revolutionary government that seized and ruled Paris for ten weeks in 1871 before the French army violently suppressed it."
- `"held responsible for the toppling of a Paris monument"` — which monument? The Vendôme Column is actually named in neither the sentence nor a gloss. For a reader with no background, "a Paris monument" is so vague it obscures the story. One clause: "held responsible for the tearing-down of the Vendôme Column, a towering bronze pillar Napoleon had built to celebrate his army." Not blocking (the narrative still makes sense) but the vagueness is conspicuous.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"the Louvre (then France's great national museum)"` — the parenthetical is fine but "then" implies it is no longer the national museum. Better: "the Louvre, France's great national museum, which would later send its 19th-century collection across the river to the Musée d'Orsay (explained below)."
- `"the Society of the Friends of the Louvre"` — named but not explained. One clause: "a membership organization that raises funds to help the Louvre acquire art it otherwise couldn't afford."

---

**STUDIO VERDICT:** Chapters 1–2 CLEAR; Chapters 3–5 NEEDS-GLOSS. The must-fix items are all small targeted additions (a half-clause each), not structural rewrites. The "Burial one chapter back" cross-reference and the unglossed Vendôme Column are the two items most likely to actually lose a young reader.

## 3. The Sower (Millet, 1850)

### Ch 1 — The painter who was an actual peasant
**Grade: CLEAR**

All key terms arrive with inline glosses. "Paul Delaroche" introduced as "a smooth and successful academic painter of grand historical scenes." "Hierarchy of genres," "history painting," "the academy," and "the Salon" all get brief inline glosses — exactly the pattern this gate requires. "Cholera" is glossed as "a fast, deadly waterborne disease that swept 19th-century cities in terrifying waves." "Barbizon School" is introduced with a sentence of explanation. Very thorough.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"portrait of Cherbourg"` — the text says "study art, first in the nearby port of Cherbourg" — clear and fine.
- `"Forest of Fontainebleau (fon-ten-BLOH), the great royal woodland southeast of Paris"` — "royal woodland" might puzzle a reader (it just means historically owned by the crown); "great royal hunting forest southeast of Paris" adds a concrete image. Minor.

---

### Ch 2 — One figure, almost life-size
**Grade: CLEAR**

The visual walk-through is excellent — the diagonal, the sowing arm, the shadowed face, the straw-bound legs, the tiny ploughman as scale trick. Everything follows without prior knowledge.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"Stand Courbet's twenty-two-foot funeral in your mind from Chapter 1 of the Realism overview"` — asks the reader to remember a specific measurement from a different article. A reader reading just the Sower work-read gets a confusing "Chapter 1 of the Realism overview" reference. This is the same cross-level problem flagged elsewhere: the internal cross-references assume a reading order. Fine if the app enforces that order; if not, a simple parenthetical ("the enormous Courbet funeral you may have just read, one level up") would help.
- `"(Painting the process and not just the result is exactly the discipline this app's writing tries to bring to history …)"` — this meta-parenthetical is charming but will confuse a reader who has come to learn about Millet and suddenly finds the app reflecting on its own writing style. Consider removing or moving to a caption.

---

### Ch 3 — Sending the peasant to the capital
**Grade: CLEAR**

Good summary of the Salon of 1850–51 as the convergence point for Millet and Courbet. "The two canvases" (Burial + Stone Breakers) referenced by name with short tags.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"in a way that was not abstract"` — the sentence "the propertied classes were nervous about the poor in a way that was not abstract" is slightly imprecise; a young reader might not catch the distinction. "Not abstract" here means "based on recent lived experience, not theoretical worry." A small clarification helps: "in a way that was recent and real, not theoretical."
- `"The Boston museum that owns the painting today puts it plainly: viewers were shocked …"` — citing the museum in-line as a voice is fine, but the comma-splice into the quotation ("puts it plainly: viewers were …") reads slightly awkwardly; worth a light tightening.

---

### Ch 4 — Peasant as monument
**Grade: CLEAR**

The Parable of the Sower is introduced and unpacked clearly. "The Angelus" gets a forward pointer with a one-line description ("two peasants standing in a field at evening, heads bowed, pausing in their work to pray at the sound of the church bell"). Clean.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"as you sow, so shall you reap"` — this is a common English saying and needs no gloss, but attributing it as a "Parable" is slightly misleading; it is a proverb, not a named parable. The Parable of the Sower (Luke 8) is correctly described. Worth distinguishing: "the old proverb 'as you sow, so shall you reap'" rather than grouping it with the parable.

---

### Ch 5 — Bought off the wall, shipped to Boston
**Grade: CLEAR**

William Morris Hunt introduced with a full who-he-is. Chain of custody (Hunt → Shaw → MFA) clear. The "versions question" section is admirably honest and well-handled. The Van Gogh section introduces him with dates and a one-liner ("a struggling Dutch painter").

SHOULD-FIX
- `"the Kröller-Müller Museum in the Netherlands"` — named without any context. One clause: "the Kröller-Müller Museum in the Netherlands (a major modern-art museum that holds the world's second-largest Van Gogh collection)." Adds useful anchor.
- `"Arles sowers of 1888"` — Arles is named without noting it is a city in southern France where Van Gogh famously worked. Very minor.

---

**SOWER VERDICT:** All five chapters CLEAR — the cleanest Millet work in the batch. The inline glosses are consistent throughout, the visual chapters are strong, and the named persons all get one-liners. The SHOULD-FIX items are polish. No MUST-FIX.

## 4. The Gleaners (Millet, 1857)

### Ch 1 — The poorest, allowed into the field
**Grade: CLEAR**

Outstanding opening. "Gleaning" is defined before the painting is even described — exactly right for a word a zero-knowledge reader will not know. The Book of Ruth is named and its story given in a sentence. "Enclosure" is introduced as "turning shared land into private property" — clear.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"Leviticus and again in Deuteronomy"` — for a reader with no Bible literacy these are just word-sounds. A half-clause: "the Hebrew Bible (the books of Leviticus and Deuteronomy)" would anchor them. Not blocking since the instruction itself is already paraphrased in plain language.
- `"Millet is one half of Realism … the loud, public, city-facing half … was Gustave Courbet"` — Courbet is introduced here without dates; other chapters consistently give dates on first mention of a person. Minor inconsistency.

---

### Ch 2 — Three women, bent over a stripped field
**Grade: CLEAR**

Excellent spatial walkthrough. The primary-color caps section ("the most saturated, most expensive color … reserved for the robes of kings, cardinals, the Virgin Mary") is a strong piece of clarity writing — it explains why the color choice matters without requiring prior knowledge.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"sheaves"` — the word appears without a gloss when listing what is in the background harvest ("tall round stacks of grain, a loaded cart, long rows of bundled sheaves"). A sheaf is a bound bundle of harvested grain stalks. One word: "bundled grain sheaves" or a brief gloss. Low priority since "bundled" is right next to it and a reader can infer.
- `"overseer"` — described as "the farm's overseer — the mounted steward who watches the work get done." The gloss is present and adequate.

---

### Ch 3 — Three poor women walk into the Salon
**Grade: NEEDS-GLOSS**

One must-fix:

MUST-FIX
- `"the Three Fates"` — the critic's famous insult ("his three gleaners have gigantic pretensions, they pose as the Three Fates of Poverty") is quoted and then explained: "three goddesses who between them spin, measure, and cut the thread of every human life — the implacable powers of destiny." The gloss is present, but it arrives **after** the quoted phrase, so a reader hits "Three Fates" cold and must read through to the next paragraph to learn what it means. Move the gloss so it precedes or wraps the first use: "the Three Fates — in Greek and Roman myth, the three goddesses who between them spin, measure, and cut the thread of every human life." Then the insult lands with its full weight on first read rather than requiring a reread. Minor structural fix, not a content gap.

- `"the shadow of 1793"` — the section header uses 1793 as a shorthand. The paragraph within the section does explain it ("the bloodiest year of the French Revolution, the year of the guillotine and the Terror, when the poor of France had cut off the heads of their betters"). The explanation is good. But a reader who has not read the Realism overview gets a section header that means nothing until several sentences later. A tiny bridging opener ("In 1857 the memory of the French Revolution — specifically its bloodiest year, 1793") would remove the landing-before-the-explanation problem.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"the bourgeoisie"` — first use in Ch 3 ("openly hostile … from the middle and upper classes"). "Bourgeoisie" is not explicitly used in this chapter; "middle and upper classes" and "propertied classes" do the work. Clean.
- `"enclosure"` — already glossed in Ch 1; no issue in Ch 3 (the concept is not invoked there).

---

### Ch 4 — What Millet meant
**Grade: NEEDS-GLOSS**

One must-fix, one should-fix:

MUST-FIX
- `"[VERIFY: …]` tags appear in published prose positions` — the draft contains three bracketed VERIFY notes left in the text:
  - `[VERIFY: Millet's politics are genuinely disputed …]`
  - `[VERIFY: this reflects the commonly reported stance …]`
  - `[VERIFY: the "no biblical consolation in this canvas" reading is one strong art-historical line …]`
  
  These are author pipeline notes, not reader-facing prose, and they will break the reading experience completely if they ship. They must be resolved (the claims verified and the brackets removed, or the assertions softened to the level of honest uncertainty in running text) before this chapter clears the gate. This is the single most urgent fix in the entire batch.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"dignity without sentimentality"` — used as a summary phrase; a 15-year-old will likely not parse "sentimentality" with precision. One clause: "dignity without the cloying sweetness of sentimental art (where the poor are made pretty so you feel a comfortable pang and nothing more)." Or, since the previous paragraph already unpacks this, the phrase can stand if it is the closing echo of text the reader just absorbed — judge in context.

---

### Ch 5 — The price reverses
**Grade: CLEAR**

The reversal from 3,000 to 300,000 francs is a great narrative hook. "Ferdinand Bischoffsheim" named as "a Paris banker" — adequate. "Jeanne-Alexandrine Louise Pommery" given her full name and identified as "head of the great Pommery champagne house, one of the most successful businesswomen in France." The accuracy note about the provenance timeline ("not through any single tidy 'gift' a year before") is well-handled for clarity.

SHOULD-FIX
- `"the Gare d'Orsay, a grand old Paris railway station that had been saved from demolition and rebuilt as a gallery"` — this is a brief gloss on the Orsay; it could add that the building's own grandeur makes it a fitting home for monumental painting. But that is commentary, not a comprehension fix. Current text is clear.
- `"Vincent van Gogh worshipped Millet, copied his peasant subjects … Millet's name runs all through Van Gogh's letters"` — fine; Van Gogh was introduced with full context in the Sower afterlife. For a reader who only reads The Gleaners, he gets "the young Vincent van Gogh" with dates (1853–1890) missing from this chapter. Cross-chapter dependency: add dates at first mention here.

---

**GLEANERS VERDICT:** NEEDS-GLOSS overall. The VERIFY tags in Ch 4 are the urgent fix — they are not a polish note, they are draft-pipeline debris that will print as raw text if the file ships. Ch 3's Three Fates gloss arrives one paragraph too late. Everything else is polish. The writing itself is some of the strongest in the batch.

## Cross-cutting patterns (all four works)

1. **Cross-level references assume a fixed reading order.** Every work contains at least one "the Realism overview (one level up) tells this in full" pointer. If a reader opens a work page directly, those pointers dangle. The pointers themselves are not blockers — the material they defer is not needed to follow the chapter — but a zero-knowledge reader benefits from a fallback clause: "if you haven't read the overview, here is the short version: [2 sentences]." This is a SHOULD-FIX pattern across all four works, not a blocker in any single one.

2. **"The academy"** appears in several chapters with thin-to-no inline gloss. Stone Breakers Ch 3 is the only place it appears without the Salon gloss already doing the heavy lifting. The other works (Sower Ch 1, Gleaners Ch 3) do inline-define it. Consistency would be improved by a brief gloss in SbSalon: "the academy (the State institution that controlled artistic training and the Salon)."

3. **Named persons get good credentials in all four works** — the consistent pattern of (name, dates, who-they-are) is largely followed. Exceptions are flagged above individually. No work leaves a name entirely in the air.

4. **"Barbizon School"** is glossed in Sower Ch 1 (thoroughly) and in Gleaners Ch 1 (adequately). No issue. A reader coming to the Gleaners first still gets a usable gloss.

5. **The VERIFY tags in Gleaners Ch 4 are pipeline debris** — they are the only item in the batch that is structurally a bug rather than a writing gap. Everything else is a smooth-the-gloss note.
