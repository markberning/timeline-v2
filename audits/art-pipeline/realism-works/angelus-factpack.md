## The work — hard facts
- **Title:** *The Angelus* (Fr. *L'Angélus*). Originally titled **"Prayer for the Potato Crop"** (*Prière pour la récolte de pommes de terre*), renamed by Millet after the commission fell through; he also added the church tower on the horizon to increase the painting's marketability. [VERIFY — artinsociety, smarthistory]
- **Artist:** Jean-François Millet (1814–1875). artistId `millet`.
- **Dates:** painted **1857–1859**. [VERIFY — Orsay, Wikipedia]
- **Medium:** oil on canvas.
- **Dimensions:** 55.5 cm × 66 cm (H × W). → **1 ft 9⅞ in × 2 ft 2 in** (≈ 1 ft 10 in tall × 2 ft 2 in wide). Small — about the size of a serving tray / large place setting. [VERIFY — Orsay, Wikipedia. Conversion: 55.5/2.54=21.85in=1ft9.85in; 66/2.54=25.98in=2ft2in]

- **Current location:** **Musée d'Orsay, Paris.** (Transferred from the Louvre to the Orsay in 1986, with the rest of the Louvre's 19th-c. collection.) [VERIFY — Wikipedia, Orsay]
- **Acquisition:** **Alfred Chauchard bequest.** Chauchard left his collection to the State in 1906 / on his death (5 June 1909); formally accepted into the Louvre's permanent collection **15 January 1910.** Use "Chauchard bequest, 1910" for the `acquired` field. ## What is literally on the canvas (verified by LOOKING at angelus.jpg)
- **A man on the LEFT** — standing, **hat off / held in both hands at his waist**, head bowed; brown jacket, blue trousers, working boots.
- **A woman on the RIGHT** — standing, **hands clasped/joined at her chest** in prayer, head bowed; cap/bonnet, dark dress with apron, a reddish bodice/sleeves visible.
- Between them on the ground, near their feet: **a basket of potatoes** (a low woven basket).
- At the man's left foot, stuck upright in the turned earth: **a two-pronged potato fork / digging fork.**
- To the right of the woman: **a wheelbarrow with a sack** (sacks of potatoes loaded on it).
- Far horizon, **right of center / behind the woman:** a **tiny church spire/tower** — the source of the bell they hear. Very small, on the flat skyline.
- The setting is a **dug/turned potato field at dusk**, flat to a low horizon.
- An **enormous, low, pale dusk sky** fills roughly the top two-thirds of the canvas, pressing down on the two small figures. A faint warm flush (sunset) at upper left; a few birds high up.
- Overall tonality: warm browns, dusk golds, muted — figures read nearly as silhouettes.

## The Angelus prayer (must inline-define)
- The **Angelus** is a Catholic devotional prayer commemorating the Incarnation (the Annunciation — the angel Gabriel announcing to Mary that she would bear Christ). Traditionally recited **three times a day — morning (~6am), noon, and evening (~6pm)** — at the ringing of the **Angelus bell** from the parish church. Named for its opening Latin line, *"Angelus Domini nuntiavit Mariæ"* ("The angel of the Lord declared unto Mary"). The painting shows the **evening** Angelus: the two have stopped work at the sound of the distant bell to pray. [VERIFY — standard Catholic devotion; Orsay description; Wikipedia]

## Millet's stated intent — the grandmother recollection (DOCUMENTED quote)
- Millet's own account of the painting's origin: **"The idea for The Angelus came to me because I remembered that my grandmother, hearing the church bell ringing while we were working in the fields, always made us stop work to say the Angelus prayer for the poor departed, very religiously and with cap in hand."** Treat as a genuine, frequently-cited statement attributed to Millet (a recollection, not a legend invented later). [VERIFY — widely cited; Wikipedia, Orsay. Frame as "Millet's own account / as Millet remembered it."]
- Key nuance for the MEANING chapter: Millet framed it as **memory and rural piety**, NOT as a sentimental religious greeting card. The picture's intent was the remembered habit of the prayer for the dead; the gushing pious / sentimental reading came LATER from its mass-reproduction afterlife.

## The commission (DOCUMENTED, with one declined-detail)
- Commissioned by **Thomas Gold Appleton** (a wealthy Boston art collector, of Irish descent), possibly with the recent **Irish potato famine** in mind — which fits the original "Prayer for the Potato Crop" title. **Appleton never collected it** (the deal fell through); Millet then reworked it (added the church tower), retitled it, and it entered the market. [VERIFY — artinsociety, Wikipedia. The "Irish famine inspiration" is *possible/suggested*, frame as "possibly" / "it may be."]

## Provenance + the 1889 bidding war (get the SEQUENCE right)
- After Millet, the painting passed through several owners and rose steeply in value during the Millet-revival boom of the 1870s–80s.
- It came into the collection of **Eugène Secrétan** (a French copper magnate). His collection was sold at auction **1 July 1889, at the Galerie Sedelmeyer, Paris**, timed to coincide with the **Exposition Universelle of 1889** (the Eiffel Tower world's fair) which had brought wealthy Americans to Paris. [VERIFY — Wikipedia, artinsociety]
- The sale became a **bidding war** between **Antonin Proust** (a former Minister/Director of Fine Arts, leading a French effort to keep it for the **Louvre**) and the **American Art Association** of New York (its agent **James F. Sutton**). Bidding reached **553,000 francs.** [VERIFY — Wikipedia, multiple]
- Proust's side nominally won at 553,000 francs **for France** — BUT the **French government refused to ratify / fund the purchase.** Proust personally could not cover it. So he turned around and sold it to **Sutton / the American Art Association** (for ~552,000–553,000 francs), and the painting **went to the United States.** [VERIFY — artinsociety, mutualart; the "France won then couldn't pay → it went to America" sequence is the documented one]
- The AAA **exhibited / toured it in America (1889–1890)** — it was a sensation, crowds paid to see it. [VERIFY — AAA held it 1889–1890; the US-tour-with-paid-crowds detail: napoleon.org/general accounts. Frame the touring detail modestly if unsure of cities — DO NOT name specific cities.]
- In **1890** the AAA **sold it back to France** — to the Paris department-store magnate **Alfred Chauchard** (co-founder of the **Grands Magasins du Louvre** department store), for **750,000 francs** (Wikipedia/Orsay) — *some French sources say 800,000 francs* (fr.wikipedia, Britannica-adjacent). Use **"about 750,000–800,000 francs"** or "750,000 francs (some accounts say 800,000)" to be safe. [VERIFY — figure variously reported; both cited]
- Chauchard **bequeathed his collection to the State** (will of 1906 / on his death 1909); **accepted into the Louvre 15 January 1910;** to the **Musée d'Orsay 1986.** [VERIFY]
- A lasting consequence: the gulf between these record prices and the poverty of Millet's surviving family helped drive the later French **droit de suite** ("right to follow" / artist's resale right) law. [VERIFY — Wikipedia. Optional color.]

## Salvador Dalí + the contested X-ray (FRAME CAREFULLY — Dalí's reading + a contested technical finding, NOT settled fact)
- **Salvador Dalí** (1904–1989, the Spanish Surrealist) was **obsessed** with *The Angelus.* As a child he saw a reproduction of it on the wall of his school and found it strangely menacing. He read the woman as poised like a **praying mantis** (the insect that devours its mate) and claimed the painting masked anguish, sexual repression, and death. [VERIFY — Wikipedia, ART in Society]
- Dalí's central claim: that the **basket of potatoes at the figures' feet was originally a small child's coffin** — that the couple are not pausing over a potato harvest but mourning a buried/stillborn child. He wrote a whole "paranoiac-critical" theory around it (*The Tragic Myth of Millet's Angelus*) and made works including *The Architectonic Angelus of Millet* (1933) and *Gala and the Angelus of Millet…* (1933). [VERIFY — Wikipedia, dalipaintings]
- **The contested technical finding:** at Dalí's prompting (the story goes), the **Louvre examined the painting with X-ray / infrared**, which reportedly revealed an **overpainted "oblong geometrical shape" / a more box-like form** beneath or in place of the present basket. [VERIFY — Wikipedia: "an 'oblong geometrical shape' at the feet of the figures"; artinsociety: "the basket… was originally a more box-like object."]
- **HARD FRAMING RULE:** Dalí read the box-shape as **vindication** (a coffin). The painting/art-history establishment did **NOT** widely accept the coffin interpretation. The honest statement: an underdrawing/earlier form that looks more box-like *may* exist; what it *means* (a coffin? an earlier basket shape? a compositional adjustment?) is **unsettled / contested.** Present it as **Dalí's reading + a disputed technical claim**, never as proof. Flag the coffin conclusion **[LEGEND]** / contested.

## Reproduction / afterlife (verified)
- Became **"one of the most widely reproduced images of the 19th century"** / "one of the most widely reproduced religious paintings of the 19th century." Prints hung in tens of thousands of French homes, schools, and churches; reached the middle class via the Industrial Revolution's cheaper printing; ended up on postcards, plates, coffee cups, devotional cards. [VERIFY — Wikipedia, Britannica]
- **Van Gogh** admired and copied Millet (incl. an *Angelus* after Millet, drawing, 1880); Millet was a lifelong hero of his. (Cross-reference the SOWER read, where Van Gogh's Millet-worship is told.) [VERIFY]
