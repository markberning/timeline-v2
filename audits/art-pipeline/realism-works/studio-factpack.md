## The subtitle — the "real allegory" oxymoron (verified)

- The subtitle calls the work a **"real allegory"** (*allégorie réelle*) — a deliberate
  contradiction in terms. An **allegory** is a picture where figures stand for ideas
  (Justice as a blindfolded woman with scales, etc.); "real" is the opposite instinct —
  paint the actual world as it is. Courbet welds the two: a picture that is both a literal
  studio scene AND a coded map of his world. Source: Wikipedia; the subtitle is the title.
- Courbet himself acknowledged the puzzle. In the letter to Champfleury he wrote: "It's the
  whole world coming to me to be painted… it is society at its best, its worst, and its
  average." Source: search summary of the Champfleury letter (widely reproduced). The exact
  "best/worst/average" phrasing is the standard translation and should be framed as a
  paraphrase-of-translation, not stamped as verbatim. [VERIFY exact words — translation]

## The 1855 refusal + the Pavilion (verified; DEFER the tent's full story)

- The **1855 Exposition Universelle** (Paris World's Fair) ran a grand official art show.
  Its jury **accepted eleven** Courbet submissions but **rejected** *The Painter's Studio*
  (too big / too strange) along with *A Burial at Ornans*. Source: Wikipedia.
- Courbet's response: with his patron **Alfred Bruyas** backing the cost, he built his own
  **Pavilion of Realism** (*Pavillon du Réalisme*), a temporary structure near the official
  exposition, charged admission, and showed ~40 of his own works including the Studio.
  Source: Wikipedia. **DEFER the tent's full story to the Realism overview** — here it is
  only the immediate aftermath of the refusal.

## Courbet's letter to Champfleury — the tripartite reading (verified, load-bearing)

Courbet explained the picture in a now-famous letter to the writer **Champfleury** (the
critic who named "Realism"). The picture splits into three:

- **CENTER:** Courbet himself, at his easel, painting a **landscape** (a view of his home
  country, the **Loue river valley** near Ornans), flanked by a **nude model** and a **small
  boy** watching; a **white cat** at his feet. Source: Wikipedia; the central self-portrait
  is undisputed. The model behind him + the boy + the cat are visible in the image and
  confirmed.
- **RIGHT — "the shareholders":** Courbet's own words (standard translation): on the right,
  **"all the shareholders, that is to say friends, fellow workers, art lovers."** Source:
  search summary of the letter (widely reproduced).
- **LEFT — "the other world":** Courbet's words (standard translation): **"the other world
  of trivial life, the people, misery, poverty, wealth, the exploited and the exploiters,
  the people who live off death."** Source: search summary of the letter.

**Framing note for the author:** these translations are the standard English renderings of
Courbet's French letter and are widely reproduced; quote them as "Courbet's own letter
describes…" / "in the standard translation," NOT as a single canonical verbatim English
quote. The *substance* (shareholders right / world of trivial life left) is solid.

### RIGHT-side figures named in Courbet's letter (verified — these are FROM the letter)
Per the letter, in order: **"Promayet with his violin"** (Alphonse Promayet, 1822–1872, a
violinist and Courbet's childhood friend from Ornans), then **"behind him are Bruyas,
Cuenot, Buchon, Proudhon"** — i.e.:
- **Alfred Bruyas** (1821–1877) — Courbet's chief patron/collector, who funded the Pavilion.
- **Urbain Cuenot** — an Ornans friend.
- **Max Buchon** (1818–1869) — poet, Courbet's friend.
- **Pierre-Joseph Proudhon** (1809–1865) — France's most famous radical/anarchist thinker,
  also an Ornans man; his portrait here was worked up from a **photograph**. Source: Wikipedia.
- **Champfleury** (Jules Husson, 1821–1889) — the critic, seated.
- **A "woman of the world and her husband, both luxuriously dressed"** — the elegant couple
  of art lovers in the foreground. Source: letter (search summary).
- **Charles Baudelaire** (1821–1867) — the poet, **at the far right, reading a large book**;
  his portrait copied from Courbet's 1847 portrait of him. Source: Wikipedia + letter.

### LEFT-side figures + objects (verified core; the rest [LEGEND] / contested)
**Verified / well-supported:**
- **The poacher** in the left foreground — a man in a wide hat with **hunting dogs** —
  **often read as Napoleon III** (the reigning emperor), identified by the hunting dogs and
  twirled moustache. **CONTESTED / [LEGEND]:** Napoleon III is NOT named in Courbet's letter,
  and X-rays show this figure was painted/altered later; the imperial reading is a widely-held
  *interpretation*, not Courbet's stated identification. Source: search summary; frame it as
  "often read as," "many scholars take him for," NOT as fact.
- **A Jewish man** and an **Irishwoman** (a beggar-woman) — Courbet said these types were
  drawn from his **1848 trip to London**. Source: Wikipedia.
- **A lay figure / mannequin** posed like a crucified martyr (read as the "death" of academic
  / Royal-Academy art). Source: Wikipedia. [Frame the symbolic reading as interpretation.]
- The left side gathers **assorted social types** — Courbet's letter calls them the world of
  trivial life: rich and poor, exploiters and exploited. Specific further IDs (a priest, a
  worker, a merchant, a republican veteran, a clown/mountebank, an undertaker, a gravedigger)
  appear across the literature but vary author to author → treat as **[LEGEND]/contested**;
  the author may say "scholars have read into this side a whole cross-section… a priest, a
  merchant, a worker, a beggar — though the exact roster is debated."
- **Objects on the left floor (visible in the image, verified by eye):** a **guitar**, a
  **dagger**, a **plumed hat** (a Romantic / cavalier hat). A **skull resting on a newspaper**
  is widely cited (the skull read as the death of the press / the academic order). Source:
  search summary; skull-on-newspaper is reported but I could NOT re-confirm the exact

## Why it is Courbet's testament (verified framing)

- The subtitle says it: a "summing up of **seven years**" (roughly 1848–1855 — from the
  revolution that birthed Realism to the fair that refused him). Courbet built his own life
  and circle into one canvas: his art (center), his allies (right), the world he paints (left).
- It is read as the **manifesto-painting / testament of Realism** — Courbet putting himself,
  literally, at the center of his world and declaring what (and whom) his art is for. Source:
  general scholarly consensus (Wikipedia, Smarthistory framing).
