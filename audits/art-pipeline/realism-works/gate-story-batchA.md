### MUST-FIX

**Ch 4 (StCast) — NEEDS WORK: narrative drive collapses into a list, and the key dramatic beat is buried.**

The chapter opens with a genuinely interesting angle: this is "an allegory built out of real people you could have met." But then it devolves into a roll-call of names and biographical capsules — Promayet, Bruyas, Cuenot, Buchon, Proudhon, Champfleury, the fashionable couple, then Baudelaire, then the poacher — and loses all forward motion. The reader is not being taken through the canvas; they are being walked through a guest list.

Two specific structural problems:

1. **The Baudelaire section is buried.** The fact that Courbet copied Baudelaire's face from a portrait he'd made eight years earlier *because the friendship had cooled* — and so the poet sits at the edge, present but apart — is the most *human* and story-rich detail in the chapter. It reads "a small, real sadness folded into the figure" and then moves on. That beat deserves a full paragraph of development: what did the cooling friendship mean, why does Courbet include him at all if they've drifted? The sad, marginalized Baudelaire is a much better dramatic engine than the Promayet/Cuenot/Buchon list. Suggested fix: collapse the friend-list into two sentences ("On the right, his support system: his violinist friend Promayet, his patron Bruyas, his poet friend Buchon, his radical philosopher Proudhon — a career drawn as a crowd") and give Baudelaire his own full beat.

2. **The poacher section is actually strong** (the honesty about what we can't confirm is good practice). But it is underselling the *visual* detail: in the image, the broad-brimmed hat and the hunting dogs at the man's feet are clearly visible. The prose should walk the reader to that specific detail in the painting before launching into the Napoleon III speculation. Currently it says "a man dressed as a poacher" without telling us where exactly he is in the frame or what his pose is.

**Suggested rewrite approach for Ch 4:** Open the same way (real people, guest list). Name the friends quickly (two sentences). Expand Baudelaire: what does it mean that he is at the edge, reading, apart from everyone? Then cross to the left and the poacher — first locate him in the canvas (lower left foreground, broad hat, dogs at his feet, visible in the painting), then do the Napoleon III dispute.

### SHOULD-FIX

**Ch 3 (StReading) — One looking claim to verify more carefully.**
The text says the boy in the smock "tilts his head back to watch the landscape appear." Looking at the image: there is a small figure at the foot of the easel, but in this reproduction it is difficult to see the head tilt with confidence. The pose described is plausible but the head direction is not clearly legible. Suggest softening to "crouches at the foot of the easel, watching" rather than asserting a specific upward tilt — or confirm against a higher-resolution source.

**Ch 3 — Left-side inventory is slightly undercooked.**
The prose says "a priest, a merchant, a laborer, a beggar" and then wisely says "hold the details loosely." The figure Courbet specifically identified — the Jewish man and the Irishwoman — could be called out with one extra sentence, since the text mentions them later but only in passing. Not blocking; SHOULD-FIX.

**Ch 5 — "Chapter, one work back" in StAfterlife reads oddly.**
"alongside its older sibling, A Burial at Ornans (Chapter, one work back in this chain)" — the chapter-reference syntax is malformed. Needs to resolve to actual cross-link or be phrased as "(one work back in this chain)" without "Chapter,". SHOULD-FIX.

### MUST-FIX

**Ch 4 (SoMeaning) — NEEDS WORK: three ideas competing, none fully developed; structural issue.**

The chapter has three distinct moves: (a) the "peasant as monument" argument, (b) the Parable of the Sower / biblical weight, (c) the political question (was Millet a socialist?). All three are solid individually. The problem is sequencing: (a) gets one good paragraph, (b) gets a full section, (c) gets two paragraphs — and then the chapter just stops. There's no integration of these three threads into a closing beat.

The bigger problem: the "peasant as monument" opening paragraph is doing the work of a summary conclusion — it's telling you what the painting *means* as a fact, before the chapter has earned it through argumentation. The reader already got all the looking in Ch 2 and the Salon reception in Ch 3; what Ch 4 should do is synthesize that into a reading. Currently it front-loads the synthesis and then re-explains supporting evidence, which makes the chapter feel backward.

Specific fix: Move the "simplest way to say what *The Sower* means" synthesis paragraph to the *end* of the chapter, where it belongs. Open instead with the biblical/scriptural weight (that's the freshest move — the reader hasn't heard this yet), which then naturally leads to the political reading (the terror/menace in the eye of the nervous beholder), which then resolves into the synthesis. The current closing paragraph — "the monument and the menace are the same brushstrokes seen by two different audiences" — is genuinely good and almost does this job, but it needs to be the *actual* final thought, followed by the "that doubleness is why this small painting refuses to settle down" line, which is the correct closer.

**Secondary fix (same chapter):** The comparison to Courbet — "Courbet did it loud, in Paris, at twenty-two feet, with a bored dog and a public sneer. Millet did it quiet, in a forest village, at three feet, with a shadowed face and no jokes at all" — is the best line in Ch 4. It is currently in the first section, before the biblical material, and it half-works there. If the structure is reordered as above, this comparison should be placed in the synthesis close instead, where it lands harder as a summation of two different temperaments arriving at the same revolution.

### SHOULD-FIX

**Ch 1 — Hierarchy-of-genres cross-reference.**
"To understand why that matters, you need one piece of background that the Realism overview one level up in this app lays out in full, and which this read will only sketch." Then it spends two paragraphs sketching it anyway. This is fine for the standalone read, but the sketch is nearly as long as the Stone Breakers' version of the same material. For a reader who has already read Stone Breakers, it is slightly redundant. Suggest condensing this to one paragraph with a terse "if this is your first Realism work, the overview at the movement level fills in the full picture." Not blocking.

**Ch 2 — One looking claim to verify.**
"his left hand grips a coarse sack or apron of grain slung at his hip." In the image, the sower's left arm is drawn in toward his body, and there is a visible cloth bundle/sack at his midsection. This is legible in the image, confirmed. ✓

**Ch 4 (GlMeaning) — NEEDS WORK: two [VERIFY] flags are ship-blockers; must be resolved before this chapter ships.**

The author has flagged two claims as unverified in the text itself:

1. *"[VERIFY: Millet's politics are genuinely disputed; the section below presents the dispute, asserts neither.]"* — This flag is in the draft for the author's own benefit; it must be removed from the rendered component before ship. The claim it guards (that the politics are genuinely disputed) is handled correctly in the prose, so the prose is fine once the flag is stripped.

2. *"[VERIFY: this reflects the commonly reported stance — Millet distanced himself from socialist readings — but it should be checked against a primary or scholarly source before shipping.]"* — This one is a real outstanding action item. The claim (Millet consistently distanced himself from socialist/political readings of his own work) appears in nearly every reputable art-history source on Millet and is confirmed by the extensive Millet scholarship available in the factpack. Remove the [VERIFY] tag after confirming it against the factpack.

3. *"[VERIFY: the 'no biblical consolation in this canvas' reading is one strong art-historical line; others stress the quietly sacred tone Millet brings to peasant labor — present as a live tension, not a verdict.]"* — This tag is also correct as an editorial hedge, and the prose already handles the tension well. Remove the tag; the live tension is already correctly presented in the surrounding prose ("He gives the gleaners his full respect and withholds easy comfort — from them and from us").

**Beyond the tags, a structural issue:** The chapter has five sections, and sections 3, 4, and 5 all recapitulate the "threat vs. dignity" distinction that was already fully developed in Ch 3 (salon). The reader heard it there; they don't need to hear it again three more times from slightly different angles. "Dignity vs. threat" (section 2 of meaning), "The artist's own line" (section 3), and "The thing it leaves out" (section 4) all circle the same point. The chapter needs consolidation: collapse "Dignity vs. threat" and "The artist's own line" into one section, then give the full paragraph to "The thing it leaves out" (the no-biblical-comfort beat is the freshest and sharpest move in the chapter — currently it is squished into the fourth of five sections). The "company it keeps" section is fine and earns its place.

**Suggested structure for GlMeaning:**
1. Open on "What Millet meant" — the truth without prettiness → dignity without sentimentality. (Keep as is.)
2. Collapse the threat/dignity and artist's-own-line beats into one section: what Millet said he meant, why the same canvas reads opposite ways, why both are there.
3. Expand "The thing it leaves out" — the no-biblical-comfort argument is the most original reading in the chapter and it is currently compressed to four sentences. Give it six to eight. This is the payoff.
4. "The company it keeps" as the close. (Keep as is.)

- The Sower: all claims confirmed. Exceptional looking.
- The Gleaners: all claims confirmed. The two-zone split and the three-cap detail are the clearest examples across the batch of looking that immediately teaches the reader to see something they would otherwise gloss over.

**Recurring MUST-FIX pattern:** Both Millet meaning chapters (SoMeaning and GlMeaning) have the same structural problem: the synthesis comes too early, before the argument earns it, and the supporting material that should build to it is arranged in the wrong order. This is an author tendency worth flagging for future works: meaning chapters should build toward the synthesis, not announce it and then re-justify it.
