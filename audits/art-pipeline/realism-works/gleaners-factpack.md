## Core identification

- **Title:** *The Gleaners* (French *Des glaneuses*, literally "the women gleaning").
- **Artist:** Jean-François Millet (1814–1875), French.
- **Date:** 1857. Completed and shown the same year. (Millet had circled the gleaning
  theme in drawings/studies for roughly seven years before this large painting.)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas.
- **Dimensions:** 83.8 × 111.8 cm = **2 ft 9 in × 3 ft 8 in** (landscape). [conversion below]
- **First shown:** the **Salon of 1857** (the official, State-run annual exhibition in Paris).
- **Now at:** **Musée d'Orsay, Paris.** On permanent view.
- **Acquired by the nation:** bequeathed to the Louvre in **1891** on the death of its
  owner, the champagne maker Mme Pommery; passed to the Musée d'Orsay when that museum
  opened in **1986** with the Louvre's 19th-century collection.

## What is in the painting (verified against `img/gleaners.jpg`)

Foreground, left to right:
- **Left woman** — deeply bent at the waist, **blue cap/bonnet**, left hand reaching down
  to the stubble, the other hand near her knee. The most acutely bent of the three.
- **Center woman** — also deeply bent, **red cap**, an apron/pouch at her waist, a small
  bunch of gathered stalks already in/near her hand. Bent nearly as low as the left figure.
- **Right woman** — the most nearly **upright** of the three (still stooped, not standing
  straight), **yellow/gold cap**, a cloth/sack tied at her waist, holding a **small thin
  bundle of gleaned stalks**. She is straightening, perhaps to ease her back.
- All three wear heavy, plain working clothes; faces are shadowed/averted, not idealized.
- The three are in the cooler, shadowed near-ground; the field behind them is sunlit.

Background (the **rich harvest they are not part of**):
- Large **haystacks / round stacks** at left, very tall.
- A **loaded cart** and **sheaves** stacked in rows.
- A **busy crew** of reapers/harvesters bringing in the abundance.
- A **figure on horseback** at the right — read as a mounted **overseer / régisseur**
  (farm steward) watching the work. [VERIFY-NUANCE: "overseer" is the standard reading;
  he is small and at distance — frame as "appears to be an overseer," not certain ID.]
- Low **farm buildings / village** at the right horizon; flat fields to a low horizon.
- **Golden, hazy late-summer light** over the whole far field; warm dusty palette.
- The **ground at the women's feet is nearly bare** — stubble and a few stray stalks;
  the point of the picture is how little is left for them.

The composition's argument: the three bent poor in front, the overflowing sunlit harvest
behind — divided by the empty middle of the stripped field. They gather what the rich crop
left on the ground; they are not part of it.

## Gleaning — define it (zero-knowledge reader)

- **Gleaning** = the practice of going over a field **after** the reapers have cut and
  carted the crop, to pick up by hand the stray heads of grain left lying on the ground.
- It was, for centuries, a recognized **customary right of the poorest** — widows,
  orphans, the old, the landless — to enter a harvested field and take what the harvest
  dropped. Not theft, not charity exactly: a **right**.
- **Deep roots:** the Hebrew Bible commands harvesters to leave the gleanings and the
  field corners for "the poor and the stranger" (Leviticus 19:9–10, 23:22; Deuteronomy
  24:19–21); the Book of **Ruth** is the famous gleaning story (Ruth gleans in Boaz's field).
- **In France specifically:** a royal edict of **1554** regulated it — gleaning permitted
  **from sunup to sundown only**, opening the harvested fields to "the poor, the wretched,
  the deprived." So it was an **ancient AND regulated** right, hedged with rules.
- **Vanishing:** by Millet's lifetime, mechanization, enclosure of common land, tighter
  landowner control, and the modernizing economy were squeezing the old gleaning right
  out of existence. Millet is painting a right that was already on its way out — part of
  why the image reads as elegiac, not just descriptive. [VERIFY-NUANCE: "vanishing" is
  the standard art-historical framing; treat the decline as well-attested trend, not a
  single dated abolition.]

## The Salon of 1857 and the hostile reception (verified)

- **The Salon** = the one official, State-sponsored, juried exhibition that made or broke
  a painter's career in 19th-century France; **the academy** (the Academy of Fine Arts)
  set the rules and the jury.
- **History painting** = the top, most prestigious category in the academy's ranking of
  subjects — grand scenes from scripture, myth, and ancient history, traditionally given
  the biggest canvases. Plain modern life and peasants sat at the bottom.
- *The Gleaners* drew an openly **hostile reaction from the middle and upper classes**.
  Two years after the upheavals of mid-century and within memory of **1848** (the
  February 1848 revolution that briefly put the ordinary poor at the center of French
  politics before the army shut it down), the propertied classes were jumpy about the
  rural poor being made large and dignified.
- **The "Three Fates" line (verified quote):** a critic wrote that "his three gleaners
  have **gigantic pretensions, they pose as the Three Fates of Poverty** … their ugliness
  and their grossness unrelieved." [The **Three Fates** = the three goddesses of Greek/
  Roman myth — Clotho, Lachesis, Atropos — who spin, measure, and cut the thread of every
  human life; invoking them turned three poor women into looming figures of doom.]
- **The "1793" fright (verified):** a critic perceived in the painting "an alarming
  intimation of **the scaffolds of 1793**" — i.e. the guillotines and Terror of the
  French Revolution. Three stooped peasants read, to a nervous bourgeois eye, as a
  political threat.
- **The scale problem:** the canvas (≈2 ft 9 in × 3 ft 8 in) was large for a mere scene
  of field labor; that much seriousness applied to peasants instead of gods/saints was
  itself provocative under the academy's hierarchy. [Note for fact-check: this is a
  *medium-large genre canvas*, NOT a Burial-scale ten-footer. The provocation is
  "monumentalizing peasants / near-history-painting dignity," not literal heroic size.
  Do NOT overclaim the dimensions.]

## Millet & the Barbizon context (defer the broad movement story to the Realism overview)

- Millet (1814–1875) was **born into a farming family** in Gruchy, in Normandy; he knew
  field labor firsthand, which is part of why his peasant pictures feel observed, not staged.
- He settled in **Barbizon** (1849), a village at the edge of the Forest of Fontainebleau
  southeast of Paris that gave its name to the **Barbizon School** — a group of painters
  who left the studio to paint rural landscape and country life directly. Millet is its
  most famous figure-painter.
- **Sibling works (cross-reference, don't re-explain the movement):**
  - *The Sower* (1850) — a lone peasant striding a dusk field flinging seed; monumental,
    near-menacing; came two years after 1848. (Already in the Realism overview.)
  - *The Angelus* (1857–59) — two peasants pausing to pray at the evening bell under a
    huge sky; became one of the most reproduced images of the century.
  - **Courbet** — the loud, public, urban-facing half of Realism (*A Burial at Ornans*,
    *The Stone Breakers*); Millet is the quiet, rural half. Frame them as the two poles
    of the same movement; the movement-level argument lives one level up.

## Provenance (verified; ends at Orsay)

1. **1857** — Millet, short of money, sold the painting for **3,000 francs** (under his
   4,000-franc asking price). Buyer: collector Binder. [VERIFY: the immediate first buyer
   is usually given as Arthur Stevens / then collectors; the well-attested facts are the
   3,000-franc sale and the later Bischoffsheim ownership. Treat the exact first-buyer
   chain as soft; the 3,000-franc figure and the Bischoffsheim→1889 auction are firm.]
2. By the 1880s owned by the banker **Ferdinand Bischoffsheim**.
3. **1889** — sold at auction for **300,000 francs** — a hundredfold jump in about thirty
   years, a vivid measure of how the despised picture had become a treasure.
4. **1889** — acquired (announced within a week of the auction) by **Jeanne-Alexandrine
   Louise Pommery** (Mme Pommery), the champagne house owner.
5. **1891** — on Pommery's death, the painting is **bequeathed to the Louvre** per the
   terms of her will — a private gift to the nation.
6. **1986** — passes to the **Musée d'Orsay** with the Louvre's 19th-century holdings when
   the Orsay opens in the converted Gare d'Orsay railway station. On permanent view today.

**⚠ Provenance discrepancy with the brief.** The task brief said the work "came via a 1890
gift." The verified route is: **acquired by Pommery 1889; bequeathed to the Louvre 1891**
on her death. There is **no 1890 gift**. The author copy uses the verified 1889/1891 dates.
(If a source pins the formal entry into the national collection to 1890, it is not the one
I found; flag for fact-check.)

## Afterlife / significance

- *The Gleaners* became **one of the most reproduced images of the 19th century** (with
  *The Angelus*) — endlessly printed, copied, hung in homes, parodied, and quoted.
- A direct influence on **Vincent van Gogh**, who revered Millet and copied his peasant
  subjects. (Verified — Millet recurs throughout Van Gogh's letters.)
- The price story (3,000 → 300,000 francs) is the cleanest possible measure of the
  reversal: the picture the bourgeois Salon recoiled from became a bourgeois trophy and
  then a national monument.

## Disputed / contested readings — flag as [VERIFY] in the draft

- **Millet's politics.** Whether Millet was a political radical, a socialist
  sympathizer, or simply a sympathetic observer with no program is **genuinely disputed.**
  Millet himself denied political intent and resisted being claimed by the socialists; his
  defenders (and he himself) framed the work as humane truth, not propaganda. Critics on
  the right read menace into it anyway. **Frame as disputed; do not assert he was a
  socialist; do not assert he was apolitical. Present both and let the reader sit in it.**
- **The mounted figure as "overseer."** Standard reading, but he is tiny and distant;
  frame as "appears to be" not certain.
- **"The three Fates of poverty"** — that is a *critic's hostile reading*, not the
  painting's stated subject. Present it as what a hostile critic saw, not as Millet's intent.
- **Religious undertone.** Some read the gleaning theme through Ruth / the Bible as quietly
  sacred (cf. *The Angelus*); others stress Millet built NO biblical consolation into THIS
  picture (the poverty foreground vs. rich-harvest distance has "no touch of the biblical
  sense of community"). Present as a live tension, not a settled meaning.
- **"Vanishing right"** — well-attested trend (mechanization/enclosure squeezing gleaning
  out), but not a single dated event; frame as a trend.
