### Squaring / underdrawing — define for the zero-knowledge reader
- **Underdrawing** = the drawn sketch an artist lays down first, on the canvas, before painting over it.
- **Squaring for transfer / the grid** = drawing a grid of squares over a small study, and a matching
  grid (scaled up) on the big canvas, then copying the drawing square-by-square to enlarge it
  accurately. The grid lines are meant to be painted over and to disappear. Here they didn't — the
  painting was abandoned before the paint covered them, so the scaffolding shows.

## 3. The multiple versions (be honest; do NOT conflate)

There is no single "the" *Third-Class Carriage* — Daumier worked the subject repeatedly:
- **MET, New York** — oil on canvas, c.1862–64, **UNFINISHED / squared for transfer.** ← THIS read.
  [CONFIRMED]
- **National Gallery of Canada, Ottawa** — a **finished oil on canvas**, c.1863–65, similar in
  composition with some variations. [CONFIRMED — NGC + Wikipedia]
- **The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore** — a **watercolor** (watercolor, ink wash, charcoal), 1864,
  commissioned by collector **William T. Walters**, part of a set of first-/second-/third-class
  carriage scenes; 20.3 × 29.5 cm. The MET unfinished canvas closely corresponds to this watercolor;
  the squaring grid on the MET picture may have been taken from it (or another earlier study).
  [CONFIRMED — Walters / Wikipedia]
- **Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco** — an **earlier oil on panel**, c.1856–58, with a **different
  arrangement** of the three main figures. [CONFIRMED — FAMSF / Wikipedia]
- Daumier had drawn railway-class scenes for years before — the **lithograph series *Les Chemins de
  Fer* (the railways)**, published in *Le Charivari*, 1843–58. The painting subject grew out of the
  prints. [CONFIRMED — Wikipedia]

**Framing honesty (for the afterlife chapter):** the MET picture is the famous one precisely *because*
it is unfinished — the visible method made it a teaching object. But it is one of a family, and the
read must say so plainly.

## 4. Third class — what it was (modern industrial life)

- French railways (and European railways generally) sold tickets in **three classes**. **First class**
  = upholstered, enclosed, comfortable. **Second class** = plainer. **Third class** = the **cheapest** —
  **hard wooden benches**, the most crowded, sometimes open or barely sheltered carriages, packed with
  the urban and rural poor moving for work. [CONFIRMED — standard 19th-c. railway history; supports
  the "third class = cheapest, hard-bench" gloss.]
- The railway itself was the face of the new **industrial age**: in the 1830s–60s France laced itself
  with rail; ordinary poor people who had never travelled now sat shoulder to shoulder in a moving box
  with strangers. That anonymous crowd-in-transit is the *modern* subject — not a peasant in a field
  (Millet) or a village funeral (Courbet), but the city's poor in motion. [CONTEXT — supports the
  "Realism of the modern city" thesis; frame as the new subject, don't overclaim a "first."]

## 5. Daumier the man (the caricaturist who became this painter)

- **Honoré Daumier, 1808–1879.** Trained himself in the new medium of **lithography** (a printmaking
  method: draw with greasy crayon on a flat limestone slab, then print many copies — the cheap mass
  medium of the illustrated press). [CONFIRMED]
- He earned his living for ~40 years as a **caricaturist / cartoonist** for the satirical papers
  ***La Caricature*** and ***Le Charivari***, producing **more than 4,000 lithographs** over his
  career — political and social satire, the news drawn sharp. **In his lifetime he was famous as a
  cartoonist, not as a painter.** [CONFIRMED]
- **The Gargantua jail (cross-reference the sibling work):** in **1831** Daumier drew ***Gargantua***
  — King **Louis-Philippe** as a bloated giant on a throne-commode, swallowing the people's taxes
  shoveled up a ramp and excreting privileges to cronies. It got him **prosecuted and jailed for six
  months** (the six-month term is the standard account; frame it as such). *Gargantua* is the Daumier
  sibling object being authored in parallel — the caricaturist who became this painter. [CONFIRMED —
  matches the existing Realism movement card + GARGANTUA-in-parallel; date 1831; six months = received
  account.]
- **The paintings were a private, late, mostly unseen pursuit.** Daumier painted seriously from the
  1840s on but the oils were **barely exhibited and barely sold in his lifetime** — he was the
  newspaper cartoonist; the easel painter was almost invisible. [CONFIRMED]
- **Late life:** after ~1865 he sank into financial trouble; left Paris for the village of
  **Valmondois**, where his friend the landscape painter **Camille Corot** helped him — by one
  well-known account Corot quietly bought him the house so he would not be evicted (frame as the
  standard story). His **eyesight failed** through the 1870s; **by ~1873 he was nearly blind.** In
  **1878**, a year before he died, a **retrospective exhibition** (held at the Durand-Ruel gallery)
  finally drew attention to him as an artist — but it lost money and he died **in poverty in 1879.**
  [CONFIRMED — Wikipedia/Britannica; "Corot bought the house" = standard account → frame as such;
  the 1878 show's venue Durand-Ruel is documented.]

## 6. Realism siblings (DEFER movement-level material to the Realism overview)

This is a WORK read — go DEEPER on THIS painting; defer the movement's general argument to
`/art/mod/real`. Cross-reference, do not re-explain:
- **Realism** = the mid-19th-c. school that insisted the real, ordinary, contemporary world deserved
  serious art at full scale and seriousness — defer the full story to the movement overview. [CONTEXT]
- **Courbet** (the firebrand) painted the *village* — *A Burial at Ornans*, the *Stone Breakers*.
  **Millet** painted the *rural poor* — the *Gleaners*, the *Sower*. **Daumier** is the **city** half
  of Realism: the modern town, its crowds, its politicians, its third-class poor. The three are
  siblings; *The Third-Class Carriage* is Daumier's contribution to the same program, aimed at the
  modern *urban* subject. [CONFIRMED — matches the Realism movement read + factions list.]
- Lineage: parents = Realism (the movement) · Daumier's own railway lithographs · Dutch genre /
  everyday-life painting (dignity in ordinary scenes). Children = Social Realism · the Ashcan School
  (the gritty modern American city) · Impressionism (the contemporary world). [CONTEXT — consistent
  with the Realism movement lineage chips; the Ashcan note already exists there.]

## 10. Annotations (4–6 "Look closer" pointers — PROSE, verified vs image)

1. **The nursing mother** — front bench, far left. Head bowed, the most tenderly worked passage;
   cradling an infant in her lap, lit by the windows.
2. **The old woman and her basket** — front bench, dead center. The most finished face in the picture:
   lined, tired, eyes forward; both hands folded over the handle of a wicker basket on her lap.
3. **The sleeping boy** — front bench, right end. Slumped asleep, head down; a dark box/chest sits in
   the lower-right corner beside him.
4. **The packed crowd, receding** — the whole upper register behind the bench. Rows of anonymous
   passengers in hats and bonnets dissolving back into the dim car; faces get sketchier toward the
   back (compounded by the unfinished paint).
5. **The two windows** — upper left. The only light in the car; pale rectangles that throw the front
   three figures into relief and leave everything else brown and enclosed.
6. **The visible grid / underdrawing** — across the unfinished passages, clearest in the upper-left
   window area. Daumier's ruled squaring grid and charcoal contours show through the thin paint — the
   scaffolding he never covered, an open window onto how he built the picture.
