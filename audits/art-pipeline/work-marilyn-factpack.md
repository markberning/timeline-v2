## 2. THE STORY

- **Made within weeks of Monroe's death.** Marilyn Monroe died on **5 August 1962**. Warhol began his Marilyn silkscreens in the weeks immediately after; *Marilyn Diptych* is one of the works from that late-1962 burst. (Sources: Tate; Wikipedia; Smarthistory — all date the series to "shortly after" / "within weeks of" her death.)
- **The source image is a single publicity still.** Every one of the 50 faces comes from **one** photograph: a publicity still for the 1953 film noir ***Niagara***, photographed by **Gene Kornman**. Warhol cropped it tight to Monroe's face — hair, eyelids, lips — and used it as the matrix for all 50 repetitions. (Wikipedia, Smarthistory.)
- **The silkscreen photo-transfer process.** Warhol's own description of the method: *"you pick a photograph, blow it up, transfer it in glue onto silk, and then roll ink across it so the ink goes through the silk but not through the glue. That way you get the same image, slightly different each time."* A photographic image is burned into a fine screen; ink is squeegeed through it onto the canvas; the screen is repositioned and pulled again, over and over. The result is **the same image reproduced mechanically, never quite identically** — registration drifts, ink loads and starves. (Quote via Wikipedia; process via Smarthistory/Khan Academy.)
- **Left = color / life; right = black-and-white / death.** The left panel is printed in bright, saturated color (yellow hair, pink face, red lips). The right panel is black-and-white, and across it the printing **degrades** — some faces over-inked and clogged, others starved and **fading toward blankness** at the right edge. The most widely held reading: the colored left = Monroe alive, the public glamour image; the fading right = her death, mortality, and the way the celebrity image dissolves into nothing. (Smarthistory; Tate interpretive pages.) **Attribute this as a reading, not a stated authorial program** — see §4 and §8.
- **Mechanical repetition / the star as mass-produced image.** The grid of identical faces mimics a contact sheet, a strip of film frames, or rows of product on a supermarket shelf. The mechanical seriality is the point: a movie star is, like a soup can, a **mass-produced, reproducible commodity image**. Overexposure empties the image of meaning. (Smarthistory; Tate "Celebrity, Social Media and Overexposure.")
- **The diptych form's religious echo.** A *diptych* is historically a two-panel hinged Christian **altarpiece / devotional icon**. By titling the work *Diptych* and using that sacred two-panel format, Warhol casts Monroe as a modern secular **icon / relic** — the repeated face read as a litany, the gilded star as an object of quasi-religious devotion. (Smarthistory explicitly invokes the Christian altarpiece format.) This is interpretation, widely cited, not an artist statement.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (before / after — REQUIRED)

**Before:** A painting was a unique object made by an artist's hand — brushstroke, touch, the trace of a single maker. Even earlier modernist abstraction (Abstract Expressionism, the dominant New York mode just before Pop) prized the gestural, autographic mark as the carrier of meaning and selfhood.

**After / the break:**
1. **Photographic silkscreen, repeated by machine.** The image is not drawn or painted by hand — it is a **found photograph mechanically reproduced** across the canvas. The "brushwork" is squeegee drag and printing error. Painting absorbs the logic of mass reproduction.
2. **Celebrity-as-commodity.** The subject is not a patron, saint, or the artist's inner life — it is a **manufactured media star**, treated exactly like a consumer product (Campbell's Soup, Coca-Cola). High art and mass-culture imagery collapse into one.
3. **The artist as "factory," not as hand.** Warhol named his studio **The Factory** and worked with assistants, screens, and serial output, presenting himself as a **reproducer / machine** rather than a singular author. The romantic ideal of the artist-genius is deliberately voided — the famous (contested) line: *"I want to be a machine"* (see §4 for the source and the variant flag).

*Net:* the locus of meaning moves from the unique hand-made mark to the **mechanically reproduced media image**, and from the artist's interior to the surface of mass culture.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced Warhol quotes — flag variants)

**Primary key passage (on repetition / emptiness):**

> "the more you look at the same exact thing, the more the meaning goes away, and the better and emptier you feel."

- **Source:** Andy Warhol, *Warhol in his own words — Untitled Statements (1963–1987)*, selected by Neil Printz; as quoted in *Andy Warhol: A Retrospective* (ed. Kynaston McShine), MoMA / Bullfinch Press–Little, Brown, 1989, pp. 457–467. (Wikiquote, sourced.) The line is also widely attributed to / quoted from Warhol & Pat Hackett's *POPism: The Warhol Sixties* (1980).
- **Variant flag:** Some popular quotation sites give *"Because the more you look at the same exact thing…"* (adding a leading "Because") and the full form ends *"…and the better and emptier you feel."* The wording above is the standard sourced form. Confirm against the print source before quoting on-page; do not invent the lead-in.

**Secondary (the "machine" / Factory line — USE WITH A CAVEAT):**

> "The reason I'm painting this way is that I want to be a machine."

- **Source:** Andy Warhol, interview with **Gene Swenson, "What Is Pop Art? Interviews with Eight Painters (Part 1)," *Art News*, New York, November 1963.**
- **Variant / trap flag (important):** Art historian **Jennifer Sichel** (2018), working from Swenson's original tape, showed the published 1963 text was **edited** — Warhol's spoken phrasing was closer to *"everybody should be a machine,"* inside a conversation about sexuality that was cut. So "I want to be a machine" is the **canonical published quote** but is **not a verbatim transcript** of what he said. If used, attribute to the 1963 *Art News* interview and note it is the edited/published form.

**Recommendation:** Use the *"the more you look at the same exact thing…"* line as the work's key statement (it bears directly on the diptych's repetition). Optionally cite the "machine" line for the "artist-as-factory" break, with the variant caveat.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6 — prose pointers, no coordinates)

1. **Fifty faces, one face.** Step back and the whole work reads as a single grid of repetition — fifty Marilyns, every one printed from the **same** *Niagara* publicity still. Sameness, not variety, is the subject.
2. **Color left vs. fading black-and-white right.** The left panel blazes in flat, hot color — yellow hair, pink skin, a slash of red lipstick, a band of turquoise eyeshadow. Cross to the right panel and the color drops out: grey, then greyer, the faces thinning and **dissolving toward blank canvas** at the far edge.
3. **Off-register printing and smears.** Look at how imperfect the reproduction is — color blocks slip out of alignment with the face, lips and eyes don't sit on their outlines, some impressions are clogged with ink and others are starved and broken. The **machine's failure is left visible**, not corrected.
4. **The cropped publicity-still face.** Notice how tightly the photo is cropped: just hair, heavy-lidded eyes with shadow, parted lips. It is a **headshot / glamour mask**, not a portrait of a person — the public image, stripped to its sellable features.
5. **The grid.** The faces line up in even rows and columns like a contact sheet, a strip of film frames, or products on a shelf — the **mass-production layout** that turns a star into a manufactured good.
6. **Two joined panels (the diptych seam).** See the work as two equal canvases set side by side — the historic **altarpiece / icon** format. The seam down the middle is also the divide the life/death reading hangs on: glamour on one side, fade on the other.

## 6. PROVENANCE

- **1962:** Painted by Warhol. The two canvases were acquired **directly from Warhol** (before he had gallery representation) by collectors **Emily Hall Tremaine and Burton Tremaine**. By account, **Emily Tremaine suggested the two canvases be shown together as a diptych**; Warhol agreed, and from then on they were treated as **one work**. *(Wikipedia; art design café. Flag: the "Tremaine suggested the diptych pairing" detail is widely repeated but anecdotal — attribute as reported, not as documented fact.)*
- **July 1980:** The Tremaines **sold the work to the Tate Gallery, London**, reportedly for **$270,000**. *(Wikipedia. The exact price is from secondary accounts; treat as reported.)*
- **Tate record:** **"Purchased 1980,"** accession **T03093**, now at Tate (shown at Tate Modern). *(Tate.)*

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct, with sources)

1. **Source photo = *Niagara* (1953), not *Some Like It Hot* / *Gentlemen Prefer Blondes* / a generic "Marilyn photo."** It is a **publicity still for *Niagara* (1953)**, photographed by **Gene Kornman**. (Wikipedia, Smarthistory.) Don't name another film.
2. **The life/death (and life-vs-oblivion) reading is interpretation, not Warhol's stated intent.** It is widely held and worth presenting — but **attribute it as a reading**. Critic **David Bourdon** noted Warhol **never assigned a definitive meaning** to the relationship between the two panels. (Wikipedia.) Same caution for the icon/altarpiece "devotional" reading: it's persuasive criticism, not an artist statement.
3. **Count is exactly 50 — 25 + 25.** Not "dozens," not "a hundred." Fifty images, twenty-five per panel. (The related but **separate** works *Marilyn x 100* and *Twenty-Five Colored Marilyns* are different paintings — don't conflate.)
4. **It is a silkscreen on canvas, NOT a hand-painting.** Medium is **silkscreen ink + acrylic on canvas**. The faces are **printed** (photo-silkscreen), not brushed. Calling it "a painting Warhol painted by hand" is wrong; the acrylic is the color ground/blocks, the image is screened.
5. **Dimensions trap.** **Overall ~205.4 × 289.6 cm (~6 ft 9 in × 9 ft 6 in); each of the two panels ~205.4 × 144.8 cm (~6 ft 9 in × 4 ft 9 in).** Don't quote a single-panel figure (205.4 × 144.8) as the whole work, and note the trivial cm rounding across sources (289.4 vs 289.6). It is **tall (over 6½ ft) and wide (over 9 ft)** — a large work, not a small print.
6. **"I want to be a machine" is the edited published quote, not a verbatim transcript** (Sichel 2018) — see §4. Don't present it as a clean direct utterance without that caveat if you lean on it.
7. **It was purchased by Tate in 1980 — not donated, not bequeathed.** "Purchased 1980," T03093.
