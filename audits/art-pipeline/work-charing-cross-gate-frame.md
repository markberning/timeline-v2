## LENS 1 — COMPREHENSIVENESS (what's missing)

The brief named four things to web-check. Three are present and well-handled; one is missing.

### Present and correct (no action)
- **Vollard's commercial commission to rival Monet's Thames series** — PRESENT and central. The
  whole `assignment` section, the hook, the `break`, and the `afterlife` are built on it.
  Web-confirmed: the Courtauld states Derain "came to London in 1906 to paint a series of works
  that would rival Claude Monet's earlier celebrated views of the city," and "Vollard dispatched
  Derain to London in March 1906." Draft's framing matches.
- **The series count** — PRESENT and correctly hedged ("dozens commissioned, around thirty
  delivered"). Web confirms the Courtauld's own figure: "Vollard bought Derain's London series in
  its entirety, 30 paintings in total of which 29 are known today." The draft's refusal to assert
  a single hard number (Met says ~50 commissioned) is the right call and is honest.
- **Derain's relationship to Monet's precedent** — PRESENT and thorough (the `subject` section
  "Monet's river, Derain's vantage," the `break` section). Web-confirmed; no erasure (see Lens 2).

### [FIX] MISSING: Derain's BRIEF Fauve moment — that he abandoned this style and turned conservative

**The brief explicitly asked to check "how the London series fits Derain's brief Fauve moment
before he turned conservative." The draft never tells the reader the Fauve moment was brief, or
that Derain turned conservative.** This is the one named comprehensiveness item the draft drops,
and it is the single most interesting fact for a reader's understanding: this incandescent pink
Thames is one of the high-water marks of a phase its own maker walked away from within two years.

Right now the draft leaves the opposite impression. The `assignment` section says "By 1906 Derain
was one of the wild beasts in good standing," the `afterlife` ends on the painting outlasting
Vollard, and the `MeanwhileSheet` pivots to Picasso/Cubism — so the reader exits thinking Derain
*stayed* a Fauve. He did not. Web-confirmed (Britannica, Wikipedia, Thyssen):

- Fauvism as a movement ran ~1904–1908 (brief).
- Derain **broke with Fauvism by 1908**, pulled toward Cézanne and nascent Cubism; he reportedly
  destroyed a good deal of his own earlier work.
- After WWI he became a leader of the **"Return to order"** / Neoclassical reaction; "the
  spontaneity and impulsiveness that had distinguished his earlier work gradually disappeared,"
  and "his more conservative style brought him financial success."

This belongs in the `break` or `afterlife` section — the painting matters MORE, not less, once the
reader knows its maker treated this style as a passing phase. Without it the work read is missing
the arc the brief asked for.

**Exact placement + fix text.** Add a short closing beat to `CrxAfterlife` (or fold into the first
`afterlife` paragraph). Suggested insert, immediately after the Courtauld-exhibition sentence in
the first `afterlife` paragraph ("...a century after he made them.") — house voice, every claim
web-backed:

> There is one more thing worth knowing, because it changes how you see the picture. Derain's
> wild-beast period was short. Within about two years he had cooled on Fauvism's pure color
> entirely, turning toward the more measured, structured painting of Cézanne, and after the First
> World War he became one of the leaders of a broad "return to order," painting careful nudes,
> portraits and landscapes in a deliberately classical, traditional manner for the rest of his
> life. The man who burned the Thames pink spent his later decades as one of the more
> conservative painters of his generation. That is part of what makes this canvas worth standing
> in front of: it is a high point of a style its own maker treated as a phase and left behind.

(If the coordinator prefers to keep `afterlife` purely about the object's provenance journey, the
alternative is to add this to the END of `CrxBreak` instead — but it must appear somewhere; the
brief named it.)

### [NICE] The "first/only" of Fauve-on-Monet is left implicit
The draft says Derain "picks a fight with a master on the master's own ground" but never states
that the whole London series (not just this canvas) was the deliberate Fauve answer to Monet's
London. It is implied across sections and is fine as-is. No overclaim is made (good — see Lens 2).
Optional: one clause noting this canvas is one of a coordinated *series* aimed at Monet, not a
lone gesture, would tighten comprehension. Not required.

### [NICE] Pissarro's London precedent is omitted
One web source (NGA-adjacent / alluringworld) notes Derain took cues from both Monet AND Pissarro's
earlier London paintings. The draft's Monet-only framing is defensible (Monet is the named
benchmark in the fact pack and the commercially relevant precedent — the 1904 Durand-Ruel show was
Monet's), so this is not an error. Mentioning Pissarro is optional enrichment, not owed.

## LENS 2 — FRAMING / FAIRNESS

**This is the draft's strongest dimension. It is honest in exactly the way the brief demanded.**
No fixes required here; recorded as confirmations.

### CLEAN — dealer-driven commission, not inspired genius
The draft never sells this as lone-genius inspiration. It opens "got on a boat to London because a
dealer told him to... This trip was a business decision, not a holiday," calls it "the commercially
obvious thing," titles the closing section "Vollard's bet," and ends on "A dealer's commercial bet
sent a 26-year-old to London to out-paint Monet." This is the correct, web-supported framing
(Courtauld: Vollard "dispatched" him; "commissioned a series"). No genius-myth inflation.

### CLEAN — Monet's precedent is not erased, and Monet is treated fairly
Monet is named the precedent throughout, credited as "the most famous of the Impressionists," and
the `break` section is careful NOT to belittle him: "Monet had pushed painting toward the truth of
light. Derain pushed it past truth altogether." Monet is cast as benchmark, not foil-to-be-beaten.
The figure nameplate even reads "The benchmark, not a participant." Good. No erasure; no cheap
"Derain beat Monet" triumphalism (the "beat Monet" language is correctly framed as *Vollard's
commercial aim*, not as an achieved verdict).

### CLEAN — no "first/only" overclaim
The draft makes no "first Fauve painting," "only," or "the painting that invented X" claim. The
break is framed as a *wager* and a *test case* ("one of its clearest test cases"), not a primacy
claim. This avoids the inflation trap.

### CLEAN — prices, hedges, attributions all honest
Provenance hedges ("probably" Guillaume, "possibly" Chrysler) are preserved and explicitly flagged
as the museum's own. No invented prices — the draft calls this out as a deliberate honesty move.
The "translate, not reproduce" idea is paraphrased, never quoted, with an in-text disclosure that
the wording has no reliable source. All correct.

### Minor framing note — [NICE], not a fix
The hook and `assignment` lean on "beat Monet" / "out-paint Monet." This is fine because the draft
consistently attributes that ambition to *Vollard's commercial brief*, not to an objective
ranking. Watch only that no later edit converts it into an asserted "and he succeeded / surpassed
Monet" verdict — that would become a framing [FIX]. As written, it is clean.
