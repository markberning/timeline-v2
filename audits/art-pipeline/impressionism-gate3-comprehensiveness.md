## How this was built

I built my own must-cover checklist independently (below), cross-checked it against
the draft and the fact pack, then used web search to verify facts and catch anything
both the pack and draft missed. I did NOT start from the prompt's candidate list —
I built my own first, then used the prompt's list to double-check I hadn't skipped
anything obvious.

Grading scale:
- **COVERED** — present, adequately treated, no action needed
- **THIN** — mentioned but not given its due weight or specificity
- **MISSING** — absent entirely

For gaps: **MUST-ADD** = genuinely blocking (an intelligent reader finishes
misinformed or with a structural hole); **SHOULD-CONSIDER** = enriching but not
blocking.

**THIN → MUST-ADD (partial).** The draft handles Caillebotte as patron/financier

## Section C — Fact-pack blind spots (things the fact pack itself missed)

**C1. The Caillebotte bequest epilogue — a blind spot in the pack.**
The fact pack (§4, §9) mentions the bequest and the "fight" but provides no
verified numbers and no resolution. The pack does not tell the coordinator that
the bequest is resolvable with specific facts (68 works, 38 accepted, Renoir
executor, Luxembourg 1897 opening, first public Impressionist display in France).
This is a fact-pack gap that needs coordinator verification before the draft can
fill it.

**Verified facts for the pack to absorb:**
- Will: 68 works (Pissarro 19, Monet 14, Renoir 10, Sisley 9, Degas 7, Cézanne 5,
  Manet 4). Source: Wikipedia, *Caillebotte bequest*.
- State accepted 38 of 68 (Renoir negotiated the terms with the Fine Arts
  Administration, Feb 1896). Source: Walsh blog; Artjourney Curator.
- The 38 went to the Luxembourg Palace; opened to the public 1897 — confirmed as
  the **first exhibition of Impressionism in a French public museum**. Source:
  multiple (Wood Floor Business, Artjourney Curator).
- Executor of the will: Pierre-Auguste Renoir. Source: Wikipedia.
- Chief academic opponent: Jean-Léon Gérôme (led the Academy objection).
  Source: Walsh blog; AIC article.

**C2. Turner and Constable in London.**
The fact pack §9 mentions "they studied Turner and Constable" during the 1870–71
London exile but the draft drops this entirely. It is not just a cultural tidbit —
it is the genealogical connection (British loose-landscape tradition → Impressionism)
that enriches the "where did plein air actually come from" question the draft already
raises in Ch. 2. For completeness the fact pack should add a Turner/Constable note
to §3 (technique genealogy) and §9 (the war context).

**C3. Cassatt's 1890 color prints.**
The fact pack §5 (Cassatt section) describes her as a "serious printmaker shaped by
Japanese woodblocks" but gives no dates or specifics. The 1890 École des Beaux-Arts
print exhibition she attended and the 1891 series of 10 color aquatints are
well-documented and frequently cited as among her most significant work. The pack
could add these for the author; they support the Japonisme beat in Ch. 5 or Ch. 6.

**C4. Pissarro as Cézanne's/Gauguin's mentor.**
The fact pack §4 identifies Pissarro as "mentor to Cézanne and Gauguin" in one
line but provides no context. The draft does not pick this up. For a movement read
that explicitly covers Cézanne passing through, the mentor relationship (Pontoise
1872, teaching Cézanne to paint patiently from nature) is a natural enrichment.

**C5. Argenteuil.**
The fact pack §9 mentions the Seine-side resorts by generic description but never
names Argenteuil specifically, even though it is the defining Impressionist working
location after Paris: Monet lived there 1871–78, Renoir and Caillebotte worked
there, and a large fraction of the canonical river and boating canvases originate
there. The pack (§6a) names the Grenouillère and the Gare Saint-Lazare but not
Argenteuil by name. This is a minor blind spot in the pack.

## Section D — MUST-ADD summary (blocking)

**MUST-ADD count: 2** (one split into two beats)

### MUST-ADD 1 — Caillebotte as a painter (Ch. 4, ~2 sentences)
**Gap:** The draft describes Caillebotte only as a wealthy financier/patron who
"bought his friends' work" and left a bequest. His identity as a significant painter
in his own right — with a distinct style (sharp photographic urban perspective,
working-class subjects that the Salon rejected as "vulgar") — is absent. A reader
finishes not knowing why Caillebotte hangs in the Orsay and the Art Institute, only
why he matters as a money man.

**Where:** Chapter 4, after the first mention of Caillebotte as financier. A brief
visual characterization at that point.

**Verified facts to include:**
- The Floor Planers / Les raboteurs de parquet (1875): floor workers stripped to
  the waist in a Haussmann-era apartment; rejected by the Salon 1875 as "vulgar
  subject matter"; shown at the **2nd Impressionist exhibition 1876** where it was
  a "sensation." (Orsay collection; Wikipedia, *Les raboteurs de parquet*.)
- Paris Street; Rainy Day (1877, Art Institute of Chicago, 6 ft 11½ in × 9 ft ¾ in):
  the new Haussmann boulevard in a downpour, shot from a slightly elevated angle,
  figures cut by the frame — essentially photographic urban realism at history-
  painting scale, a counterpart to Monet's La Grenouillère on the other end of
  the movement's subject range.
- His style: sharp-edged, almost photographic perspective, yet modern subjects and
  Impressionist color; a bridge between the movement's plein-air wing and Degas's
  urban indoor wing.

### MUST-ADD 2 — Caillebotte bequest epilogue (Ch. 7, ~3 sentences)
**Gap:** The draft promises "a later story" about the bequest but never delivers it.
Since this is the movement-level read and there is no "later" read that picks it up,
the promised story is simply dropped. The bequest fight and Luxembourg opening are
the movement's actual institutional ending — France's delayed, resistant acceptance
of what it had spent two decades refusing. Without it, the "France never came around
in time, the buyers came from across an ocean" theme lacks its domestic closing beat.

**Where:** Chapter 7, after or near the 1886 dissolution paragraph.

**Verified facts to include (web-confirmed):**
- Caillebotte died February 1894; his will left **68 Impressionist canvases** to
  the French state (Pissarro 19, Monet 14, Renoir 10, Sisley 9, Degas 7, Cézanne 5,
  Manet 4).
- The Fine Arts Administration resisted; **Jean-Léon Gérôme** (the Academy's
  leading figure — same man whose Salon taste the movement had spent two decades
  fighting) led the objection.
- Renoir, executor of the will, negotiated terms in **February 1896**: the state
  accepted **38** of the 68 (the other 30 were refused).
- The **38 hung at the Luxembourg Palace in 1897** — the **first public display of
  Impressionism in a French national museum**, 23 years after the mocked seascape
  at Nadar's studio.
- The paintings later migrated to the Louvre and eventually to the Musée d'Orsay.
- The irony: the academy that rejected the movement for two decades had to be
  legally forced by a dead man's will to hang it on a public wall.

Sources: Wikipedia (*Caillebotte bequest*); Walsh blog (2013); ArtJourney Curator
(Substack); Art Institute of Chicago article on Caillebotte.
