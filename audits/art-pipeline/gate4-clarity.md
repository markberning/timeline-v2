# Gate 4 — Newcomer / Clarity critique
**Section:** Lay of the Land — "The world before the revolt" (kind: ERA)
**Draft file:** `audits/art-pipeline/lay-of-the-land-draft.md` (Part A)
**Critic:** Newcomer / clarity gate — reading cold as a sharp 15-year-old who has never set foot in a museum.

## Term-by-term verdict

### Académie des Beaux-Arts
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Introduced with an immediate inline gloss: "the art wing of the Institut de France, the country's official body of approved excellence." That is enough. A newcomer catches the shape: official government stamp of approval. No problem.

### Institut de France
**Verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (SHOULD-FIX)**
Used inside the Académie gloss as the parent body, but not itself explained. "The country's official body of approved excellence" describes the Académie, not the Institut. A newcomer reading carefully will notice that "the art wing of [something]" is a partial definition — they don't know what the Institut de France actually is (a cluster of five learned academies covering art, science, literature, etc.). Not a comprehension blocker for this chapter's argument, because the point is the Académie's authority, not the Institut's structure. SHOULD-FIX: one parenthetical — e.g. "(France's umbrella body of official learned societies)" — closes the gap cleanly.

### École des Beaux-Arts
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Immediately glossed: "the state art school." Plain and sufficient.

### Prix de Rome
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Immediately glossed: "a State-funded scholarship to study the old masters." Does the job.

### Villa Medici
**Verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (SHOULD-FIX)**
Named as where Prix de Rome winners were sent, but not identified. A newcomer only learns it's in Rome; they have no idea it's a specific French academy building. The draft says "shipped its winner off to the Villa Medici in Rome" — fine if you're satisfied with Rome as the destination. But a reader might wonder: is this a hotel? A museum? A private palace? One clause — "the French Academy in Rome" or "France's resident study house in Rome" — would close it. Not a comprehension blocker (the point is they went to Rome to copy old masters), so SHOULD-FIX.

### *fini*
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Introduced with an excellent inline definition: "a surface blended so smoothly that you could not find a single brushstroke if you went looking." The italics signal it's a technical term, the gloss is vivid and concrete. No problem.

### hierarchy of genres
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Introduced by name and immediately unpacked: gods at the top, a bowl of fruit at the bottom, the principle stated plainly. The term itself isn't used as a jargon handle the reader is expected to remember — it's explained and then the explanation carries the argument. Good.

### history painting
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Defined inline: "grand scenes from myth, scripture, ancient history and allegory, full of the human body at heroic size." That is the clearest definition in the piece. A newcomer knows exactly what this is.

### still life
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Defined by example immediately: "a bowl of fruit, a dead pheasant, painted small." No gloss needed beyond that.

### the Salon
**Verdict: CLEAR**
First use: "The Salon was the Académie's official public exhibition, and it was the only theatre in town — the one place where a wide Paris public, hundreds of thousands of people, came each year to look at, argue over and actually buy new art." Strong. The word "Salon" could theoretically confure a newcomer (they know it as a hair salon), but "official public exhibition" disambiguates it instantly.

### "on the line"
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Defined immediately: "The prime band, at eye level, was called being 'on the line' — seen, studied, reviewed, sold." Clear and vivid.

### "skied"
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Defined immediately: "A picture hung up near the rafters was said to be 'skied,' and a skied canvas was, for all practical purposes, an invisible one." No problem.

### daguerreotype
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Introduced with a solid inline definition: "the first practical photograph, a hard, perfect image fixed on a silvered copper plate." A newcomer knows what a photograph is; this tells them the daguerreotype was the first one. Clean.

### plein-air
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Defined in parentheses immediately after first use: "working outdoors, in front of the subject, in the open air." Belt-and-suspenders thorough, in fact. No problem.

### avant-garde
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Defined at the close: "a military term, the scouts who go out in front of the main army, borrowed for the artists who got somewhere first." Good. The definition is earned — it lands at the moment the word is needed.

### "imperial collection"
**Verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (SHOULD-FIX)**
The phrase appears in the sentence "bought for the imperial collection by the Emperor, Napoleon III." The concept of an "imperial collection" — that the Emperor of France ran a state art collection — is implicit, not explained. A newcomer knows what an Emperor is, and can infer that he owns stuff, but "imperial collection" reads as a slightly formal phrase for "his art collection." This isn't a comprehension blocker; the point (the Emperor bought it) is clear. SHOULD-FIX: "bought for his personal collection by the Emperor" or "bought for the state art collection" — though the current text actually works fine as a status signal. Lowest priority on this list.

## Person-by-person verdict

### André Félibien
**Verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (MUST-FIX)**
Introduced as "the writer André Félibien" — but "writer" is thin for a name that's carrying a fairly significant claim (he formalised the hierarchy of genres in 1667 and it ran for two centuries). A newcomer reads "the writer André Félibien" and has no foothold: a novelist? A journalist? A pamphleteer? The one-liner they need is something like "a French art critic and court historian" or "a royal court historian who wrote the era's most influential theory of art." Not having it doesn't break comprehension of the hierarchy argument, but it does leave a named person floating with no identity — which is exactly the gate's test. MUST-FIX: add a single clause.

### Alexandre Cabanel
**Verdict: CLEAR (BORDERLINE SHOULD-FIX)**
The draft does NOT give Cabanel a who-they-are intro before describing his painting. It jumps straight to "Stand in front of Alexandre Cabanel's *The Birth of Venus*." A newcomer has no idea who Cabanel is. The argument is made using the painting, not the man — so the painting's role in the Salon system is explained — but Cabanel himself is never tagged. One clause on first use ("a French academic painter" or "the Salon's reigning master") would ground him. Strictly speaking the section works without it — the painting is what matters, not the man's biography — but his name is used three times, including "Cabanel was made a professor," and a newcomer might well wonder: who is this? SHOULD-FIX.

### Napoleon III
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Named as "the Emperor, Napoleon III." A 15-year-old reader knows "Emperor" means ruler of a country. The reference is brief and functional — he bought a painting. No further identification needed here.

### John Goffe Rand
**Verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (SHOULD-FIX)**
Introduced as "an American painter in London" — that is actually a reasonable one-liner for the purpose. He's a minor figure here; what he *did* (patented the paint tube) is what matters. "An American painter in London" is slightly confusing (why is an American painter in London?) but not a blocker. The real gap is that newcomers might not understand why a painter patenting a paint tube is worth knowing: the draft explains this — "for the first time let a painter carry his colours out the door" — so the so-what is clear. This is fine as is; SHOULD-FIX only if you want to drop "in London" and just say "an American inventor-painter."

### Baron Haussmann
**Verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (MUST-FIX)**
"Under Napoleon III, the prefect Baron Haussmann drove wide new boulevards through the medieval tangle." The word "prefect" is unexplained jargon. A sharp 15-year-old in the US or UK does not know what a French "prefect" is (they know it as a school monitor, not a city administrator). The phrase "Baron Haussmann drove wide new boulevards" lands well enough that the argument survives, but Haussmann is introduced as a titled name + a job title neither of which are explained. Minimum fix: "Napoleon III's chief city-planner, Baron Haussmann" — or drop "prefect" entirely since his title matters less than his function. MUST-FIX: name + function.

## Unexplained leaps and stumble-points

### "a single narrow staircase with the State standing at the top of it"
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Strong metaphor, immediately unpacked. No stumble.

### "the only theatre in town"
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Metaphorical, but the sentence that follows explains exactly what it means (the one place Paris came to see art). Clear.

### "The hang was the verdict before the verdict"
**Verdict: CLEAR**
Epigrammatic, but the preceding paragraph has explained everything needed to understand it. A sharp 15-year-old will get it.

### "Its home kept moving in these years (it had outgrown the Louvre by 1848 and used various halls through the early 1850s before settling, from 1857, into the cavernous iron-and-glass Palais de l'Industrie)"
**Verdict: NEEDS-GLOSS (SHOULD-FIX)**
"Iron-and-glass Palais de l'Industrie" lands fine as a physical description. But a newcomer may briefly stumble on "outgrown the Louvre" — they know the Louvre is a famous art museum but may not know it was the *original* home of the Salon. That said, this is a parenthetical aside, not load-bearing. SHOULD-FIX: "outgrown its original home in the Louvre" — one word.

### "The principle was blunt: gods at the top, a bowl of fruit at the bottom, with the more ordinary subjects strung out in between."
**Verdict: CLEAR**
"More ordinary subjects strung out in between" covers portrait, landscape, genre without naming them. A newcomer gets the gist. Fine.

### "if only painting modern life had been allowed"
**Verdict: CLEAR**
The irony lands clearly given what precedes it (the hierarchy of genres, the preference for gods over greengrocers). A 15-year-old reading carefully will catch it.

### The "Meanwhile" card — "A new buyer changes who needs the jury."
**Verdict: CLEAR**
The Meanwhile body (private dealer, rising middle class, smaller pictures) covers the point, but "the faint outline of a way to make a living as a painter without ever winning the jury's medal" slightly repeats the main body's closing paragraphs. Not a clarity issue — duplication, which is a different gate.

> Problem: "writer" is a placeholder. A newcomer has no idea who this is or why his 1667 opinion ran the show for two centuries.
> Fix: one clause — e.g. "the royal court historian André Félibien" or "the French art theorist André Félibien, whose 1667 ranking…"

> Fix: replace "the prefect" with a plain function — "Napoleon III's chief city-planner, Baron Haussmann" — or drop "prefect" and just use his name + the boulevard detail, which is self-explanatory.
