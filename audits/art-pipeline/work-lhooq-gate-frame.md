### [FIX] C-1 — Missing precedent: Sapeck / the Incoherents (1883/1887)
The "before/after" break section asserts the gesture *de novo*: "Before Duchamp drew on it, here is what a masterpiece *was*… To honor it, you kept your hands off." But satirical defacement of the *Mona Lisa* is documented as predating Duchamp by a generation — **Sapeck (Eugène Bataille), *Le Rire* (Mona Lisa smoking a pipe), exhibited with the Incoherents in Paris, 1883/1887.** This is noted *on the L.H.O.O.Q. Wikipedia page itself*: "The subject of the *Mona Lisa* treated satirically had already been explored in 1887 by Eugène Bataille (aka Sapeck)… It is not clear whether Duchamp was familiar with Bataille's interpretation." Omitting it makes the draft read as if Duchamp invented Mona-Lisa-baiting, which is a comprehensiveness gap AND a fairness overclaim (see F-1, same item).

**Suggested text** — add one sentence to `LhoPostcard`, after the *Jocondisme* paragraph ("…the thing you made a pilgrimage to and revered."):

> Duchamp was not even the first to tweak her: back in 1883, at a Paris show by a group of pranksters called the Incoherents, the artist Sapeck (Eugène Bataille) had printed a *Mona Lisa* smoking a pipe. Whether Duchamp knew that picture isn't recorded. What he added was the thing that made it stick: a pun and a claim that the defaced card was, itself, his art.

This keeps Duchamp's actual originality (the *readymade* claim, not the doodle) while puncturing the lone-inventor frame.

### [NICE] C-2 — *L.H.O.O.Q. Shaved* edition year
The draft and fact pack both say "1965" for *Shaved*; some catalog records date the *rasée* edition/invitation 1965, which is fine. No change needed, but if a date is ever questioned, the safe phrasing is already in the draft ("issued in 1965"). Leave as is.

### [NICE] C-3 — Rrose Sélavy dating precision
The draft says Duchamp was inventing Rrose "around exactly this period" and "across the same few years." Strictly, Rrose first surfaces 1920 (the name) / 1921 (the doubled "Rrose," the Man Ray drag photos) — i.e. just *after* the 1919 card, not concurrent with it. The draft's "around this period / same few years" is loose enough to be defensible and the fact pack flags "Rrose first appears 1920–21." No fix required, but the cleanest truthful framing is "the very next year," not "exactly this period." Optional tightening of `LhoMaking`:

> Around this same moment — Rrose surfaces the very next year — Duchamp was inventing a **female alter ego** for himself…

## 2. FRAMING / FAIRNESS

### genius-myth — GOOD, one residual gap
The draft mostly avoids lone-genius inflation: it situates the work in Paris Dada (names Tzara, Breton, Picabia), frames the gesture as "exactly the right kind of joke, in that company," and credits the readymade lineage to *Fountain*. It does NOT call Duchamp a genius or claim he single-handedly invented modern provocation. The one residual gap is C-1/F-1 (the Sapeck precedent) — addressing that fully discharges the genius-myth concern.

### first/only overclaim — WELL SCOPED, hold the line
The draft is disciplined here and should be praised: it writes "widely cited as an early seed of **appropriation art**," "one of the first proofs that the source image can *be* the medium," and "an early seed" — never a flat "the first appropriation work." That matches the record (Wikipedia does not call it the first; standard accounts call it *a* seminal early instance). **No change to the appropriation claims** — they are correctly hedged. Do not let a reviser "strengthen" them into a "first."

### [FIX] F-1 — "Defacing the most revered painting on earth" section title implies a first
The `LhoBreak` section title is "**Defacing the most revered painting on earth**," and the before/after structure implies Duchamp originated the act of defacing it. Combined with the absence of the Sapeck precedent (C-1), a reader takes away "Duchamp was the first to deface the Mona Lisa," which is false. The fix is the same one-sentence Sapeck insertion in C-1 plus a one-clause hedge inside `LhoBreak`. **Suggested addition** to the `LhoBreak` "after" paragraph, in the appropriation rupture (after "…recontextualizes it, and signs the result as his own work."):

> Mona-Lisa-baiting itself was older than Duchamp — the Incoherents had done a pipe-smoking version in the 1880s. What was new was the *claim*: that the altered card, signed and titled, was a finished artwork, not a cartoon.

This preserves the genuine rupture (the readymade claim) while denying the false "first to deface" reading.

### fairness — pun vulgarity: GOOD
The pun's vulgarity is handled as content, not as a gratuitous shock. The draft gives the literal "She has a hot ass" / "hot in the arse" plainly, explains *cul* is "coarse French for the backside," and then correctly distinguishes Duchamp's own polite "fire down below" as a *gloss*, not a translation — exactly the two-registers framing the fact pack demands. It does not leer or repeat the obscenity for effect. No change.

### fairness — gender reading as documented intent vs. later interpretation: GOOD
This is handled with real care. The "it becomes a man" quote is presented as **Duchamp's own documented words** (sourced to the Sweeney NBC interview, 1955/56, reprinted in *The Writings of Marcel Duchamp*), and the draft explicitly defends the verbatim "**not**" against the widespread mis-citation that inverts it. The draft does *not* over-read the gender politics into a 21st-century thesis Duchamp didn't hold; it stays on Duchamp's stated "discovery." The Rrose connection is presented as a parallel game, not as proof of an authorial gender ideology. This is the correct intent-vs-interpretation discipline. No change.

### [NICE] F-2 — "almost everyone gets it wrong" / "careless quoters" tone
Twice the draft scolds the reader's likely error ("because almost everyone gets it wrong," "careless quoters mangle it," "the rule to carry," "Quote the line with the 'not'"). This is accurate and useful but tips slightly into the commanding/instructional register the house voice tries to avoid (cf. philosophy-voice "no commanding" note; same spirit applies app-wide). Not a fairness defect, purely tone. Optional softening: keep ONE of these correction-flags (the "not" one is the most load-bearing) and convert the others to plain statement, e.g. change "the single most important fact about the object, because almost everyone gets it wrong:" → "the fact the object is most often misremembered for:". Leave the substance intact.

### proportion / anachronism — CLEAN
- No anachronisms. The 1883 Incoherents, 1911 theft / 1913 recovery, 1919 making, 1920 *391*, 1942 goatee correction, 1965 *Shaved* are all correctly dated in the draft (and the draft explicitly polices the 1919-vs-1920 date slide).
- The naming of **Sherrie Levine and Elaine Sturtevant** as appropriation-art descendants is proportionate and accurate, and correctly placed as "later artists," not contemporaries.
- Proportion across sections is good: the afterlife/versions material (the genuine difficulty) gets the most room, the pun gets its own section, the break is tight. No facet is over- or under-weighted.

## SUMMARY OF FINDINGS

- **[FIX] C-1 / F-1** — Add the Sapeck / Incoherents (1883/1887) *Mona Lisa* precedent (one sentence in `LhoPostcard`, one clause in `LhoBreak`). Without it the draft implies Duchamp was the first to deface the *Mona Lisa* — false, and contradicted by the work's own Wikipedia page. This is the only fairness-material fix.
- **[NICE] C-3 / F-2** — Tighten Rrose dating to "the very next year"; soften the doubled "everyone gets it wrong" scolding to one instance.
- **HOLD THE LINE:** the appropriation-art claims are correctly hedged ("early seed," "one of the first"); the pun-vulgarity handling and the gender-as-documented-intent handling are both right. A reviser must NOT inflate these into "first appropriation work" or into a modern gender thesis.

No BLOCKERS. Apply C-1/F-1, optionally C-3/F-2, and the work passes this gate.
