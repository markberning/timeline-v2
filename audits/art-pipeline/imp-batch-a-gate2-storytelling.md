# Gate 2 — Storytelling & "Looking" Critique
## Impressionism Batch A: La Grenouillère · Bal du moulin de la Galette · Paris Street; Rainy Day · The Cradle

Critic pass. Judges each work as story AND as art writing. Voice benchmark:
`sunrise-work-final.md`. Grades: STRONG / GOOD / NEEDS WORK / REWRITE.

---

## Batch-wide top 5 findings (ranked by impact across all 4 works)

**#1 MUST-FIX — "looking" chapters bury the canvas behind context paragraphs.**
In Grenouillère, Moulin de la Galette, and Paris Street the description is solid,
but in every case the chapter opens with a sentence or two about *what the reader is
about to do* before actually showing them anything. The benchmark (`the-morning`)
opens: "Start with the canvas, because it is gloriously little." That's a move —
it physically puts the reader at the canvas and immediately starts pointing. Three of
the four drafts do less. They warm up with methodological throat-clearing ("Now look
at the canvas itself, methodically," "Stand in front of the picture and the first
thing you notice…") and then get going. Cut the runway; open mid-canvas.

**#2 MUST-FIX — Vague praise standing in for description: "striking", "luminous",
"masterful."**
Every work has at least one place where the prose calls something "brilliant" or
"beautiful" or "masterful" instead of showing the reader what makes it so. The
benchmark never says a passage is "beautiful"; it says "the water is brushed in
quick, loose horizontal strokes, wet and slithery, no attempt to render individual
waves." Specific beats vague every time. Locations flagged per work below.

**#3 MUST-FIX — Dry wit is present but kept on a short leash. Earned wry lines get
one sentence when they could breathe for two.**
The benchmark has earned throwaways that land because they don't rush away from
themselves: "He thinks the Met's picture is a scratch." (Grenouillère) or "Leroy got
his immortality — as the man who tried to bury Impressionism and accidentally
christened it." Both have one beat of comic timing built in. In these drafts the wry
line almost always comes at the end of a paragraph and is immediately followed by
the next factual point, collapsing the joke. Specific locations below.

**#4 SHOULD-FIX — Analogies thin out exactly when the reader needs them most: at
complex technique moments.**
The benchmark's two-crew equiluminance analogy is the model — a hard science concept
made vivid by comparing the visual system to two work crews who don't talk. In these
drafts the technique chapters (broken color, dappled light, one-point perspective,
the gauze passage) explain correctly but don't reach for the analogy that would make
the mechanism *felt*, not just understood. Locations flagged below.

**#5 SHOULD-FIX — "Look closer" pointers across all four works are solid but
occasionally restate the chapter prose instead of adding a new micro-observation.**
The pointer is at its best when it sees something the prose chapter didn't zoom into —
a detail only visible when you're actively searching the canvas. Several pointers
below are paraphrases of the prose chapter's argument rather than a genuine new
instruction. These are SHOULD-FIX, not MUST-FIX, since the pointers generally work;
they're just not yet at benchmark level.

## 1. La Grenouillère

### Grades by section

| section | grade |

---

### `seine` — STRONG

The hook works. The setup is vivid and earns its length — the railway paragraph is
doing real work (it's the *mechanism* for why this setting exists), the class-texture
of the Frog Pond is satisfying, and the "two broke friends did what broke friends in
their twenties have always done: they walked" is the best dry-wit line in the batch.
The detail about multiple sessions vs. one golden morning is foregrounded, not buried.

**One SHOULD-FIX:**
The sentence "That's the whole setup of this picture" is a bit of light hedging —
stopping the momentum to announce that the setup is done. The benchmark just moves.
Consider cutting it.

---

### `making` — GOOD

The tableau/pochade opposition is clearly set up and the Bazille letter quotation lands
well. The "tiny research lab" line earns its place. The broken-color mechanism is
explained correctly.

**MUST-FIX:**
> "Monet would put down a stroke; Renoir would try one of his own; one would push
> the pure-color side, the other the soft-pearl side."

This is the closest the chapter gets to the actual *paint behavior* — but it's told
at one remove. What does "the pure-color side" look like on canvas? What does "the
soft-pearl side" look like? The benchmark would name a color and a surface: "Monet
lays a jab of slate blue; Renoir drags a softer, more pearl-gray smear next to it."
The reader can picture a jab of slate blue. They can't picture "the pure-color side."

**MUST-FIX — vague praise:**
> "That is the discovery, and it was found at this exact spot."

"The discovery" is fine, but "found at this exact spot" is the kind of italicized
flourish that tells the reader to feel something rather than showing them what was
found. The preceding sentence already makes the discovery concrete (paint that reads
as moving water). Cut the period-ending declaration; let the mechanism land without
the fanfare.

**SHOULD-FIX — analogy opportunity missed:**
The broken-color section explains the method accurately but doesn't reach for the
analogy that would make it vivid. The benchmark uses comparisons as its first tool:
the equiluminance explanation uses a two-crew analogy; the harbor description uses
"a soft gray smear rather than a crisp drawn object." Here we need a one-sentence
comparison for what unmixed color patches look like to the eye from five feet back.
Something like: *a pointillist mosaic that your eye grouts for you* — the viewer does
the mixing, same as mortar filling tile gaps. The idea of "viewer does the mixing"
is stated but never made felt.

---

### `looking` — GOOD

The visual tour is methodical and accurate. The key observations (the people-as-marks
passage, the no-mirrored-reflections observation) are the chapter's best moments.

**MUST-FIX — throat-clearing opening:**
> "The painting itself is small for what it does — about two and a half feet tall and
> a little over three feet wide (the museum's catalogue number is 74.6 by 99.7
> centimeters, the source-of-truth measurement; in plain feet and inches that's
> roughly **2 ft 5½ in × 3 ft 3¼ in**). It is oil on canvas, signed and dated lower
> right *Claude Monet 69*. The view looks across a wide, slack stretch of the Seine
> on a hot summer afternoon."

This is three sentences of metadata before the reader sees anything. The benchmark
opens `the-morning` with "Start with the canvas, because it is gloriously little"
and then immediately: "The water and the sky take up almost the whole surface…" That
move puts the reader at the canvas before the dimensions are even mentioned. Suggested
fix: lead with the first visible thing (the round island, dead center, in a fat-hat
of summer trees), then drop the dimensions in a parenthetical in the second sentence.

**MUST-FIX — vague praise:**
> "Now to the part of the canvas where the picture actually lives — the water itself."

"Where the picture actually lives" tells the reader how to feel about what follows. Cut
it; just open on the water. The water description is strong enough to make its own case.

**SHOULD-FIX:**
> "The picture practically rattles with the sound of a crowded riverbank."

"Rattles" is vivid and earns its place — BUT the sentence is the last sentence of
the chapter, which means it closes on an auditory metaphor when we've been in a
visual mode throughout. It's a surprising move but slightly off-key as a closing
note for a chapter called "What is on the canvas." Consider moving this image to
earlier in the chapter (perhaps after the trees paragraph) and closing on a more
visual observation.

---

### `break` — STRONG

The four-part structure is clean. The Baudelaire/Manet thread is well-handled. The
refusal of "Impressionism was invented here" is explicit, confident, and exactly right.

**One SHOULD-FIX:**
> "Painting could now *do* the riverbank in a way no smooth-blended tableau ever had."

"Could now *do*" is a bit gestural. The sentence would hit harder if it named the
specific thing painting could now do: *show moving water as moving water, not as a
description of what the mind knows water is.* That turns "could do" into a claim with
a specific referent.

---

### `afterlife` — STRONG

The three-way denouement (lost Berlin tableau / Stockholm Renoir / Met pochade) is
well-paced and the closing line — "The pochade kept its strokes visible. It's still
moving." — is the best closing sentence in the batch. The Havemeyer story is well-
handled and the Louisine detail (buying Impressionists in her twenties when they were
nobodies) gives her a shape.

**One SHOULD-FIX:**
> "That's the whole pivot of this picture's status."

This is the second time in the draft that a clause announces an emotional shift rather
than just making it. The Bazille letter quotation that follows is so good it doesn't
need the announcement. Cut "That's the whole pivot of this picture's status" and
open directly with the quotation.

## 2. Bal du moulin de la Galette

### Grades by section

| section | grade |
|---------|-------|

| `making` | STRONG |
| `looking` | GOOD |
| `show` | GOOD |

---

### `montmartre` — STRONG

The opening panorama earns every word. The leisure-history paragraph (railways +
working-day change) is exactly the kind of mechanism-first explanation the benchmark
demands, and it lands because it explains *why* this world was new, not just that it
was. The Renoir biography lands economically. "Don't write him in your head as the
celebrated Renoir" is a perfect little lean on the fourth wall.

**One SHOULD-FIX:**
> "That picture is the one we're about to look at."

Standard forward-pointer; the benchmark would just trust the reader to know we're
going there. Cut it and let the next sentence ("It is over five feet wide…") speak
for itself.

---

### `making` — STRONG

The history-painting scale argument is well made and lands as a real provocation. The
dappled-light passage is the best technical explanation in the batch: it names the
physical phenomenon (discrete round patches, edges soft), explains the technique
(discrete dabs of warm pink, lilac, pale violet), and clinches it with a specific
canvas moment (the pink-violet patch on the man's back). This is how the benchmark
does it.

**MUST-FIX — vague praise:**
> "The 35-year-old broke painter is going to put the windmill, the lanterns, the
> pink-and-blue dresses, the dappled afternoon sun, and the whole new Sunday of the
> new city on a canvas the size that the Salon reserved for the deaths of kings."

"The whole new Sunday of the new city" is a strong phrase but it comes at the end of a
list and it's doing the job of three concrete details it isn't yet giving. What *is*
the whole new Sunday? The chapter actually tells us: the railways, the free afternoon,
the clerks and shopgirls in their best clothes under a windmill. If you want the
rhetorical line to land, anchor it to a single sharp image rather than an abstraction:
"…and put the clerks in their Sunday clothes under the windmill on a six-foot canvas"
has the same payload without the abstraction.

**SHOULD-FIX — wit restrained:**
> "He signed it lower right: *Renoir. 76.*"

This is a clean, dry full-stop for the making chapter, and it's good. But the
benchmark would probably hold on it one beat longer — something like: "He signed it
lower right: *Renoir. 76.* Then he had to figure out how to carry a six-foot canvas
down a Montmartre hill." The wit in this draft is tasteful and well-controlled; it
could be let off the leash one more time without overplaying.

---

### `looking` — GOOD

The macro-to-micro structure is genuinely useful for a canvas this complicated, and
the "sharp corner → dissolving crowd → canopy" reading is insightful. The pink-and-
blue striped-dresses rhyme observation is excellent.

**MUST-FIX — opening throat-clearing:**
> "Now look at the canvas itself, methodically, because the picture is busy and a
> casual glance reads it as a happy blur. Slow down."

The instruction to slow down tells the reader they've been moving too fast, which
risks feeling like a scolding. The benchmark opens mid-canvas. Here, the structural
anchor is the dancing couple at dead center; lead with them: "Find the couple dancing
almost dead-center, just behind the seated friends — a woman in pale pink, back
half-turned, her partner facing her." Then give the methodology. The reader slows
down when you show them something worth slowing down for.

**MUST-FIX — missed visual specificity:**
> "Around them, fanning out into the middle distance, is the **sea of dancers** — a
> crowd of couples, painted as soft blurs with no facial detail, a wash of bowler hats
> and pale dresses receding into the garden."

"A wash of bowler hats and pale dresses" is accurate but not yet vivid. The benchmark
would identify *one* specific hat, *one* specific streak of color at the center of the
crowd that the reader can find and use as an anchor. Something like: "…bowler hats and
pale dresses dissolving backward, with two or three of the women's dresses catching the
strongest, freshest pink-and-blue strokes in the whole picture — coins of saturated
color floating in the warm haze." (This observation is actually *already* in the fact
pack and in the next sentence; it's been split from its most useful location.)

**SHOULD-FIX:**
> "Six feet of Sunday afternoon, organized like a piece of music."

Strong closing simile. It would be even stronger if it named *which part* of the
music — "organized like a piece of music that opens on one face and ends in pure
atmosphere" gives the reader a map of the arc, not just the feeling of it.

---

### `show` — GOOD

The Caillebotte/commission correction is well done and the stakes of the 1879 purchase
are clearly drawn. The Rivière cameo (the man in the painting defending the painting
in print) is handled nicely.

**MUST-FIX — vague praise:**
> "Renoir's first decision, before he ever loaded a brush, was to take history-painting
> scale and aim it at a dance."

Wait — this sentence is in `making`, not `show`. In `show`, the equivalent lapse is:

> "The split — half scorn, half delight — was the standard temperature of any
> Impressionist work in the 1870s, and *Bal du moulin de la Galette* sat near the
> center of the year's argument."

"Sat near the center of the year's argument" is a good phrase. But the chapter could
use one specific critical voice (hostile OR admiring) to give that split a face.
Rivière's praise is named in general terms; can a specific phrase from his piece be
quoted or closely paraphrased? Even one line of what the hostile critics said about
the sunspots would make the argument felt. The draft notes that critics found the
dabs "plain wrong" — that's the right observation but it's summarized rather than
quoted. If the fact pack has a specific hostile phrase, use it; if not, flag it for
the fact-check pass.

**SHOULD-FIX:**
> "From there — but here we get ahead of ourselves."

This is a narrative-pause device and it works here in a mild way, but the benchmark
never uses false cliffhangers. The chapter could just end: "The 1879 purchase is the
hinge. The next chapter opens it." More confident.

---

### `afterlife` — STRONG

The institutional journey (Luxembourg → Louvre → Jeu de Paume → d'Orsay) is well
paced. The closing arc sentence is excellent: "with its pink-violet sunspots and its
two pink-and-blue striped dresses and its lanterns waiting to be lit, on a Sunday
afternoon that has never quite ended." That earns the bench-mark comparison.

**One SHOULD-FIX:**
> "It didn't stay at the Louvre forever."

Mild tease before a short institutional beat; the sentence could be cut and the
Jeu de Paume sentence can open the paragraph cold. Saves a slightly telegraphed
transition.

## 3. Paris Street; Rainy Day

### Grades by section

| section | grade |
|---------|-------|
| `haussmann` | STRONG |
| `making` | STRONG |
| `looking` | STRONG |
| `show` | GOOD |

---

### `haussmann` — STRONG

The best context chapter in the batch. "A city as a single coordinated artwork —
beautiful in a faintly tyrannical way, because *one man* made all these decisions.
Which is exactly what people hated about it at the time, and exactly why it still
photographs so well." — this is the voice benchmark hit in this batch: dry wit fully
extended, self-contained, allowed to land before the next sentence arrives. The wedge-
building setup is clean and the payoff ("You can stand on the Place de Dublin today
and see it") earns its place.

**SHOULD-FIX:**
The closing sentence — "Approvingly." as a one-word paragraph — is a tonal choice
that works, but the tone is already well-established in the chapter without the coda.
It tips from confident into slightly affected. Consider cutting it or absorbing it
into the preceding sentence.

---

### `making` — STRONG

The engineer-painter characterization is precise and satisfying: "call him an
*engineer-painter who could afford to paint without selling*" is the kind of short
formulation the benchmark uses for recurring concepts. The one-point perspective
explanation is unusually clear — "the curbs of a straight boulevard: in real life
they're parallel, but on a flat picture they appear to converge on one spot" — and
the comparison to a railway viaduct makes Caillebotte's method felt, not just explained.

**MUST-FIX — vague praise:**
> "And the paint surface itself is *cool*. This is the headline difference between
> Caillebotte and the painters hanging in the same rooms with him."

"Cool" is doing triple duty here (temperature, emotional register, paint handling) and
the sentence announces it as a headline without yet showing the reader what cool
paint looks like differently from warm paint. The next sentence does give an example
("the cobblestones are individual cobblestones, painted one by one"), but the sequence
would be stronger if the cobblestones came first and *cool* came as a conclusion:
"Each cobblestone painted individually. Every top hat edge sharp. The wet shine
rendered, not suggested. Caillebotte's surface is *cool* — smooth, nearly
invisible at viewing distance — against a room full of Monets that rattle with
visible brushwork." Lead with the specific canvas evidence, name the quality second.

**SHOULD-FIX:**
> "Which is going to be a problem in April. Hold that thought."

This is a teaser device and it's mildly effective, but the benchmark avoids this
form of forward-pointer (it trusts the narrative to carry the reader). A better close
might name the paradox directly: "The most Impressionist subject, painted with the
most un-Impressionist hand. Caillebotte is twenty-eight and has just put that
contradiction on a nine-foot canvas for the most argued-about exhibition of the year."

---

### `looking` — STRONG

The best "looking" chapter in the batch. The lamppost-as-plumb-line observation is
excellent, the couple's description is precise and grounded (the pearl earring, the
fur trim, "the slightly bored, slightly far-away expression"), and the cropped-man
paragraph is vivid and historically accurate. The palette-no-warm-color closing
observation is the kind of subtractive trick the benchmark deploys.

**SHOULD-FIX — one missed micro-beat:**
> "The umbrellas, accordingly, are not protecting against rain in progress — they're
> protecting against the dampness of the sky and probably the threat of more rain
> shortly."

This is an astute observation — but the chapter has already told us the storm has
passed. What would make it land harder is the micro-detail that *proves* it: the
absence of cast shadows already noted, plus the specific fact that the pavement is
wet but no running water is visible. The observation is right but slightly underbuilt.
Add the physical evidence: "Look at the pavement — wet, but no runnels of water, no
puddle reflecting sky. The rain has stopped. The umbrellas are an insurance policy,
not active gear."

---

### `show` — GOOD

The paradox of the "least Impressionist picture in the most Impressionist show" is
well articulated, and the juxtaposition with Moulin de la Galette hanging in the
same room is sharply drawn. The false-Salon-attribution correction is handled
efficiently.

**MUST-FIX — vague reception:**
> "The reviews were mixed, in the way reviews of Caillebotte tended to be his whole
> career."

This is honest — the fact pack doesn't preserve specific review quotes — but the
sentence reads as a shrug. The draft already acknowledges that "some critics were
intrigued, some were baffled." Use that intrigued/baffled split to make the moment
feel like something was at stake. "Intrigued" and "baffled" are still quite vague;
what specifically would have intrigued or baffled a viewer in 1877? The preceding
paragraph tells us: the picture was the most geometrically rigorous thing on the wall,
the only one that referenced photography, the only one that had refused warm color.
Those were the *specific* things that would have intrigued or confused. Push one step
further into the probable reaction.

**SHOULD-FIX:**
> "He was twenty-eight years old. He had hung the largest and the most thoroughly
> designed picture in the show."

This is a good strong close to the setup paragraph, but it leaves out the killer
detail: Caillebotte also *organized* the show and *paid for* it. Add "The man who
organized the exhibition and wrote checks to make it happen had also put his own
canvas dead center." That detail adds the self-possession that makes the twenty-eight-
year-old land as a force of character rather than just a youngster.

---

### `afterlife` — STRONG

The opening separation of "Caillebotte-bequest pictures" from "Caillebotte's own
work" is exactly right to lead with — this is the most common reader confusion and
the draft handles it cleanly. The fifty-six years of private hands is well paced.
The closing historical-irony paragraph ("Caillebotte's bequest is in Paris. His own
great picture of Paris is in Chicago") is strong.

**One SHOULD-FIX:**
> "An American industrialist with a habit of swallowing collections buys it."

"Swallowing collections" is a nice line but it's a qualifier on Chrysler rather than
a fact about the picture. The sentence works better as: "In 1954 an American
industrialist — **Walter P. Chrysler Jr.**, son of the automobile magnate — bought
it out of the family for the first time." Then add the characterization separately if
you want to keep the flavor, rather than folding the flavor into the factual record.

## 4. The Cradle (Le Berceau)

### Grades by section

| section | grade |
|---------|-------|
| `sister` | STRONG |
| `making` | STRONG |
| `show` | GOOD |
| `looking` | NEEDS WORK |
| `afterlife` | GOOD |

---

### `sister` — STRONG

This is the best chapter-one in the batch. The voice is controlled, the stakes are
real ("Edma was, in the eyes of several of the people who saw them at work, **the more
naturally talented of the two**" is delivered without sentimentality and hits harder
for it), and the closing two-sentence summary — "One sister, with brushes in her hand,
painting the other sister, who put hers down" — is the benchmark for this kind of
clean structural declaration. The correction of the "Berthe and her baby" standard
error is foregrounded cleanly without becoming a lecture.

**One SHOULD-FIX:**
> "Read that sentence again, because the standard cradle blurb gets it wrong almost
> every time."

The instruction "Read that sentence again" is the right instinct — the correction
matters — but in practice on a phone it's a slightly awkward move. The reader has
already read it once; what they need is not to re-read but to *understand why it
matters that it's Edma*. The following sentence does that perfectly: "The seated woman
in *The Cradle* is **not Berthe Morisot.** It is her sister **Edma Pontillon**." Consider
cutting "Read that sentence again" and trusting the bolded NOT/It-is structure to land.
The correction is clear enough without the prompt.

---

### `making` — STRONG

The wall-of-access passage is the stand-out writing in the batch. The specific list of
places Morisot could not go — café, racetrack, Folies-Bergère, brothel, laundry,
Seine-side dock — is exactly what the benchmark asks for: not a claim ("she faced
restrictions"), but an itemized physical exclusion that makes the constraint felt as
a space with walls around it. "It is not a gentle preference for the domestic. It is
a hard outer ring of places a respectable woman of Morisot's class was not permitted
to be, and therefore not permitted to see, and therefore not permitted to paint." is
the sharpest single sequence of analysis in the batch.

**MUST-FIX — the wall-of-access landing:**
This is the craft-test the brief calls out specifically: does the chapter land the
wall-of-access as a *wall*, not a "gentle preference"? It does. The brief's concern
is satisfied. The making chapter passes.

**SHOULD-FIX — analogy opportunity:**
> "She painted the interior with the same modern eye Monet was bringing to the harbor
> — the same speed of brushwork, the same attention to how light actually moves
> through a real room at a real hour, the same refusal to over-finish…"

This comparison to Monet is doing good work, but it's at the level of technique
abstract ("speed of brushwork") rather than a canvas-to-canvas image. The benchmark
would give this comparison a specific visual: where Monet is building the harbor in
"dense, choppy layers of gray and orange," Morisot is working in *opposite conditions*
— filtered light through window glass, a curtain between the painter and the subject.
The draft says this ("sunlight through a sheer curtain") but doesn't make it the
hinge of the comparison. Consider restructuring so the Monet/harbor image is one
specific brushstroke (gray and orange jabs on a harbor), and the Morisot image is one
specific brushstroke (a few thin translucent passes of oyster-white on a curtain).
Then the comparison between them is a visual comparison, not an abstract method one.

---

### `looking` — NEEDS WORK

This is the craft test chapter for this work, and most of it is very good. The gauze
description is where the work succeeds most distinctly. But there are two places where
the prose tells the reader how to feel about what they are seeing rather than making
them see it, and both are MUST-FIX.

**MUST-FIX #1 — the gauze description: does it let the reader SEE the netting?**

The brief's specific test: "does the gauze description actually let the reader see
the netting?" The answer is mostly yes, but one sentence undercuts it:

> "Look at how it is *painted*. The gauze is not described. It is not drawn. It is
> sketched in with what looks, on close inspection, like a handful of rapid,
> translucent strokes — a few sweeping passes of pale oyster-white paint, dragged thin
> enough that in places the warmer ground color underneath shows through."

"What looks, on close inspection, like a handful of rapid, translucent strokes" is
hedged in a way that weakens the image. It's as if the author isn't sure of the
observation and is pre-apologizing. Drop the hedge and commit to the image: "Look
closely: a handful of rapid translucent strokes — pale oyster-white, dragged thin
enough that the warmer ground underneath glimmers through in places. That's all that's
there." The uncertainty about whether the passage is *alla prima* is correctly hedged
later; the description of the strokes themselves doesn't need a hedge.

The oscillation passage ("Step closer and the strokes come back to being strokes. Step
back and they're gauze again.") is excellent and should not change.

**MUST-FIX #2 — the "miraculously" problem:**

> "Read at arm's length, those few thin strokes resolve, almost miraculously, into
> **fabric**…"

"Almost miraculously" is exactly the kind of vague praise the brief asks you to flag.
It tells the reader to be impressed without earning the impression. The benchmark
never says a thing is miraculous; it says exactly *why* something seems that way. Here
the mechanism is already explained in the next sentence (the oscillation). Remove
"almost miraculously" and let the explanation do the work: "…resolve into **fabric** —
into the unmistakable softness of real gauze hanging in folds."

**MUST-FIX #3 — the "that single passage" claim:**

> "That single passage — that translucent glimpse — is what every serious painter who
> looked at this canvas in 1874 noticed first."

"Every serious painter" is both too sweeping and too vague. Is this documented in the
fact pack? The correct framing is "critics at the 1874 exhibition singled out the gauze
and the lightness" (which the `show` chapter does correctly). Either ground this in the
documented reception or cut it. As written it reads as historical inflation — the kind
of claim the gate-2 critic should flag.

**SHOULD-FIX — closing sentence:**

> "That's what's on the canvas: a woman in a dark dress watching a baby through a
> curtain of white paint, in a room with the noise turned off."

This is a beautiful single sentence and belongs at the end of the chapter. But the
sentence before it — "The next chapter is what happened when Morisot took it to Paris
and hung it on a wall" — breaks the image by forward-pointing. Trust the closer:
cut the forward-pointer and let "a woman in a dark dress watching a baby through a
curtain of white paint, in a room with the noise turned off" be the last thing.

---

### `show` — GOOD

Well-structured. The listed/not-sold correction is handled crisply and the instruction
to "refuse the conflation" is the right voice for that kind of recurring error. The
Eugène-not-Édouard correction is handled well (and by ending the chapter with it, the
prose creates a satisfying twin-correction structure: identity of subject corrected in
`sister`, identity of husband corrected in `show`).

**MUST-FIX — vague history:**
> "The reviews were a mixed bag tipping toward warm."

Same issue as Paris Street `show`. "Mixed bag tipping toward warm" is fine general
framing but does nothing for the reader who wants to know what specifically was
noticed. The very next sentence says "they noticed the gauze, they noticed the
lightness, they registered (with varying degrees of comfort) that the only woman in
the room had painted one of the most technically distinctive things on the wall."
That sentence is *more* specific than the general framing that precedes it. Flip the
order: lead with the specific observations (gauze, lightness, the only-woman-in-the-
room register), *then* summarize as "mixed bag tipping toward warm." Specific → general
hits harder than general → specific.

**SHOULD-FIX:**
> "Morisot, to her enormous credit, took none of this as a verdict on the work."

"To her enormous credit" is the author leaning on the reader's shoulder. The
benchmark does not editorialize in this way; it trusts the reader to draw the right
conclusion. Replace with: "Morisot took none of this as a verdict on the work." The
act speaks for itself.

### `afterlife` — GOOD

The re-introduction thesis ("The 1930 purchase isn't just an acquisition. It's a
reintroduction.") is the chapter's strongest sentence and a genuine critical insight.
The closing — "The sister who quit got her likeness into the Louvre. The sister who
didn't quit put it there." — is a near-perfect two-sentence closer: parallel structure,
built-in irony, no sentimentality. This is benchmark quality.

**MUST-FIX — "almost disappears" not paid off:**
> "What happened to *The Cradle* after the 1874 show is the thing nobody tells you
> about the picture, and it is the part of the story where the painting almost
> disappears."

"Almost disappears" is promised but not quite delivered — the next paragraph explains
that it went to Edma and sat in the family, which is indeed a kind of disappearance,
but the drama of the word "almost" implies a near-miss that's more urgent than "hung
in a Pontillon house." The more interesting claim is the one two paragraphs later:
for 56 years the picture was invisible to the growing scholarly and curatorial
apparatus that was building Impressionism's canon. *That* is the real near-disappearance.
Move the "almost disappears" framing closer to the canon-building observation, not to
the gift-to-Edma moment.

**SHOULD-FIX:**
> "It did not become famous because the public found it. It became famous because the
> Louvre bought it."

Strong. But "the public found it" is slightly passive. Something like: "Most iconic
Impressionist works have a continuous reception trail — critics writing, dealers
selling, collectors fighting. *The Cradle* doesn't. It went to Edma and vanished from
the trail entirely, then had to be reintroduced *as* a masterpiece by a national
museum, half a century after the fact." This gives the contrast more texture and earns
the "almost disappears" promise from the opening.

## Look Closer pointers — cross-batch assessment

**Grenouillère:** All six are solid. #5 (the water) is the strongest — "Step back and
your eye does the mixing on its own; the whole surface comes alive" is genuine looking
instruction. #1 (the Camembert) restates the prose chapter too closely; add one new
micro-observation: the *shadow* the Camembert casts on the water beneath it.

**Moulin de la Galette:** #3 (pink-violet sunspot) is the best pointer in the whole
batch — "Up close it looks like a bizarre mauve smear; from across a room it reads
instantly as a coin of afternoon sunlight falling through leaves" is exactly the
oscillation pointer format at its best. #6 (warm palette / no pure white) is good
but reads as a palette observation, not a looking instruction. Suggest opening with
a finding task: "Scan the canvas for pure white. Keep looking — there really isn't
any." Then the observation lands as a discovery rather than a statement.

**Paris Street:** #4 (wet cobblestones) and #6 (vanishing point) are excellent — both
give the reader a specific finding task and reward it. #7 (cool palette) has the same
issue as Moulin de la Galette #6: it's an observation rather than a finding task. Use
the same fix: "Look for any warm color — not a yellow, not a pink, not an orange.
There isn't one."

**The Cradle:** #2 (translucent gauze) and #3 (sleeping baby through veil) are the two
best pointers in the batch and they are sequenced correctly — find the gauze first,
then look through it for the baby. The "Step closer and they come apart into strokes
again. Step back and they're gauze again" instruction is actionable on a phone.
#5 (dark dress / white cradle) is accurate but it's an argument, not a pointer —
it tells the reader what the dress is *for* rather than what to *find*. Suggest
reframing: "Find the darkest mark on the canvas — Edma's plain dress, almost matte,
painted as a single quiet mass. Now ask why everything else reads as so much lighter:
the dress is the gravitational center that makes all the white air around it sing."
