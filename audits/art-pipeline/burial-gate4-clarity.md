# Gate 4 — Clarity / Newcomer check: A Burial at Ornans

**VERDICT: CONDITIONAL PASS.** No must-fix issues break comprehension for a total newcomer — the author defines most key terms inline and the overall logic flows clearly. Four must-fix items exist where term order or framing will lose a reader with zero prior knowledge; eight nice-to-have items would sharpen the read further.

---

## Must-Fix (breaks or risks breaking comprehension)

### MF-1 — "the academy" used before it is defined

**Phrase:** Ch 1, first mention of Courbet: *"none of the academy's polish"*

The word "academy" appears mid-sentence in a parenthetical gloss on Realism, before it has ever been explained. The next substantive definition of the academy doesn't arrive until Ch 1, section 3 (*"and the academy decided what was permitted to matter that much"*). A newcomer reading *"none of the academy's polish"* has no idea what institution this is.

**Fix:** In the Ch 1 Courbet intro, change to: *"with none of the official art establishment's polish"* — or add a one-clause gloss: *"none of the academy's polish (the Academy of Fine Arts, the French state institution that set the rules for painting and ran the Salon)"*. The fuller Salon/academy explanation in Ch 3 can then reference this briefly.

---

### MF-2 — "history painting" introduced before the genre hierarchy is explained

**Phrase:** Ch 1, section 3: *"(history painting, the prestige category, the human body at heroic scale)"*

The term "history painting" first appears inside a parenthetical in the middle of the genre-hierarchy paragraph — but that parenthetical is defining it at the same time it uses it. The problem is ordering: the sentence reads *"grand scenes from myth and scripture and ancient history (history painting, the prestige category…) at the top"* — a newcomer must parse the term while simultaneously learning the hierarchy it belongs to. This is fine and salvageable, but the gloss is incomplete for a zero-knowledge reader because it doesn't explain that "history" in this context does NOT mean "historical events" in the modern sense — it means narratives drawn from scripture, myth, and classical antiquity.

**Fix:** Extend the parenthetical: *"(history painting — the prestige category; 'history' here meant scripture, myth, and ancient legend, not the news — the human body at heroic scale)"*. Without this, a newcomer will read "grand scenes from … ancient history (history painting)" and think it's about depicting, say, the Battle of Waterloo.

---

### MF-3 — "the movement read" assumed to be familiar, used without orientation on first occurrence

**Phrase:** Ch 1, section 1: *"whose larger story the movement read tells (the man who would become the public face of Realism…)"*

The phrase "the movement read" is used as if the reader already knows what it refers to. In the production app this is a cross-link, so a first-time reader following only this work-level article will not know what "the movement read" is.

**Fix:** On first use, gloss it in plain language: *"whose larger story is told in the Realism overview (one level up in this app)"* or *"the Realism movement article above this one"*. Subsequent uses of "the movement read" or "the era overview" are fine once this anchor is in place.

---

### MF-4 — "memento mori" defined but placed mid-sentence in a way that buries the gloss

**Phrase:** Ch 2, skull section: *"a memento mori (the old artistic reminder that you, too, will die)"*

The definition is technically present, but it is tucked into a long parenthetical that follows a factual description of the bones. The structure is: *"[description of bones], dug up to make room. It is the bluntest possible memento mori (the old artistic reminder that you, too, will die) — not a symbolic figure of Death…"* A skimming reader will miss the gloss. The placement is fine; the issue is that the parenthetical gloss competes with the em-dash contrast clause that follows it, and a reader in a hurry will read the em-dash clause as the definition.

This is also a house-rule violation: the second half of the sentence uses an em-dash for a contrast (*"— not a symbolic figure of Death"*), not as a definition, which is allowed — but the proximity of the parenthetical definition and the em-dash contrast clause will cause confusion about which is the gloss.

**Fix:** Break the sentence. *"It is the bluntest possible memento mori — an old artistic convention meaning: you, too, will die, rendered here not as an allegorical scythe-carrying skeleton but as the actual bones of whoever was buried here last."* This removes the competing punctuation and makes the definition unmissable.

## Nice-to-Have (would help a newcomer; no comprehension failure without them)

### N-1 — "Franche-Comté" never glossed phonetically

**Phrase:** Ch 1: *"the Franche-Comté, the hilly region in eastern France that runs up to the Swiss border"*

The geographic gloss is good, but the French name will trip a non-French-speaking reader. Low-stakes, but a quick pronunciation hint (*"Franche-Comté (frahnsh-kohn-TAY)"*) matches the Ornans treatment nearby.

---

### N-2 — "the Salon" defined two chapters after it is first implied

**Phrase:** Ch 1, section 3: *"the one thing it forbade… the academy decided what was permitted to matter that much."* Then Ch 3: *"the Salon — the official annual State exhibition, the one show in France where a career was made or buried"*

The Salon is implied in Ch 1 (the academy controls what matters, what gets big canvases) but is not explicitly named or defined until Ch 3, the phrase *"you met it one level up"*. That parenthetical defers to the movement read rather than defining it fresh. A newcomer reading this work-level article without the movement context needs a brief inline gloss the first time the Salon is named — which is Ch 3 section 1 and the current definition there is actually fine. The issue is just that a newcomer might wonder in Chs 1–2 "where was this painting going to go?" without a hint that the Salon exists.

**Fix (minor):** In Ch 1 section 3, when describing the academy's control of scale/prestige, add: *"…the academy decided what was permitted to matter that much — and the Salon, the State's official annual art exhibition, was the single public arena where those decisions were enforced."* This primes the Salon concept so Ch 3 lands harder.

---

### N-3 — "genre painting" is mentioned implicitly but never named

The hierarchy-of-genres section covers history painting (top) and "plain modern life" (bottom) but never names the bottom rung as **genre painting**. This is unlikely to confuse a newcomer — they do not need the term to follow the argument. But the term shows up in the movement read, and a reader who just came from there will expect it. Either add a brief parenthetical (*"plain modern life — what the academy called genre painting — at the very bottom"*) or leave it as is.

---

### N-4 — "genre" used as a synonym for "category" without flagging it

**Phrase:** Ch 1, section 3: *"hierarchy of genres"* and then the same paragraph uses "prestige category" — the word "genre" is French for "kind/type" and in English is also a standalone art term. A newcomer reading fast may parse "hierarchy of genres" as "hierarchy of types/styles" (films, music, etc.) rather than "hierarchy of subject-matter categories." The hierarchy-of-genres explanation is solid, but a one-word gloss helps: *"the hierarchy of genres (subject-matter categories)"*.

---

### N-5 — "porcelain" / "poreless" comparison assumes familiarity with the academic nude style

**Phrase:** Ch 3, Offense one: *"a nude goddess sliding poreless along a wave, every surface licked smooth"*

The reader is told this is "the Salon's reigning style" but isn't told whose painting or what it's called. The draft's fact ledger notes this is a terse callback to the movement read (Cabanel's *Birth of Venus*). For a newcomer on this article alone, the image is vivid and comprehensible — it works fine without the name. Slight nice-to-have: *"…like Cabanel's famous *Birth of Venus*, the goddess-on-a-wave paintings the Salon loved…"* gives the comparison a hook.

---

### N-6 — "1848 revolution" glossed only in one clause; the political stakes aren't quite landed

**Phrase:** Ch 3, Offense three: *"1848, the revolution that threw out a king and, for a few months, put the ordinary people of France — workers, peasants, the poor — briefly at the center of their own history"*

This is clear and the movement read tells the full story. For a newcomer, "threw out a king" raises the question: which king, by what means? A tiny addition — *"1848, the February revolution that toppled King Louis-Philippe and briefly established a republic before the army crushed it"* — gives the reader the two facts needed to feel the fear the propertied classes had. Without knowing the republic was crushed, the "raw memories" framing is slightly abstract.

---

### N-7 — "Paris Commune of 1871" mentioned without any gloss

**Phrase:** Ch 5: *"the Paris Commune of 1871"*

The draft defers to the movement read (*"the movement read tells how his politics caught up with him"*). That deferral is appropriate for nesting, but "the Paris Commune of 1871" is dropped with zero gloss for a reader who just wants to understand why Courbet died in exile. A one-clause gloss here: *"the Paris Commune of 1871 — a brief revolutionary workers' government that Courbet publicly supported"* — lets the sentence carry its own weight without re-narrating.

---

### N-8 — Em-dash used as a definition marker in two places (house-rule violation)

The house rule is: **use parentheses for definitions, not em-dashes.** Two instances slip through:

1. Ch 4, Romanticism section: *"**Romanticism** was the generation of painting just before Courbet — the school the movement read describes as…"* — the em-dash here is doing the defining work. Change to a parenthetical: *"**Romanticism** (the generation of painting just before Courbet — the school that…)"* — or restructure so the em-dash is clearly a contrast, not the gloss.

2. Ch 5, Orsay: *"the **Musée d'Orsay** opened — the Paris museum dedicated to nineteenth-century art, installed in a converted former railway station on the Left Bank"* — the em-dash introduces the definition. The rule says this should be a parenthetical: *"the **Musée d'Orsay** (the Paris museum dedicated to nineteenth-century art, installed in a converted former railway station on the Left Bank) opened."*

## Summary

The draft is strong — it defines "hierarchy of genres," "history painting," "Realism," "frieze," "beadle," "memento mori," "Salon," and "Romanticism" explicitly, and the logic from offense to meaning to legacy flows without a break. Four issues require fixes: "the academy" is used a beat before it is explained (MF-1); "history painting" needs a gloss that the 'history' in the term means myth/scripture, not modern history (MF-2); "the movement read" needs a one-phrase orientation for readers who arrive without that context (MF-3); and the memento mori sentence tangles a definition with an em-dash contrast clause in a way a skimming reader will misread (MF-4). Eight nice-to-haves exist — the most useful being the Paris Commune gloss (N-7), the 1848 revolution detail (N-6), and the two em-dash-as-definition violations (N-8). No structural reordering is needed; every fix is a sentence-level patch.
