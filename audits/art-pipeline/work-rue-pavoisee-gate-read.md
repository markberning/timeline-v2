# GATE READ — Dufy, *La Rue pavoisée* (Street Decked with Flags), 1906 · WORK read

Gate: **STORYTELLING + LOOKING + CLARITY** (the combined storytelling/looking/clarity
critic in the art pipeline). Judged against the absinthe house-voice reference.
Scope: Part B prose (the five `Rue`-prefixed components). PART A const not judged here.

**Verdict: PASS, with fixes.** No BLOCKERs. The draft is story-first, the LOOKING
chapter does genuine guided looking, and the jargon is glossed for a zero-knowledge
reader. The fixes below are tightening and two real clarity gaps. The voice matches the
absinthe reference closely (scene-set, dry wit, comparison-driven, no meta-narration).

---

## 1. STORYTELLING

Strong overall. The "port painter handed his own subject" through-line carries all five
chapters; the wit lands without straining ("a regatta on land," "color came off the
leash," "add nothing," "the thing you can't make twice"); comparisons do real work
(Monet's flag streets as the recurring foil; the 14th-of-July = Fourth-of-July anchor).
No flat encyclopedia stretches of the bad kind, and no meta-narration tics ("in this
chapter," "as we'll see," "this section covers") — the draft tells the story straight.

### [FIX] Monet repeated three times across LOOKING + BREAK, nearly verbatim
The "Monet's flags melt into atmosphere / Dufy holds them as flat shapes you could cut
out with scissors" beat appears in the LOOKING chapter (`RueLooking`, final two
paragraphs) AND again in the BREAK chapter (`RueBreak`, third paragraph), each time
making the same point with the same "atmosphere → flat planes" structure. Within
LOOKING it is fine; landing the identical contrast again in BREAK reads as a recap.
- Exact text (LOOKING): *"But where Monet lets the flags melt into atmosphere, Dufy holds them as flat shapes you could cut out with scissors."*
- Exact text (BREAK): *"He freezes the flags into flat planes, drains out the atmosphere, and uses color not to record a passing instant of light but to build the space of the picture outright."*
- Fix: keep the full scissors image in LOOKING (it is a looking beat). In BREAK, compress the Monet contrast to the one thing BREAK uniquely adds — Monet using color to catch a *fleeting moment* vs. Dufy using color to *build the space* — and cut the re-description of melting/flattening already given in LOOKING. One clean sentence, not a re-run.

### [NICE] "the festival doing the Fauve's work" reused as a title and a refrain
The phrase "the festival did the Fauve's job/work for him" appears in MAKING ("the
festival itself doing the color work"), LOOKING ("The 14th of July did the Fauve's job
for him"), the BREAK section title, and BREAK body. It is a good line and a deliberate
spine, so this is not a defect — but it is at the edge of over-use. Consider varying the
wording on one of the four hits so the refrain feels chosen, not stuck.

### [NICE] "before you have named a single object" used twice
Appears in LOOKING final paragraph (*"before you have named a single object in it"*) and
is the same idea as the chapter-opener's *"The holiday is in the painting before you have
found anything in it."* Both are good; flagged only so the reviser can vary one if it
reads as an echo on a close read.

## 2. LOOKING

This is the strongest axis. `RueLooking` does exactly what the gate demands and matches
the absinthe LOOKING chapter's method: it sets the whole-canvas first impression ("a wall
of color … more like a hung carpet or a scatter of confetti"), then walks the eye through
named, located zones in a deliberate order — center tricolor → up the street (cascade of
patches) → houses + crowd → whole-surface palette — with concrete directives ("let your
eye settle on the middle," "follow the street upward," "Pull back one more time"). The
reader is genuinely made to SEE:
- the central flag as a **flat bar of pure blue/white/red, three clean stripes, "like a label stuck to the glass"** — concrete and correct;
- the **cascade of red/white/blue flag-patches** up the receding street, "confetti," "each one a clean little block";
- the **green-and-pink houses in steep, raised (high-viewpoint) perspective**;
- the **festival crowd as loose patches of life, not drawn-out figures**;
- the **high-key palette** ("vivid reds, blues, greens and pinks at close to full strength") as the festival's mood;
- the **flat color zones building space, not light** ("color *is* the street").

All six required looking targets from the brief are hit. The flat-color-zones point is
made through the Monet contrast, which is the right move.

### [FIX] "Up the street" — "tumbling down toward you" fights the rest of the picture's logic
In `RueLooking`, the "Up the street" paragraph: *"a cascade of red, white and blue patches
tumbling down toward you."* The whole chapter (correctly) establishes the street
*receding/climbing UP and AWAY* from the viewer in steep raised perspective. "Cascade …
tumbling down toward you" reverses that vector and can confuse the reader about which way
the street goes.
- Fix: keep "cascade" (good word) but resolve the direction — e.g. the flags running *up and back* away from you, the patches reading as a cascade *strung up the receding street*, not tumbling toward the viewer. Align the motion with the established recession.

### [FIX] Crowd location is vaguer than the rest of the looking guidance
`RueLooking`, "The houses and the crowd" paragraph: the crowd is placed only as "Down
below, in the lower part of the street under all that flagging." Every other zone gets a
crisp where-to-look; the crowd is the one zone the reader may not actually find, because
the prose never says what to look for (small loose dark/light dabs at the base of the
street, between the buildings). The annotation in PART A is more concrete than the
chapter prose here.
- Fix: add one concrete visual cue for the crowd — what the patches look like and where they sit relative to the flags/houses — so the reader can locate it the way they can locate the central flag.

### [NICE] "the size of a smallish window … it more or less is one"
`RueLooking` opener. Lovely, and it pays off the portrait-orientation point. Keep. No
action — flagged as a model of the looking voice, not a defect.

## 3. CLARITY (every art term glossed for a zero-knowledge reader)

Very good. The draft glosses on first use, in-line, in parentheses or appositive, exactly
in the absinthe manner. Confirmed glossed: **Fauvism** (twice — name origin from *fauve*
"wild beast," and the "color came off the leash" mechanism, MAKING), **Salon des
Indépendants** (MAKING), **the tricolore / tricolor** ("the blue-white-red flag of the
French Republic," MAKING), **the 14th of July** (= the French national day, "rough
equivalent of the American Fourth of July," MAKING), **regattas** (LE HAVRE), **pavoisée /
pavois** (the naval dressing-overall term, LE HAVRE), **Cubism** (AFTERLIFE), **provenance**
(AFTERLIFE), **bequest** (AFTERLIFE), **engraver/printmaker** sense is clear, **steep/raised
perspective** is described in plain terms. Pronunciations are given for the French names,
matching house style.

### [BLOCKER → downgraded to FIX] "high key / high-key" is the gate's named term and is NOT glossed
The brief names "high key" as a required-to-gloss term, and PART A's annotation list and
the section blurb both use "high-key palette." In Part B the concept is *described* well
("vivid reds, blues, greens and pinks at close to full strength, the opposite of muted
Impressionist tone," LOOKING) but the **term "high key" itself is never used or defined in
the Part B prose** — `RueLooking` says "a high, festive register" instead. So Part B does
not actually leave an unglossed "high key" floating (which is why this is a FIX, not a
BLOCKER): the risk is the reverse — if the work-read UI surfaces the PART A
blurb/annotation ("a high-key holiday palette," "The high-key holiday palette") to the
same zero-knowledge reader, that surface uses the bare term **unglossed**.
- Fix (choose one): (a) in `RueLooking`'s palette paragraph, introduce the actual term once and gloss it — e.g. "a high, festive register — painters call it *high key*, meaning the whole palette pitched bright and light, near full strength, with the darks kept out" — so the reader owns the word the metadata uses; or (b) ensure the PART A blurb/annotation term "high-key" is itself glossed on whatever surface renders it. Option (a) is cleaner and keeps the gloss in the gated prose.

### [FIX] "aplats" appears only in PART A, not glossed in Part B — confirm it never reaches the reader unglossed
PART A annotations use **"aplats"** ("broad flat areas (aplats) of vivid, unmodelled
color"). Part B never uses the word, and that is fine for the prose. Flagging so the
coordinator confirms: if any annotation surface ships "aplats" to the reader, it must
carry its gloss (broad flat areas of unmodulated color) — the PART A text does gloss it
inline, so this is a verify-don't-change note, not a prose defect.

### [FIX] "high-keyed" used in MAKING before the concept is built
`RueMaking`, describing Matisse's *Luxe, calme et volupté*: *"a beach scene built out of
high-keyed, unmixed, almost shadowless color."* This is the first appearance of the
"high-key" idea and it is used as a bare adjective with no gloss, several paragraphs
before the palette is explained in LOOKING. A zero-knowledge reader meets "high-keyed"
cold here.
- Fix: either gloss it lightly in place ("high-keyed — bright, light, near full strength") or swap to plain words here ("bright, unmixed, almost shadowless color") and reserve the termed gloss for LOOKING per the FIX above.

### [NICE] "modelling it round in light and shade" / "model in light and shade"
Used in LOOKING and the PART A annotation. The meaning is inferable from context and the
draft immediately contrasts it ("He does not round it. He does not shade it"), so it
reads clearly even cold — but "modelling" is a craft term. Optional one-clause gloss
("modelling — shading a flat shape so it looks round and solid") would fully close it for
the zero-knowledge reader. Low priority.
