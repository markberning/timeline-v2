## §0 LEGEND / ERROR CATCHES (read first — the brief asked for these)

1. **"BOWLING" as a word fragment on the canvas — NOT CONFIRMED. Do NOT write it.**
   The two prominent words that ARE verified on the canvas are **"VALSE"** (waltz)
   and **"POLKA"** — both named as "the two most prominent words" and "VALSE
   anchors the bottom-right." No reliable source reading the painting lists
   "BOWLING." Treat any "BOWLING" reading as **[UNVERIFIED]** and leave it out
   unless a future source confirms it against the image. [DOCUMENTED for valse/polka;
   UNVERIFIED for bowling — Smarthistory/arthistoryproject vs. nothing]

2. **The nude rides a pair of SCISSORS, not a goose. Do NOT write "goose."**
   Every source describing the figure says **scissors** (a realistic nude woman
   astride an open pair of scissors). No source supports a goose or other bird.
   The brief's "scissors/goose" resolves to **scissors only.** [DOCUMENTED —
   MoMA/Britannica/Smarthistory/Sartle all say scissors; goose UNSUPPORTED]

3. **"Montmartre" — soften to "Pigalle / the foot of Montmartre."** The Bal
   Tabarin sat at **36 rue Victor-Massé, in the 9th arrondissement** (the Pigalle
   district at the foot of the Montmartre hill, just south of the 18th), not in
   Montmartre proper. "A Pigalle dance hall below Montmartre" is the safe phrasing.
   [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia Bal Tabarin; Paris Musées address record]

4. **The cancan — frame it carefully.** The Bal Tabarin DID host the French
   cancan/quadrille (and took it on more fully after the Moulin Rouge fire of
   1915), so the cancan is true to the *venue*. But the words Severini actually
   painted are **waltz (VALSE) and polka**, and the dance the dancers are
   doing is not nailed to "the cancan" by any source — describe them as dancers
   in mid-whirl, with the cancan as the hall's signature act, not as a caption of
   the specific figures. [DOCUMENTED venue link; the specific-figures claim UNVERIFIED]

5. **The "hieroglyphic" in the title is the artist's, not a museum coinage** — it
   is Severini's own metaphor: the painting is a *dynamic hieroglyph*, a single
   sign-cluster that encodes the whole sensation of the night. Safe to state.
   [DOCUMENTED — title is the artist's]

6. **"Self-portrait in a straw hat at lower right" — LEGEND/DISPUTED.** "Some
   scholars believe the figure at lower right in a straw hat is Severini himself."
   Frame as a reading, never as fact. [DISPUTED — Sartle attributes to "some scholars"]

## §2 HOOK (candidate — author may rewrite in house voice)

A Paris dance hall remembered from the inside out: real sequins glued into the
paint, the words *valse* and *polka* floating in the spin, a cancan whirl
shattered into a hundred simultaneous splinters of light — and, tucked in the
chaos, a tiny nude riding an open pair of scissors. Futurism's friendliest
masterpiece, painted by the one Futurist who actually lived in Paris.

## §3 NARRATIVE BEATS (~5 — the spine the author fills)

**Beat 1 — Severini, the Futurist who lived in Paris.**
Born Cortona, Italy, 1883; settled in **Paris in November 1906** — he later said he
was "born intellectually" there. He fell in with the French avant-garde
(Picasso, Braque, Apollinaire, Modigliani; a studio near Dufy, Braque, Valadon)
*before* his Italian colleagues did, which made him the movement's bridge. In
**Feb 1910** he co-signed the *Manifesto of the Futurist Painters* and in **April
1910** the *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting*, alongside Balla, Boccioni,
Carrà and Russolo. He married **Jeanne Fort** (daughter of the poet Paul Fort) in
**1913**. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia/Britannica; the "born intellectually in Paris"
line is Severini's own, framed as his words]

**Beat 2 — The bridge: Futurism meets Cubism meets Paris nightlife.**
Futurism wanted to paint speed, energy, the modern city's dynamism; Cubism gave
Severini the *grammar* to do it — faceting, fragmentation, and the trick of
**collaging real words into the picture** (lifted directly from Picasso's and
Braque's lettered Cubism). After Severini helped bring the Italians to Paris in
**1911**, the Futurists absorbed Cubist analysis to break energy into facets. But
where Boccioni loved the machine, **Severini chose the dancer** — light, movement,
the body in a crowd — as his vehicle for dynamism. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia/
Britannica/Smarthistory]

**Beat 3 — The Bal Tabarin itself.**
A real cabaret-dance hall at **36 rue Victor-Massé**, Pigalle, at the foot of
Montmartre (9th arrondissement), opened **20 December 1904** by the composer-
bandleader **Auguste Bosc**. An instant hit — costume balls, floor shows, and
later the French cancan/quadrille (which it took on more fully after the Moulin
Rouge fire of 1915). It ran until the end of **1952**; demolished **1966**. This
is the world Severini is remembering. [DOCUMENTED — Wikipedia Bal Tabarin; Paris
Musées]

**Beat 4 — Painted from memory: simultaneity, not a snapshot.**
Severini did NOT paint at the hall; he painted *the memory of it* — in keeping
with the philosopher **Henri Bergson** (whose ideas of memory, duration and
intuition were everywhere in this circle), he tried to fuse memory, present
sensation and motion into one field. Futurism called this **simultaneity**: the
canvas packs many moments and viewpoints of the same night at once, with **"lines
of force"** driving the eye. So the picture isn't a window onto one instant — it's
a single compressed *hieroglyph* of the whole experience. [DOCUMENTED —
Smarthistory/Khan Academy; the Bergson link is the standard teaching reading]

**Beat 5 — The carnival of fragments (and the real sequins).**
Onto this whirl Severini scattered legible-but-dreamlike incidents — two central
dancers, floating words, a black cat's head, an Arab/North-African man on a camel,
martini glasses, and the famous nude astride a pair of scissors — read as cabaret
acts, costume-ball turns, and the night's drink-fuelled nonsense. The masterstroke:
**real sequins fixed into the paint on the dancer's dress**, so the surface
literally catches and throws light the way the hall's lamps did. Glamour rendered
*as* glamour — the medium is the subject. [DOCUMENTED — MoMA/Britannica/Sartle/
Smarthistory]

## §4 "LOOK CLOSER" ANNOTATIONS (6 — prose `where`, no crop coordinates)

Each is a verifiable claim about what is literally on the canvas; checked at gate-1
against the painting image. NOTE the catches in §0 (no "bowling"; scissors not goose).

1. **The two dancers at the heart of it** — *Center, the pair of whirling women.*
   Two dancing women dominate the middle: one with **blond curls on the left**, one
   with **dark/brown hair on the right**, in a swirling **pink-and-purple flounced
   dress** (one source: "white, blue and pink"). They are the still-spinning core
   the whole canvas radiates from. [DOCUMENTED — arthistoryproject/Sartle/Britannica;
   dress color varies by source, so keep it to "pink-and-purple flounced" + hedge]

2. **The real sequins** — *On the dancer's dress, looping patterns across the
   center.* Not painted dots — **actual sequins fixed into the paint**, set in
   looping lines over the dress, so the surface flashes real light as you move.
   This is the work's signature material trick. [DOCUMENTED — MoMA medium line;
   Britannica; Sartle]

3. **The floating words** — *Scattered through the swirl; "VALSE" anchors the
   lower right.* Severini collaged painted words into the picture, the Cubist
   lettering device: the two clearest are **"VALSE"** (waltz) and **"POLKA"** —
   dances named right on the canvas, sound made visible. **(Do NOT add "BOWLING" —
   unverified, see §0.)** [DOCUMENTED for valse/polka — Smarthistory/arthistoryproject]

4. **The nude on the scissors** — *Tucked into the upper-left chaos, a small
   realistic figure.* A **realistic nude woman astride an open pair of scissors** —
   the strangest of the dream-fragments, read as a cabaret/costume-ball gag, a jolt
   of the figurative inside the abstraction. **(Scissors, not a goose — see §0.)**
   [DOCUMENTED — MoMA/Britannica/Smarthistory/Sartle]

5. **The lights / the shimmer** — *Throughout the upper field, the broken
   brilliant facets.* The hall's electric lighting is everywhere — the picture is
   built to read as light *splintering* off mirrors, glasses and spangles; the
   sequins make that light physically real on the surface rather than only
   depicted. (No single "chandelier" object is reliably identified by name in the
   sources — describe it as the broken blaze of the hall's lights, not a named
   fixture.) [DOCUMENTED that light is the subject; a discrete "chandelier"
   UNVERIFIED — keep general]

6. **The night's odd cast** — *Among the fragments: a camel-rider, a cat, the
   glasses.* Scattered legible incidents fill the spin: a **North-African / Arab
   man riding a camel**, a **black cat's head**, and **martini glasses** (the
   night's drink). Read as memories of cabaret turns and costume parties, not a
   single coherent scene — the point is simultaneity, many moments at once.
   [DOCUMENTED — Britannica/Smarthistory/Sartle; "self-portrait in a straw hat at
   lower right" is a DISPUTED scholars' reading — see §0, optional to mention as a
   hedge, never as fact]

**[DISPUTED] — the artist's own provenance account.** In a MoMA artist's
questionnaire, **Severini said he first sold the painting to the dealer Léonce
Rosenberg in Paris, who then sold it to Richard Wyndham.** Severini *did* sign a
three-year contract with Rosenberg in 1919 — but **MoMA states this provenance
"could not be confirmed."** Write the Marinetti→Wyndham→MoMA chain as the
documented record, and the Rosenberg step as the artist's unconfirmed claim.
[DOCUMENTED chain + DISPUTED Rosenberg step — both from the MoMA record]

## §6 KEY FIGURES (nameplates)

- **Gino Severini** (1883–1966) — the painter. Italian Futurist who lived in Paris
  from 1906; the movement's bridge to Cubism; chose dancers over machines.
  [DOCUMENTED]
- **Filippo Tommaso Marinetti** (1876–1944) — founder/impresario of Futurism
  (wrote the 1909 Founding Manifesto); held this canvas on consignment in Milan to
  c.1925. [DOCUMENTED]
- **The Cubists — Picasso & Braque** — whose faceting and **collaged lettering**
  Severini absorbed in Paris; the source of the painted words. **Apollinaire** (the
  poet-critic) and **Modigliani** were also in his Paris circle. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Henri Bergson** (1859–1941) — the philosopher of memory/duration/intuition
  behind the "painted from memory / simultaneity" reading. [DOCUMENTED — standard
  teaching frame, attribute as influence/reading not as Severini's stated source]
- **Richard Wyndham** (1896–1948) — British collector, owned it by 1935; his estate
  sold it to MoMA in 1948. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Auguste Bosc** — composer-bandleader who opened the Bal Tabarin in 1904.
  [DOCUMENTED — venue context]
- **Léonce Rosenberg** — Paris dealer; Severini's 1919 contract dealer and the
