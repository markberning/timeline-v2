### Dimensions — RESOLVE CAREFULLY

- **Canonical / catalogue-raisonné figure: 48 × 63 cm** (height × width). This is the
  number on WP-EN, WP-FR, NGA, Google Arts & Culture, and the standard literature.
  **Use 48 × 63 cm as the work's dimensions.**
- **Imperial conversion (this app is imperial-only):**
  - 48 cm = 18.9 in = **1 ft 6.9 in (round to 1 ft 7 in)**
  - 63 cm = 24.8 in = **2 ft 0.8 in (round to 2 ft 1 in)**
  - So: **≈ 1 ft 7 in × 2 ft 1 in** (height × width). (Exact: 18.9 in × 24.8 in.)
- **CAUTION — the Marmottan online notice lists "H. 50 cm × W. 65 cm" *without frame*
  and "H. 75 × W. 91 × D. 10 cm" *with frame.*** The 50×65 figure is an outlier vs
  every catalogue source. Treat **48 × 63 cm** as the figure to ship (it is the
  near-universal published dimension); do not silently switch to 50×65. If the museum
  number is ever cited, footnote it as the framed/measured variant. (NGA print
  catalogue: "18 15/16 × 24 13/16 in." ≈ 48 × 63 cm — confirms 48×63.)

## 2. WHAT IS LITERALLY ON THE CANVAS (for gated "Look closer" PROSE pointers)

Painted looking out over the **outer harbour (avant-port) of Le Havre at dawn**, from
a window of Monet's hotel room facing **southeast** (Olson; MARMOTTAN: "view of Le
Havre's outer harbor at dawn, painted from the artist's hotel window").

**Verified elements, with placement:**

- **The sun:** a small **orange / orange-red disk**, set **upper-centre, slightly left
  of centre**, fairly high above the horizon (Olson: rendered as it looked ~20–30 min
  after sunrise). [MARMOTTAN, WP-EN, Olson]
- **The sun's reflection:** a **broken vertical streak of orange** running straight down
  the water from the sun toward the foreground — choppy dabs, not a smooth column.
  MARMOTTAN notes the sun and its reflections "were added at the end." [MARMOTTAN, WP-EN]
- **Foreground rowing boats / figures:** a **dark silhouetted rowing boat in the
  foreground (lower-centre / lower-right)** with a standing/rowing figure, and a
  **second, fainter boat + figure** just behind/beside it.
  ⚠️ **BOAT-COUNT CAUTION (sources differ — do NOT over-precise):** WP-EN says "two
  small rowboats in the foreground." WP-FR describes "one figure propelling a rowboat
  in the foreground; a second, indistinct figure in the middle distance." Safe wording:
  **"two dark little boats with ferryman/oarsman figures in the foreground, the nearer
  one sharper than the farther."** Do not assert an exact figure count beyond "a figure
  in each."
- **Background industrial port of Le Havre (hazy, blue-grey):** masts of sailing ships
  / **clipper masts (left)**; **dockside / loading cranes and derricks**; **factory
  chimneys and steamship smokestacks belching smoke**, silhouetted against the misty
  sky (chimneys/masts cluster toward the **right**). The smoke drifts — Olson read the
  plume direction as a light wind from the (north)west. [WP-EN, WP-FR, MARMOTTAN]
- **Finish / handling:** deliberately **un-finished, sketch-like**; almost no detail;
  **loose horizontal strokes for the water**; thin, rapid, wet-into-wet. This lack of
  "finish" is the thing the critics attacked (§4). [WP-EN, WIKISOURCE]
- **Composition:** horizontal, **divided roughly into thirds**; muted blue-grey
  palette pierced by the single warm note of the sun. [WP-FR]

> Author note: the painting reads as **atmosphere over an industrial working harbour**,
> not a picturesque marine. The smokestacks and cranes are the point, not background
> clutter (§7).

## 3. THE TONAL-LUMINANCE SCIENCE  **[DOCUMENTED]**

**The finding:** the orange sun has **almost exactly the same *luminance* (perceived
brightness / grey-value) as the background sky behind it.** Measured with a photometer,
sun and sky come out essentially equal. So in a **black-and-white (greyscale)
reproduction the sun nearly vanishes** — its dramatic "pop" comes from **colour /
chroma contrast (warm orange against cool grey-blue), not from a brightness
difference.** [HARVARD-MAG; WP-EN]

**Who analysed it:** **Margaret Livingstone**, professor of neurobiology at Harvard
University, in her work on the neuroscience of vision (book *Vision and Art: The
Biology of Seeing*; summarised in HARVARD-MAG, Jul 2003). Her line, widely quoted:
*"If you make a black and white copy of Impression: Sunrise, the Sun disappears
[almost] entirely."* In the magazine: the sun "has the same value, or luminance, as
the sky." [HARVARD-MAG, WP-EN]

**The mechanism (state it accurately):**
- The human visual system splits into two streams. Livingstone's shorthand:
  a **"Where" system** (older, shared with most mammals; carries **luminance,
  position, depth, motion** — broadly the magnocellular pathway) and a **"What"
  system** (newer, primate; carries **colour and object recognition** — broadly
  parvocellular). [HARVARD-MAG]
- The **"Where" / luminance system is essentially colour-blind.** Because the sun is
  **equiluminant** with the sky, the luminance system **cannot locate it** — to that
  system the sun isn't there. The **colour system** sees the orange disk plainly.
- **Why it seems to flicker / pulse / vibrate:** position and motion are computed by the
  luminance ("Where") system. Since that system can't pin the sun's location, its
  **position and motionlessness are poorly defined**, so the sun **"may seem to vibrate
  or pulsate"** — the painting reproduces the unstable, shimmering feel of looking at a
  real low sun through haze. [HARVARD-MAG]
- **Caveat to keep the claim honest:** "the sun disappears entirely" in greyscale is the
  popular shorthand; Livingstone's own phrasing is **"[almost] entirely."** Equiluminant
  ≈ nearly-equal, not provably identical to the last unit. Write "almost exactly the
  same brightness," not "mathematically identical."

> Related (optional, deeper): a 2021 study (Martinez-Conde / Macknik et al., *PNAS*-adjacent)
> argued **microsaccades** (tiny involuntary eye movements) drive the perceived
> flicker. Use only if going deep; the Livingstone equiluminance account is the
> headline and is the one to lead with.

## 4. THE NAME — the crux  **[mixed: event DOCUMENTED, title-blurt LEGEND-ish]**

**The exhibition (DOCUMENTED):**
- The **First Impressionist Exhibition**, mounted by the *Société anonyme des artistes
  peintres, sculpteurs, graveurs* ("Anonymous Society of Painters, Sculptors,
  Engravers…"), ran **15 April – 15 May 1874**. [WP-FR]
- Venue: the **former studio of the photographer Nadar**, **35 boulevard des
  Capucines, Paris**. [WP-EN, WP-FR]
- ~30 participating artists (Monet, Degas, Pissarro, Renoir, Sisley, Cézanne, Morisot,
  Guillaumin…), 200+ works, ~3,500–4,000 visitors over the run. [WP-EN]
- *Impression, soleil levant* hung as **catalogue No. 98** (the number Leroy cites). [WP-FR; WIKISOURCE]

**Leroy's review (DOCUMENTED):**
- **Louis Leroy**, satirist, in **_Le Charivari_, 25 April 1874**, headline
  **"L'Exposition des impressionnistes" ("The Exhibition of the Impressionists").** [WP-EN, WIKISOURCE]
- Written as a **mock dialogue**: Leroy's narrator tours the show with a horrified
  conservative academic painter, **"Monsieur Joseph Vincent."** At No. 98:
  > "*What does that canvas depict? Look at the catalogue.*"
  > "*Impression, Sunrise.*"
  > "*Impression — I was certain of it. I was just telling myself that, since I was
  > impressed, there had to be some impression in it … and what freedom, what ease of
  > workmanship! Wallpaper in its embryonic state is more finished than that
  > seascape.*"
  [WIKISOURCE — verbatim English translation]
- The **"wallpaper… more finished than that seascape"** jibe is the line to quote; it's
  an attack on the *lack of finish* (§2).

**Monet's own account of the title (LEGEND-flavoured — it is HIS later recollection):**
- Quoted (e.g. in WP-EN): *"They asked me for a title for the catalogue, it couldn't
  really be taken for a view of Le Havre, and I said: 'Put Impression.'"* **Tag this as
  Monet's reminiscence years after the fact, not a contemporaneous record.** [WP-EN — labelled as Monet's account]
- **Contested wrinkle:** WP-FR credits the on-the-spot retitling (from *Vue du Havre* /
  "View of Le Havre" to *Impression, soleil levant*) to **Edmond Renoir** (the painter's
  brother, who helped compile the catalogue), at the moment titles were being gathered.
  So "who actually said the word for the catalogue" is itself **contested** — Monet's
  memory vs. the Edmond-Renoir account. Present it as *the title was decided in the
  rush of cataloguing; Monet later claimed he chose "Impression."*

**THE NUANCE THAT MUST SURVIVE EDITING — Leroy did NOT coin "impression" from nothing:**
- The word was **already in play**: it was **Monet's own painting title**, and Monet had
  used "impression" in titles before. Leroy lifted it straight off the catalogue. [WIKISOURCE, WP-EN]
- **Jules-Antoine Castagnary**, a sympathetic critic, used **"impressionists"
  *approvingly*** in **_Le Siècle_, 29 April 1874** (four days after Leroy), defining them
  as painters who render *the sensation evoked by the landscape rather than the
  landscape itself.* [WP-FR; corroborated by multiple secondary sources]
- **Correct framing:** Leroy **popularised / weaponised** the term (turned a picture
  title into a mocking label for the whole group); he did **not** invent the word, and
  the *positive* sense was alive within days via Castagnary. **The artists themselves
  adopted "Impressionists" by their 1877 exhibition.** Do NOT write "Leroy coined
  Impressionism." Write "Leroy's sarcasm gave the movement the name that stuck."

## 5. THE THEFT & RECOVERY  **[DOCUMENTED — great story]**

- **Theft: Sunday 27 October 1985**, a **daylight armed robbery** of the Musée
  Marmottan. **~5 masked gunmen** held up guards and visitors at gunpoint and walked
  out with **nine paintings**. [CBC ARCHIVES; WP-EN; WASHINGTON POST 1990]
- The haul included **five Monets** — *Impression, soleil levant* the prize — plus
  works by **Renoir (two)** and **Berthe Morisot**, among others. [CBC; WASHINGTON POST 1990]
- **Recovery: December 1990.** The nine Marmottan paintings (plus an unrelated 17th-c.
  picture) were recovered in a **villa in Porto-Vecchio, southern Corsica**, via police
  work; the investigation linked the theft to **organised crime (a Japanese mob /
  *yakuza* connection was reported)**. The works had been missing ~5 years. [WASHINGTON POST, 7 Dec 1990; WP-EN]
- WP-EN names two men, **Philippe Jamin and Youssef Khimoun**, in connection with the
  theft. [WP-EN]
- **Back on view at the Marmottan in 1991.** [WP-EN]

> Watch the wording: "armed daylight robbery, nine works, five Monets" is solid;
> "yakuza/Japanese organised crime" was *reported* — phrase as "police tied it to
> organised crime, with reported links to the Japanese underworld."

## 7. CONTEXT THE WORK READ NEEDS (deeper than the movement read)

- **Le Havre is Monet's home port.** He **grew up in Le Havre** (the family moved there
  when he was ~5); the working harbour, the Channel light, the sea fog are his native
  visual material. Painting Le Havre at dawn is Monet painting *home*. [WP-EN, biographical standard]
- **The Franco-Prussian War (1870–71) and London exile sit just before it.** Monet fled
  to **London** during the war (to dodge conscription / the chaos), where he saw
  **Turner and Whistler** and the atmospheric Thames — fog, smoke, dissolved form.
  Critic **Ernest Chesneau** explicitly read Turner/Whistler echoes in *Impression*. He
  returned to France via the Netherlands and was **back painting Le Havre by 1872.** [WP-FR; standard biography]
- **Why an industrial working harbour, not a pretty marine:** the smokestacks, cranes,
  steam packets and derricks are **deliberately modern.** This is the *modern,
  reawakening port city* at dawn — the smoke and machinery are the subject, in the
  spirit of painting modern life (Baudelaire/Manet milieu), the opposite of a polished,
  timeless Salon seascape. [WP-FR, MARMOTTAN framing]
- **"Impression" vs a finished Salon marine:** this is a **rapid, on-the-spot
  *impression*** — thin, sketchy, unfinished by Salon standards — precisely the quality
  Leroy mocked. A Salon marine would be highly finished, detailed, varnished, "complete."
  Calling it *Impression* was partly an honest label ("this is a sketch-impression, not
  a Vue du Havre") — which Monet's own account confirms. [§4; WP-EN]
- **It did not hang alone:** Monet showed **several works at the 1874 exhibition**
  (catalogue lists multiple Monet entries, e.g. *Boulevard des Capucines*, a
  *Déjeuner*, and Le Havre/Argenteuil canvases). *Impression, soleil levant* was **one
  of his entries (No. 98)**, not a solo hang — useful to deflate the myth that this lone
  picture "was" the exhibition. [WP-FR; WIKISOURCE references multiple Monet works]

## 9. PROPOSED 5-CHAPTER / MOVEMENT MAP (WORK shape)

The work arc: **where it came from → the making / the morning → reception & the name →
what it broke / meaning → afterlife (theft & legacy).** One-line throughline + section id each.

1. **`le-havre` — "Home port at war's end."**
   Monet's boyhood Le Havre; the 1870–71 Franco-Prussian War and his London exile
   (Turner, Whistler, Thames fog); back painting his home harbour by 1872 — the modern,
   smoke-and-cranes working port, not a pretty marine. *Throughline: the painter
   returns home with a new way of seeing fog and light.*

2. **`the-morning` — "7:35 a.m., a hotel window."**
   The making: one rapid dawn impression from the hotel window over the avant-port; what
   is on the canvas (sun, broken reflection, two dark boats, masts/cranes/smokestacks);
   the deliberate lack of finish; the equiluminant-sun science (the sun that vanishes in
   greyscale and shimmers in colour). Olson's forensic date. *Throughline: a sketch of a
   single fleeting morning, painted fast and on purpose unfinished.*

3. **`the-name` — "Wallpaper more finished than that seascape."**
   1874, Nadar's studio, No. 98; the catalogue title (Monet's account / Edmond Renoir);
   Leroy's *Charivari* mockery and the wallpaper jibe; Castagnary's approving use; how a
   sneer became the movement's name. *Throughline: a put-down title sticks and christens
   a revolution.*

4. **`the-break` — "Why it broke the rules."**
   What it actually overturned: impression over finish, sensation over description,
   modern industrial subject, colour-contrast over brightness — and the honest caveats
   (NOT the first Impressionist painting; it *named*, didn't *found*). *Throughline: not
   the first of its kind, but the one that defined the kind.*

5. **`afterlife` — "Sold for a song, stolen at gunpoint, immortal."**
   Provenance (800 fr → 210 fr → de Bellio → Donop de Monchy → Marmottan 1940); the 1985
   armed heist; the 1990 Corsica recovery; its status today as the icon that named a
   movement. *Throughline: the cheap, mocked sketch becomes one of the most famous
   paintings on earth.*

## 10. FALSEHOODS TO REFUSE (author + gate checklist)

- ❌ "The **first** Impressionist painting." → ✅ "the painting that **named** Impressionism."
- ❌ "**Leroy coined** the word Impressionism." → ✅ "Leroy **popularised/weaponised** it; the word was Monet's title; Castagnary used it approvingly within days."
- ❌ "A **sunset** over Le Havre." → ✅ a **sunrise** (Olson 2014; the sun is in the east).
- ❌ The sun is the **brightest** point. → ✅ the sun is **equiluminant with the sky**; its pop is colour, not brightness.
- ❌ Stating an **exact boat/figure count** as fact. → ✅ "two dark little boats with figures."
- ❌ Held at **MuMa / André Malraux, Le Havre.** → ✅ **Musée Marmottan Monet, Paris** (inv. 4014).
- ❌ Quoting Monet's title-blurt as **contemporaneous fact.** → ✅ flag it as **his later recollection**.
- ❌ Dimensions "**50 × 65 cm**" as the work size. → ✅ **48 × 63 cm** (≈ 1 ft 7 in × 2 ft 1 in); 50×65 is the museum's framed/variant figure.
