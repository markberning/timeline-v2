## 2. THE STORY

*Whaam!* takes a single panel of cheap war-comic art and blows it up to the size of a history painting.

- **The source.** Lichtenstein adapted a panel from **"Star Jockey,"** a story in **DC Comics' *All-American Men of War* No. 89 (cover-dated Jan/Feb 1962)**, drawn by **Irv Novick**. (Wikipedia; CBR; multiple art-history sources. The issue is consistently cited; the exact cover date appears as both "Jan–Feb 1962" and "Feb 1962" — same issue, flag the month spread as trivial.)
- **What he changed.** Lichtenstein split the single comic panel into a **diptych** and rebalanced the graphic and narrative weight. In the source, the firing aircraft is a **North American F-86 Sabre**; in art-historical description it is often read as a **P-51 Mustang** (sources differ — treat the plane's exact model as not load-bearing). The two halves are tied together by the **missile's smoke trail** crossing the gap between the panels.
- **Left panel:** a fighter plane firing a rocket diagonally across the picture. A **yellow caption box** with black hand-lettering runs along the top: **"I PRESSED THE FIRE CONTROL … AND AHEAD OF ME ROCKETS BLAZED THROUGH THE SKY …"**
- **Right panel:** the **enemy plane exploding** in a red-and-yellow fireball, with the giant onomatopoeia **"WHAAM!"** bursting out in a yellow-edged blast.
- **The deadpan move.** Lichtenstein takes the **lowest, most disposable kind of commercial illustration** — a war-comic panel meant to be glanced at and thrown away — and presents it, hand-painted at mural scale, **signed as fine art**. The violence is real (a man is being killed) but the picture renders it **flat, bright, and cartoonish** — no gore, no drama in the handling, just clean primary color and the comic's own visual clichés. The detachment *is* the content.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required — before / after)

**Before.** Fine-art painting drew on "high" sources — myth, history, the figure, landscape, the gestures of the artist's own hand (Abstract Expressionism, the dominant mode Lichtenstein was reacting against, prized exactly that visible, emotional brushwork). Comic strips, advertising, and pulp illustration were **commercial craft**, beneath the gallery wall.

**After.** Lichtenstein puts a **comic-book war panel on a 13-foot canvas, hand-painted, and hangs it as a serious painting.** Three breaks at once:
1. **Source.** The "worst" of commercial art — a throwaway war comic — becomes legitimate raw material for high art.
2. **Surface.** The **Ben-Day dot** (the mechanical printer's dot screen used to cheaply shade newsprint) becomes a deliberate, hand-made **fine-art surface** — the texture of mass reproduction, painted by hand.
3. **The hand.** The work looks **mechanical, impersonal, "programmed"** — the opposite of the expressive brushstroke. It is hand-made to look machine-made. That inversion (machine look, human labor) is the Pop break in one image.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — flag variants)

**Primary quote (use this):**

> "I want my painting to look as if it has been programmed. I want to hide the record of my hand." — Roy Lichtenstein

- **Source:** quoted in the **Pasadena Art Museum exhibition catalogue, *Roy Lichtenstein* (1967)** — interview/text by **John Coplans** (commonly cited at p. 12). This is the most-cited primary attribution.
- **VARIANT flag:** a looser paraphrase circulates — *"I wanted to look programmed or impersonal but I don't really believe I am being impersonal when I do it."* — **do not quote this as verbatim**; the clean two-sentence "programmed … hide the record of my hand" version above is the one to ship.

**Secondary quote (optional, on the commercial-source posture):**

> "I'm interested in what would normally be considered the worst aspects of commercial art." — Roy Lichtenstein

- Widely attributed to Lichtenstein; **less precisely sourced** in this research pass than the "programmed" quote. **Flag:** verify exact wording/source before quoting in the body, or attach a softer attribution ("Lichtenstein said he was interested in…"). The "programmed / hide the record of my hand" quote is the safe verbatim KEY STATEMENT.

**On appropriation (use only as paraphrase unless re-verified):** Lichtenstein argued his copies were transformed — *"The closer my work is to the original, the more threatening and critical the content … my work is entirely transformed in that my purpose and perception are entirely different."* (His defense of the practice; source attribution loose in this pass — **paraphrase, don't quote verbatim** without re-checking.)

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — NO coordinates)

1. **Two panels, one action.** It is a **diptych** — two separate canvases. Read it left to right: the firing plane on the left, the explosion on the right, with the **rocket's smoke trail** carrying your eye across the seam between them.
2. **The firing plane (left).** A fighter aircraft cuts diagonally down across the left canvas, a rocket streaking from it — the cause.
3. **The exploding plane (right).** The enemy aircraft erupts in a **red-and-yellow fireball** that fills the right canvas — the effect. A man is dying, rendered as a clean graphic burst.
4. **"WHAAM!"** The onomatopoeia detonates across the right panel in bold yellow-and-black comic lettering — the painting's title is literally painted into it.
5. **The red/yellow caption box (left).** Along the top of the left panel, a **yellow box** carries the pilot's hand-lettered line — *"I pressed the fire control…"* — the comic's narrative voice, kept intact and enlarged.
6. **The hand-painted Ben-Day dots.** Look at the flat color areas: the **regular grid of dots** is not printed — Lichtenstein laid it down by hand (scrubbing paint through a perforated metal screen / stencil). The mechanical look is hand-labor.
7. **(bonus) Flat primaries + hard black outlines.** Everything is **flat primary color** (red, yellow, blue) inside **thick black outlines** — no modeling, no shadow, no brushwork. The comic's economy, monumentalized.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct, with sources)

1. **The source is a real, identifiable panel — credit it.** *Whaam!* is based on **Irv Novick's** panel from **"Star Jockey," *All-American Men of War* #89 (1962)**. The **appropriation/credit question is genuine and live**: Lichtenstein credited the *comic* but not the *artist*, paid no royalties, and sought no permission — a recurring complaint about his comic-based work (the original artists included **Russ Heath, Tony Abruzzo, Irv Novick, Jerry Grandenetti, Jack Kirby**). Comics artist **Dave Gibbons** (2013 BBC Four doc) called it "copycat," arguing it should read *"WHAAM! by Roy Lichtenstein, after Irv Novick."* **Do not soft-pedal this; name Novick and the issue.** (Wikipedia; CBR.)
2. **Medium = Magna, an early acrylic.** The "acrylic" is **Magna** — a **solvent-based acrylic resin** paint (Bocour), used alongside **oil**. Don't call it ordinary water-based acrylic. Tate's short credit just says "acrylic paint and oil paint on canvas"; the fuller catalogue-raisonné description adds **graphite pencil** (the under-drawing). (Tate; lichtensteincatalogue.org RLCR 808.)
3. **Ben-Day dots are HAND-PAINTED, not printed.** This is the single most-misstated fact. Lichtenstein **applied the dots by hand** — scrubbing/dabbing paint through a **perforated metal screen or stencil** — to *imitate* the mechanical printing dot. The point of the work is that it **only looks** mechanically reproduced. Never write that the dots were printed or screened by machine.
4. **Dimensions / two panels.** It is a **two-panel diptych**, ≈ **172.7 × 406.4 cm overall (≈ 5 ft 8 in × 13 ft 4 in)** — give the **overall** figure as the headline, and note the diptych structure. The tiny cm spread between sources (406.4 vs 405.9 cm) is **rounding, not a discrepancy**.
5. **The plane model is unsettled.** Source comic shows an **F-86 Sabre**; popular description often says **P-51 Mustang**. Treat the exact aircraft as **not load-bearing** — don't assert one model as fact in the body.
6. **Acquisition detail.** Firmly sourced: **Tate purchased it in 1966.** The **£3,940 / Ileana Sonnabend** detail is Wikipedia-only in this pass — flag as secondary, or omit.

1. **≈ 13 ft wide** — the two-panel diptych spans **≈ 406 cm / ≈ 13 ft 4 in**: a throwaway comic panel enlarged to the scale of a history painting.
