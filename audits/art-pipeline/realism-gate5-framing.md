## 1. Erasure / mishandling of women — Rosa Bonheur

**Verdict: FAIR**

The narrative gives Bonheur a full chapter (chapter 6) and does not reduce her to
the trousers-permit anecdote. The structure is intentional and correct:

- Chapter 6's opening section ("The most famous painter you weren't told about")
  establishes her positional importance *before* naming the permit, framing her
  first as "one of the most celebrated painters of any kind, of either sex."
- Her method (slaughterhouse and cattle-market study for anatomical precision) is
  described as disciplined professional work, not eccentricity.
- The permit section is titled "The trousers — Agency, not anecdote." The title
  is the argument: the permit is explicitly re-read as evidence of *professional
  commitment and structural obstacle*, not a curiosity.
- The section closes: "She is not a footnote to a movement of men. She is one of
  the people who proved the movement could win an audience — and she won a bigger
  one than most of them." That is an unambiguous assertion of artistic standing.
- The "handoff" section (chapter 6 close) names her alongside Courbet, Millet and
  Daumier as the four people who drove the movement: "The fight Courbet picked,
  Millet deepened, Daumier sharpened and Bonheur popularized had been, on that
  point, won." She is not a satellite; she is the fourth verb in the sentence that
  closes the story.

The permit framing is also well-calibrated against the documented record.
Independent verification confirms: Bonheur did obtain an official police permit
to wear men's clothing (documented as early as 1852 in the police archives, and
consistent with a French law prohibiting women from wearing trousers that was
not repealed until 2013). The characterisation as a work-enabling choice made
for professional reasons is accurate. The prose hedges the permit as "reported"
rather than asserting it flat — an appropriate caution.

**One SHOULD-FIX: Bonheur's life-partner is silently erased.**

The draft says Bonheur "built one of the most successful artistic lives of her
century without a husband, a patron's leash, or the official school." This is
technically true but omits a significant fact that shapes how to read "lived on
her own terms": Bonheur shared her life and household for forty years with
**Nathalie Micas** (1824–1889), her life-partner, who managed the household,
handled business correspondence, and prepared canvases — a partnership that was
a material enabler of the large-scale work the draft celebrates. After Micas's
death in 1889, Bonheur formed a companion relationship with the American artist
Anna Klumpke.

The omission is not erasure of *Bonheur* (she is the subject and is treated with
full seriousness), but it is a partial erasure of her actual life. The framing
"without a husband" is technically accurate but functions as a slight misdirection:
it invites the reader to picture a solitary autonomous woman, when the historical
reality is a forty-year domestic partnership that sustained the work.

This is a SHOULD-FIX rather than a MUST-FIX because:
- The narrative's explicit goal for this chapter is Bonheur-as-Realist-peer,
  not a biography. The partnership is contextual, not required to establish her
  artistic standing.
- The movement read is not a life-narrative level; that belongs to a future
  artist-level read.
- The existing framing ("lived openly on her own terms") is not false.

**What the fix should look like (if added):** A single clause substituted into
the existing sentence, e.g. "ran her own household and career alongside her
long-time companion Nathalie Micas." The author should not over-explain; the
point is that the domestic reality behind the art is a real partnership, not
solitary independence. If added, this is new biographical material and must go
through the fact-checker first.

**Verified facts for that fix:**
- Nathalie Micas (1824–1889) lived with Bonheur for ~40 years until Micas's
  death; managed the household and prepared canvases
  (Wikipedia: Nathalie Micas; National Museum of Women in the Arts profile)
- Bonheur's own words on the relationship: "Had I been a man, I would have
  married her..." (widely cited; appears in multiple scholarly sources)
- After Micas: Anna Klumpke became Bonheur's companion in her final years
  (sources: Wikipedia Rosa Bonheur; Smithsonian Magazine 2022)

## 2. Romantic-genius myth — Courbet's leadership vs. peers as equals

**Verdict: FAIR**

The narrative gives Courbet the most space (chapters 2 and 3) but structures the
whole arc to undercut the lone-titan reading:

- Chapter 3's manifesto section ends with an explicit corrective: "It should be
  said that Courbet did not invent the impulse single-handed, however much his ego
  would have liked the credit. He named it, theorized it, and gave it a scandal big
  enough to make headlines. But the deepest, quietest, most lasting Realist pictures
  were being painted at the same moment by a man who never built a tent and never
  wanted one…" This is a clean structural move — Courbet's ego is named as a
  feature of the historical record, while the narrative distributes the real
  achievement.
- Millet (chapter 4) is treated as Courbet's equal in depth of conviction and
  arguably superior in visual impact: *The Gleaners* and *The Angelus* are given
  full descriptive attention, not a summary.
- Daumier (chapter 5) is positioned as both a parallel practitioner and a
  different-register Realist (press satire + painting), with the *Gargantua* jailing
  framed as a distinct and arguably sharper political act than Courbet's salon
  scandals: "Daumier got jailed over *power*."
- The "handoff" (chapter 6) distributes all four agents equally in the closing
  verdict sentence (see §1 above).

The working people Courbet and Millet depicted are treated as the subject of the
chapter's moral argument, not props. *The Stone Breakers*' destruction in the 1945
bombing is framed as a political metaphor ("the great picture of the people history
grinds up was itself ground up by history"), and *The Gleaners* is given a full
reading of its compositional politics. The rural poor are not sentimentalised or
anonymized — the chapter names the structural reason gleaning existed (the poorest's
right to the harvest's scraps), situates it historically (post-1848 fear of the poor),
and states Millet's sympathy and politics as genuine.

No imbalance found on this axis.

## 3. The political reading — 1848, the rural/urban poor

**Verdict: FAIR**

The political dimension is neither sanitised nor turned into a lecture. The
chapter handles it as historical context that makes the paintings legible:

- Chapter 1 names 1848 directly: "the people of Paris threw out a king…briefly
  seized the foreground." This grounds the movement in politics without turning it
  into an ideological tract.
- The Gleaners section explicitly names the critics' political reading ("Some saw
  in those three monumental peasant women a warning — the rural poor, dignified and
  made enormous, only a few years after 1848 had shown what the poor could do when
  they stopped staying small") and endorses it as a genuine reading, not a modern
  bolt-on: "It is fairer to say Millet's sympathy was genuine and his politics were
  real, than to pretend the painting is just a tender study of country life."
  That is a clean, honest framing.
- Daumier's *Gargantua* jailing is framed as a political act: "Daumier got jailed
  over *power*."
- The Angelus is given its devotional reading without forcing a political one — an
  accurate distinction (the two paintings do carry different registers).

Independent verification confirms:
- The critics' response to *The Gleaners* is documented: one critic called it a
  reminder of "the scaffolds of 1793"; another saw in it the threat of socialism and
  a "thorn of revolution and the guillotine of 1793." The draft's characterisation
  ("alarmed them… only a few years after 1848") is historically accurate.
- Courbet's socialism was explicit and documented (he was a friend of Proudhon);
  the chapter's treatment reflects this without over-claiming that all Realists
  shared the same politics.

No sanitisation; no lecture. The axis is well-handled.

## 4. Fairness to the academy / Salon

**Verdict: FAIR**

The Salon is introduced in chapter 1 as a coherent institutional system with
internal logic, not a cartoon villain:

- The hierarchy of genres is explained on its own terms ("gods at the top,
  greengrocers at the bottom") before being critiqued.
- The Salon is identified as the "one show in France where a painter's career was
  made or buried" — a description of real institutional power, not a sneer.
- Cabanel's *Birth of Venus* is used as the counterpoint (via a callback to the
  era read), but the draft does not mock it; it describes it as "licked-smooth" and
  "poreless" — accurate art-historical characterisation, not a dismissal without
  evidence.
- The jury's reaction to the *Burial* is explained as structural anxiety, not
  mere stupidity: "The jury could feel the threat even when it couldn't name it."
  That sentence gives the institution credit for perceiving something real.
- The Pavilion chapter's "Meanwhile" box frames the official show as the
  necessary foil for the gesture — "Courbet's tent only works because of what it
  stands next to" — which is a genuinely fair reading of the relationship.

One note (advisory, not a fix): The hierarchy of genres section uses the phrase
"history painting — grand scenes from myth, scripture and ancient history, the
human body at heroic size — as the only subject serious enough to earn a man real
glory." The qualifier "real glory" is slightly loaded. It reads accurately, not as
a sneer — glory within this system WAS the goal — but the phrasing could be read
as the draft endorsing the Realist critique rather than describing the system.
Given the context and the overall balance, this is not a finding worth blocking on.
Advisory only.

## 5. Eurocentric / over-claim

**Verdict: FAIR**

The "first modern movement" claim is explicitly scoped. Chapter 1 states: "That
makes Realism a fair candidate for the first modern movement — the first shot in
the long argument with the jury that runs through the rest of this era — **with one
honest caveat: 'first' here means *first in Western painting*, the only world this
story claims to map.**"

That caveat is present, complete, and correctly placed — not buried, not hedged
half-heartedly. It mirrors the approach the era read takes and is consistent with
the framing gate's requirement for this project (the scope is allowed; the
overclaim is not).

The Russian "Meanwhile" box adds a cross-cultural note ("Realism was not only a
French event. In Russia… France was simply where it broke first and loudest") that
further undermines any French/Western exceptionalism without undermining the
narrative's own scope.

No overclaim found.
