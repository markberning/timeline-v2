## 1. CORE FACTS

- **Full title:** *I was a Rich Man's Plaything*
- **Artist:** Sir Eduardo Paolozzi (1924–2005), Scottish-Italian — born in Leith, Edinburgh, to Italian immigrant parents.
- **Date:** 1947.
- **Medium:** Collage — printed papers / magazine and advertisement cuttings pasted on card. (Tate object type: "printed papers on card." Note: this original is a unique paper collage; do **not** confuse it with the **1972 screenprint** edition of the same image — see §8.)
- **Dimensions (SMALL):** support **35.9 × 23.8 cm** = **14.1 × 9.4 in** (Tate gives support 359 × 238 mm). About the size of a sheet of letter paper — emphasize the smallness.
- **Current location:** **Tate, London** (Tate Britain; "view by appointment," Prints and Drawings Rooms). Accession number **T01462**.
- **Series:** one of the collages later gathered as Paolozzi's ***BUNK!*** series (the group Tate holds as *Ten Collages from BUNK*, dated 1947–52). *BUNK!* takes its name from Henry Ford's line "History is more or less bunk."

## 2. THE STORY

Paolozzi built the picture out of **American magazines and advertisements** — pulp fiction, glossy ads, and cheap print — the kind of material flooding out of the United States that ordinary Britons, three years into post-war austerity and still on ration books, could barely get hold of. Much of his raw material came from **magazines given to him by American ex-servicemen**; he gathered and assembled it across his **Paris years, 1947–1949**, where he was living when he made this collage (the BUNK material was collected during that Paris period; some sources also note London shop sources). To Paolozzi, who had never been to America, this was a window onto an "exotic society, bountiful and generous."

The composition layers found fragments:
- The **largest clipping** is the cover of a pulp romance/confessions magazine, ***Intimate Confessions***, supplying the title words *"I was a Rich Man's Plaything"* along with lurid teaser lines like "Daughter of Sin" and "Woman of the Streets," over a **pin-up** of a woman in red.
- Over her, a **man's hand holding a pistol** that fires the word **"POP!"** in a white cartoon burst. The "POP!" was cut from the **packaging of a toy pop gun** — i.e. it was already printed commercial graphic, not lettering Paolozzi drew.
- A **Coca-Cola** advertisement fragment (lower right).
- A **WWII bomber** aircraft (lower left), carrying the wartime slogan **"Keep 'em flying!"** (commonly identified as a Lockheed Hudson/Ventura-type).
- A **cherry and a slice of cherry pie**.
- A **"Real Gold"** logo (a California citrus-juice brand).

That printed **"POP!"** — made in **1947** — is frequently cited as the **first appearance of the word "pop" inside a work of this kind**, roughly a **decade before** the movement got its name (Lawrence Alloway is credited with popularizing "Pop art" in the 1950s). Paolozzi did not exhibit the collage when he made it; he first showed it (with other BUNK collages) **publicly in 1952**, projected via an **epidiascope**, in his **"Bunk" lecture at the ICA, London — the inaugural event of the newly formed Independent Group**.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK *(required — before / after)*

**Before:** Serious art in 1940s Britain still treated mass-market commercial print — pulp covers, soda ads, toy packaging, pin-ups — as beneath art, as throwaway. The "found" image, if used at all (Dada/Surrealist photomontage), was deployed for shock or political montage, not as a celebration of consumer abundance. And "fine art" collage drew on refined or fragmentary materials, not the full-color American advertising stream.

**After:** Paolozzi treats the **ready-made commercial image as the actual raw material of art** — lifting an ad, a pulp cover, a pop-gun wrapper intact and arranging them into a picture about glamour, consumption, and American mass culture. He does this in **austerity Britain in 1947**, when those American goods were objects of longing, and he does it **years before** Pop Art exists as a named movement and roughly a decade before Warhol and Lichtenstein. The break: the everyday printed surface of consumer capitalism becomes a legitimate subject *and* medium — the readymade commercial image, early.

(Frame plainly per house voice — don't over-claim it "invented Pop"; it prefigures it. See §8.)

## 4. KEY STATEMENT *(verbatim, sourced — with variant flags)*

Paolozzi left fewer crisp aphorisms about *this single collage* than the pipeline would like; the most-cited statement of his collage method, surfaced from **Tate's i-Map** material, is:

> "...a classical artist might have done 500 drawings, one's looking at 500 images involved with a kind of global situation and one's choosing an image which acts as a **metaphor** for one's particular feeling. But unless one emphasises and arranges the images into **patterns of irony** the point will be lost."

— attributed to Eduardo Paolozzi (Tate i-Map). **FLAG:** I obtained this from Tate i-Map text via search excerpt; the Tate i-Map transcript page returned HTTP 503 during research, so the **exact wording / punctuation and the original interview source/date are UNVERIFIED at primary source** — confirm against the live [Tate i-Map transcript](https://www.tate.org.uk/imap/imap2/paolozzi-transcript.shtml) before quoting in the read.

**Reliable alternative (title-source quote, fully verifiable):** the *BUNK!* series name comes from **Henry Ford's** remark, "**History is more or less bunk.**" (Ford, in a 1916 *Chicago Tribune* interview — well-documented; this is Ford's line, not Paolozzi's.) Use this only as the series-name source, attributed to Ford, never to Paolozzi.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (~6, prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The pulp-romance pin-up.** The biggest element is a magazine cover — a woman in red from *Intimate Confessions* — surrounded by its own sensational cover lines ("Daughter of Sin," "Woman of the Streets"). The title of the whole collage is literally lifted from this cover.
2. **The "POP!" pistol burst.** A man's hand and pistol fire a white cartoon "POP!" — cut from a toy pop-gun's packaging. This is the famous detail: a 1947 printed "POP" sitting inside the picture years before "Pop art" was a phrase.
3. **The Coca-Cola logo.** A fragment of a Coca-Cola advertisement anchors a lower corner — the single most recognizable badge of American consumer culture, dropped in whole.
4. **The bomber.** A WWII aircraft with the wartime slogan "Keep 'em flying!" — the war's machinery still bleeding into the consumer imagery of the immediate post-war moment.
5. **The cherry pie.** A cherry and a slice of cherry pie — American domestic plenty in a single sweet, appetizing fragment, jarring against the gun and the pin-up.
6. **The collage texture itself.** Look at how it's made: separate printed scraps with their own edges, type, and color registration, butted and overlapped on card. The seams are visible on purpose — the picture is an assembly of mass-print surfaces, not a unified illustration.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct with sources)

1. **"First use of the word POP" — scope it carefully.** The defensible claim: this 1947 collage is the **first known instance of the word "pop" appearing inside a work in this Pop-adjacent context**, predating the named movement. The traps to avoid: (a) it did **not invent or name** Pop Art — the term "Pop art" was coined/popularized later (Lawrence Alloway, 1950s); (b) the movement was **not named after this "POP!"** — the printed "POP!" is a gunshot onomatopoeia cut from **toy-gun packaging**, a coincidence, not the source of the movement's name; (c) don't say it's the "first Pop artwork" flatly — say it **prefigures**/anticipates Pop by ~a decade.

2. **Title source.** The title *I was a Rich Man's Plaything* is **not Paolozzi's invented phrase** — it's lifted verbatim from the cover of the pulp magazine **Intimate Confessions**, the largest clipping in the collage. Attribute it to the found cover, not to the artist's wit.

3. **Dimensions.** Small: **35.9 × 23.8 cm (14.1 × 9.4 in)**, support 359 × 238 mm per Tate. Don't inflate it — people assume a big canvas; it's letter-paper-sized. (Give inches per house rule; cm in parens only as needed.)

4. **The 1952 Independent Group date.** The collage was **made in 1947** but **first shown publicly in 1952**, in the **"Bunk" lecture at the ICA, London**, which was the **first/inaugural meeting of the Independent Group**, projected by **epidiascope**. Don't merge the two dates (made 1947 ≠ shown 1952) and don't place the lecture at the Tate.

5. **Collage vs. 1972 print.** There are two things with this title: the **unique 1947 paper collage** (Tate T01462 — this work) and a **1972 screenprint** reproduction issued as part of the 46-print *BUNK!* portfolio (e.g., V&A CIRC.542-1972; RAF Museum; National Galleries of Scotland). The read is about the **original 1947 collage** — make sure the wired image and all facts describe the collage, not the screenprint.

6. **Paris vs. London sourcing.** Material was **collected during his 1947–49 Paris residency**, much of it **from American ex-servicemen** (some London-shop sourcing is also noted). Don't over-pin it to a single "a GI in Paris handed him magazines" anecdote as if documented verbatim — say "from American servicemen, during his Paris years."
