## Movement hook

Five painters who never agreed about anything got grouped together by an English critic in a London gallery twenty-four years after the youngest of them had painted his best picture. The label stuck. None of them ever heard it.

## hookLong

"Post-Impressionism" is one of the strangest labels in art history, because the people inside it were not a movement, did not meet, did not write a manifesto, and were mostly dead by the time anyone called them this. Roger Fry — an English critic with a London gallery to fill in the autumn of 1910 — needed an umbrella name for a roomful of French pictures that came after Impressionism but weren't Fauvism or Cubism yet. He picked *Post-Impressionists* almost on the fly, and the name stuck to five very different painters working in five different cities, mostly in disagreement with one another, all of them answering Impressionism in their own way. Cézanne wanted weight. Van Gogh wanted feeling. Gauguin wanted flat color and symbol. Seurat wanted science. Toulouse-Lautrec wanted the modern dance hall. Five answers, one room. This is the story of those five answers — and of the wider cast around them — and of the English critic who, very late, walked through and gave them a name.

## Chapter 1

- **id:** `why`
- **eyebrow:** Paris · 1886
- **dateLabel:** Why the eye wasn't enough
- **title:** After the moment, the structure
- **blurb:** Impressionism caught the dazzle; what it couldn't catch was weight. At the last Impressionist show, the next generation walks in.

### SectionHeader: rue Laffitte · spring 1886 — The room where it tips over — first

Picture a long, hot, awkward upstairs room in Paris in the late spring of 1886, above a fashionable restaurant called the Maison Dorée on rue Laffitte. The walls are crowded with paintings hung two and three high. Outside on the boulevard the city is doing its modern thing — boulevards, carriages, cafés, the whole bright Haussmann pageant the previous generation had spent twenty years figuring out how to paint. This is the **8th Impressionist Exhibition** (the eighth and last group show the Impressionists would ever mount — they had been doing them since 1874, the year a hostile critic gave them the name as a joke), and it runs from **15 May to 15 June 1886**. Most of the founding Impressionists are not even here. Monet, Renoir, Sisley and Caillebotte have all defected back to the Salon (the official state-run annual exhibition, the only door to a career in French art for two centuries) for this round. The cooperative is on its last legs.

And the picture stopping every visitor in front of it is by a 26-year-old none of them had quite figured out yet.

It is enormous — about six and a half feet tall, ten feet wide. It shows a Sunday afternoon on a small island in the Seine, a crowd of stiff, dignified Parisians and their dogs and parasols arranged across a striped lawn under flat dappled light. And the entire surface — every blade of grass, every parasol, every hat — is built out of millions of tiny separate dots of pure color, laid down like a mosaic. Not strokes. *Dots.* Step back four feet and it shimmers; step back ten and it locks into place. **Georges Seurat**'s *A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte* (he had been working on it for two years; it is now in the Art Institute of Chicago) is the painting that — politely, almost academically — kills its parents.

What you are watching in that room, if you knew to look, is a generation tipping over. Some of the older Impressionists walked out muttering. Monet had refused to show alongside it. Pissarro, the oldest and steadiest of the Impressionists, had stood up for Seurat and Signac and demanded they be included; for that he got called a deserter by his own friends. The younger painters in the room — Seurat himself, his partner Paul Signac, a few others coming up — stayed. They had seen what the elders had done, and they thought they could see what it had missed.

### SectionHeader: The trick the eye couldn't catch — Weight, meaning, feeling

What Impressionism had done, brilliantly, was teach a generation to paint *light*. The flicker of a shadow on snow. The smear of an orange sun across a gray dawn harbor. The dazzle of a summer afternoon at a riverside bathing spot. The Impressionists had figured out how to put the *instant* on canvas — the optical truth of a single moment, before the light changed and ruined it. (The previous level of this read tells that story in full.)

But the instant is, by definition, weightless. It doesn't stay. It doesn't *mean* anything beyond itself. And as the 1880s wore on, that was starting to feel like a wall. The younger painters looking at the late Impressionist canvases — at Monet's haystacks dissolving into colored air, at Renoir's pink-and-cream bathers losing their bones — could see, plainly, that something was missing. Where was the architecture? Where was the structure that lasts? Where, for that matter, was the *feeling* — the inside of a picture, the thing it was about, not just the thing it looked like?

The objection had four parts, and you can put one anchor against each:

- **Weight.** A mountain has weight. Light passing over a mountain does not. Impressionism had taught itself to paint the second and quietly given up on the first. — *Cézanne, in Aix.*
- **Feeling.** A wheatfield is yellow at noon. So what? What does the wheatfield *feel* like to a man who has lost his mind a little bit and stands in front of it weeping? — *Van Gogh, in Arles.*
- **Meaning.** A Breton peasant kneeling in church doesn't *see* a Bible story; she *imagines* one. How do you paint imagination, not vision? — *Gauguin, in Pont-Aven.*
- **Science.** The Impressionists were eyeballing optical mixing — broken color, complementary shadows — by instinct. What if you did it properly, by the laws of physics, with method? — *Seurat, in Paris.*

A fifth painter — Toulouse-Lautrec — was the youngest of them and was already in Montmartre, painting the dance hall not the way Renoir had painted it (a sunlit Sunday afternoon, lovely and a little blurred) but the way it really looked at midnight, lit by a flat green gaslight, full of paid performers and tired punters. None of these five would have called what they were doing "Post-Impressionism." None of them would have called it anything together at all. They worked in different cities, often barely knew each other, and would mostly have been horrified to be hung in the same room. But each was, in his own way, picking up where Impressionism had run out of road.

### SectionHeader: Aix-en-Provence — A mountain that refuses to dissolve

End the chapter in Provence. It is the summer of 1886 still; the rue Laffitte show is closing in Paris, and 500 miles to the south an angrier, slower, more patient painter has set up his easel in front of a mountain.

The mountain is **Mont Sainte-Victoire** — a long limestone ridge rising about a mile out of the plain east of the town of Aix, where **Paul Cézanne** (1839–1906) has lived since he was a boy, where his father ran a small bank, and where he now lives off the inheritance that bank produced. Cézanne is 47 in 1886, a stocky, awkward, doubt-ridden man who has been ridiculed in Paris for twenty years, who showed in only two of the Impressionist exhibitions (the first, in 1874, and the third, in 1877) and quietly withdrew, and who is now painting essentially in isolation. He has just married his longtime partner Hortense Fiquet, mostly to legitimize their son; his father has just died, leaving him at last financially safe.

And he is painting that mountain. He paints it from the north, from the west, from his brother-in-law's terrace, from the road, from the studio he will eventually build at Les Lauves a few years later. He paints it once. He paints it twice. He paints it, by the time he is done in 1906, about thirty times in oil and many more times in watercolor. Every time, the mountain is built out of small blocky strokes of color set side by side like masonry — not blended, not dissolved, but stacked into planes of weight that read, slowly, as the mountain.

What he is doing, in the year the Seurat hangs in Paris, is the exact opposite of Impressionism. The Impressionists tried to catch the instant of light eating the world. Cézanne is trying to find the *thing the light is eating* — the underlying structure that survives weather and time. The mountain, for him, is the test case. The mountain refuses to dissolve. (He will say it, much later, in a famous letter we'll get to in Chapter 3: nature should be treated by means of *the cylinder, the sphere, the cone*. Architecture, not atmosphere.)

So here are the two ends of the same year. In Paris, a 26-year-old has finished a vast pointillist canvas that quietly buries Impressionism in dots. In Provence, a 47-year-old is staring at a mountain and rebuilding it out of bricks of paint. Neither of them knows the other has done it. Neither calls himself anything in particular. Twenty-four years later, an English critic will hang them in the same room and give them all a name.

## Chapter 2

- **id:** `five`
- **eyebrow:** 1886–1905
- **dateLabel:** The five who weren't a group
- **title:** Cézanne, Van Gogh, Gauguin, Seurat, Lautrec
- **blurb:** Five painters in five cities, mostly hating each other, each answering Impressionism a different way. The list isn't a movement — it's a label, applied later.

### SectionHeader: The shape of the room — Five answers, no creed — first

If you had walked into an art-historical lecture in Paris in 1905 and asked, "who are the Post-Impressionists?", you would have got a blank look. The word didn't exist. The French had more precise, smaller categories: **Néo-Impressionnistes** (Seurat and his pointillist partner Paul Signac), **Synthétistes** or **École de Pont-Aven** (Gauguin, Émile Bernard and the cluster of painters around them in Brittany), **Nabis** (a younger group orbiting around the Pont-Aven idea — we get to them in Chapter 7), **Symbolistes** (Odilon Redon and the painters of dreams). What French art history did not have was an umbrella big enough to hold all of these *plus* Cézanne in his Provençal sulk *plus* Van Gogh, who was Dutch and dead, *plus* Toulouse-Lautrec, who painted brothels.

The five we now call the anchors — **Cézanne, Van Gogh, Gauguin, Seurat, Toulouse-Lautrec** — never sat in a room together. The only documented overlap is the famous nine-week disaster in Arles when Gauguin came to stay with Van Gogh (Chapter 4); the rest are five separate stories. They are five answers to Impressionism, not one creed. Here is one paragraph per anchor — the move each one made, the rest of the chapter to be filled in later.

### SectionHeader: Cézanne — The architect

**Paul Cézanne** (1839–1906; Aix-en-Provence). He thought Impressionism was "only an eye" — meaning brilliant at the optical surface and helpless about the underlying structure. (His own line about Monet, reported later by Émile Bernard: *Monet is only an eye, but what an eye!* — an admiring insult.) He wanted to take the Impressionist palette and the Impressionist habit of working from nature and use them to rebuild the picture into something solid — planes of color stacked like masonry, weight you could feel, geometry that lasted. He worked slowly, mostly alone, mostly in Provence, ignored by the Paris market for thirty years. Then in 1895 a young dealer named **Ambroise Vollard** gave him a one-man show (Galerie Vollard, Paris, around 150 works), and other painters began to notice. After he died in 1906, a memorial retrospective at the **Salon d'Automne** (the modernist annual exhibition founded in Paris in 1903 as an alternative to the official Salon) — opening October 1907, about 56 works — finished the job. Picasso and Braque were there. Within months they had started Cubism. Chapter 3 is Cézanne's.

### SectionHeader: Van Gogh — The colorist of feeling

**Vincent van Gogh** (1853–1890; Dutch, working mostly in France). He didn't pick up a brush seriously until he was 28, which is to say he had a ten-year career, beginning to end. What he did with it was decide that color was not optics — it was *feeling*. A yellow wheatfield was not the wavelength of yellow; it was what yellow *did* to the heart of the man standing in it. He wrote it to his brother Theo in August 1888, from Arles: *Instead of trying to reproduce exactly what I have before my eyes, I use color more arbitrarily in order to express myself forcibly.* He spent two and a half years working at top speed — Arles, then the asylum at Saint-Rémy, then the village of Auvers-sur-Oise — produced something like 900 paintings in his life, sold very few of them for actual money, and died by gunshot in a field outside Auvers in July 1890 at age 37. Chapter 4 is Van Gogh's.

### SectionHeader: Gauguin — The synthesist

**Paul Gauguin** (1848–1903; French, but he died on the other side of the planet). An ex-stockbroker (he lost his job in the 1882 Paris stock-market crash and decided that since he was broke anyway he might as well be a painter) who came up through Impressionism but quickly decided it was too literal. He wanted flat areas of saturated color, separated by dark contour lines, like stained glass or like Japanese woodblock prints, with the picture's *symbolism* — its meaning, what it was about — running on top of the optical surface rather than buried inside it. He worked this out in a small Brittany village called **Pont-Aven** in 1888 — and the name stuck to the cluster of painters who worked there with him (the **École de Pont-Aven**, "school of Pont-Aven"). Then, in 1891, he sailed for Tahiti to pursue an idea of "primitive" art that turns out, in 21st-century light, to be complicated in ways we'll have to deal with honestly (Chapter 5). He died on the island of Hiva Oa in the Marquesas in 1903, 54 years old. Chapter 5 is Gauguin's.

### SectionHeader: Seurat — The scientist of color

**Georges Seurat** (1859–1891; Paris). Academically trained — he studied at the **École des Beaux-Arts** (the official state art school, the very gatekeeper the Impressionists had spent twenty years working around) under a former pupil of Ingres — and he carried the academic patience with him into the studio. He read color theory: **Michel Eugène Chevreul**'s 1839 book on the *law of simultaneous contrast of colors* (a French chemist's discovery that two colors placed side by side will alter each other in the eye, the basis of every later instinct about complementary shadows), and the American physicist **Ogden Rood**'s *Modern Chromatics* (1879, translated into French in 1881). Out of these he built **Divisionism** — sometimes called **Pointillism** — the technique of laying small, separate dots of pure color side by side so the eye, at viewing distance, mixes them into a luminous whole, brighter and more vibrant than any mixture pre-blended on the palette. He spent two years on a single canvas (*La Grande Jatte*, the painting that opened this chapter), then spent the rest of his short life making more. He died, suddenly, in spring 1891, at 31. He had been working since he was 19. Chapter 6, with Lautrec.

### SectionHeader: Toulouse-Lautrec — The chronicler of Montmartre

**Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec** (1864–1901; born into the old French aristocracy — his full name was *Henri Marie Raymond de Toulouse-Lautrec-Monfa*, descended from the medieval Counts of Toulouse). He broke both thigh bones in adolescence (the left in 1878, the right in 1879) and the legs stopped growing. He finished his life at about 5 ft tall on a normal adult torso. The medical cause is *inferred*, not proven — modern best guess is pycnodysostosis, a rare inherited disorder so closely associated with him it is sometimes called "Toulouse-Lautrec syndrome," but no autopsy was done, and the diagnosis is a 20th-century guess from his case history. What he did with his short, painful life was move to **Montmartre** (the working-class hilltop neighborhood of Paris where the rents were cheap and the dance halls and brothels and cabarets had all colonized the cleared land) in 1884 and paint it — the cabaret, the Moulin Rouge, the cafés-concerts, the brothels — borrowing the flat-color, hard-contour design of Japanese prints and making the modern poster a fine-art medium. He drank heavily, contracted syphilis, and died at 36, in his mother's château. Chapter 6 is his, paired with Seurat.

### SectionHeader: The room they end up in — A retrospective label

These five lived in different rooms — Aix, Auvers, Pont-Aven and then Tahiti, Paris, Montmartre — and sometimes hated each other (Gauguin and Van Gogh in Arles, December 1888, will turn out badly). Cézanne thought Gauguin was "a Chinese painter" and a thief of his ideas. Lautrec liked Van Gogh well enough — they met in Paris in 1886 — but their pictures could not have been more different.

They got grouped because, in **1910**, an English critic named **Roger Fry** needed a name for a roomful of pictures he had borrowed for a London gallery — Cézanne, Van Gogh, Gauguin, Seurat, Lautrec, plus a wider cast of younger Frenchmen the public had also never seen. By 1910, Cézanne had been dead four years, Van Gogh twenty, Gauguin seven, Seurat nineteen, Lautrec nine. Not one of the five painters Fry was naming was alive to argue with him. He called them, in want of a better word, *Post-Impressionists*. The name stuck because the public didn't have a better one. Chapter 8 is the story of how that room came together.

## Chapter 3

- **id:** `cezanne`
- **eyebrow:** Aix-en-Provence · 1870s–1906
- **dateLabel:** The mountain refuses to dissolve
- **title:** Cézanne — the cylinder, the sphere, the cone
- **blurb:** A banker's son in Provence, painting a mountain over and over, building the world out of planes. When he died, the young painters in Paris discovered him. Cubism started six months later.

### SectionHeader: Aix · the inheritance — A bank, a son, a refusal — first

Start with the father, because in Cézanne's case the father is what makes the painter possible. **Louis-Auguste Cézanne** was a self-made man — a hatter who got into banking in Aix-en-Provence in the 1840s, co-founded the *Banque Cézanne-Cabassol*, and by the 1860s was one of the richest men in town. He wanted his son to be a lawyer. Paul, born 19 January 1839, dutifully tried — he was at law school in Aix in 1859 and 1860 — and then, with his father's eventual grudging permission, decamped to Paris in 1861 to paint.

What it bought him was the rarest thing a French painter could have in the late 19th century: the freedom not to sell. Cézanne never had to flatter the Salon, never had to court a dealer, never had to paint to a buyer's taste, because his father's money kept the household running and, when the father died in 1886, kept it running through Cézanne's own death in 1906. While his Impressionist friends in Paris were burning themselves out trying to make rent, Cézanne in Aix was free, year after year, to be slow.

And slowness is the heart of him. Cézanne worked through a canvas with the patience of a man laying a stone wall. He could spend an entire morning on a single brushstroke, considering it, mixing it, putting it down, scraping it back off. He told an early friend, the writer Émile Zola — they had known each other since school in Aix — that he was trying to make of Impressionism *something solid and durable, like the art of the museums.* (The quote is paraphrased, reported later by Bernard; treat it as the painter's aim, not a verbatim line.) The Impressionists tried to catch the moment. Cézanne wanted to build the thing the moment was passing over.

### SectionHeader: The two great series — Cards and bathers, mountain and apples

He worked in series. Once he found a subject he could believe in, he stayed with it for years.

The **Card Players** is one. Between about **1890 and 1895** he painted **five versions** — at his own farmhouse, the **Jas de Bouffan** ("the gust-house," the rambling Cézanne family estate just outside Aix) — of two or sometimes more local farm laborers sitting at a small wooden table, a bottle between them, looking down at their cards in absolute silence. The five canvases run from the largest at the **Barnes Foundation** in Philadelphia (about 4 ft 5 in × 5 ft 11 in, five figures — three players plus two onlookers) down to the smallest at the **Musée d'Orsay**, Paris (about 1 ft 6¾ in × 1 ft 10½ in, just two players). They are silent paintings — peasant gravity rendered with the seriousness Caravaggio would have given a saint. The fifth version sold privately in 2011, reportedly to the Qatari royal family, for a figure variously reported between $250 and $320 million; at the time it was the highest price ever paid for a painting.

The **Bathers** is the other. Across the **1890s and into 1905** he painted **three large versions** of female nudes arranged in a wooded landscape — the biggest, at the **Philadelphia Museum of Art**, is about **7 ft × 8 ft 2 in**, and Cézanne worked on it for seven years. They are not Renoir's pink-cream bathers in a sunny meadow. The figures are blocky, gendered only in outline, with faces like masks; the trees lean inward to form a Gothic arch over the scene. This is not eroticism, it is **architecture made of bodies** — the picture's structure is what the picture is about. (The Bathers will haunt Picasso when he comes to paint the *Demoiselles d'Avignon* in 1907, a fact Picasso later admitted.)

And then there is **Mont Sainte-Victoire** — the limestone ridge east of Aix. From the early 1880s to the year he died in 1906, Cézanne painted the mountain something like **30 times in oil and many more in watercolor**. The early versions, painted from a hill called **Bibémus** or from his brother-in-law's terrace at **Bellevue**, are recognizable landscapes — there is a foreground field, a middle ground of red-tile roofs, a far ridge of blue-gray rock. The late versions — painted from the studio he built at **Les Lauves**, just north of Aix, in 1902 — strip out almost everything. By 1906 the mountain is barely a mountain anymore. It is a slope of overlapping blue and green and orange planes, with the white of the canvas left showing through in places, the way the unstuccoed brick shows through a Roman ruin. The mountain has become its own scaffolding.

The **Philadelphia Museum of Art** version of 1902–04 (about 2 ft 4¾ in × 3 ft 0¼ in; The George W. Elkins Collection) is the one art-history reproduces most often when it wants to make the point. The **Kunstmuseum Basel** version, 1904–06, is the most abstract — the all-planes one, the mountain almost dismantled.

### SectionHeader: 15 April 1904 — One sentence, one young painter

Now the letter, because this is where Cézanne accidentally gives modernism its catechism.

A young painter named **Émile Bernard** (1868–1941) — French, formerly close to Gauguin in Pont-Aven, where the two of them had worked out **Synthetism** together in 1888 — had come down to Aix in early 1904 to visit Cézanne. They talked about painting. Bernard went home to Paris and the two of them began a correspondence, Bernard asking advice, Cézanne giving it. On **15 April 1904** Cézanne wrote him a letter (the original is at the **Courtauld Gallery** in London; the manuscript number is ms-1932-SC-1-1, acquired with the Samuel Courtauld bequest of 1932) that contained the sentence the whole 20th century would quote:

> *Allow me to repeat what I said when you were here: treat nature by means of the cylinder, the sphere, the cone, the whole put into perspective, so that each side of an object or a plane is directed towards a central point. Lines parallel to the horizon give breadth … Lines perpendicular to that horizon give depth.* (Translation Danchev, 2013; the French original is *traiter la nature par le cylindre, la sphère, le cône, le tout mis en perspective…*)

Be careful what this is. It is not a manifesto. It is one sentence in a long, informal letter to a younger painter, who had specifically asked Cézanne for technical advice. Cézanne was *not* prescribing Cubism in advance. He meant something more modest and more practical: when you look at a tree, look for the underlying cylindrical mass of the trunk; when you look at an apple, see the sphere it is built from; when you look at the roof of a barn, see the cone. Build the picture out of those geometries first, then add the rest. It is a sketcher's piece of advice — the kind of thing a teacher tells a student to stop them painting outlines and start them painting volumes.

What Picasso and Braque and Bernard himself did with the sentence — quote it back at the world as the founding theorem of modern art — was their decision, not Cézanne's. Cézanne never knew the line would become the line. He died two and a half years later.

### SectionHeader: October 1906 — The rainstorm

He died on **22 October 1906**, in Aix, at 67. The way he died was characteristic: he had been working outdoors, was caught in a thunderstorm, kept painting, walked home soaked, collapsed in the street, was carried back to his bed, developed pneumonia, and died days later. He had been at work on a portrait of his gardener Vallier and on another Mont Sainte-Victoire. The mountain was unfinished. There were always more.

Now watch what happens to him in death, because the speed of it is the point.

Cézanne had been a Paris cult for the last decade of his life — the Vollard show in 1895 had given him a market, and **the Salon d'Automne** (founded 1903) had begun giving him memorial rooms while he was still alive: **1904** (a room of about 30 of his works), **1905** (10 works), **1906** (10 works again). Each one was watched closely by the younger painters in Paris. Then in **October 1907**, one year after his death, the Salon d'Automne mounted the full retrospective — about **56 works**, hung together, the late ones included — and every young painter in Paris filed through it.

**Pablo Picasso** was 25, living broke at the top of a Montmartre tenement. **Georges Braque** was 25, a Norman, just then a young Fauve (the wild-color movement that had been named in 1905). **Henri Matisse** was 37. **André Derain** was 27. **Maurice de Vlaminck** was 31. They all walked through that show. They all came out of it changed.

Picasso would say, later, that *"Cézanne was my one and only master."* Braque would later say that the late Cézannes were like discovering a country no one had been in. Within months of the 1907 retrospective Picasso was painting *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* — the picture with which Cubism begins — and within a year he and Braque were stacking houses into little cubes at L'Estaque. (The Cubism page is the next door over.)

It is hard to say one show alone caused all of that — the memorial rooms of 1904–06 had been priming the ground for three years, and the Demoiselles was already in progress in early 1907 — but the cumulative effect of the late Cézannes coming out of Aix into Paris is the single most concentrated transmission line in modern art. The mountain refused to dissolve, and the young men in Paris saw it.

## Chapter 4

- **id:** `van-gogh`
- **eyebrow:** Arles · 1888–1890
- **dateLabel:** Colour as feeling
- **title:** Van Gogh — the yellow house, the wheat, the gun
- **blurb:** A late starter who painted his life — under 900 canvases in about ten years — and died at 37 in a wheatfield outside Paris, having sold barely anything in his lifetime.

### SectionHeader: Zundert · 1853 — A late start, a brother — first

The hardest fact about Van Gogh, the one the romance always blurs, is how *late* he started. **Vincent van Gogh** was born in **Zundert** — a small village in the southern Netherlands, just north of the Belgian border — on **30 March 1853**, the eldest surviving son of a Dutch Reformed pastor. He did not begin to paint seriously until **1881**, when he was 28 years old. He died nine years and four months later, at 37. The career is shorter than a college sports star's.

What had he done in the first 28 years? He had tried to be an art dealer. The firm was **Goupil & Cie**, the great Paris-based 19th-century picture-selling house; Vincent worked at their branches in The Hague, London, and Paris between 1869 and 1876, and was eventually fired for being temperamental with customers. He had tried to be a lay preacher, in the **Borinage** coal-mining district of southern Belgium, where he gave away his possessions to the miners and was dismissed by the Mission for "excessive zeal." He had been in and out of his parents' house, the houses of strange women he tried to live with, the houses of artist relatives. He was, by the standards of his bourgeois Dutch family, a wreck.

The one thing that does not fail him is his younger brother **Theo van Gogh** (1857–1891), four years younger, who by the 1880s has himself become an art dealer in Paris — at **Boussod, Valadon & Cie**, the successor firm to Goupil. From the late 1870s until Vincent's death, Theo sends Vincent money every month. He houses him in Paris when Vincent finally comes. He sells what little he can sell. He writes him hundreds of letters; Vincent writes him hundreds back. Without Theo there is no painter Van Gogh. (Hold this, because the brothers die six months apart.)

Vincent picks up a brush around 1881. He spends the next five years in the Netherlands and Belgium painting peasants and weavers in deep, muddy browns and greens — the most famous of these is **The Potato Eaters** (1885, Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam), a dark earth-colored picture of five weavers around a lamp eating boiled potatoes. It is not yet the Van Gogh of legend. The colors are Rembrandt's. Then in **March 1886** Vincent moves to Paris, into Theo's apartment on the rue Lepic in Montmartre, and meets the Impressionists.

### SectionHeader: Paris, then Arles — A color education and a yellow house

Paris was Vincent's two-year color crash course. He saw the Impressionists at the 8th show (Chapter 1) and at the dealers' shops. He met **Pissarro**, who became a generous teacher. He met **Toulouse-Lautrec**, who took to him. He encountered Japanese woodblock prints in volume — Theo's apartment ended up papered with them. He started experimenting with bright, separated strokes of color and abandoned the muddy palette. He painted nearly 200 canvases in those Paris months. He also drank, fought with Theo, and wore his brother out.

By February **1888** he had had enough of Paris and got on a train south. He stepped off in **Arles**, in Provence, looking — he wrote — for "the Japan of the south," meaning the clean, bright, hard light he thought the south of France would give him in place of Paris gray. The town was a sleepy provincial Roman survival of about 20,000 people on the Rhône.

In May 1888 he rented half a small two-story building at **2 place Lamartine** — the **Yellow House**, painted yellow on the outside, with green shutters — intending to make it the headquarters of an artists' colony he would persuade his friends to come and join. He furnished it, slowly, on Theo's money. He bought a bed. He painted a picture of his bedroom (the **first version** of *Bedroom in Arles*, October 1888, now Van Gogh Museum — a small room in lemon yellow and cobalt blue and brick red, almost a child's diagram of a room, *about 2 ft 4½ in × 3 ft 0 in*, oil on canvas). He painted the sunflowers — twelve giant single-stalked sunflowers in a yellow vase against a yellow wall, painted as decoration for the Yellow House's spare bedroom, the one he was preparing for the friend he was hoping above all to come south.

That friend was **Paul Gauguin** (Chapter 5). Van Gogh had met Gauguin in Paris in 1887 and admired him intensely. He spent the summer of 1888 lobbying — through Theo, who was now Gauguin's dealer too — for Gauguin to come down to Arles and live with him in the Yellow House. Gauguin, broke and out of options, finally agreed. He arrived on **23 October 1888**.

### SectionHeader: October–December 1888 — Nine weeks, two painters, one ear

What followed was nine weeks of co-habitation that destroyed the friendship, ended the dream of the southern colony, and produced — partly out of sheer adrenal terror — some of the best work either painter ever made. (Gauguin's side of this is Chapter 5; here is Vincent's.)

They worked side by side, painted the same subjects (the public garden, the local Roman ruins, a portrait of the local postman's wife, the night café). They argued constantly. Gauguin thought Van Gogh should paint less from nature and more from memory; Van Gogh hated working from memory. Gauguin thought the cooking was atrocious; Van Gogh, who was buying it, took it personally. They drank heavily — absinthe, the bitter green wormwood-based liquor that 1880s painters lived on. By mid-December they were screaming at each other.

The night of **23 December 1888** is the famous one. The exact sequence is debated — Gauguin's account, written years later, is not entirely trustworthy — but the broad shape is documented: there was a quarrel; Gauguin walked out of the Yellow House intending to spend the night at a hotel; Vincent followed him into the street, possibly with a razor; Gauguin scared him off; Vincent went back to the Yellow House and, in the bathroom, **cut off the lower lobe of his left ear** (not the whole ear — this is the standard correction; the part severed was the lower portion of the auricle, an inch or so of flesh). He wrapped it in newspaper, walked it down to a brothel he frequented, and gave it to one of the women working there, a woman known in the records as **Rachel**, asking her to "keep this object carefully." He went home, passed out from blood loss, and was found the next morning. Gauguin telegraphed Theo, then left town.

Vincent was hospitalized in Arles. In May 1889, he voluntarily admitted himself to the asylum at **Saint-Paul-de-Mausole**, in **Saint-Rémy-de-Provence**, fifteen miles north of Arles. He stayed for a year. He was permitted to paint. He produced, during that year, some of the most famous canvases of his life — including ***The Starry Night***, painted in **June 1889**, from memory and the view out his barred east-facing window: a sleeping village under an immense rolling sky of swirling spiral stars and a crescent moon, a flaming cypress in the foreground. (Now at MoMA, New York, acquired 1941; about 2 ft 5 in × 3 ft 0¼ in.) It is the most reproduced single picture in Western art after the *Mona Lisa*, and Van Gogh painted it locked up.

### SectionHeader: Auvers · summer 1890 — The wheatfield and the gun

In May 1890 he left Saint-Rémy and travelled north — first to Theo and Theo's wife Jo in Paris for three days, then thirty kilometers north of Paris to the village of **Auvers-sur-Oise**, where Theo had arranged for him to be under the care of **Dr Paul Gachet**, a homeopathic physician who painted on the side and collected the Impressionists. Van Gogh painted Gachet's portrait twice. (The second one — ***Portrait of Dr Gachet*** — would sell at Christie's New York on 15 May 1990 for **$82.5 million** to the Japanese paper-industry tycoon Ryoei Saito, the auction record for an Impressionist or Post-Impressionist work for years.)

He painted Auvers in a frenzy. He had been working at the rate of about a canvas a day for two and a half years; in Auvers he kept it up. The town church. The wheatfields under stormy skies. *Wheatfield with Crows* (Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam), a flat horizontal wheatfield under a roiling blue-black sky with three paths splitting away from the viewer and a flock of crows lifting off. The film *Lust for Life* (1956) called this his last picture; the Van Gogh Museum's current view is that it isn't — *Tree Roots* (also Auvers, July 1890) is probably the last canvas he worked on. Treat *Wheatfield with Crows* as one of his last, not the last.

On the afternoon of **Sunday, 27 July 1890**, Van Gogh walked out into a field near Auvers — the exact spot is traditional but never proven, and the traditional location is "a wheatfield" but this is romance not record — and shot himself in the chest with a small revolver. The shot did not kill him. He staggered back to the **Auberge Ravoux**, the inn where he was lodging, and went up to his room. The Ravoux family fetched two doctors, but the bullet was lodged where they couldn't get at it. Theo arrived from Paris the next morning. Vincent died in the early hours of **29 July 1890**, around 1:30 a.m., with Theo at his side. He was 37. Theo reported his last words to their mother as *"La tristesse durera toujours"* — *"the sadness will last forever."*

(One hedge that is important to make. In **2011** the biographers Steven Naifeh and Gregory White Smith published a hypothesis that Van Gogh did not shoot himself, but was shot — accidentally or maliciously — by a 16-year-old boy named **René Secrétan** who was vacationing in Auvers and had been tormenting him. The argument rests on the strange direction of the entry wound and on second-hand testimony recorded decades later. It is **a hypothesis**, not the consensus; the Van Gogh Museum continues to treat the death as a suicide. Mention the Naifeh-Smith theory as the most-discussed alternative; do not write it as fact.)

**Theo** died six months later, on **25 January 1891**, age 33, in Utrecht — of complications related to syphilis that had been advancing for years. He had not got over Vincent. Jo, Theo's wife, was left with a one-year-old baby (also named Vincent), Theo's papers, Theo's letters from Vincent — hundreds and hundreds of them — and most of the unsold paintings. She spent the rest of her life building Vincent's posthumous reputation; she is the reason the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam exists.

### SectionHeader: Sales in life — One painting, with a footnote

The famous line is that Van Gogh sold only one painting in his lifetime. The truth is a little more textured.

The marquee sale is **The Red Vineyard** — a square canvas of grape pickers in a fiery red-orange autumn vineyard at sunset, painted in November 1888 in Arles. It was exhibited at **Les XX** ("the Twenty"), the avant-garde Belgian exhibiting society's annual show in Brussels (**18 January – 23 February 1890**). A Belgian painter and collector named **Anna Boch** — the sister of Eugène Boch, a young painter whose portrait Van Gogh had done in Arles — bought it for **400 francs** (about **£16** at the time). The sale was completed in **March or April 1890**, while Van Gogh was still alive and just leaving Saint-Rémy. He saw the money. The picture is now in the **Pushkin State Museum**, Moscow (it was bought in the early 20th century by the Russian collector Sergei Shchukin, whose collection was nationalized after the 1917 revolution).

That is the only sale of a painting at a real price during his life. The Van Gogh Museum has, however, qualified the "only one painting" line: there were at least a handful of other minor transactions — a self-portrait sold to a London dealer, a few studies that moved through Theo's hands, exchanges with other painters. So the precise wording is: *the only painting he is known to have sold to a public buyer at full price.* Not literally the only piece of paper that ever changed hands.

It is still a brutal arithmetic. He painted around 900 canvases. He sold one for money he could spend. He died face-down in a field. And within twenty years his pictures would be the most fought-over canvases in Europe.

## Chapter 5

- **id:** `gauguin`
- **eyebrow:** Pont-Aven · 1886; Tahiti · 1891–1903
- **dateLabel:** Flat colour, big symbol
- **title:** Gauguin — Pont-Aven, Tahiti, the colonial question
- **blurb:** An ex-stockbroker who walked out of his marriage, found his style in Brittany, then sailed to Tahiti chasing a fantasy that was a French colony in real life. The pictures matter. The biography is harder.

### SectionHeader: Paris · 1882 — The crash, the wife, the leap — first

**Paul Gauguin** was born in Paris on **7 June 1848**, the son of a French journalist father and a Peruvian-French mother. His maternal grandmother was **Flora Tristan**, the socialist writer and women's-rights activist (one of the more interesting ancestors any French painter ever had). When Gauguin was 1, his family sailed for Peru, where his mother had relatives; his father died en route. The boy grew up in **Lima** until he was 7, in Spanish, surrounded by his mother's family — that early displacement matters; he never quite settled back into being French.

By his 20s he was a Parisian stockbroker, working at the firm of Galichon Bourdon. He was good at it. In **1873** he married **Mette-Sophie Gad**, a Danish woman; they would have **five children** between 1874 and 1883. He started painting on the side and collecting Impressionists with his bourse income. By the early 1880s he was a serious enough amateur to show in three of the late Impressionist exhibitions.

Then in **1882** the Paris stock market crashed — the famous *krach de l'Union générale*, January 1882, the largest French financial panic of the 19th century — and Gauguin lost his job. He decided, with the casual ruthlessness that would mark the rest of his life, to take this as a sign. He would commit full-time to painting. He moved the family to **Copenhagen** in 1884 to stay with Mette's parents. The Danish in-laws found him impossible. By **1885** he had walked out of the marriage. He returned to Paris alone. He never lived with his wife and children again. He sent the occasional letter, the occasional small remittance; he was, by any honest accounting, an absent father.

(There is no charitable way to tell this part of the story. Gauguin was not poor, in 1885, in the way Van Gogh was poor. He was a man with a wife and five small children whom he chose to leave behind so that he could be a painter. The art does not exist without that decision. The decision is part of the art's price.)

### SectionHeader: Pont-Aven · 1886, 1888 — Brittany, Bernard, and a red field

In summer 1886 — the summer of the Seurat *Grande Jatte* in Paris, Chapter 1 — Gauguin went on cheap holiday to **Pont-Aven**, a small Brittany village on the Aven river that had become a painters' colony because the pension rates were low and the local women still wore picturesque white starched bonnets. It was his first encounter with the place that would crack his style open.

He came back to Pont-Aven in **summer 1888**, this time with a clear program. He met a younger painter named **Émile Bernard** — 19 years old, energetic, theoretically minded, the friend who would later be the recipient of Cézanne's *cylinder, sphere, cone* letter (Chapter 3). The two of them began working out what they would call **Synthetism** (sometimes also **cloisonnisme**, after the cloisonné enamels — small areas of colored glass separated by metal wires — that the technique visually resembled): paintings built out of large, flat areas of saturated color, separated by hard dark contour lines, with depth, modeling and atmospheric finish ruthlessly cut out. The picture was no longer a window onto a real scene. It was an arrangement of color *areas*, designed for emotional or symbolic effect.

The picture that demonstrates the move is **Gauguin's *Vision after the Sermon (Jacob Wrestling with the Angel)***, painted at Pont-Aven that autumn (now Scottish National Gallery, Edinburgh; *about 2 ft 4½ in × 3 ft 0 in*, oil on canvas). Look at what he does. In the lower half of the picture, a crowd of Breton peasant women in their starched white bonnets are coming out of mass; they have just heard the Sunday sermon about Jacob wrestling the angel. Their eyes are closed in something between prayer and exhaustion. In the upper half of the picture — separated from the women by a hard diagonal trunk of an apple tree slicing the canvas — Jacob and the angel are *actually visible* in the act of wrestling, against a field of **pure flat red**. Not red ground, not red landscape, not red light: a flat saturated red, the kind of red a child reaches for first in a paint box.

What is going on, then, in this picture? Where is the wrestling really happening? Not in the field. The wrestling is in the *minds* of the Breton women, who heard the sermon, closed their eyes, and *imagined* it. The painter has put the inside of their imagination on the canvas, as a separate zone, separated by a tree. The whole machinery of "a picture as a window onto a real scene" is dismantled. What you are looking at is *meaning*, painted directly.

Gauguin tried to give the canvas to the parish church at Pont-Aven. The priest refused it — he could see immediately that the picture was *about* the women, not *about* Jacob, and that this was not a religious painting in any sense he would put on his wall. Gauguin took it back to Paris. The painters who saw it understood what he had done; the public never did.

### SectionHeader: Arles · 23 October – 23 December 1888 — Nine weeks in the Yellow House

This is Chapter 4's territory from the other side. Gauguin went south to **Arles** in October 1888 because he had run out of money, because Theo van Gogh would send him a small stipend for being there, and because Vincent had been pleading for him to come. He arrived at the Yellow House on 23 October. He left two days after the ear-cutting on 25 December.

Gauguin's later memoir of the nine weeks — *Avant et après*, written years later in Tahiti — is largely a self-protective recasting. The reliable contemporaneous record is Vincent's letters to Theo. They argued constantly. They painted side by side. Gauguin painted Vincent painting sunflowers; Vincent painted Gauguin's chair (empty, in the Yellow House at night, with a single candle and two novels on the seat — one of the saddest still lifes in painting). They drank. The breakdown is in Chapter 4.

What Gauguin took from Arles, beyond the trauma, was a confirmation that his future was not in France. He had been broken by the dependency on Van Gogh, on the Yellow House scheme, on the European art market that wouldn't buy his pictures. He went back to Paris with the idea, now hardening, that he would go somewhere very far away.

### SectionHeader: 1 April 1891 — The boat to Tahiti

On **1 April 1891**, Gauguin sailed from Marseille for **Tahiti**. He arrived in Papeete on 9 June 1891. He had told the friends who saw him off — including the Symbolist writers Mallarmé and Charles Morice — that he was going to find a *primitive*, pre-Christian, pre-industrial paradise where he could paint freely, untouched by European convention. He was, in that sense, exporting a French romance of the *primitif* about as far as it could go.

What he was actually doing was sailing into a French colony. **Tahiti had been a French protectorate since 1842 and a formal colony since 1880.** Papeete had cathedrals, gendarmes, a port, French administrators, French missionaries, French gunboats. The Tahitian population had been decimated by introduced diseases over the previous century; the surviving Tahitians lived under colonial rule, paid colonial taxes, and were Catholic for the most part. Gauguin's *primitive* Tahiti was a fantasy he carried with him in his luggage; the real Tahiti was a French outpost where French colonial police came round when he made trouble.

He painted there anyway, and what he painted was extraordinary — the saturated, flat-color, dreamlike Tahitian pictures we now know him by: ***Manaò tupapaú (Spirit of the Dead Watching)***, 1892 (Buffalo AKG Art Museum, formerly Albright-Knox), a young Tahitian girl lying face-down on a bed under a yellow blanket, watched by a dark spirit-figure in the background; ***Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?***, 1897–98 (Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, *about 4 ft 6½ in × 12 ft 3½ in*, oil on canvas), a long horizontal mural Gauguin painted as his testament before attempting suicide with arsenic in late 1897 (he survived — the dose was too strong and he vomited it up). The title is inscribed in the picture's upper left, in French. Gauguin intended the picture to be read right-to-left: a baby on the right, an adult reaching for a fruit in the center, an old woman on the left. Birth, life, death.

### SectionHeader: The part of the story that doesn't sand down

Now the part of Gauguin's story that 21st-century writing on him cannot skim past, and that earlier 20th-century writing did skim past.

In Tahiti and later in the Marquesas, Gauguin lived with three successive Polynesian girls, all of whom were **adolescents** — **Teha'amana** (in Tahiti, from 1892, traditionally said to have been 13 when he took her into his house; the figure is uncertain but she was certainly a child); later **Pau'ura a Tai** (in Tahiti, around age 14 when their relationship began in 1896); and in the Marquesas, **Vaeoho Marie-Rose** (around 14 when she moved in with him in 1901). He had children by at least two of them. He referred to all three as his "wives" in his journals.

It is important not to project 21st-century legal categories backward without thinking — adolescent marriage was legal and common in late-19th-century France and across French colonial territory — and it is also important not to use that context to disappear what these relationships actually were. Gauguin was a European man in his 40s using colonial power and money to set up sexual partnerships with Polynesian girls who could not, by any honest reading of the situation, have refused him on equal terms. Several of his greatest paintings — including *Manaò tupapaú* — depict these girls. The pictures are the pictures, and they will not be undone; but a serious account of Gauguin cannot pretend the biography is just colorful.

He also had advanced **syphilis** by his last years (he had contracted it in France years earlier, and was treating it badly), and he spent his last decade in pain. In **September 1901** he moved from Tahiti to **Hiva Oa** in the **Marquesas**, the more remote island chain, and built himself a wooden house there which he called the *Maison du Jouir* — the "House of Pleasure," carved with sculpted nudes around the doorway. He was constantly at war with the local French colonial administration; he kept writing pamphlets attacking the colonial authorities for their treatment of the Polynesians, which is to his credit, while continuing his personal exploitation of Polynesian girls, which is not.

He died on **8 May 1903** on Hiva Oa, age 54, of a heart attack while taking morphine for the syphilis pain. The French Catholic bishop, with whom Gauguin had been feuding bitterly, buried him in the local cemetery. His pictures and effects were auctioned cheap on Tahiti the following autumn. Within fifteen years they would be unaffordable in any market on earth.

## Chapter 6

- **id:** `seurat-lautrec`
- **eyebrow:** Paris · 1884–1901
- **dateLabel:** Theory and the dance hall
- **title:** Seurat's dots, Lautrec's posters
- **blurb:** Two Paris painters who could not have been more different in temperament, both dead in their thirties. Seurat made color a science. Lautrec made the modern poster a fine art.

### SectionHeader: Paris · 1884 — A jury-free Salon — first

Open at the **Société des Artistes Indépendants**. In **1884**, a group of younger Paris painters — exhausted by the official Salon's rejections and unwilling to wait for the Impressionists to figure out their next move — got together and founded a new exhibiting society. The point of it was simple: **no jury, no prizes, no medals**. Anyone who paid the dues could hang. They would mount an annual **Salon des Indépendants** every spring (the first one ran from 15 May to 1 July 1884, in an army barracks on the Champs-Élysées; it would move to a beer hall later that year and to the Pavillon de la Ville de Paris in subsequent years), and that would be that. No more begging the academic gatekeepers for permission.

Among the founders were **Paul Signac** (more on him in a moment), **Odilon Redon** (the Symbolist painter of charcoal monsters and pastel dreams), **Albert Dubois-Pillet**, and **Henri Edmond Cross**. And a 24-year-old painter who had just had his first major canvas — a six-and-a-half-by-ten-foot picture of working-class Parisian men sunbathing on the Asnières riverbank — rejected by the official Salon a few weeks earlier. They hung his rejected picture instead.

The 24-year-old was **Georges Seurat**.

### SectionHeader: Seurat — A short life, a long method

**Georges-Pierre Seurat** was born in Paris on **2 December 1859**, into a moderately prosperous family. His father was a property speculator and an oddity — he lived semi-separately from the rest of the family in a house in the Paris suburbs, and visited his wife once a week for dinner. His mother raised the children in central Paris.

Seurat trained academically — first at a free local drawing school, then in **1878** at the **École des Beaux-Arts** under **Henri Lehmann**, who had been a pupil of the great Neoclassical painter Ingres. So Seurat, alone among the Post-Impressionist anchors, came up through the official system. He drew the way they had drawn it at the École for fifty years — patient, meticulous, the human figure constructed out of clearly modelled tonal values. (His private drawings, even at the end of his life, are extraordinary — soft conté-crayon studies in which a figure emerges out of velvety black grain like a photograph slowly developing.)

But he was also reading. Seurat is the painter who took color theory **out of the science books and into the studio with method**. He read **Michel Eugène Chevreul**'s ***De la loi du contraste simultané des couleurs*** (Paris, 1839 — a French chemist's discovery, made while running the Gobelins tapestry works, that two colors placed next to each other affect each other in the eye, the basis of the entire concept of complementary shadows). He read **Charles Blanc**'s ***Grammaire des arts du dessin*** (1867). He read the American physicist **Ogden Rood**'s ***Modern Chromatics*** (New York, 1879; translated into French in 1881 as *Théorie scientifique des couleurs*), which taught optical mixing — the idea that small adjacent dots of pure pigment will combine in the eye at viewing distance into a more luminous mixed color than any premixed pigment.

Out of these he built his method. He called it, when he had to, **chromo-luminarisme** (a name that did not stick). The art critic **Félix Fénéon** named it instead **Néo-Impressionnisme** in 1886, in his review of the 8th Impressionist Exhibition, and that name took. Outsiders called it **Pointillism** or **Divisionism** — the picture built out of thousands of small, separate, regular dots of pure pigment, optically combining at the viewer's eye into shimmering whole tones. The principle was simple: do scientifically, by formula, what the Impressionists had been doing by gut instinct.

The pictures came out slowly. Seurat would spend two summers and a winter on a single canvas, making dozens of preparatory studies before laying down the first dot.

- ***Bathers at Asnières*** (1884, **National Gallery, London**, *about 6 ft 7 in × 9 ft 10½ in*, oil on canvas). The rejected canvas that hung at the first Salon des Indépendants in 1884. Working-class men and a boy sunbathe along the Seine outside Paris, with the factory chimneys of Clichy in the background. Painted, mostly, before Seurat had fully arrived at the dot — the surface is built of small parallel strokes rather than separate points — but the program is already there.
- ***A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte*** (1884–86, **Art Institute of Chicago**, *about 6 ft 9½ in × 10 ft 1 in*). The picture from Chapter 1: the painting that quietly killed Impressionism at its own last exhibition. Forty-eight Parisians and three dogs on a small Sunday island. Two years of work. The dot is everywhere — including, by 1889 when Seurat added it, on a painted dotted *border* around the canvas itself. (The original frame at AIC reproduces this border.)
- ***Models (Les Poseuses)*** (1886–88, Barnes Foundation, Philadelphia) — three nudes in his studio, posed against the *Grande Jatte* hanging on the back wall; a picture in which the artist almost slyly compares the modern flesh of his models to the dotted flesh of his Sunday strollers.
- ***Parade de cirque (Circus Sideshow)*** (1887–88, Metropolitan Museum of Art).
- ***The Circus (Le Cirque)*** (1890–91, Musée d'Orsay) — his last picture, **unfinished at his death**, of a trick rider on a galloping horse with the audience banked up behind, almost cartoonish in its caricatured contours. He died with the canvas still wet in places.

He died **suddenly**, on **29 March 1891**, age 31. The cause of death is **contested** — Wikipedia summarizes the sources as "variously attributed to a form of meningitis, pneumonia, infectious angina, and diphtheria." His infant son **Pierre Georges**, by his common-law partner Madeleine Knobloch, died of the same illness a few days later. It is safest to write "a sudden infection — possibly diphtheria, possibly meningitis; the record is unclear."

The career was seven years long.

### SectionHeader: Toulouse-Lautrec — Aristocrat in the dance hall

The other half of this chapter is the painter who, by temperament, was Seurat's exact opposite. **Henri Marie Raymond de Toulouse-Lautrec-Monfa** was born on **24 November 1864** in **Albi**, in southwestern France, into one of the oldest families in Europe — direct descendants of the medieval **Counts of Toulouse**, who had ruled the south of France in the high Middle Ages before being shattered by the Albigensian Crusade in the 13th century. His parents were **first cousins** — the **Comte Alphonse de Toulouse-Lautrec** and **Adèle Tapié de Celeyran** — and consanguineous marriage of that closeness, repeated over generations in a small aristocratic gene pool, is the accepted cause of the inherited disorder that defined Henri's life.

At **13** he broke his **left femur**. At **14**, the right. The breaks healed badly. The legs stopped growing, and the rest of him went on. His adult height was somewhere around **5 ft 0 in** on a normal adult torso. He walked with a stick. The modern diagnostic guess — *inferred*, not proven — is **pycnodysostosis** (sometimes called "Toulouse-Lautrec syndrome"), a rare recessive genetic disorder of bone density. No autopsy was done, no DNA exists; the diagnosis is a 20th-century inference from his medical history. Treat it as a guess.

He went to Paris to train as a painter, studied briefly under the academic painter Léon Bonnat and the slightly more open-minded **Fernand Cormon**, in whose studio he met Van Gogh (Chapter 4) and Émile Bernard (Chapter 5) in 1886. By **1884** he had moved to **Montmartre** — the hilltop neighborhood at the northern edge of Paris that, after Haussmann's gutting of the old city center, had been colonized by the working class, the cabarets, the dance halls, the brothels, and the painters who couldn't afford anywhere else. He took a studio at 7 rue Tourlaque in 1887 and stayed in the neighborhood for the rest of his short life.

What he painted was Montmartre. Not the picturesque Montmartre that the Impressionist Renoir had painted (the sunlit *Bal du moulin de la Galette*, 1876, all dappled light and prettiness), but the *working* Montmartre — the cabaret-concert at midnight, the dancers on stage, the bored prostitutes between customers, the regulars slumped at the corner table, the lurid gas-lit complexion of a face seen too close. He borrowed from **Japanese prints** the flat planes of color, the hard contours, the off-center cropping that puts a figure at the very edge of a canvas — the same Japanese-print debt the Impressionists had, but pushed harder, with the figures more silhouette-like and the modern Paris of paid pleasure as the subject.

His best work is in three media: **paintings**, **posters**, and **lithographic prints**.

- The painting: ***At the Moulin Rouge*** (1892–95, **Art Institute of Chicago**, *about 4 ft 0½ in × 4 ft 7¼ in*, oil on canvas). The famous Moulin Rouge — the great dance hall on the place Blanche in Montmartre, opened 1889 — at its tables. Five regulars sit around a center table; in the background, a small group walks toward us, with Lautrec himself painted into the picture, the short top-hatted man in the back beside his tall cousin Gabriel. In the right foreground a face looms up close, sliced off by the canvas edge, lit a lurid pale **green** from below — this is the English dancer **May Milton**. The off-square cropping is straight out of a Japanese print. The picture was reframed shortly after Lautrec's death and the right-hand strip with May Milton's face was removed for decades, until AIC reattached it in 1959.
- The poster: ***Moulin Rouge: La Goulue*** (1891, color lithograph, multiple impressions in collections including MoMA, the Met, the V&A, Albi and the BNF). The poster that made the medium. Designed when Lautrec was 26, posted on Paris hoardings to advertise the Moulin Rouge's star dancer **Louise Weber** ("La Goulue" — the *Glutton*), it was the first time a serious painter had put his full design intelligence into a commercial street poster. Three flat zones of color: yellow ground, La Goulue's white frilled bloomers kicking up at the center, the silhouettes of the audience as a black frieze behind her. The whole modern poster industry — Mucha, Cassandre, the entire 20th-century commercial graphic look — descends from this single sheet of paper, glued to a wall in Paris in November 1891.
- The brothel pictures: in the early 1890s Lautrec moved more or less into a series of legal brothels on the rue d'Amboise and the **rue des Moulins** and painted the women off-shift. ***In the Salon at the Rue des Moulins*** (1894, **Musée Toulouse-Lautrec, Albi**) shows the brothel waiting room — three women on red plush sofas, dressed in long camisoles, bored, waiting. There is no leer in the painting. The women are people, off the clock, and Lautrec looks at them with the same matter-of-fact gaze he turns on the dancers and the drinkers.

He drank himself to death. Heavily. **Absinthe** mostly, the bitter green wormwood liquor that the late-19th-century Paris bohemian drank by the liter. In 1899 his family had him institutionalized for alcoholism for a few months. He came out, drank again, and added syphilis to the bill. He died at his mother's château, **Château Malromé**, in Gironde, southwestern France, on **9 September 1901**, age **36**, of complications of both. The **Musée Toulouse-Lautrec** in **Albi**, his birthplace, opened in 1922 in the medieval Palais de la Berbie; his mother and the family donated about 1,000 works to it. It holds the largest single collection of him on earth.

Seurat and Lautrec are linked here not because they were friends (they were barely acquainted) but because they died young, in Paris, having between them shown two opposite ways out of Impressionism: science on the one hand, the modern Parisian night on the other. Neither was at peace.

## Chapter 7

- **id:** `nabis`
- **eyebrow:** Paris · 1888–1905
- **dateLabel:** The Nabis and the wider field
- **title:** Bonnard, Vuillard, Denis — and the cast around them
- **blurb:** Around the five anchors orbits a wider cast — a younger group called the Nabis, the Symbolists, the pointillists' propagandist, the customs-officer who painted jungles. The room gets crowded.

### SectionHeader: October 1888 · Pont-Aven — A cigar-box panel and a few words from Gauguin — first

The wider cast of Post-Impressionism begins in October 1888, in a corner of the same Brittany village we left in Chapter 5. **Paul Sérusier** (1864–1927) was a 24-year-old French painter from a comfortable Paris family — a student at the **Académie Julian** (a private Paris art school, founded 1867 by Rodolphe Julian, that took the students the École des Beaux-Arts wouldn't or couldn't take, including women and foreigners; almost everyone in the next generation of French painting passed through it). Sérusier was on holiday in Pont-Aven. He sought out Gauguin, who was then in his Vision-after-the-Sermon mode (Chapter 5), and asked him for a lesson.

Gauguin took him out to a small wood by the river called the **Bois d'Amour**, set him in front of a stretch of trees reflected in the Aven, and gave him a small wooden panel to paint on — the lid of a cigar box, roughly. According to Sérusier's later account (told to his friend Maurice Denis), Gauguin's instruction was something like: *What do you see, that tree? Is it really green? Then use green, the most beautiful green on your palette. And that shadow, rather blue? Don't be afraid to paint it as blue as possible.* Use pure color. Don't blend. Don't naturalize. Paint what the picture *needs*, not what your eye literally reports.

Sérusier did. The panel is tiny — about **10½ × 8½ inches**, oil on wood — and it shows a few flat patches of orange, green, blue and red that *read* as a landscape only if you already know it's a landscape. He carried it back to Paris and showed it to his fellow students at the Académie Julian.

They lost their minds over it. They named the panel **Le Talisman** ("The Talisman") and treated it like a religious icon. (It is now in the Musée d'Orsay, Paris, RF 1985-13, having survived in Sérusier's family for almost a century.) And they named themselves, in **late 1888**, the **Nabis** — the Hebrew word for "prophets," picked by a Hebraist friend, the poet Henri Cazalis, on the deliberately self-mocking ground that they were now the prophets of a new art. The core members were Sérusier himself, **Pierre Bonnard**, **Édouard Vuillard**, **Maurice Denis**, **Ker-Xavier Roussel**, **Paul Ranson**, the sculptor **Aristide Maillol**, and the Hungarian **József Rippl-Rónai**. They met weekly at Ranson's studio (which they jokingly called "the Temple"), wore beards, gave each other Nabi nicknames (Bonnard was *le nabi très japonard*, the "very Japanese-like" one) and produced, over the next decade and a half, the **most direct descendants of Gauguin's Synthetism**.

### SectionHeader: Maurice Denis — A teenager writes the catechism

The Nabis' theorist was **Maurice Denis** (1870–1943). In **August 1890**, in a periodical called ***Art et Critique***, an essay appeared titled ***Définition du néo-traditionnisme***, signed *Pierre Louis* (a pen name). It contained, in its opening sentences, the line that the 20th century would quote at every student who ever picked up a brush:

> *Se rappeler qu'un tableau, avant d'être un cheval de bataille, une femme nue, ou une quelconque anecdote, est essentiellement une surface plane recouverte de couleurs en un certain ordre assemblées.*
>
> "Remember that a picture — before being a battle horse, a nude woman, or any other anecdote — is essentially a flat surface covered with colors assembled in a certain order."

Pierre Louis was Maurice Denis. **He was 19 years old.** He had been a Nabi for about a year. He had taken Gauguin's instruction to Sérusier in the Bois d'Amour and condensed it, with the breathtaking precision of a young painter who hasn't yet learned to hedge, into one sentence. The picture is **first** a surface with colored shapes on it; *then*, perhaps, also a story or a thing. (The idea was not entirely original — the French philosopher Hippolyte Taine had said something like it in his *Philosophie de l'art*, 1865 — but Denis's wording is the one that got carved over the door of every 20th-century art school.)

It is a fair argument that no single sentence pushed European painting further toward abstraction than that one. By the time Kandinsky paints the first wholly abstract watercolor in Munich in 1910, Denis's 1890 line has had twenty years to soak in.

### SectionHeader: Bonnard and Vuillard — The intimate Nabis

The two Nabis who would have the longest, most productive lives took Gauguin's flat-color program inward, toward **the bourgeois domestic interior** — the dining room, the sitting room, the bathroom — and stayed there for fifty years.

**Pierre Bonnard** (1867–1947) painted his lifelong companion **Marthe** (born **Maria Boursin**; she changed her name to Marthe de Méligny when they met, claiming a noble background she did not have; Bonnard and she would only finally marry, after thirty years together, in 1925) over and over — in the bath, drying after a bath, in a yellow dress, in a red dressing gown, at the breakfast table — for the rest of her life. The greatest pictures are the late ones: the ***Nude in the Bath*** series (Centre Pompidou; 1925 onward), in which Marthe lies submerged in a clawfoot bathtub under shifting violet and rose and orange light, and the dining-room pictures (***Dining Room in the Country***, 1913, Minneapolis Institute of Art) in which the table, the wallpaper, the open garden door and the family dog all dissolve into a humming field of complementary colors. Bonnard outlived everyone — almost the whole movement — and was still painting in Provence into the 1940s. His late work is sometimes called "almost Fauvist," sometimes "almost abstract"; it is really neither. It is the Nabi instinct followed all the way out.

**Édouard Vuillard** (1868–1940) painted smaller, denser interiors than Bonnard, packed with **pattern**. His mother was a corsetmaker; he grew up in apartments full of fabric samples and wallpaper, and as a painter he refused to let the wallpaper recede behind the figures. In a Vuillard interior, the wallpaper, the carpet, the woman's dress and the curtains all fight for the same depth of color, and the figure of *Mother and Sister of the Artist* (c. **1893**, **Museum of Modern Art, New York**, *about 18 × 22 in*) is half-absorbed into the floral wallpaper behind her. The dominant patrons of the Nabis were the **Natanson** brothers — Polish-French Jewish founders of the cultural review ***La Revue blanche*** (Paris, 1889–1903) — who commissioned decorative panels from Vuillard for their Paris apartments. Through *La Revue blanche*, Vuillard met everyone from Mallarmé to Proust (who would later put a portrait of a Vuillard-like character into *Remembrance of Things Past*).

### SectionHeader: The other tributaries — Signac, Redon, Rousseau, Cassatt

A movement Roger Fry would in 1910 lump under the single umbrella "Post-Impressionism" has a much more crowded actual room.

**Paul Signac** (1863–1935). Seurat's partner from 1884 onward, the second great Divisionist. After Seurat's death in 1891, Signac became the **propagandist** for the technique — the one who took Seurat's instinct and theorized it for an audience. In **1899** he published **D'Eugène Delacroix au néo-impressionnisme** (Paris, Éditions de la Revue blanche), the book that codified Divisionism as a coherent theory and linked it backward, somewhat tendentiously, to the great Romantic colorist Eugène Delacroix. Signac became president of the Société des Artistes Indépendants in 1908 and ran the Salon des Indépendants for almost three decades. His own great picture, in this context, is ***Portrait of Félix Fénéon*** (1890, Museum of Modern Art, New York) — the critic who had named Néo-Impressionnisme rendered as a dapper, top-hatted profile against a swirling, dot-by-dot decorative background of colored arabesques. (The picture's full subtitle is something like *Opus 217. Against the Enamel of a Background Rhythmic with Beats and Angles, Tones and Tints, Portrait of M. Félix Fénéon in 1890* — Signac liked his titles operatic.)

**Odilon Redon** (1840–1916). The **Symbolist** tributary. Two careers in one body: in the first half of his working life, until about 1895, Redon was the painter of ***noirs*** — disturbing, dreamlike charcoal drawings and black-and-white lithographs of single floating eyes, spider-faced creatures, monsters, severed heads, the inner cinema of late-19th-century French nightmares. Then around **1895** (he was about 55) he switched media almost entirely to **pastel and oil**, and his late style is the photographic negative of his early one: luminous, saturated, *colored* — bouquets of flowers blazing on dark grounds, mythological figures emerging from clouds of color, the famous ***Cyclops*** (Kröller-Müller Museum, Otterlo) showing a vast, gentle one-eyed giant peering shyly over a hillside at a sleeping nude. Redon connects Post-Impressionism to the Symbolist movement in literature (Mallarmé, Maeterlinck) and is the painter the Nabis admired most among their elders.

**Henri Rousseau** (1844–1910) — known as ***Le Douanier***, "the customs officer," because he had spent his working life as a tollkeeper at one of Paris's city gates collecting duties on goods coming into town. He was **self-taught**, completely outside the art world, painted on weekends, and was for most of his life laughed at by the Paris critics as a naïve amateur. His subject was the **tropical jungle** — vast, hallucinatory paintings of dense leafy forests filled with tigers, lions, monkeys, snake-charmers, and reclining nudes — even though he had never left France. The "jungles" came from the **Jardin des Plantes**, the Paris botanical garden, where Rousseau would sit and sketch leaves and flowers and the few colonial-imported animals on display. His largest jungle picture, ***The Dream*** (1910, Museum of Modern Art, New York; *about 6 ft 8½ in × 9 ft 9½ in*), was painted in his last year and shown at the **Salon des Indépendants** in spring 1910. Rousseau died on **2 September 1910**. Picasso adored him. In November 1908, in his Bateau-Lavoir studio in Montmartre, Picasso hosted a half-mock, half-serious banquet in Rousseau's honor — the famous **Banquet Rousseau**, attended by Apollinaire, Gertrude Stein, Braque, Marie Laurencin — at which Rousseau took the tribute entirely at face value and told Picasso the two of them were the greatest living painters, Rousseau in the modern style and Picasso in the Egyptian. (Hedge his placement, by the way: Rousseau is **adjacent** to Post-Impressionism, not central. He is better called an outsider whom the avant-garde elected as a forerunner.)

**Mary Cassatt** (1844–1926). Pittsburgh-born, French-resident from 1874 onward, the only American to be part of the inner circle of the Impressionist exhibitions (recruited by Degas, who admired her; she debuted with them in 1879). Cassatt sits half in Impressionism (her early work) and half in Post-Impressionism (her later work), and the dividing line is sharp. In **1890** the **École des Beaux-Arts** in Paris mounted a major exhibition of **Japanese woodblock prints** — over 700 works, the most ambitious show of *ukiyo-e* ever held in Europe up to that date. Cassatt went, took notes, came home and reinvented her practice. Her late work — the color drypoints and aquatints of mothers bathing children, of women at their dressing tables, of the bourgeois interior — is overtly indebted to Japanese prints, with their flat color zones, hard outlines, and odd off-center cropping. ***The Child's Bath*** (1893, Art Institute of Chicago) is the signature canvas: a mother in a striped dress, sleeves rolled, bending forward to wash her young child's feet in a white china basin on her lap, the picture seen from a high vantage that flattens the floor into a decorative pattern — a Japanese-print composition rendered in oil paint. Cassatt continued painting until her eyesight failed (around 1914) and lived on for another twelve years, increasingly blind, in France. She is the bridge between Impressionism's American audience and the later wave of American collectors of Post-Impressionism.

The wider cast goes on — **Vallotton**, **Maillol**, **Maurice Marinot**, the lesser Pont-Aven painters, the late Pissarro who experimented briefly with pointillism before going back to his own way — but the shape of the room is now clear. By 1900 there are not five Post-Impressionists, there are forty or fifty painters, none of whom call themselves Post-Impressionists, working in clusters across France, each pulling away from Impressionism in his own direction. All they need is somebody to walk into the room and put a name on the door.

## Chapter 8

- **id:** `fry`
- **eyebrow:** London · 8 November 1910
- **dateLabel:** Roger Fry hangs a show
- **title:** How a category got its name
- **blurb:** A London critic with a gallery to fill needs a name for a roomful of French pictures. He picks one on the fly. It is the name we still use.

### SectionHeader: London · autumn 1910 — A critic, a gallery, a deadline — first

**Roger Fry** was 44 years old in 1910, an English critic and Bloomsbury-adjacent intellectual who had spent the previous decade as the European paintings curator for the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and had recently been fired in a bureaucratic row with the Met's president (J. Pierpont Morgan). He was back in London looking for work. He had also, more importantly, been spending his summers in France, watching what was happening in the studios there — what Cézanne had finally done before his death, what Matisse and Picasso and Derain were now doing — and he had begun to think that England had no idea any of it had occurred.

In the summer of 1910 he proposed an exhibition to the **Grafton Galleries** — a commercial gallery on **Grafton Street, Mayfair**, in central London — bringing recent French painting to a British audience for the first time. The Grafton agreed. Fry rounded up a small organizing crew: **Desmond MacCarthy**, the literary critic (who served as the show's secretary and helped draft the catalogue introduction), and **Clive Bell**, the young art writer who would in 1914 publish *Art* and the concept of "significant form." Lady Ottoline Morrell helped patronize the thing. Fry went to France, twisted arms at dealers — Vollard among them — and came back to London with about 228 works in crates: roughly 206 paintings and drawings plus 22 sculptures and ceramics. **Cézanne** (with the most works), **Van Gogh**, **Gauguin**, plus **Manet** (borrowed as the borrowed elder father-figure, the way the Impressionists had always positioned him), plus the living wave of French painters Fry wanted Britain to see: **Matisse**, **Picasso**, **Derain**, **Vlaminck**, **Maurice Denis**, **Redon**, **Seurat**, **Signac**, **Sérusier**, **Vallotton**.

The show needed a title. Fry, late at night and against a printer's deadline, scribbled out the running title **Manet and the Post-Impressionists**. He later told *The Nation* (1911) that he had picked the name almost on the fly — he had rejected alternatives like "expressionists" — and that he needed an umbrella that would cover all of these dead and living French painters without committing to any program. *Post-Impressionists* was elastic enough to fit them all. He stuck the catalogue together with that on the cover.

The show opened on **8 November 1910** and ran through **15 January 1911**.

### SectionHeader: "An extremely bad joke or a swindle" — The London press loses its mind

The English press response, when it came, was one of the great public freak-outs in art history.

**Wilfrid Scawen Blunt**, the poet and diarist, walked through the show on 15 November and recorded in his diary that it was "either an extremely bad joke or a swindle" — that the paintings were "the work of madmen" and that the whole thing was an outrage to civilization. **Robert Ross** — the critic best known today as Oscar Wilde's executor — wrote in the *Morning Post* that the show represented "a widespread plot to destroy the whole fabric of European painting." *The Times* called it "the rejection of all that civilization has done, the good and the bad." Charles Ricketts, the painter, dismissed it as childish. The visitor book at the Grafton Galleries collected ordinary public verdicts of the same flavor: "Pure pornography." "The work of madmen." The phrase **"the work of a lunatic"** appears, with small variations, across more than a dozen separate reviews.

It was, in absolute terms, a commercial success. Over the show's two months **about 25,000 visitors** paid to see it, generating gate receipts and sales of around **£4,600**. Many had clearly come to laugh — to see what all the screaming was about. The Grafton mounted a special public lecture series to defend the show; Fry himself gave talks; MacCarthy answered letters in the press. The **Bloomsbury Group** — Fry himself, the painter Vanessa Bell, her husband Clive Bell, her sister Virginia Woolf, the writer Lytton Strachey, the economist John Maynard Keynes, the painter Duncan Grant — effectively coalesced around the defense of the show. They would say later that the British Modernist sensibility was born in the rooms of the Grafton that winter.

The retrospective epitaph belongs to **Virginia Woolf**, who in 1924 — fourteen years after the show — gave a lecture published as *Mr Bennett and Mrs Brown* in which she remarked, in a line so famous it has overgrown its own context: **"On or about December 1910 human character changed."** Woolf was talking about a whole shift in modernist sensibility, not just the Grafton show. But it is widely understood that the Grafton show was what she had at the back of her mind. The phrase nails the moment.

### SectionHeader: 5 October – 31 December 1912 — The sequel

Two years later, in **1912**, Fry mounted a **Second Post-Impressionist Exhibition** at the Grafton Galleries — formally titled ***Second Post-Impressionist Exhibition. British, French and Russian Artists.*** It ran from **5 October to 31 December 1912**. The poster was designed by Vanessa Bell and Duncan Grant. The French section was selected by Fry himself and included **about 5 Cézannes, about 19 Matisses, and about 13 Picassos**, plus Derain, Vlaminck, Bonnard, Marquet. The British section featured Bloomsbury's own painters — Fry, Vanessa Bell, Duncan Grant — plus Wyndham Lewis and Stanley Spencer. The Russian section, curated by **Boris Anrep**, featured Goncharova, Larionov, and Roerich.

By the time the second show opened, the controversy had cooled. The press response was softer. The public had stopped coming to laugh. The category **Post-Impressionism** was, by Christmas 1912, a *known* category in the English-speaking world. Fry had got the umbrella to stay open.

### SectionHeader: 17 February 1913 — New York gets it

The Atlantic crossing of Post-Impressionism happened the next year, in **New York**, when an enormous show — the **International Exhibition of Modern Art**, better known as **the Armory Show** — opened on **17 February 1913** at the **69th Regiment Armory** on Lexington Avenue. It ran through **15 March 1913**, then travelled to Chicago and Boston. It contained roughly **1,250 works by about 300 artists**, about two-thirds American and one-third European. Within the European third were **about 13 Cézannes, about 18 Van Goghs, about 12 Gauguins, several Seurats**, the largest **Redon retrospective** outside France (about 32 Redons), plus Matisse, Picasso and Marcel Duchamp. (Duchamp's ***Nude Descending a Staircase No. 2*** — a Cubist-Futurist figure in motion painted that year — was the show's succès de scandale, mocked by the American press as "an explosion in a shingle factory.")

The Armory Show is to American modernism what the 1910 Grafton show is to British modernism. It is where American audiences encountered the entire Post-Impressionist and early-modernist range at once, jumbled together in two regimental drill halls. American collectors went home and started buying.

### SectionHeader: One last fact — They were all dead

End on the strangeness of it.

When Roger Fry opened his Grafton Galleries show on 8 November 1910, every one of the five painters he had put at its center was already in the ground.

- **Cézanne** had been dead **four years** — died Aix, 22 October 1906.
- **Van Gogh** had been dead **twenty years** — died Auvers, 29 July 1890.
- **Gauguin** had been dead **seven years** — died Hiva Oa, 8 May 1903.
- **Seurat** had been dead **nineteen years** — died Paris, 29 March 1891.
- **Toulouse-Lautrec** had been dead **nine years** — died Château Malromé, 9 September 1901.

The name was retrospective. The room was retrospective. The category itself was a London critic's late, improvised umbrella for five French painters who had never met as a group, never written a manifesto, never agreed about anything, and were all in their graves before the umbrella opened. The name stuck, anyway, because the public didn't have a better one.

What that umbrella covered, looking back from here, was the **bridge from Impressionism into 20th-century modernism**. Without those five painters — and without the wider cast around them, the Nabis and the pointillists and the Symbolists and the customs-officer with his jungles — there is no **Fauvism** (Matisse and Derain lighting up the Salon d'Automne in 1905, in the year Cézanne is still alive and painting his last Mont Sainte-Victoires), no **Die Brücke** (the German Expressionist group founded in Dresden on 7 June 1905, lit fuse Van Gogh and Gauguin), no **Der Blaue Reiter** (Munich, December 1911, lit fuse Gauguin's symbolism and Cézanne's color planes), no **Cubism** (Picasso and Braque, Paris, 1907–14, direct out of the late Cézanne), and no **abstraction** (Kandinsky in *Concerning the Spiritual in Art*, 1911, citing Cézanne by name; Mondrian's pre-1914 trees built straight out of Cézanne and the Nabis).

Five painters and a wider cast. Five answers to one question — *what comes after Impressionism?* — and one English critic, hanging a London show on a printer's deadline, who needed a name and gave them the only one we now use. The mountain refused to dissolve. The wheatfield kept burning. The Breton women kept seeing things their eyes couldn't see. The dots kept building the Sunday lawn. The cabaret lights kept catching on the dancers' faces. And then, around the turn of the new century, the painters all died, one by one, and a generation of younger painters in Paris and Munich and Dresden and London picked up the pieces and ran with them.

That generation's room is the next door over.
