# Gate read — Boccioni, *States of Mind: The Farewells* (1911, MoMA)

**Scope:** Part B chapter prose only (the five `Far`-prefixed components in
`work-farewells-draft.md`, lines 115–264). Three gates run together: **STORYTELLING**,
**LOOKING**, **CLARITY**. Part A const, fact ledger, and Notes-for-coordinator are
context, not graded here. House-voice reference: the absinthe narratives.

**Verdict: PASS with fixes.** No BLOCKERs. The draft is strong, story-first, and
genuinely teaches a zero-knowledge reader to SEE the canvas — it clears all three
gates. The fixes below are real (a few repeated tics, one slightly thin scene-set, two
soft-edged looking cues, and a small clarity gap on "triptych") but none are
ship-blocking. The two-version Paris/Cubism material is well used; the looking section
does its job.

---

## GATE 1 — STORYTELLING

**Overall: PASS, strong.** Story-first throughout, dry wit lands (the parted-twins
symmetry, the manifesto's "own bleak irony"), comparisons are present (Seurat,
altarpieces, serving tray), scenes are set. No meta-narration of the "in this chapter
we will explore" kind that the voice bans. A handful of repetition tics and one thin
spot.

### [FIX] Forward-reference tic repeated three times — sand one down
The draft promises "we'll come back to this" three separate times in close range, which
starts to read as a verbal habit rather than a flourish:
- L131: "(We will come back to Futurism, and to its harder edges, in a later chapter.)"
- L139: "as we will see when we get to the canvas, its own kind of line."
- L142–143 the whole "And here is the strange fact that the rest of this story turns on…"

These are individually fine and the absinthe voice *does* use forward-pointers ("Hold
that thought; in two chapters…"). But three inside the FarStation component alone is one
too many. **Fix:** keep the Futurism pointer (L131) and the "second version" hook
(L142–143, it's the chapter's engine), and cut the explicit promise in L139 — "its own
kind of line" can stand without "as we will see when we get to the canvas." The payoff
in FarLooking still lands without the pre-announcement.

### [FIX] "It would be dishonest to leave…" — honesty-label tic the voice bans
L230–231: *"It would be dishonest to leave Futurism as a cheerful festival of fast cars,
because its founding document had a genuinely ugly side…"* The "it would be dishonest
to…" framing narrates the narrator's own integrity, which is exactly the honesty-label
move flagged in the philosophy-voice guidance and absent from the absinthe ref (Degas's
darker facts are stated flat, never prefaced with "to be honest"). **Fix:** drop the
self-justifying clause and state it plainly:
> *Futurism was not only a cheerful festival of fast cars. Its founding document had a
> genuinely ugly side, and it is part of the honest history of this picture.*
This keeps "honest history of this picture" (which describes the *subject*, fine) but
removes the narrator vouching for himself.

### [FIX] "and it is worth slowing down for" / "it is worth slowing down for" — reader-command tic
L203 (FarLooking): *"This is the trick of the picture, and it is worth slowing down
for."* Mild, but it's a directive *about how to read* rather than the thing itself, and
it pairs with several other imperatives in the looking section (see LOOKING gate — those
are appropriate for a guided-looking chapter, but this one is meta). **Fix:** cut "and
it is worth slowing down for"; the sentence is stronger ending on "This is the trick of
the picture." The slowing-down then happens because the prose does it, not because it's
announced.

### [NICE] FarStation scene-set is slightly thinner than the absinthe opener
The absinthe ref opens by physically walking the reader up to Place Pigalle and into the
café — a vivid located scene before any argument. FarStation opens on the *ambition*
("set out to do something most painters would have called a contradiction in terms"),
which is a fine and characterful hook, but it's an idea-scene, not a place-scene. The
reader doesn't get put on a Milan platform under the iron shed until L128 ("a platform
under a great iron shed") and even then glancingly. This is a stylistic choice, not a
defect — the work being abstract, leading with the ambition is defensible. **If a light
touch is wanted:** a single concrete sensory beat early (the shed, the steam, a real
platform in 1911 Milan) would match the absinthe opener's grounding. Optional.

### [NICE] The afterlife close is excellent — flag as a keeper
L256–260 (the parted-twins symmetry + "carried away himself, on the very kind of machine
he had spent his short life trying to make beautiful") is exactly the dry, earned,
non-saccharine close the house voice wants. No change. Noting it so a later editor
doesn't flatten it.

## GATE 2 — LOOKING

**Overall: PASS, strong.** FarLooking genuinely renders emotion-as-lines and walks the
reader concretely through every required element: the swirling/curving arcs as the
"engine of feeling," the per-panel line geometry (curve/oblique/vertical), the embracing
couple dissolving into the storm, the locomotive and its number 6943 as the still center,
the telegraph poles/wires, the faceted station as the Paris souvenir, and the radiating
vortex pulled together at the end. The reader is told *where* to look and *what the line
is doing emotionally*, which is the whole point. Two cues are softer than they should be,
and one required item (the green departure lines) is correctly *not* asserted — but the
brief lists it, so it needs a note.

### [FIX] "The green swirling departure lines" — color is dropped on purpose; confirm the brief expects this
The brief asks whether the looking section makes the reader see "the green swirling
departure lines." **The draft deliberately does NOT call them green** (Fact ledger
L295 + Notes L299: §④ flagged "green" as unconfirmed and dropped it pending image
verification). The lines ARE described concretely by character (swirling/curving arcs,
the engine of feeling) — so the *looking* is done; only the *color word* is withheld.
This is the correct call under born-verified rules and should NOT be "fixed" by adding
"green" back. **Action: none on the prose.** This is a heads-up that the brief's
checklist item is satisfied minus the unverifiable color, by design — and that the
VERIFY-AT-IMAGE step (Notes L298) must confirm the green before any future edit restores
the word. Flagging so the gate isn't read as a miss.

### [FIX] The locomotive's location cue is doubled and slightly hedged twice
The number/locomotive is the keystone of the looking section, and it's handled well
(L202–203). But the "where" is given as "Toward the center-right, half-buried in the
swirl" and the *digits-are-hard-to-read* hedge appears in both the annotation (Part A
L86) and the prose (L203). Within Part B alone it reads once, so this is fine — but note
for the coordinator that the same hedge fires in the annotation `detail` and the prose;
keep them from reading as a copy-paste when both render on one page. **Fix (prose side,
minor):** the parenthetical at L203 is good as-is; no change needed to Part B. Logged for
cross-surface dedupe at integration.

### [FIX] "Look up through the haze and you will catch…" — telegraph poles cue is real but the visual is under-anchored
L207–208: the poles/wires are described as "thin, hard, straight uprights … cutting
against the curving waves," which is good directional contrast. But the reader is told to
"look up through the haze" without a clearer fix on *where in the frame* — the absinthe
looking section is relentless about position ("upper-right corner," "lower-left half,"
"partway up the zig-zag"). **Fix:** anchor it: e.g. *"Look up through the upper haze and
you'll catch the thin, hard, straight uprights…"* The annotation (Part A L88) says
"rising through the upper haze" — pull that "upper" into the prose so the reader's eye
has a region, not just a direction. Also: the prose silently resolves the
poles-vs-"railway post"/oil-tower ambiguity (Notes L298) in favor of poles-and-wires;
that's flagged as VERIFY-AT-IMAGE and must be eyeballed before lock, but the prose
hedges it acceptably ("Sources describe a 'railway post' here" is in the annotation,
though NOT in Part B prose). Consider carrying one short hedge into the prose if the
image check is still pending at integration.

### [NICE] The per-panel line geometry is the best looking-beat in the draft
L189–191 (curves here / oblique next door / strictly vertical in the third) is exactly
the "see emotion rendered as direction" the gate wants, and it's concrete and memorable.
Keep verbatim.

### [NICE] The "small canvas vs huge feeling" framing earns its keep
L182 (two-feet-three by three-feet-two, "a little wider than a serving tray laid flat,"
"a whole emotional weather system … compressed into less than a yard of canvas") sets
scale physically before the looking starts — matches the absinthe "kitchen tray stood on
end" move. Good.

## GATE 3 — CLARITY

**Overall: PASS.** Every load-bearing term is glossed inline on first use for a
zero-knowledge reader: Futurism (L131, "an Italian avant-garde — that is, a
self-consciously radical art group out ahead of the mainstream"), avant-garde (glossed
in the same breath), States of Mind / *Stati d'animo* (L128, "the conditions of the soul,
the inner weather of a person"), triptych (L136), Divisionism (L154, with the Seurat
cross-ref), Cubism (L162, Picasso/Braque + faceted planes), lines of force / *linee-forza*
(L193, with the correct 1912-not-1911 dating), manifesto (L223), provenance (L246 and
again L256… see below), Fascism (contextualized L231). Two real gaps and one redundancy.

### [FIX] "triptych" is glossed, but "panel" rides on it without the visual click
L136: triptych = "a single work made of three separate panels meant to hang together,
the format old altarpieces used." Good gloss. But a zero-knowledge reader may still not
picture that *The Farewells* is **one of three physical canvases** vs. three scenes in
one frame. The prose mostly carries it (L139 "Boccioni gave each its own panel"), and
the afterlife "reunited on one wall" (L254) confirms three objects — so it resolves. But
the first mention could click harder. **Fix (light):** at L136, after "three separate
panels meant to hang together," add a four-word anchor: *"…three separate panels — three
physical canvases — meant to hang side by side, the format old altarpieces used."* Makes
the three-objects fact unmissable on first contact.

### [FIX] "Cubist faceting" / "faceting" used before its plain-language unpack reaches the looking reader
"Faceting" and "faceted shards" are glossed well in FarParis (L162, "broke it into flat,
hard, intersecting planes, faceted shards"). But FarLooking (L208) uses "faceted shards"
and "faceting" again assuming the FarParis gloss — which is safe **only if the reader
arrives in chapter order.** The reader can deep-link to the looking section. **Fix:**
FarLooking L208 already says "broken into hard, intersecting, faceted shards, sharp
planes slicing across the curves" — that *is* a self-contained gloss (it defines faceted
= intersecting planes inline). On re-read this is actually fine. **Downgrade to [NICE]:**
no change required; the looking section re-glosses faceting on its own terms. Noting it
because it's the one term most likely to be hit cold by a deep-linker, and it survives.

### [FIX] "provenance" is glossed twice, nearly identically
L246 (FarAfterlife): "its *provenance* — meaning the documented chain of who has owned a
work, from the artist's hand to wherever it hangs now." Then the same chapter doesn't
repeat it — good. But the **absinthe ref also glosses provenance** (its afterlife, L192:
"the documented chain of who owned a work of art, from the painter's hand to wherever it
sits now"). Within *this* draft it's glossed once, so **no defect here** — flagging only
so the coordinator doesn't let the near-identical boilerplate gloss read as canned across
works if both render in one reading session. **Action: none required for Part B.**

### [FIX] "Divisionism" gloss is good but the Seurat cross-ref assumes the reader knows *Grande Jatte*
L154: "(It is a close Italian cousin of the dotted French method behind Seurat's
*Grande Jatte*.)" For a zero-knowledge reader, "Seurat's *Grande Jatte*" is itself an
unglossed reference — it works as a *gesture* ("there's a famous dotted French painting")
but a reader who's never heard of it gets nothing concrete. The core Divisionism gloss
("many small, separate strokes of pure color set side by side, so the eye mixes them at a
distance") is fully self-sufficient, so the Seurat line is bonus, not load-bearing.
**Fix (light, optional):** add three words so the reference teaches even cold: *"…the
dotted French method (tiny dots of pure color) behind Seurat's *Grande Jatte*."* Or
leave it — the primary gloss already does the work. Low priority.

### [NICE] "lines of force" dating is handled exactly right for clarity AND accuracy
L193 glosses *linee-forza* plainly ("directional lines that carry an object's energy and
feeling out into everything around it") AND correctly tells the reader the term post-dates
the painting (1912 catalogue), so the reader isn't misled into thinking Boccioni painted
"under" a slogan he hadn't coined yet. This is the model for how to gloss a term that's
both jargon and chronologically tricky.
