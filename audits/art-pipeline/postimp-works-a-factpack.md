# Post-Impressionism — WORK reads, Fact Pack A

Ground-truth for three Post-Impressionist WORK reads in the art vertical. Every
claim is tied to a source. The author writes from THIS, not from memory. Anything
unconfirmed is marked **⚠️ UNVERIFIED**. Anecdotes are flagged **[DOCUMENTED]**
(traceable to letters / museum records) or **[LEGEND/DISPUTED]** (popular but
unproven or false).

## 1. The Starry Night — Vincent van Gogh (id `starry-night`)

### Identity
- **Title:** The Starry Night. No native-language title proper; van Gogh referred to
  it in French in his letters as *la nuit étoilée* ("the starry night") and described
  it as a "study of a starry sky" / *étude de ciel étoilé*. [Wiki-EN, VGM letters]
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890).
- **Date:** June 1889 (painted in the week of ~17–18 June 1889). [MoMA: "Saint Rémy,
  June 1889"; Wiki-EN; VGM letter ~18 June 1889 to Theo]
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas. [MoMA]

### Dimensions (BOTH units — convert to ft/in for the app)
- **cm (as MoMA states):** 73.7 × 92.1 cm (h × w).
- **in:** 29 × 36 1/4 in (≈ 29.0 × 36.3 in).
- **ft/in for app:** **2 ft 5 in × 3 ft 1/4 in** (2.42 ft × 3.02 ft). Confidence: high. [MoMA]

### Location + accession / credit line
- **Current location:** The Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York. In MoMA's
  permanent collection since 1941. [MoMA]
- **Accession number:** 472.1941. [MoMA, Wiki-EN]
- **Credit line (exact):** "Acquired through the Lillie P. Bliss Bequest (by
  exchange)." Confidence: high. [MoMA] — note the precise wording is *by exchange*:
  MoMA did not buy it with cash but traded other works funded/enabled by the Bliss
  bequest. Wikipedia states the exchange involved two Cézannes and a Toulouse-Lautrec
  (⚠️ exact works swapped not independently re-confirmed against MoMA's own page —
  treat the "by exchange" credit line as the safe ground-truth; describe the trade
  loosely if at all).

### Provenance chain
Confidence: high on the spine (Wiki-EN, consistent with VGM); treat intermediate
dealer dates as solid but secondary.
1. Vincent van Gogh's estate → **Theo van Gogh** (Vincent's brother) on Vincent's
   death July 1890; Theo died Jan 1891.
2. **Johanna ("Jo") van Gogh-Bonger** (Theo's widow), from 1891 — the figure who
   built van Gogh's posthumous reputation.
3. 1900: sold to poet **Julien Leclercq**, Paris.
4. 1901: **Émile Schuffenecker** (painter/collector).
5. 1905: **Jo van Gogh-Bonger repurchased** it.
6. 1906: **Oldenzeel Gallery**, Rotterdam.
7. 1906–1938: **Georgette P. van Stolk**, Rotterdam.
8. 1938: **Paul Rosenberg** (dealer; fled France for the US in 1940).
9. 1941: **The Museum of Modern Art**, by exchange via the Lillie P. Bliss Bequest.
[Wiki-EN provenance; MoMA credit line]

### Where it came from (the artist's situation)
- After the December 1888 breakdown in Arles (the ear-mutilation episode following the
  rupture with Gauguin), van Gogh voluntarily admitted himself on **8 May 1889** to the
  **Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum** at **Saint-Rémy-de-Provence**. [Wiki-EN, VGM]
- He had an east-facing bedroom window with iron bars and was given a separate
  ground-floor room as a studio. The night sky over the valley was a recurring
  subject in this period. [Wiki-EN]

### The making (technique, when/where, how long)
- **Painted in his ground-floor studio during the day**, depicting (a reworked version
  of) **the view from his barred east-facing bedroom window before sunrise**. [Wiki-EN,
  MoMA]
- **[DOCUMENTED]** In a letter of ~18 June 1889 to Theo: "this morning I saw the
  countryside from my window a long time before sunrise, with nothing but the morning
  star, which looked very big" (the "morning star" = **Venus**, which astronomical
  records confirm was prominent at dawn in Provence that spring). [VGM letters, Wiki-EN]
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Same period: "At last I have a landscape with olive trees, and also
  a new study of a starry sky." [VGM letter ~18 June 1889]
- Thick impasto, swirling directional strokes; sky in ultramarine and cobalt blue,
  stars/moon in Indian yellow and zinc yellow (pigment analysis). [Wiki-EN]
- Completed within roughly that single week of June 1889. Exact hours unknown — do
  NOT claim "in one night"; it was a daytime studio work. **⚠️ "painted in one sitting
  / one night"** is not supported.

### Reception
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Van Gogh himself was lukewarm-to-negative: he called it a "night
  study" and, in a late-November 1889 letter to Émile Bernard, described it as a
  relative **failure**, saying he had "reached for stars that are too big." It was part
  of his running argument with Gauguin about painting from imagination vs. from nature.
  [Wiki-EN, VGM letters]
- It was **not** a celebrated work in his lifetime; it sold no better than the rest.
  Fame is entirely posthumous.

### Afterlife (how it became canonical)
- Carried by Jo van Gogh-Bonger's promotion of the estate, passed through European
  collections, and reached MoMA in 1941, where it became — per MoMA's own framing — a
  "touchstone of modern art" and one of the most recognizable paintings in the Western
  canon. Its iconic status is essentially a 20th-century, post-MoMA phenomenon.
  [MoMA, Wiki-EN]

### "Look closer" candidate pointers (verifiable, on the canvas)
1. **The single largest, brightest "star," lower-right of the central cypress** — this
   is **Venus, the morning star**, the one celestial body van Gogh names in his letter.
   Why it matters: it's the one element we can tie to a documented observation. [VGM letters]
2. **The crescent moon, upper right** — glowing yellow-orange. Why it matters: it is
   **astronomically wrong** for the date (the real moon was a waning gibbous), so it's
   an artistic choice, not a record. [Wiki-EN]
3. **The dark flame-like cypress, foreground left** — pushed far larger and closer than
   life; cypresses were a Saint-Rémy motif van Gogh painted repeatedly. Why it matters:
   shows his deliberate compositional exaggeration. [Wiki-EN]
4. **The village with a tall, thin church steeple, lower center** — the steeple is more
   Dutch/northern than Provençal and the village is largely **invented/composed** (not
   the actual view from the window). Why it matters: proof the picture is synthesized,
   not transcribed. [Wiki-EN]
5. **The eleven stars and the swirling/rolling sky** — the great curling eddies have no
   direct precedent in the actual sky; pure expressive invention. Why it matters: the
   heart of why the work reads as modern/Post-Impressionist. [Wiki-EN]
6. **The rolling blue hills along the horizon (the Alpilles)** — the low mountain range
   south of Saint-Rémy, the one topographically real backdrop element. [Wiki-EN]

### DOCUMENTED vs LEGEND — handle with care
- **[PARTLY LEGEND] "Painted from his asylum window at night."** Nuance: it depicts the
  *view* from his east-facing bedroom window, but was **painted by day in the studio**,
  and the foreground village is largely invented. NOT painted "from memory" either —
  "from memory" is also inaccurate. Use the precise framing. [Wiki-EN, MoMA]
- **[LEGEND] The swirls depict his mental illness / a literal vision.** Interpretive,
  unprovable. Van Gogh explicitly denied a religious/romantic program for it. Present as
  interpretation, not fact. [Wiki-EN]
- **[LEGEND/MYTH — handle carefully] "Van Gogh only ever sold one painting in his
  life."** This myth is usually attached to fame stories. Reality: **The Red Vineyard**
  is the only painting **definitively documented sold** (to Anna Boch, ~400 francs,
  Brussels Les XX exhibition, early 1890). But the Van Gogh Museum itself notes he also
  **traded** works and his uncle commissioned some, and there's evidence of at least
  one other sale. So: "only one *documented* sale" is defensible; the flat "sold only
  one painting ever" is a myth. The Starry Night itself was **never sold in his
  lifetime.** [Van Gogh Museum / The Art Newspaper / Red Vineyard-Wiki]

### Born-verified image (pre-1931, US public domain)
- **Main / hero:** `File:Vincent van Gogh - Starry Night - Google Art Project.jpg`
  (high-res Google Art Project capture of the MoMA canvas). Also commonly used:
  `File:Van Gogh - Starry Night - Google Art Project.jpg`. Both on Commons.
  Confidence: high (painting 1889 → PD). [Commons]
- Detail crops exist as tiled `...-x0-y0`, `-x1-y0`, etc. on Commons for close-ups

## 2. The Bedroom (Bedroom in Arles) — Vincent van Gogh (id `bedroom-arles`)

### WHICH VERSION THIS READ COVERS
**There are three autograph versions.** This read covers the **FIRST version, October
1888, now in the Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam** (per the task brief). The other two are
described below so the author keeps them straight and does not mix details. [Wiki-EN, VGM]

| Version | Date | Size (cm) | Location | Distinguishing tell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1st (THIS read)** | mid-Oct 1888 | 72 × 90 | **Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam** | original; later water-damaged in a flood |
| 2nd | Sept 1889 | 72.4 × 91.3 | Art Institute of Chicago | full-size *répétition* (copy) made after the 1st was relined |
| 3rd | late Sept 1889 | 57.5 × 74 | Musée d'Orsay, Paris | smaller *réduction* made for his mother & sister Wil |

Versions are told apart by the **pictures on the wall**. Keep the read's facts to the
1st version. [Wiki-EN, AIC, d'Orsay, VGM]

### Identity (1st version)
- **Title:** The Bedroom. French (his own usage): *La chambre à coucher* / commonly
  *La Chambre à Arles*; Dutch: *De slaapkamer* / *Slaapkamer te Arles*. [Wiki-EN, VGM]
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890).
- **Date:** mid-**October 1888**. [Wiki-EN, VGM letters]
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas. [VGM]

### Dimensions (BOTH units)
- **cm (1st version):** 72 × 90 cm (h × w). [VGM, Wiki-EN]
- **in:** ≈ 28 1/3 × 35 1/2 in (28.3 × 35.4 in).
- **ft/in for app:** **2 ft 4 in × 2 ft 11 1/2 in** (2.36 ft × 2.95 ft). Confidence: high.

### Location + accession
- **Current location:** Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam.
- **Ownership:** Owned by the **Vincent van Gogh Foundation** (set up by the artist's
  nephew, Vincent Willem van Gogh) **since 1962**, on **permanent loan** to the Van Gogh
  Museum. [Wiki-EN, VGM]
- **Accession / object ref:** Van Gogh Museum inv. **s0047V1962**; catalogue raisonné
  **F482 / JH1608**. Confidence: high. [VGM collection record, Van Gogh Worldwide F482]

### Provenance chain (1st version)
- **The first version never left the family estate.** Vincent → Theo (1890) → Jo van
  Gogh-Bonger → V.W. van Gogh (nephew) → **Vincent van Gogh Foundation (1962)** →
  permanent loan to Van Gogh Museum (museum opened 1973). Confidence: high. [Wiki-EN, VGM]

### Where it came from (the artist's situation)
- Van Gogh had moved into the **Yellow House at 2, Place Lamartine, Arles** (the house
  he rented and hoped would become a "Studio of the South" artists' colony). The bedroom
  picture was painted in **October 1888**, shortly before Gauguin arrived to join him.
  [Wiki-EN, VGM]
- **[DOCUMENTED]** He painted it during/after a stretch of being **worn out and
  bedridden for several days**, and conceived it as an image of **rest**. [Wiki-EN,
  VGM letters]
- The real room was **not rectangular** — a trapezoid, with an obtuse angle at the
  front-left and an acute angle at the right; that genuine odd geometry partly explains
  the "tilted" walls and floor (it's not purely distortion). [Wiki-EN]

### The making (technique + his stated intent)
- **[DOCUMENTED]** In a mid-October 1888 letter to Theo, van Gogh said the picture was
  "simply my bedroom, but **the color has to do the job here**," meant "to be suggestive
  of rest or of sleep in general," so that "looking at the painting should rest the mind,
  or rather the imagination." [VGM letters, Wiki-EN]
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Technique stated in the same letter: "**the shadows and cast shadows
  are removed; it's colored in flat, plain tints like the Japanese prints**." Flatness
  and Japonisme are his own words, not later interpretation. [VGM letters]
- **[DOCUMENTED] His intended color scheme** (from the letter — note the *intended*
  colors; some have shifted with age, e.g. the walls now read bluer than the "pale
  violet" he names):
  - Walls: pale violet · Floor: red tiles · Bed & chairs: butter/fresh-butter yellow
  - Sheets & pillows: lemon green (very pale) · Blanket/coverlet: scarlet/blood red
  - Window: green · Washstand/dressing table: orange · Basin: blue · Doors: lilac
  [VGM letters]
- He made small sketches of the composition inside letters to Theo and to Gauguin.
  [Wiki-EN, VGM]
- **⚠️ "Painted in a single day"** — frequently repeated; not firmly documented. Safer:
  painted quickly over a short span in mid-October 1888. Do not assert "one day" as fact.

### Why a second version exists (key story beat — DOCUMENTED)
- While van Gogh was hospitalized (1889), the **Yellow House was flooded**; the first
  canvas suffered **water damage** and was sent to Theo and **relined** to save it.
  Van Gogh then painted the **full-size second version (Sept 1889, now Chicago)** as a
  faithful copy, and a **smaller third version** for his mother and sister. So the
  three-version story is rooted in the flood damage to version 1. [Wiki-EN, AIC, VGM]

### Reception & afterlife
- Not exhibited/sold to acclaim in his lifetime; like Starry Night, its fame is
  posthumous and built through the family estate and the Van Gogh Museum. The Bedroom
  is now among the most reproduced van Gogh images. (No single "key exhibition" makes it
  canonical the way a sale would — present the afterlife as estate-driven canonization.)
  [Wiki-EN, VGM]

### "Look closer" candidate pointers (verifiable, on the canvas)
1. **The tilted floor and converging walls** — the room genuinely was a trapezoid
   (obtuse front-left angle), so the "wrong" perspective is part-real, part-flattening.
   Why it matters: corrects the lazy "he just couldn't do perspective" read. [Wiki-EN]
2. **The two pillows and turned-down bed for one person** — Why it matters: it's a
   single sleeper's room, framed by van Gogh as an image of rest, not loneliness per his
   letter. [VGM letters]
3. **The two rush-seated yellow chairs** — the "butter yellow" furniture he names in
   the color list. Why it matters: lets you point directly at his documented palette. [VGM letters]
4. **The pair of portraits on the wall (right)** — in the first version these are
   portraits of his friends (the "poet" Eugène Boch and the "lover" Paul-Eugène
   Milliet). The pictures on the wall are the tell that distinguishes the three versions.
   ⚠️ Exact identification of which portrait is which differs slightly between sources —
   describe as "small portraits of friends" and flag if naming them. [Wiki-EN]
5. **The window at the back** — looks onto Place Lamartine / the public garden; the
   shuttered casement is the room's one opening to the outside. [Wiki-EN]
6. **Flat color, no cast shadows** — point out the absence of modeling shadows; this is
   the Japanese-print flatness he explicitly describes. [VGM letters]

### DOCUMENTED vs LEGEND
- **[DOCUMENTED]** intent ("rest/sleep," color does the work), flat Japanese-print
  technique, the color list, the flood damage prompting v2/v3 — all from his letters /
  museum record.
- **[DISPUTED/⚠️]** "Painted in one day" — unproven.
- **[INTERPRETATION]** Readings of the room as a symbol of his isolation or instability
  are later interpretation; his own stated meaning is rest. Present accordingly.

### Born-verified image (pre-1931, US public domain)
- **Main / hero (1st version, Van Gogh Museum):** the canonical Commons image for F482.
  ⚠️ **Verify the exact filename at fetch time** — the Google Art Project Commons files
  most often surfaced (`File:Vincent van Gogh - The Bedroom - Google Art Project.jpg`
  and `File:Vincent van Gogh - Van Gogh's Bedroom in Arles - Google Art Project.jpg`)
  correspond to the **2nd (Chicago)** and **3rd (Orsay)** versions, NOT the Amsterdam
  first version. For the first version use the Van Gogh Museum F482 file on Commons —
  confirm it shows the Amsterdam version (the three look near-identical; check the wall
  portraits and the museum credit on the file page). Confidence: medium on filename,
  high on PD status (1888). [Commons — verify]

## 3. A Sunday on La Grande Jatte — 1884 — Georges Seurat (id `grande-jatte`)

### Identity
- **Title (AIC's exact form):** *A Sunday on La Grande Jatte — 1884*.
- **Full descriptive English title (Wikipedia):** A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of
  La Grande Jatte.
- **Original French:** *Un dimanche après-midi à l'Île de la Grande Jatte*. [AIC, Wiki-EN]
- **Artist:** Georges Seurat (1859–1891).
- **Dates:** **1884–1886.** Worked May 1884 – March 1885, then Oct 1885 – May 1886.
  ⚠️ Note: Seurat **reworked the painted dotted border ~1888–89**; the core canvas is
  1884–86. [AIC, Wiki-EN]
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas. [AIC]

### Dimensions (BOTH units)
- **cm (as AIC states):** 207.5 × 308.1 cm (h × w). (Wikipedia gives 207.6 × 308.) [AIC]
- **in:** ≈ 81 3/4 × 121 1/4 in.
- **ft/in for app:** **6 ft 9 3/4 in × 10 ft 1 1/4 in** (≈ 6.81 ft × 10.10 ft). It is a
  very large painting — roughly 7 × 10 feet. Confidence: high. [AIC]

### Location + accession / credit line
- **Current location:** The Art Institute of Chicago.
- **Credit line:** **Helen Birch Bartlett Memorial Collection.** [AIC]
- **Accession number:** **1926.224.** Confidence: high (consistent across sources;
  ⚠️ the museum's own page is the authority — verify the exact number string at fetch
  time, but 1926.224 is the standard citation). [AIC, Wiki-EN]
- **Acquisition note:** Frederic Clay Bartlett and his wife Helen Birch Bartlett bought
  it in 1924; it entered the Art Institute in 1926 as a memorial to Helen Birch Bartlett.
  A condition reportedly restricts external loans. [Wiki, AIC]

### Provenance chain
Confidence: solid (Wiki-FR/Wiki-EN); spine:
1. **Georges Seurat** → after his sudden death (1891), his family.
2. **Émile Seurat** (the artist's brother) held it; in **1900 sold it for ~800 francs**
   to **Léon-Casimir Bru**, who bought on the advice of his daughter, painter **Lucie
   Cousturier**. [Wiki-FR]
3. **Lucie Cousturier** (inherited from her father) → sold **1924** to the gallery
   **Charles Vildrac**, Paris.
4. **Vildrac resold it the same year (1924)** to **Frederic Clay Bartlett & Helen Birch
   Bartlett**, Chicago.
5. **1926: gift to the Art Institute of Chicago** (Helen Birch Bartlett Memorial
   Collection). [Wiki-FR, AIC]
- ⚠️ Madeleine Knobloch was Seurat's partner (model for *Young Woman Powdering
  Herself*), but the Grande Jatte passed through Seurat's **brother Émile**, not
  Knobloch — don't conflate. [Wiki]

### Where it came from (the artist's situation)
- Seurat, in his mid-20s, was building a rigorously systematic alternative to
  Impressionism, grounded in contemporary **color theory** (Chevreul, Rood — optical
  mixing). The Grande Jatte was his monumental statement piece, following his first
  large canvas *Bathers at Asnières* (1884). [Wiki-EN, Met essay]
- Subject: an actual leisure spot — **La Grande Jatte, an island in the Seine** just
  west of Paris (between Neuilly and Levallois) — where Parisians of mixed classes
  strolled on Sundays. [Wiki-EN]

### The making (technique, two years, the dots)
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Two years of work (1884–86), preceded by **dozens of preparatory
  studies** — small oil panels (*croquetons*) and conté-crayon drawings done on the
  island, plus a near-full-size painted study (the study is at the Met). [Met, Wiki-EN]
- **Technique — the pointillism/divisionism distinction (handle precisely):**
  - The surface is built from countless small dots/strokes of pure color meant to
    **mix optically in the viewer's eye** rather than on the palette.
  - **Seurat himself preferred the term "Divisionism" (or *chromoluminarisme*)** for the
    underlying method (dividing color into its components); **"Pointillism"** (the dot
    technique) was a label others applied, often mockingly at first. The painting is the
    founding monument of both terms and of **Neo-Impressionism**. [Wiki-EN]
- **The painted border:** Seurat later (~1888–89) added a **border of painted dots**
  around the image itself, framed in turn by a plain white wooden frame — still how the
  AIC displays it. The dotted border is part of the artwork. [Wiki, AIC]
- **Figures:** roughly **48 people** (the commonly cited count — ⚠️ "about 48," not a
  hard official number), plus animals: a **woman with a pet monkey** (foreground right,
  on a leash), at least **three dogs**, people fishing, a man playing a horn, a
  child/girl in white near the center. [Wiki-EN]

### Reception
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Debuted at the **eighth and last Impressionist exhibition, opening 15
  May 1886**, where Seurat and the Neo-Impressionists (Signac, Pissarro) were given a
  separate room. Reception was **divided** — some saw the next step for painting, others
  were put off by its airless, "frozen," emotionless quality. [Wiki-EN]
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Critic **Félix Fénéon** championed it and **coined the term
  "Neo-Impressionism" in 1886** in writing about this circle. [Wiki-EN, Neo-Impressionism-Wiki]

### Afterlife
- Became the canonical icon of Neo-Impressionism / pointillism; an Art Institute of
  Chicago signature work; saturated popular culture (e.g. it anchors Sondheim's musical
  *Sunday in the Park with George*, and appears in *Ferris Bueller's Day Off*). Present
  pop-culture afterlife lightly and only if useful. [Wiki-EN]

### "Look closer" candidate pointers (verifiable, on the canvas)
1. **The dotted painted border around the whole image** — not a frame, it's *painted*;
   Seurat added it ~1888–89 to control the transition to the wall. Why it matters: the
   artwork includes its own border. [Wiki, AIC]
2. **The woman with a monkey on a leash, foreground right** — the *singerie* detail
   most people miss; the monkey (*singe*) was also a pun/symbol of female impropriety in
   the era. Why it matters: it's the picture's sly social note. [Wiki-EN]
3. **The standing woman with a fishing rod at the left waterline** — "fishing"
   (*pêcher* / *pécher* pun) read by some as a hint at the area's reputation; at minimum
   she anchors the left edge. Treat the pun as interpretation, not fact. [Wiki-EN]
4. **The little girl in white, near center** — the one figure rendered almost without
   dots, brightly lit, the compositional/optical focal point. [Wiki-EN]
5. **The dots themselves, seen up close vs. far** — optical mixing: the grass is built
   from greens, yellows, oranges, blues that fuse at viewing distance. The single best
   "why it's a break" detail. [Wiki-EN, Met]
6. **The darkened/brown patches in the sunlit grass** — Seurat used a **zinc yellow
   (chrome) pigment that has chemically darkened to brown** over time; the lawn was
   meant to be far brighter. Why it matters: what you see is partly faded — a real
   conservation fact, not the original effect. [Wiki-EN]

### DOCUMENTED vs LEGEND
- **[DOCUMENTED]** two-year making with many studies; debut at the 1886 eighth
  Impressionist exhibition; Fénéon coining "Neo-Impressionism"; the painted dotted
  border; zinc-yellow darkening.
- **[PRECISION, not legend] Pointillism vs Divisionism:** don't use them as synonyms —
  Seurat favored "Divisionism" for the method; "Pointillism" names the dot facture. Get
  this right; it's a common error.
- **[SOFT/⚠️] "exactly 48 figures," exact dog/animal counts, the pun-based readings
  (fishing = prostitution, monkey = loose morals)** — these are popular interpretations
  and approximate counts; flag as "about" / "some scholars read." Not hard fact.
- **[INTERPRETATION] The scene as social satire of class/leisure** — a defensible major
  reading, but a reading; present as interpretation.
