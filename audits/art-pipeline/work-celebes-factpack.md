## 2. THE STORY

Ernst painted *Celebes* in **Cologne in 1921**, while he was the engine of **Cologne
Dada** — the anti-art, anti-bourgeois movement born out of the wreckage of WWI. But
*Celebes* is the picture where Dada tips over into something else. It is a still, almost
silent dream-scene rather than a Dada provocation: a monstrous mechanical beast stands
alone on a bare plain under a flat sky, and the longer you look the less sense it makes
and the more inevitable it feels.

The "elephant" at the center is not invented from nothing. Ernst built its body from a
**photograph of an African communal corn-bin** (a large clay grain-storage silo that
stands on two stubby legs) that he found in an **anthropological publication**. He took
that hollow clay container, gave it a **metallic, boiler-like skin**, and bolted on
appendages it never had: a **tusked snout on a flexible hose/trumpet**, a frilly metal
collar, a little **horned mechanical head**. The corn-bin's two squat legs become the
"elephant's" legs; the whole thing reads as part-animal, part-machine, part-idol.

Around it Ernst scatters dream-logic non-sequiturs: a **headless female mannequin** in
the lower right, one gloved hand raised as if beckoning the beast; **fish swimming
through the sky**; a tower/totem-pole; a small dark airplane-like shape trailing smoke.
Nothing connects, and that *is* the point — this is **collage logic carried into oil
paint**. Ernst's Dada collages spliced unrelated printed images into impossible
machines; here he does the same operation with a brush, so the seams are invisible and
the impossible thing looks solidly, photographically *there*.

That is why *Celebes* is so often called **"the first masterpiece of Surrealist
painting"** — it delivers the dream-image, painted with deadpan realism, three years
before André Breton's **Surrealist manifesto of 1924** even named the movement. (See §8
for how to scope that claim honestly.) The lineage it sits in is **Giorgio de Chirico's**
metaphysical townscapes — empty plazas, long shadows, mannequins, dread — which Ernst
had absorbed from reproductions.

## 3. WHY IT'S A BREAK (required — before / after)

**Before** *Celebes*, the dream-image in modern art lived mostly in two separate places:
in **de Chirico's** metaphysical paintings (uncanny but hand-painted invention) and in
**Dada photo-collage** (Ernst's own scissored, printed, obviously-cut machine-creatures).
Dada's whole posture was disruption — cut, mock, deny "art." A painted dream that asked
to be *believed* as a single coherent scene was not yet the program.

**The break:** *Celebes* fuses the two. Ernst takes the **collage operation — the chance
collision of unrelated things — and executes it in oil paint**, with the smooth,
photographic finish that makes the viewer accept the monster as physically real. The
seams vanish; the impossible becomes solid. This is the **hinge from Dada into
Surrealism**: the dream is no longer a joke about images, it is a *world*. It is the
moment the collage-mind stops being scissors-and-glue and becomes a way of *painting*.

**After:** this becomes the template for high Surrealist painting — **Dalí, Tanguy,
Magritte** all paint the irrational with hard realist conviction so the impossible reads
as fact. Ernst himself wrote the theory under it: collage as "the chance meeting of two
distant realities on an unfamiliar plane" (§4). *Celebes*, painted **1921**, is the
proof-of-concept three years ahead of the **1924** manifesto that named what he'd done.

## 4. KEY STATEMENT (verbatim, sourced — with variant flags)

There is **no reliably-sourced Ernst quote specifically *about Celebes itself*** that I
could verify to a primary source. Treat any "Ernst on Celebes" line as unverified.
The strong, citable statement is Ernst's **general definition of collage**, which is the
correct key passage for this work because *Celebes* is collage-logic-as-painting.

**Ernst's collage definition (the load-bearing quote):**
> "the systematic exploitation of the coincidental or artificially provoked encounter of
> two or more unrelated realities on an apparently inappropriate plane — and the spark of
> poetry created by the proximity of these realities."

- ⚠ **Translation variant — flag, don't blend.** This circulates in several English
  renderings (e.g. "the chance meeting of two distant realities on an unfamiliar
  plane"; "the coupling of two realities, irreconcilable in appearance, upon a plane
  which apparently does not suit them"). These are **competing translations of the same
  French text**, not different quotes. Pick ONE rendering, quote it cleanly, and cite it
  — do not stitch phrases from two translations into one "quote."
- **Provenance of the idea:** Ernst is explicitly generalizing **Lautréamont's**
  famous image (from *Les Chants de Maldoror*): *"beautiful as the chance encounter of a
  sewing machine and an umbrella on a dissecting table."* If you quote Ernst, name
  Lautréamont as the source he's expanding — that's the honest framing.

> SHIP RULE: verify the exact wording + a real citation (a printed Ernst text /
> reputable scholarly source) for whichever translation is used, at authoring time. Do
> NOT assert a verbatim Ernst quote from memory.

## 5. LOOKING POINTS (prose pointers — no coordinates)

1. **The boiler / corn-bin body.** The huge rounded mass at the center is the "elephant."
   Its true source is a photographed African clay **corn-bin** — note the squat pair of
   legs (the silo's legs) and the riveted, metallic, **boiler-like** surface Ernst gave
   it. It reads as machine and animal at once.
2. **The tusked snout / trumpet on a hose.** Reaching out from the body's front is a
   long flexible **tube ending in a tusked, trumpet-like mouth** — the "elephant's trunk"
   that isn't a trunk. Follow how it curls back toward the body.
3. **The little horned mechanical "head."** Atop/at the end of the form sits a small
   **horned head** with a frilly metal collar — too small for the body, like a bolt-on
   afterthought, which heightens the wrongness.
4. **The headless female mannequin, lower right.** A pale, **headless female figure**
   stands at the right, one **gloved hand raised** as if summoning or directing the beast.
   The classic de-Chirico/Surrealist mannequin — human, but blank and dismembered.
5. **The flying fish.** Up in the sky, **fish swim through the air** (upper left). The
   single clearest dream-logic violation: the wrong creatures in the wrong medium.
6. **The bare horizon.** A **low, empty horizon** under a flat, washed sky. The emptiness
   does the work — it isolates the monster, gives it scale and silence, and turns the
   whole scene into a stage. (Also present and worth a glance: a tower/totem pole at right
   and a small dark **airplane-like shape trailing smoke**.)

## 7. RIGHTS

- **The painting was first published in 1921.** Under US copyright law, works published
  before 1930 are in the **public domain in the United States** — so the image is
  **PD-US**, which is the basis on which it sits on Wikimedia/English Wikipedia (the
  wired file). This is the safe, plain statement for our (US-context) use.
- **Max Ernst died in 1976.** In the **EU / life+70 jurisdictions his work is under
  copyright until end of 2046.** Any "museum ©" / artist-estate © notice (e.g. via
  ADAGP/DACS) is an **EU-side restriction**, not a US one.
- **Bottom line:** safe to use as PD-US (pre-1930 publication). Do not import an
  EU-only "© Max Ernst Estate" claim as if it governed our use; do credit Tate as the
  holding collection.

## 8. APOCRYPHA / TRAPS (correct, with sources)

1. **The corn-bin source — TRUE, but get the attribution right.** The central form *is*
   based on a photograph of an **African communal corn-bin/grain silo**, refigured as the
   "elephant." Ernst himself confirmed it. **Two scholarly framings exist — present
   carefully, do not over-specify:**
   - One account (traced to **John Craxton's** identification, "subsequently confirmed by
     Ernst"): the bin is from the **Konkombwa people of the southern Sudan**, the image
     from an **English anthropological journal**.
   - Another account attributes the photograph to **Robert Schomburgk**, of a two-legged
     clay **guinea-corn bin** from the **Konkomba (West African) culture**, published in a
     travel memoir / anthropological journal.
   - ⚠ **These disagree on "southern Sudan" vs "West Africa" and on the exact people
     (Konkombwa/Konkomba) and photographer.** SAFE prose: *"based on a photograph of an
     African communal corn-bin (a clay grain silo on two legs) that Ernst found in an
     anthropological publication."* Do **not** pin it to a single tribe/region/photographer
     as settled fact unless you cite the specific scholarly source you're following.

2. **The schoolboy-rhyme title — TRUE.** Ernst told Penrose the title came from a bawdy
   **German schoolboys' rhyme** ("Der Elefant von Celebes…"). "Celebes" is also the old
   name for the island of **Sulawesi** (Indonesia). Both facts are real; the rhyme is the
   *title's* origin, the island is what the word *means*. Don't claim the painting is
   "about" Indonesia — it isn't; the word arrived via the rhyme.

3. **"First Surrealist masterpiece" — SCOPE IT.** This is a real, widely-repeated
   art-historical assessment (often phrased "the first masterpiece of Surrealist painting
   in the de Chirico tradition"). **But it is a critical judgment, not a date-stamp, and
   it is technically anachronistic:** *Celebes* was painted in **1921**, while **Surrealism
   was not founded until Breton's manifesto of 1924.** Correct framing for prose: *"often
   called the first masterpiece of Surrealist painting — though Ernst painted it in 1921,
   three years before Surrealism had a name."* Attribute it as a claim ("often called"),
   never assert it flatly as if uncontested or contemporaneous.

4. **Dimensions — minor trap.** The precise Tate figure is **125.4 × 107.9 cm**
   (1254 × 1079 mm). Many sites round to "125 × 108 cm" or "126 × 108 cm." Use the Tate
   figure; convert to ft/in (≈ **4 ft 1.4 in × 3 ft 6.5 in**), never cm-only in prose.

5. **"Elephant" trap.** It is not a depiction of an elephant. The form is a corn-bin
   reimagined; "elephant" comes from the rhyme/title and the trunk-like snout. Don't
   describe it as Ernst "painting an elephant."
