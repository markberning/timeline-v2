**kind:** work · **movement:** Neo-Impressionism / Post-Impressionism (the Divisionist

## C. THE FIVE NARRATIVE BEATS (sourced ground truth)

### Beat 1 — The summer of 1904 at Saint-Tropez, with Signac
- Summer **1904**, Matisse (then 34, born 1869) leaves Paris for **Saint-Tropez** on the
  Côte d'Azur and works near **Paul Signac**, the leading living Neo-Impressionist, and
  **Henri-Edmond Cross**, who lived nearby. [DOCUMENTED] Wikipedia.
- Matisse had already read Signac's theoretical book ***D'Eugène Delacroix au
  néo-impressionnisme*** (serialized 1898, book 1899) — the manifesto of the divided-color
  method — so he arrived primed; the summer was where he *practiced* it under Signac's eye.
  [DOCUMENTED] Wikipedia.
- He painted an **oil study** on the spot that summer (now **MoMA**, *Study for "Luxe,
  calme et volupté,"* 1904), then worked the full canvas up over the following winter in
  Paris. The Orsay picture is the **finished painting**, not the on-the-spot sketch.
  [DOCUMENTED] MoMA + Centre Pompidou date "Autumn–Winter 1904."

### Beat 2 — Neo-Impressionism / Divisionism / Pointillism, explained
*(Author MUST inline-define all three for a zero-knowledge reader — and keep them
distinct, this is a clarity trap.)*
- **Neo-Impressionism** — the movement Seurat founded in the 1880s and Signac led after
  Seurat's 1891 death: build a picture out of small, separate touches of *unmixed* color
  that the eye blends at a distance, on the theory that optical mixing stays brighter than
  mixing paint on the palette. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Divisionism** — the *theory/method* itself: "divide" each color into its components and
  lay them side by side (the term Signac preferred). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Pointillism** — strictly, the *technique* of doing that with regular **dots**; often
  used loosely as a synonym. *Matisse did NOT use neat round dots* — he used **short,
  blocky dashes / square touches** of color, a looser, coarser stroke than Seurat's. Frame
  the words precisely: the method is Divisionism; "pointillist" describes the look but his
  marks are dashes, not pure points. [DOCUMENTED] Smarthistory/Wikipedia.
  *(Compare the corpus: the Grande Jatte read already nails "Divisionism ≠ Pointillism.")*

### Beat 3 — The 1905 Salon des Indépendants debut
- *Luxe, calme et volupté* was exhibited at the **Salon des Indépendants in spring 1905**
  (Paris; the jury-free, no-prizes exhibiting society co-founded by Seurat & Signac in 1884
  — gloss it). [DOCUMENTED] Wikipedia.
- **Crucial timeline for the author (anti-overclaim):** this was **BEFORE** the Salon
  d'Automne of **autumn 1905**, where Matisse's hot-color canvases drew critic **Louis
  Vauxcelles**'s jibe "*Donatello parmi les fauves*" ("Donatello among the wild beasts")
  and gave **Fauvism** its name. So at its debut *Luxe, calme et volupté* read as an
  ambitious **Neo-Impressionist** picture, not a Fauvist one. It POINTS toward Fauvism; it
  is not yet Fauvism. [DOCUMENTED] (See LEGEND-WATCH §A.)

### Beat 4 — Signac buys it
- **Paul Signac bought the painting** (the older master buying the younger man's homage to
  his own method). It passed down in **Signac's family** — the Centre Pompidou provenance
  reads "ancienne collection **Mme Cachin-Signac**" (Signac's daughter **Ginette** married
  into the Cachin family; she is the link). [DOCUMENTED] Wikipedia + Centre Pompidou.
- **No purchase price is documented** in the consulted sources — **do NOT invent one.**
  (Several popular pages say "Signac hung it in his villa *La Hune* in Saint-Tropez";
  treat the villa detail as **[DISPUTED]** — plausible and widely repeated but not in a
  museum source consulted; frame it as reported or omit.)

### Beat 5 — How it points toward Fauvism
- Even working in the dot, Matisse was already pushing color past description: the figures
  carry **green and blue in their skin**, the sand and sky run to **arbitrary, emotive
  color** that answers the picture's mood, not the eye's report. The discipline of the
  method couldn't contain that impulse. [DOCUMENTED] Smarthistory.
- Within months Matisse **abandoned the systematic dot** — he found that dividing the color
  also divided the *form*, dissolving the firm outline and flat color-area he wanted. The
  break led straight to the flat, fierce, arbitrary-color canvases of **Fauvism** (1905),
  so this painting is repeatedly called the **"starting point of Fauvism."** [DOCUMENTED]
  Wikipedia. **Scope the claim "Western"/within-Matisse; don't state "modern art begins
  here."** See §F for the quote that supports "the dot destroyed his form."

## D. SIX "LOOK CLOSER" ANNOTATIONS ({label, where, detail})
*(Each is a verifiable claim about what is literally on the canvas — fact-checker will
re-check each against the painting image. `where` = a written location phrase, NO crop
coords.)*

1. **The bathers on the shore** — *where:* center and right foreground, the cluster of
   **nude women** on and around a spread cloth at the water's edge. *detail:* The picture's
   subject is a group of nude bathers relaxing on a Mediterranean beach — a timeless
   golden-age idyll, not a real moment. The poses (toweling off, reclining, standing)
   descend from the long "bathers in a landscape" tradition; Matisse stages them as a
   pastoral fantasy, not a Saint-Tropez snapshot. [DOCUMENTED]

2. **The picnic spread** — *where:* lower center, the cloth laid on the sand with figures
   gathered around it. *detail:* A picnic on the shore — a teapot/coffee service and food
   on a white cloth — turns the scene into a leisure ritual. Widely read as Matisse's
   answer to **Manet's *Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe* (1863)** and to the bather-picnic theme;
   treat the Manet echo as a scholarly reading, not the artist's stated intent. [DOCUMENTED
   as a common reading / frame as interpretation]

3. **The divisionist dots (really dashes)** — *where:* everywhere across the surface, most
   testable in the sand and sky. *detail:* The whole picture is built from **separate
   touches of unmixed color** meant to blend in the eye, not on the palette — but Matisse's
   marks are **short, blocky dashes**, coarser than Seurat's neat dots. Stand back and they
   fuse into shimmering light; up close the surface is a mosaic of distinct strokes.
   [DOCUMENTED]

4. **Arbitrary color creeping in** — *where:* the **figures' skin** (greens, blues, pinks
   that no body is) and the heightened sand and water. *detail:* The color has slipped its
   leash from description: flesh carries **green and blue**, the shore and sky run hotter
   and stranger than nature. This is the Fauvist impulse — color chosen for feeling, not
   accuracy — already at work inside the orderly dot. [DOCUMENTED] Smarthistory.

5. **The Arcadian shore (and the boat)** — *where:* the background, the **bay, the distant
   sailboat, and the line of hills/sky**. *detail:* A calm bay with a **sailboat** and
   far shore sets the scene in an idealized, sun-struck nowhere — the "calm" and "luxury"
   of Baudelaire's refrain made into place. The landscape is a generalized golden-age
   coast, not a topographic view of Saint-Tropez. [DOCUMENTED]

6. **The painted touch as the subject** — *where:* compare a tightly stippled passage
   (sky/water) against the looser, larger strokes on the bodies. *detail:* Matisse varies
   the stroke — denser, smaller touches in the atmosphere, broader ones on the figures — so
   the **method itself is on display.** The unevenness is the tell that this is a young
   painter testing a borrowed system, not a doctrinaire pointillist. [DOCUMENTED — keep as
   a looking observation, hedge any "deliberate" intent claim]

> **Author note:** annotations 2, 4 lean interpretive — frame the Manet echo and the
> "Fauvist impulse" as readings, not asserted fact. Exact left/right placement of
> individual figures should be re-checked against the canvas at the image gate before locking.

## LEGEND-WATCH — documented vs. legend (the fact-checker's primary axis)

**§A — "the starting point of Fauvism" — [DOCUMENTED but PRECISION-CRITICAL].**
Widely and reliably stated (Wikipedia, museum literature). The trap is letting it imply the
painting *is* Fauvist or *was received* as Fauvist. It is a **Neo-Impressionist /
Divisionist** picture; **Fauvism** was named at the **Salon d'Automne, autumn 1905**, by
Vauxcelles — **after** this painting's spring-1905 Indépendants debut. Frame it as the
**doorway** Matisse walked through, then closed behind him. Do **not** write "modern art
begins here" or any universal-origin claim (Eurocentric overclaim).

**§B — the Matisse "breaking up colour / breaking-up of form" quote — [DISPUTED / UNVERIFIED].**
The line — "Breaking up colour leads to the breaking-up of form and outline … nothing but a
tease of the retina that destroys the repose of surface and outline" — is widely repeated
online as Matisse's verdict on Divisionism, BUT it was **NOT found on Wikiquote's Henri
Matisse page**, and the consulted sources do not pin it to a dated, primary text (it is
*not* a verbatim line of the 1908 *Notes of a Painter* as some pages imply). **Do NOT quote
it as a verified Matisse quotation.** Either (a) **paraphrase** the idea in house voice
("Matisse came to feel the dot broke up his color and, with it, his form and outline" — the
substance is well attested), or (b) if quoted at all, attribute loosely ("as he later put
it") only after a primary source is found. Per born-verified doctrine, an unverifiable quote
ships as **paraphrase, never as quotation.**

**§C — "Signac hung it in his villa *La Hune*" — [DISPUTED].** Popular-site detail, not in
the museum sources consulted. Frame as reported or drop.

**§D — the Manet *Déjeuner sur l'herbe* echo — [INTERPRETATION, not artist's stated intent].**
A standard and reasonable art-historical reading of the picnic/bathers; frame it as a
reading ("often read as Matisse's answer to Manet"), not as documented intent. Note: some
popular pages give Manet's *Déjeuner* as **1864** — the correct date is **1863** (shown at
the Salon des Refusés that year). Use **1863** if Manet is dated at all.

**§E — "1904 vs 1904–05."** Both appear in print. The museum (Centre Pompidou) dates the
finished canvas **"Autumn–Winter 1904."** Use **1904** in the stats/year field; mention
"finished over the winter of 1904–05" in prose. The MoMA **oil study** is a *different
object* — don't let its details bleed into the Orsay painting's stats.

**§F — purchase price — [ABSENT].** No price is documented for Signac's purchase or for the
1982 dation. **Invent none.** "Bought by Signac" / "entered the national collections by
dation, 1982" carry the beat without a figure.
