# The Age of Exploration — How a Few Wooden Ships Made One World

**Goal:** Narrative for the Age of Exploration, roughly 1415 to 1700 — the three centuries in which a handful of small, poor kingdoms on the western edge of Europe learned to ride the ocean winds in circles and, almost by accident, stitched every inhabited landmass on Earth into a single, violent, world-spanning web of trade. The story opens in 1415, when a Portuguese army storms the Moroccan port of Ceuta and a young prince named Henry glimpses the wealth of the Saharan gold trade — at the very moment China, having sent the largest fleets the world had ever seen across the Indian Ocean under the admiral Zheng He, abruptly turns its back on the sea and leaves it open. From there the book follows the slow, terrified Portuguese creep down the coast of Africa, the invention of the ship and the sailing trick that made it possible, the grim commercial turn that seeded the Atlantic slave trade, and the rounding of the bottom of Africa that finally opened the door to the East. Then the breakout: a miscalculating Genoese named Columbus sails west for the queen of Castile and blunders into a hemisphere nobody in Europe knew was there; the pope and two crowns draw a line down a half-mapped globe and split the planet between them; Vasco da Gama bullies his way into the sophisticated, ancient trade world of the Indian Ocean; Ferdinand Magellan's expedition limps home as the first humans to circle the Earth, having lost their captain to a chieftain's blade on a Philippine beach and most of their number to starvation and storm. Then the conquests — Cortés and the fall of the Aztec city of Tenochtitlan, Pizarro and the fall of the Inca, both achieved less by Spanish swords than by native rebellions and the invisible weapon of Old World disease, which killed perhaps nine in ten of the people of the Americas in the deadliest demographic catastrophe in recorded history. Then the machinery the conquests built: the silver mountain of Potosí, the forced-labor draft that fed it, the piece of eight that became the first world money, the missions and the Inquisition, and the lonely European voices — Las Casas above all — who stood up to ask whether any of it was moral. Then the latecomers, the English and French and Dutch who broke the Iberian monopoly with pirates and joint-stock companies and the first lasting northern colonies. And finally the world the voyages made: the Columbian Exchange of crops, animals, and germs that rewrote diets and populations on every continent; the industrialized slave trade that forcibly carried more than twelve million Africans across the ocean and the African rulers like Afonso of Kongo and Queen Njinga who documented and resisted it; the new map, drawn by Mercator and Ortelius, of a round and single Earth. It is one of history's great accidents and one of its great crimes at the same time, and almost everything about the modern world — its economy, its borders, its languages, its grievances — traces back to a few wooden ships learning how to come home.

A note on names and pronunciation. This is a story told in five or six languages at once — Portuguese, Spanish, Nahuatl, Quechua, the tongues of West Africa and the Indian Ocean — so the names come at the reader thick and strange, and the first time an unfamiliar one appears it gets a plain-English pronunciation in parentheses, stressed syllable in capitals, as in *Tenochtitlan (teh-noch-TEET-lan)* or *Atahualpa (ah-tah-WAHL-pah)*. A few conventions are worth settling at the outset. Spanish and Portuguese names usually put the given name first and the family name second, the way English does, so in *Vasco da Gama*, Vasco is the man; the little Iberian particles (*da*, *de*, *de la*) mean roughly "of" or "from." Many of the famous explorers are known to English by anglicized names — *Columbus* was Cristoforo Colombo in his native Genoese Italian and Cristóbal Colón in the Spanish he sailed under; *Magellan* was the Portuguese Fernão de Magalhães — and this book uses the familiar English forms while noting the originals where it matters. The peoples on the receiving end of the voyages had their own names for themselves that often differ from the labels Europe pinned on them: the people we usually call the *Aztecs* called themselves the *Mexica (meh-SHEE-kah)*, and this book uses both. Dates are CE throughout, since the whole story unfolds well after the year 1000, so there are no BCE dates to track. Miles come first, kilometers in parentheses, for an American reader.

A note on "first" claims and on how we know any of this. As in every timeline in this project, when this book says "first," it almost always means "first that we have surviving evidence for, from the European side." That last clause matters more here than almost anywhere else in the series, because the Age of Exploration is the single most lopsidedly documented encounter in history. The Europeans wrote constantly — ships' logs, royal instructions, merchants' ledgers, missionaries' reports, soldiers' memoirs, epic poems — and they wrote as the people doing the sailing, conquering, and selling, which means the record is saturated with their assumptions, their flattery of their kings, and their need to justify what they were doing. The people they met mostly did not write in alphabets the Europeans preserved (the Mexica and Maya had their own books, and the Spanish burned most of them), so their side comes down to us secondhand, filtered, or reconstructed from archaeology, oral tradition, and the handful of indigenous and mixed-blood writers who learned the conquerors' script and used it to record their own world — people like the Nahua students of the Florentine Codex (Chapter 9) or the half-Inca chroniclers of Peru. Where this book describes what the Taíno, the Mexica, the Inca, the Zamorin of Calicut, or the kings of Kongo thought and did, it is reading carefully against the grain of sources written by their enemies, and it says so when the reading is uncertain. The cure for a one-sided record is not to pick the most dramatic version of events but to flag where the drama comes from a chronicler with a motive — the conquistador inflating the odds he beat, the missionary inflating the souls he saved, the poet turning a trade route into Homer. Where the chronicle, the codex, the ledger, and the spade disagree, the text will say which it is trusting and why.

# Chapter 1 — The Wall at the End of the World

Stand on a beach almost anywhere in the world in the year 1400 and look out at the ocean, and you are looking at the edge of the knowable. Not the edge of the world — educated people in 1400 knew perfectly well the Earth was a sphere; the flat-earth business is a nineteenth-century myth about the Middle Ages, not a medieval belief. What the open ocean was, for nearly every civilization on the planet, was the place the maps ran out. You could sail along a coast, hopping from headland to headland with land always in sight. You could cross a familiar sea like the Mediterranean, where the far shore was known and the routes were old. But the deep blue-water ocean, the part with no coast in any direction, was a one-way trap: the winds that blew you out were assumed to be the same winds that would never let you back, and beyond the horizon lay, as far as anyone knew, either nothing or monsters or the boiling latitudes where the sun came down and cooked the sea. The ocean was a wall. This book is the story of how, in less than a century, a few small kingdoms turned that wall into a road — and of everyone who was living on the far side of it.

Here is the strange thing to hold in your head before the European part of the story starts, because it is the great cosmic accident at the root of everything: the civilization best positioned to cross that wall first, by an enormous margin, had just decided not to. That civilization was Ming China (the dynasty that ruled China from 1368 to 1644), and its instrument was a man named **Zheng He** (jung HUH), a Muslim eunuch admiral (a castrated man; the Chinese court relied on eunuchs as a class of palace servants and officials precisely because, unable to found dynasties of their own, they were thought to owe everything to the emperor) who between 1405 and 1433 commanded the largest wooden fleets the world would see for centuries. We should be careful here, because the numbers in the Chinese sources are exactly the kind of colorful, possibly-inflated detail this book is meant to flag: the chronicles claim some of Zheng He's "treasure ships" were over 400 feet long, several times the length of anything Europe was building (Columbus's *Santa María*, the largest ship in this book's opening voyages, was about 62 feet), and modern scholars argue hard over whether ships that size could really have been built and sailed. What is not in doubt is the scale of the enterprise. Zheng He led seven huge expeditions across the Indian Ocean, with hundreds of vessels and tens of thousands of men, reaching Southeast Asia, India, the Persian Gulf, Arabia, and the east coast of Africa — bringing home giraffes for the emperor's zoo and overawing foreign ports into sending tribute. (The Chinese side of these voyages, and the long argument over why China then walked away from the sea, is the subject of a chapter in the Ming dynasty's own narrative.) China had the ships, the navigators, the wealth, and the reach to round Africa and meet Portugal coming the other way, a full lifetime before Portugal got there.

And then China simply stopped. After 1433 the voyages ended. The reasons are debated — the staggering expense, a court faction that distrusted the eunuchs who ran the fleets, a turn inward to fight the resurgent Mongols on the northern land frontier, a Confucian official class (the scholar-bureaucrats who ran China by the teachings of the ancient philosopher Confucius, which prized farming, frugal government, and inward order over commerce and overseas glory) that saw foreign adventures as vulgar and wasteful — but the upshot is stark: the new policy discouraged private oceangoing trade, the great shipyards fell idle, and in time the detailed records of the voyages were lost or destroyed, very possibly burned by officials who wanted to make sure nobody could argue for doing it again. The mightiest seafaring power on Earth folded up its sails and turned its back on the ocean at the precise moment a tiny kingdom at the other end of the world was about to push out into it. Had China kept going, the entire story of the next four centuries — who colonized whom, whose languages and religions spread, whose diseases traveled which direction — might have run in reverse. It didn't. The Indian Ocean and the door to the Atlantic were left wide open, and the people who walked through were not the obvious candidates.

So let's meet the unlikely candidates, and ask the obvious question: why *Portugal*? Look at a map of Europe in 1400 and Portugal is almost a joke — a narrow strip of land on the far southwestern corner of the continent, hard up against the Atlantic, with maybe a million people, no great cities, no spices, no gold of its own, and a single powerful neighbor, the Spanish kingdom of Castile, forever threatening to swallow it. By every measure of wealth and population, France, England, the Italian city-states, or the Ottoman Empire (the rising Muslim power that would soon dominate the eastern Mediterranean) should have led the way out onto the ocean. But Portugal had a few peculiar advantages that the giants lacked, and they all pushed the same direction. It faced the Atlantic and *only* the Atlantic — it had no rich eastern frontier to be distracted by, so the ocean was the one direction it could expand. It had a long Atlantic coast full of fishermen who understood that particular, brutal, deep-water sea. It had absorbed, through centuries of contact with the Muslim world that had ruled most of Iberia, a working knowledge of Arab and Mediterranean navigation, astronomy, and ship design. And it had just acquired, in 1385, a brand-new royal family with everything to prove.

That family was the **House of Aviz** (AH-veesh), and it came to the throne in a way that mattered. In 1385 a man named **John** (in Portuguese, João) — the illegitimate son of a previous king, and master of the Aviz, one of the crusading military-religious orders (brotherhoods of warrior-monks, half army and half religious community, formed to fight Muslim powers — Iberia had spent centuries in the *Reconquista*, the long Christian campaign to take the peninsula back from the Muslim states that had ruled most of it since the 700s) — won the crown of Portugal essentially on the battlefield, beating back a Castilian attempt to absorb the country and founding a new dynasty as **John I** (reigned 1385–1433). A king who wins his throne in battle, against the odds, at the head of a crusading order, founds a line with restless energy and a chip on its shoulder, and John's sons inherited both. They needed glory, they needed income, and they needed a project bigger than little Portugal could offer at home. They found it across the strait, in Africa.

The project began with a raid, and the raid is the date historians usually pin as the start of the whole European age of overseas expansion: 1415, the conquest of **Ceuta** (say-OO-tah). Ceuta was a wealthy Muslim port on the North African coast directly across the narrow strait from Gibraltar, the Mediterranean terminus of the trans-Saharan trade — the place where caravans that had crossed the desert from the gold-rich kingdoms of West Africa unloaded their cargo for shipment around the Mediterranean. It belonged to the **Marinid sultanate**, the Muslim dynasty then ruling Morocco. In the summer of 1415 King John I threw a Portuguese army across the strait and stormed it. Militarily it was a triumph and economically it was close to useless — once the Portuguese held the port, the caravans simply rerouted to other ports, so Portugal captured the faucet and the water stopped flowing. But Ceuta did two things that mattered far more than the loot. It gave the Aviz princes a foothold and an appetite for North Africa. And it showed them, up close, where the gold was actually coming from: somewhere far to the south, beyond the desert, in Africa itself. If the wealth crossed the Sahara to reach the coast, the Portuguese reasoned, then a ship that could sail down the *Atlantic* coast of Africa might reach the source directly, cutting out the Muslim caravans and the Muslim middlemen entirely. The thought was the seed of everything.

The prince in whom that thought took root has become, fairly or not, the symbol of the entire enterprise: King John's third son, **Henry** (Infante Dom Henrique), known to history by an English nickname he never used in life, **Prince Henry the Navigator** (1394–1460). It is worth being honest about Henry right away, because the legend has grown well past the man. He was at Ceuta as a young man, and he spent the rest of his life — more than forty years — sponsoring, funding, and pushing voyages of exploration down the African coast, financing them in large part from the revenues of the **Order of Christ**, the wealthy Portuguese successor to the Knights Templar (the crusading order suppressed a century earlier), which he ran as its administrator. Henry's ships sailed under the Order's red cross, which tells you that in his own mind this was as much a crusade against Islam as a commercial venture. But the romantic picture of Henry as a great navigator personally bent over charts, or as the founder of a kind of scientific institute, is mostly later invention. He himself rarely went to sea after Ceuta. He was a sponsor and a sponsor's organizer — a venture capitalist of exploration — and that, it turns out, was exactly what the job required. The breakthroughs of the next decades were less about one genius than about a deep-pocketed patron willing to send ship after ship into the unknown, absorb the failures, and try again next year.

There is a famous and very seductive version of how Henry did it: that he gathered at **Sagres** (SAH-gresh), on the wild, wind-scoured southwestern tip of Portugal where the land runs out into the Atlantic, a kind of think tank — pilots, astronomers, mapmakers, shipwrights, the **School of Sagres**, the supposed brain of the African voyages. The trouble is that the evidence for any formal "school" is thin, and modern historians treat it largely as legend; what Henry plainly *did* have was a circle of skilled people he patronized and a base in the Algarve from which expeditions were organized and debriefed. The reason this matters is that it captures the real character of what Portugal was doing, which was genuinely new: turning exploration from a one-off gamble by a private adventurer into a *sustained state and institutional project*, repeated year after year, with each voyage's hard-won knowledge — which currents ran where, which winds blew when, how far the last ship got before its crew lost their nerve — collected and fed into the next. That accumulation, ship by ship, decade by decade, was the actual engine. It is the least glamorous part of the story and the most important.

So picture the world as it sat at the threshold of this book. China, the natural front-runner, has just walked off the field. The ocean is a wall everyone else assumes can't be re-crossed. A small, poor, ambitious kingdom on the edge of Europe, freshly ruled by a battle-won dynasty hungry for glory and gold, has found in the African coast a project worthy of it, and has a prince with a crusading order's treasury willing to fund the slow, methodical pushing-out into the blue. What none of them has yet is the two things the pushing-out actually requires: a ship that can come home against the wind, and the nerve to sail past the first place everyone is sure the world ends. Both are coming, and the place they will be won is a low, ugly headland on the African coast that for years no European ship would pass. That is the next chapter.


# Chapter 2 — Crawling Down Africa

The hardest thing about sailing into the unknown turns out not to be the unknown. It is getting back. A ship that sails out on a steady wind and a favorable current has, by definition, put a steady wind and a favorable current between itself and home, and for centuries that simple, terrifying arithmetic was the real wall at the edge of the world. The story of how Portugal crawled down the coast of Africa is the story of solving that arithmetic — partly with a new kind of ship, mostly with a new kind of nerve, and finally with a sailing trick so counterintuitive that grown sailors had to be argued into trusting it. This chapter is about the machine and the mind that beat the ocean's one-way trap.

Start with the machine, because without it nothing else was possible. The ship that did the early work was the **caravel** (KAIR-uh-vel), and it was a deliberate piece of engineering for a specific problem. It was small — often only fifty to eighty feet long, far smaller than the great trading ships of the Mediterranean — and that smallness was the point. A small ship draws little water, which lets it nose into shallow river mouths and along uncharted coasts without running aground; it can be handled by a small crew, which means less food and water to carry on a voyage of unknown length; and it is cheap enough that a patron can afford to lose a few. But the caravel's genius was in its rig — the arrangement of its masts and sails — and specifically in its use of the **lateen sail** (luh-TEEN). Picture the two basic ways to hang a sail. A *square sail* hangs crosswise to the ship like a bedsheet on a line; it is wonderful for running straight before a following wind, useless for going against it. A *lateen sail* is a tall triangle, hung from a long slanting yard along the length of the ship; it works like an airplane wing turned on its side, and it lets a ship sail at an angle *into* the oncoming wind — not straight into it, no sail can do that, but close-hauled, slicing across it, then turning and slicing back the other way, zigzagging upwind in a maneuver called tacking. The lateen was not a Portuguese invention; they borrowed it from Arab and Mediterranean sailors who had used it for centuries. But mounting it on a tough little Atlantic explorer was the combination that turned the open ocean from a one-way trap into something a ship could work both directions. The caravel could go look at a coast and then claw its way home.

Now the nerve, which was the harder part, and it has a name and a place: **Cape Bojador** (boh-zhah-DOR), a low, unremarkable bulge of the Saharan coast a few hundred miles south of the Canary Islands. To modern eyes there is nothing there — a flat brown headland, some surf, a stretch of shoal water where currents kick up spray and the seabed shelves treacherously. To the sailors of Henry's first expeditions it was the place the world ended. Beyond Bojador, everyone "knew," the sea boiled, the sun came down close enough to scorch a man black (this was, horribly, part of how Europeans then explained the existence of dark-skinned Africans), the water turned to thick green muck a ship would mire in, and monsters waited for anyone fool enough to find out. None of this was true, but it didn't have to be true to stop a fleet, because the deeper, rational fear underneath the legends was real: south of the cape the prevailing wind and current run *southward*, so every mile a ship sailed past Bojador was a mile it would have to fight to come back, and nobody yet knew how far that fight would be or whether a ship could win it. The legends were just the costume the genuine arithmetic wore. For more than a decade Henry sent captains south and, year after year, they sailed to the cape, looked at the spray and the legend, and turned around.

The wall broke in 1434, and the man who broke it was a captain named **Gil Eanes** (zheel ee-AHN-ish). Henry had sent him the previous year and he had come back, like everyone else, without rounding the cape. Henry sent him again, and this time — sailing well out to sea to swing wide around the dreaded shoals rather than hugging the coast through them — Eanes passed Cape Bojador, found ordinary ocean on the far side, no boiling water and no monsters, and, crucially, came home alive to say so. It is genuinely one of the great anticlimaxes in the history of exploration: the terrible barrier turned out to be a stretch of empty, slightly choppy sea, and the moment one man proved it the spell that had frozen the whole enterprise simply broke. This is worth dwelling on because it is the pattern of the entire age. The biggest barriers were psychological, and the way past them was almost always a single demonstration that the imagined horror wasn't there — after which everyone wondered what the fuss had been about and the next barrier rose a little farther south. Eanes did not discover a new sea route. He discovered that the old terror was a bluff.

Past Bojador the creep resumed, and now the arithmetic of getting home became the central technical problem, because every mile south was harder to return from. The Portuguese solved it not with a machine but with a discovery about the ocean itself, and it is the single most important and least intuitive idea in this whole book: the **volta do mar** (VOHL-tah doo MAR), Portuguese for "the turn of the sea." Here is the problem. The winds of the North Atlantic do not blow randomly; they blow in a vast, slow, clockwise loop — the trade winds pushing southwest near Africa, the westerlies pushing back northeast up near Europe. A ship coasting south along Africa rides the favorable southward winds easily, but if it tries to sail straight back north along that same coast, it is fighting those winds the entire way, tacking endlessly, making almost no progress, and dying of thirst while it does. The volta do mar was the realization, learned the hard way over years of near-disasters, that the way home was *not* to fight back up the coast at all. Instead you sailed *away* from your destination — out into the open, empty middle of the Atlantic, on a great curving arc to the northwest, until you reached the latitude of the westerlies, which then blew you home to Europe. To get back to Lisbon you first sailed toward the middle of nowhere. Every nerve in a frightened sailor's body screams against deliberately steering away from land and home into open ocean on faith that an invisible wind will eventually curve you back, and it took the Portuguese decades to fully trust it. But the volta do mar is the key that unlocked the Atlantic, and once it was understood the same principle — find the wind that wants to take you where you're going, even if you have to sail the wrong way first to reach it — would be applied to every ocean on Earth. It is, in a real sense, the central technique of the entire Age of Exploration.

The Portuguese learned the loop the way you learn any current — by getting flung around in it — and the place they learned it was a scatter of Atlantic islands they stumbled onto and settled along the way, the first overseas Portuguese real estate and the first laboratories for everything to come. The closest, **Madeira** (muh-DEER-uh), an uninhabited volcanic island off the Moroccan coast, was settled from around 1420; its dense forest gave the island its name (*madeira* is Portuguese for "wood") and its steep slopes, once cleared and terraced, turned out to be superb for a crop that would cast a long and terrible shadow over this whole story: **sugar cane**. Sugar was a luxury in medieval Europe, sweet and scarce and absurdly profitable, and Madeira became one of the first places Europeans grew it on a large scale — which meant it needed a large, captive workforce, and so this pretty island became, quietly, the prototype of the plantation: a single cash crop, grown for distant markets, worked increasingly by enslaved labor. (The model would harden into its full and terrible form a lifetime later on the island of São Tomé off Central Africa, where sugar and enslaved African labor first fused into a system at scale — the laboratory the Atlantic-slave-trade narrative treats as the true birthplace of the plantation.) We will come back to that with the weight it deserves in Chapter 3. Farther out, reached by the early 1430s and lying hundreds of miles into the open Atlantic, were the **Azores** (uh-ZORZ or AY-zorz), and their position was a gift, because they sit right in the band of the homeward westerlies. A ship that had learned to swing out to the Azores on its way home had, in effect, learned the volta do mar in miniature, and the islands became the great waypoint where the homeward arc touched land. The settling of these islands also did something subtler: it made the ocean familiar. Sailors who had grown up assuming the deep Atlantic was a void now had places out in it to aim for, resupply at, and return to. The wall was becoming a neighborhood.

It is worth stopping to feel how *slow* all of this was, because the pace is the truest thing about it and the thing most easily lost in a summary. We are talking about decades — from Gil Eanes at Bojador in 1434 to the Senegal River around 1444 to Sierra Leone by the 1460s — to advance down a coastline that a modern ship covers in a couple of days. Each season's voyage pushed a little farther, planted a marker, brought home a few captives or a little gold dust and a great deal of information, and turned around. There was no master plan to reach India, not for the first several decades; there was a patron who kept paying, a slowly growing body of knowledge about winds and currents and coasts, and a frontier of fear that retreated a few hundred miles per generation. The cross-civilizational contrast is sharp and worth marking: where Zheng He's China had crossed the entire Indian Ocean in a single grand sweep and then quit (Chapter 1), Portugal did the opposite — it advanced by inches, never quit, and let the inches accumulate into something China's single magnificent burst never became, namely a permanent, self-sustaining, ever-extending machine for reaching farther. The tortoise was beating the hare partly because the hare had gone home.

By the time the creep had reached the lush, green, well-watered coasts south of the Sahara, two things had changed that would define the rest of the African story and curdle its character. First, the desert dread was gone — past a certain latitude the coast turned fertile and populous, plainly no boiling wasteland, and the sailors' nerve held easily. Second, and far more consequentially, the voyages had started to pay. For decades the African expeditions had been a money pit, justified by crusading zeal and royal ambition and the hope of someday reaching the gold at its source. Then, in the 1440s, the captains began bringing back two cargoes that finally turned a profit and changed everything about why Portugal sailed south. One was gold dust, traded at the coast. The other was human beings. The little ships that had crawled past the wall at the end of the world, carried by the lateen sail and the volta do mar and the patient money of a crusading prince, had found something to fill their holds with. What that was, and what it did to Africa and eventually to the Americas, is the subject we turn to next, and it is the darkest in the book.


# Chapter 3 — Gold, God, and the First Slaves

This is the chapter where the Age of Exploration acquires its second face, the one it never loses again. Up to now the story has been mostly adventure — small ships, brave or terrified sailors, an ocean conquered by patience and a clever sail. But the moment the African voyages began to pay, in the 1440s, they began to pay partly in human beings, and from that point forward exploration and enslavement are braided together so tightly that you cannot honestly tell one without the other. The voyages built the modern world. They also built the Atlantic slave trade, the largest forced migration in human history. Both statements are true at once, and this chapter is where they fuse. It should be read slowly and without flinching.

Begin with what the Portuguese were actually after, because it was never primarily people — it was gold. For centuries the gold that gilded the cathedrals and coinage of Christian Europe and the Muslim Mediterranean had come from West Africa, from the goldfields south of the Sahara, carried north across the desert by camel caravan to ports like Ceuta (Chapter 1). The civilizations that controlled this trade were not the marginal, unknown peoples of European imagination; they were among the richest states on Earth. The Mali Empire, at its height in the 1300s, had been so awash in gold that when its emperor Mansa Musa crossed Egypt on pilgrimage to Mecca he reportedly handed out so much of it that he wrecked the value of gold in Cairo for years (the pilgrimage and the Cairo gold crash are told in full in the Mali Empire's own narrative). By the time the Portuguese arrived at the coast, the dominant power of the West African interior was the rising **Songhai Empire** (SAWNG-hy), the largest state in African history, then in the act of eating the fading Mali that had ruled before it (a transition each empire's own narrative tells from its side), ruling the great bend of the Niger River and the fabled trading city of Timbuktu, with its libraries and its scholars. The whole strategic idea behind the Portuguese creep down the coast was to reach the source of that gold by sea and cut the Saharan caravans — and the Muslim merchants who ran them — out of the deal entirely. This was the prize. It was also, in the Portuguese mind, a continuation of the crusade: to strike at the wealth of Islam and find Christian allies behind it.

The coast they reached was not empty and it was not primitive, and this needs saying plainly because the European sources are built to make it sound otherwise. When Portuguese ships pushed past the desert into the green, well-watered coasts of Senegambia (the region around the Senegal and Gambia rivers) in the 1440s and 1450s, they met organized states with kings, courts, armies, taxes, and long-established trade. The dominant power there was the **Jolof Empire** (JOH-lof), a confederation of Wolof-speaking kingdoms whose rulers traded gold, ivory, hides, and — already, before any European arrived — enslaved captives, war prisoners and debtors sold within long-standing African and trans-Saharan slave markets. One of the most vivid windows we have onto this world comes from a young Venetian named **Alvise Cadamosto** (ahl-VEE-zeh kah-dah-MOH-stoh), who sailed for Portugal in the 1450s and wrote detailed, curious, mostly admiring accounts of the Wolof courts, the markets, the gold-for-salt trade of the interior (salt being so scarce in the Sahel that it traded weight for weight against gold), and the customs of the people he met. Cadamosto's writings matter because they are a reminder, in a European hand, that the Portuguese were sailing not into a void but into a dense and sophisticated commercial world that had its own rules, its own rulers, and its own long history — a world they would join, exploit, and ultimately deform, but did not create.

Now the hard part. In 1444 a fleet returned to the southern Portuguese port of **Lagos** (LAH-goosh — the town in the Algarve, not the city in Nigeria) carrying a cargo of some 235 enslaved Africans, captured and bought on the coast, and they were auctioned off on the dock. A royal chronicler named Gomes Eanes de Zurara, writing for Prince Henry, left an account of that auction that is genuinely harrowing — he describes families torn apart on the sand, parents separated from children, people wailing and looking up to heaven — and then, in the same breath, he reassures his royal reader that it was all to the good because the captives' souls would now be saved by baptism. That two-facedness, the witnessed human agony and the smooth theological justification side by side on the same page, is the exact moral signature of the entire enterprise, and Zurara captured it without quite meaning to. The 1444 auction is usually marked as the start of the European Atlantic slave trade — the opening date of the Atlantic slave trade's own full narrative, which carries this story to its industrial scale. It is important to be precise about what was new and what was not. Slavery itself was ancient and nearly universal — it existed in Africa, in the Islamic world, in Europe, in the Americas, everywhere — and the trans-Saharan trade in enslaved Africans was already old. What the Portuguese began was something with a new shape: a *maritime, Atlantic* trade in enslaved Africans, run by Europeans, by sea, directly from the African coast, that over the next four centuries would grow into an industrialized horror carrying more than twelve million people across the ocean. In 1444 it was a few hundred captives on a Portuguese dock. The machine it would become is the subject of the final chapter of this book.

Why did it grow? Because it was profitable, and because the profit had somewhere to go. Remember the sugar of Madeira (Chapter 2). Sugar cane is a brutal crop to grow and process — it demands enormous amounts of backbreaking labor in tropical heat — and as the Portuguese expanded sugar production on Madeira and then on other Atlantic islands, the labor was supplied increasingly by enslaved Africans bought on the coast their ships now reached. Here, on these little islands, the pieces of a system that would later devour the Americas first locked together: a cash crop grown for distant markets, plantations consolidating the land, and an enslaved African workforce shipped in to work them. Nobody designed this as a master plan. It assembled itself, piece by profitable piece, each step a reasonable business decision to the men making it and a catastrophe to the people it consumed. By the time anyone might have stepped back to see the whole shape of it, the shape was already load-bearing.

As the voyages pressed on past Senegambia in the following decades, the Portuguese began to do what they did best: not conquer territory, but seize and fortify the *points* where trade happened. The first and most important was built in 1482 on the stretch of coast the Portuguese called the Mina ("the mine"), in what is now Ghana, the so-called Gold Coast. There they raised **Elmina Castle** (el-MEE-nah), formally São Jorge da Mina — the first permanent European fortification in sub-Saharan Africa, a massive stone fortress-warehouse on the shore. It was built with royal blessing and over the objections of the local ruler, whose permission the Portuguese commander secured with a mix of gifts and the implicit threat of the cannon. Elmina began as a fortified depot for the gold trade, a strongbox where Portuguese goods (cloth, brass, beads, horses) were exchanged for African gold under the protection of stone walls and guns. But its dungeons would, in the centuries to come, hold a far more terrible cargo, and Elmina became one of the most notorious holding-stations of the Atlantic slave trade — the place where captives were warehoused in the dark before being loaded onto ships. Its stone walls still stand. It is worth knowing that the most famous monument of European exploration in West Africa is a prison.

Farther south, the creep down the coast also produced something quite different from a fort and a market: a genuine diplomatic encounter between two kingdoms, briefly conducted as something like equals. In 1483 the Portuguese captain **Diogo Cão** (dee-OH-goo KOWNG) sailed past the vast mouth of the **Congo River** and made contact with the **Kingdom of Kongo** (KONG-goh), a powerful, well-organized Central African state with a capital city, a king, provincial governors, and a sophisticated political structure. (Note the spelling: *Kongo* the kingdom and *Congo* the river and later the colonial territories are related names for related things, but in this book *Kongo* means the African kingdom.) What followed was not, at first, a conquest. The Kongolese king and the Portuguese crown opened a relationship of embassies and gifts; Kongo sent young noblemen to Portugal to be educated, and in 1491 the reigning king, Nzinga a Nkuwa, accepted Christian baptism, taking a Portuguese royal name. His son, who would rule as **Afonso I** (ah-FON-soo), went much further, making Catholicism the religion of his court and modeling his kingdom in part on European lines — a Central African Christian monarchy, allied with Portugal, joining Christendom not at sword-point but largely by choice. For a moment it looked as though the African encounter might run on lines of alliance and exchange rather than extraction. It did not last, and the reason it did not last was the slave trade, which would hollow out Kongo from the inside even as its kings wrote letters in elegant Portuguese begging Lisbon to make it stop. Afonso's anguished, literate, real-time documentation of the destruction of his own kingdom is one of the most extraordinary sources of the entire era, and we will give it the space it deserves in the final chapter. For now, hold the image: a Christian African king, contemporary of Columbus, writing to a fellow Christian king to plead for the survival of his people against the traders Portugal kept sending.

There is one more small, strange thing the Portuguese left behind on these coasts, and it captures the era's peculiar blend of religion, propaganda, and law better than almost anything: the **padrão** (pah-DROWNG), plural *padrões*. These were carved stone pillars, several feet high, topped with a cross and inscribed with the Portuguese royal arms and the date, which captains planted at the farthest point each voyage reached. A padrão was, all at once, a claim-marker ("the king of Portugal was here first"), a religious symbol (the cross, planting Christendom on a pagan shore), and something very like a press release in granite, a permanent advertisement of how far Portugal had gotten. Diogo Cão planted one at the mouth of the Congo and another, in 1486, at Cape Cross on the bleak Namibian shore, the southernmost marker Portugal would reach before the great breakthrough. The padrões march down the African coast like mileposts of the creep, each one a little farther south than the last, granite proof of the patient, inch-by-inch advance.

So by the 1480s the African enterprise had found its mature and terrible form. Portugal was not trying to rule Africa; it was trying to tap it — to seize the points where wealth changed hands, fortify them, and pull gold, ivory, and human beings out through them by sea. The crusading dream of striking at Islam's wealth had blurred into a frankly commercial operation, and the commerce had acquired, alongside the gold, a traffic in people that would metastasize into one of history's defining crimes. But the original strategic goal — to reach around Africa to the riches of the East — was still unmet. The padrões had crept as far as Namibia, and ahead lay the question that had haunted the whole project from Ceuta onward: did Africa have a bottom you could sail around, and if so, what lay on the other side? Answering it would require not just nerve and a good ship but a new kind of knowledge — the ability to know where on Earth you were when there was no coast to tell you. That science, and the storm that finally carried a Portuguese ship around the bottom of the world, are the next chapter.


# Chapter 4 — Rounding the Cape

There is a question that sits underneath every voyage in this book, and the farther the ships sailed the more it pressed: *where am I?* On a coastal voyage the answer is easy — you are off that headland, past that river mouth, a day's sail from that cape, with the land itself as your map. But out in the open ocean, or creeping down a coast nobody has charted, with the volta do mar carrying you on long arcs out of sight of any land at all, the question becomes genuinely terrifying. A captain who does not know where he is cannot know how far he has come, whether the strange new coast ahead is the one his rivals reached last year, or how to find his way back to it next season. Before Portugal could attempt the great prize — sailing around the bottom of Africa to the riches of the East — it had to solve, at least roughly, the problem of finding yourself on a featureless sea. This chapter is about that science, and about the storm that, the moment the science was good enough, blew a Portuguese captain around the corner of the world.

The breakthrough was learning to read position from the sky, and specifically learning to measure **latitude** — how far north or south you are. Latitude is the easy half of the where-am-I problem, because nature hands you a free reference: the height of certain heavenly bodies above the horizon changes in a fixed, predictable way as you move north or south. In the Northern Hemisphere, the simplest trick is the Pole Star (the North Star), which sits almost directly over the North Pole and barely moves; measure its angle above the horizon and you have your latitude, near enough. The trouble is that as the Portuguese sailed south toward the equator, the Pole Star sank lower and lower until it dropped below the horizon entirely and was gone, useless. So they switched to the sun, measuring its height at noon — but the sun's noon height changes through the year as well as with your position, so to turn a sun sighting into a latitude you needed astronomical tables telling you exactly where the sun should be on any given day of any given year. Those tables existed because of one man above all, the German astronomer **Regiomontanus** (ray-jee-oh-mon-TAH-nus, the Latin pen-name of Johannes Müller, who lived 1436–1476), whose printed tables of the positions of the sun and stars — the *Ephemerides* — let a sailor convert a measured angle into a position on the Earth. It is one of the quiet, profound facts of this story that the age of global navigation rode out on the back of pure mathematical astronomy. The sailor squinting at the sun off the coast of Africa was, without quite knowing it, holding the far end of a chain that ran back to scholars in their studies working out the motions of the heavens.

The squinting was done with instruments, and they were crude, ingenious, and sometimes blinding. The grandest was the **mariner's astrolabe** (AS-truh-layb), a heavy brass ring, often a foot or more across, with a pivoting sighting arm and degree markings around the rim; you hung it from your thumb so it dangled straight down by gravity, swung the arm until it pointed at the sun or a star, and read the angle off the scale. Heavy brass was the point — a light instrument swung wildly on a pitching deck, so the astrolabe's weight steadied it. Cheaper and simpler was the **cross-staff** (sometimes called Jacob's staff), a wooden rod with a sliding crosspiece: you held the end of the rod to your eye and slid the crosspiece until its bottom edge lined up with the horizon and its top edge with the sun, then read your angle off the rod. The cross-staff was so simple a poor ship could afford one, but it had a vicious flaw — using it meant staring more or less straight at the sun, and over a career of sightings it slowly blinded the navigators who relied on it. Both instruments were maddeningly imprecise by modern standards, thrown off by every roll of the deck and every error of the eye, good to perhaps a degree of latitude on a good day, which is dozens of miles. But "dozens of miles" out in an ocean of thousands was the difference between knowing roughly where you were and not knowing at all, and that was enough to change everything.

It is worth pausing on the other, harder half of the where-am-I problem, the one nobody could solve yet, because its absence shaped every voyage in this book. That is **longitude** — how far east or west you are. Latitude you can read from the sky directly; longitude you cannot, because the Earth turns, and to know your east-west position you essentially need to know precisely what time it is back at a reference point compared to local time where you are — and there was no clock in the 1400s that could keep accurate time on a heaving, damp, temperature-swinging ship for weeks on end. (The problem would not be properly cracked until the invention of the marine chronometer in the 1700s, three centuries on.) So every navigator of the Age of Exploration sailed knowing his latitude tolerably and his longitude almost not at all, guessing his east-west position by *dead reckoning* — estimating speed and heading and elapsed time and plotting where that ought to have put him, an educated guess piled on an educated guess. This is why so many of the voyages in this book overshoot, undershoot, get lost, and stumble onto things by accident. They were sailing with half a map of where they were. The wonder is not that Columbus thought he'd hit Asia; the wonder is that anyone got anywhere at all.

With the science of latitude maturing through the 1470s and 1480s, the prize of rounding Africa came within reach, and a new king took up the chase with the cold focus of a spymaster. He was **John II** of Portugal (reigned 1481–1495), nicknamed by later admirers *the Perfect Prince*, and he ran exploration like a state intelligence operation. He poured money into the southern push, and just as importantly he ran the knowledge of it as a state secret — Portuguese charts of the African route were guarded like military codes, because a chart that showed the way to the gold and the spice was worth more than gold. John also understood that you should scout your target before you assault it, and so he launched, alongside the sea voyages, an extraordinary overland espionage mission. In 1487 he sent a trusted agent named **Pêro da Covilhã** (PEH-roo dah koo-vee-LYANG), fluent in Arabic and disguised as a Muslim merchant, eastward across the Mediterranean and the Islamic world to do reconnaissance on the actual destination: the spice ports of the Indian Ocean. Covilhã made it. He reached India, saw the pepper markets of the Malabar coast and the trade of the Arabian Sea and the Swahili ports of East Africa, and sent back a report — confirming that if a ship could only get around Africa into the Indian Ocean, a thriving, well-mapped trade in the very spices Europe craved was waiting at the other end. Portugal was, in effect, mapping the destination by land while it fought its way toward the door by sea.

Tangled up in all this hard-nosed espionage was a dream that sounds, to modern ears, almost too medieval to be real, but it drove serious policy for decades: the legend of **Prester John** (PRESS-ter, a corruption of "Priest"). For centuries Europe had clung to a story that somewhere far beyond the Muslim world lay a mighty *Christian* kingdom ruled by a priest-king named John — at various times imagined in India, in Central Asia, and finally in Ethiopia — fabulously rich and just waiting to be found. The strategic fantasy was irresistible: if Christian Europe could only make contact with this lost Christian ally on the *far* side of the Muslim world, the two could catch Islam in a pincer between them and crush it. This was not a fringe notion; the hunt for Prester John was an explicit goal of the Portuguese push, named in royal instructions, and one of the things Covilhã was sent to find. (There really was a Christian kingdom roughly where the legend's later versions placed it — Ethiopia, an ancient Christian realm in the Horn of Africa — and Covilhã eventually made his way there and lived out his life at its court, so the legend was not entirely empty, just wildly embroidered.) It is a useful corrective to remember that the men who built the modern world's trade routes were not cool-headed economists; they were a volatile mix of merchants chasing pepper, mathematicians plotting stars, and crusaders chasing a phantom Christian king across the map. All three motives sailed on the same ships.

And then, in 1488, the door swung open — by accident, in a storm, against the captain's will. John II sent a small squadron south under **Bartolomeu Dias** (bar-too-loo-MEH-oo DEE-ahsh) to find the bottom of Africa. Dias pushed past Cape Cross and the last of the padrões (Chapter 3), down a coast growing colder and stormier, when a fierce gale caught the ships and drove them south, out of sight of land, for days. When the storm let up and Dias turned east to find the coast again, there was no coast — the land had fallen away behind him. He sailed north and east searching for it and finally raised the shore again, and when he did, the coastline was running *east*. Africa had a bottom, and the storm had blown him clean around it without his ever seeing the turn. He had reached the Indian Ocean. His exhausted, frightened crew, far past any point a European ship had ever been, refused to go on, and Dias was forced to turn back just short of the actual goal of India. On the return voyage he finally saw the great rocky promontory the storm had carried him past, and he named it the **Cape of Storms** for what it had done to him. King John, hearing the news back in Lisbon and grasping at once what it meant — that the sea road to the spice of the East was now, in principle, open — renamed it. He called it the **Cape of Good Hope** (the southwestern tip of Africa, near modern Cape Town), and the new name is the whole point: the corner that had been a wall for the entire length of this book had just become a doorway. Everything the Portuguese had built since Ceuta — the caravel, the volta do mar, the conquest of fear at Bojador, the chain of forts and padrões, the astrolabe and the star tables and the spy in his merchant's disguise — had been pointing at this. The bottom of Africa was rounded. The East lay open. And it would be nearly a decade before Portugal sailed through the door, because in the meantime, a Genoese dreamer with a badly wrong map was about to sail the *other* way and change the story entirely.


# Chapter 5 — Columbus and the Spanish Gamble

In the same years that Portugal was patiently, scientifically working its way around the bottom of Africa, a stubborn, self-taught, and badly mistaken mariner from the Italian port of Genoa was peddling a completely different idea to anyone who would listen: forget Africa, forget rounding the long way; the Earth is a sphere, so if you sail *west* across the open Atlantic, you will come around the back of the globe straight to Asia. The man was **Christopher Columbus** — Cristoforo Colombo in his native Genoese, Cristóbal Colón in the Spanish he would eventually sail under — and the most important thing to understand about him is that his idea was wrong, his math was wrong, and he was right anyway, in the worst possible way, by crashing into a hemisphere that nobody in Europe knew existed. This chapter is about how that happened, and about the people who were already living on the far side of his miscalculation.

First, the gap his plan filled, because it explains why anyone listened to a crank with bad arithmetic. Europe's hunger for the spices of the East — pepper, cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg, which preserved and flavored food and were worth fortunes — had a supply problem, and the supply problem had just gotten worse. The spices came overland and by Indian Ocean ship from Asia to the eastern Mediterranean, where Italian merchants (the Genoese, the Venetians) bought them and resold them across Europe at enormous markups. The whole chain ran through the Muslim-controlled lands of the Near East. And in 1453 the **Ottoman Empire** (OT-uh-mun), the rising Turkish-Muslim power, captured **Constantinople** — the thousand-year-old capital of the Christian Byzantine Empire (the surviving eastern half of the old Roman Empire, Greek-speaking and Orthodox Christian, which had outlasted Rome itself by a thousand years), the great city on the strait between Europe and Asia (modern Istanbul) — and in doing so tightened a Muslim grip across the old trade roads to the East. The fall of Constantinople did not literally seal the spice routes overnight, and historians debate how much it really choked trade versus how much it simply frightened Europe, but it powerfully sharpened the sense that the land road to Asian wealth ran through hostile hands and that a *sea* road bypassing the Muslim middlemen would be worth almost any gamble. Portugal's answer was to go around Africa. Columbus's answer was to go the other way entirely.

His pitch rested on a mistake, and it's worth being precise about it because the popular version gets it backwards. Educated Europeans knew the Earth was round; the question was how *big* it was, and on that the experts were right and Columbus was wrong. The learned consensus, descended from ancient Greek geographers, held that the Earth was large and that the open ocean between western Europe and eastern Asia was vast — far too vast for any ship of the day to cross before its crew starved. Columbus, cherry-picking the most optimistic ancient sources and making errors of his own, convinced himself the globe was much smaller and Asia much wider, so that the ocean gap was modest, a few weeks' sail. He was badly, dangerously wrong; the experts who told him so were correct. Had there been nothing but open ocean between Europe and Asia, Columbus and his crews would simply have sailed off into the Atlantic and died of thirst somewhere around where the Caribbean actually is. What saved him was a continent he didn't know was there, sitting in the ocean at almost exactly the distance his wrong math predicted Asia would be. He was rescued from one error by his total ignorance of a far larger truth. This is, when you think about it, the single luckiest accident in the history of the world.

Getting anyone to bankroll the gamble took him years of rejection. Portugal's King John II, who actually understood navigation and had his own experts run the numbers, turned Columbus down flat — the Portuguese knew the ocean was too wide and were anyway committed to the African route they'd just cracked with Dias (Chapter 4). Columbus took his pitch to Spain, where it landed in front of the most powerful partnership in Europe. In 1469 the marriage of **Isabella I of Castile** (iz-uh-BELL-uh) and **Ferdinand II of Aragon** had fused the two great crowns of Spain — Castile in the center and west, Aragon in the east — into a single dominant power, the union usually called the Catholic Monarchs. For years Isabella's advisers told her, correctly, that Columbus's geography was nonsense, and for years she put him off. What changed was 1492, a hinge year if ever there was one. In January 1492 the Catholic Monarchs completed the **Reconquista** (the centuries-long Christian campaign to retake Iberia from Muslim rule, Chapter 1) by capturing **Granada**, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain. With that war finally won, the crown suddenly had soldiers, crusading momentum, and royal attention freed up for a new project, and the religious triumphalism of the moment made a wild westward gamble to outflank Islam and reach Asia feel like the next chapter of the same holy story. (The same year, in the same crusading spirit, the monarchs expelled Spain's Jews — 1492 was a year of conquest and expulsion as much as discovery.) Isabella, against her experts' advice, said yes. The deal she struck made Columbus admiral and governor of any lands he found and gave him a cut of the profits — terms so generous they tell you the crown thought the odds of his coming back at all were long.

He sailed in August 1492 with three small ships — the *Niña*, the *Pinta*, and the larger *Santa María* — and a crew of about ninety, out past the Canary Islands and into the open Atlantic, riding the easterly trade winds west (a westward application of exactly the wind-knowledge the Portuguese had been accumulating for decades). The crossing took five weeks; the crews grew mutinous as the empty ocean stretched on past every promise Columbus had made about land. And then, before dawn on October 12, 1492, a lookout sighted a low island, and Columbus made landfall on an island in the **Bahamas** that the local people called Guanahani and he renamed San Salvador. He believed, and would believe to his dying day, that he had reached the outer edge of Asia, the islands off the coast of China or Japan or "the Indies." He was about four thousand miles short. It is from that single, permanent error that we get one of history's most consequential misnomers: he called the people he met *Indians*, because he thought he was in the Indies, and the name stuck to the indigenous peoples of two entire continents who had nothing to do with India at all.

The people he actually met were the **Taíno** (TY-noh), and they deserve to be seen clearly, not as the passive, gentle backdrop the European sources reduce them to. The Taíno were the dominant people of the Greater Antilles — the large Caribbean islands of Hispaniola, Cuba, Puerto Rico, Jamaica — an Arawak-speaking people who had settled the islands over centuries of seaborne migration up the chain from South America. They were not scattered bands; they ran organized chiefdoms, each headed by a hereditary chief called a *cacique* (kah-SEE-keh, a Taíno word that passed through Spanish into English as "cacique," still meaning a local boss). They lived in substantial villages of timber-and-thatch houses around central plazas, farmed cassava (a starchy root that was their staple) and maize and sweet potatoes in raised mounded fields, made fine pottery and elaborate carved religious objects, played a ritual ball game in stone-lined courts, and navigated the islands in large dugout canoes — the Taíno word *canoa* is another of theirs that we still use, along with *hammock*, *barbecue*, *hurricane*, and *tobacco*. They had their own religion, their own art, their own complex politics of rival caciques. When Columbus described them as gentle and generous and "would make good servants," he was, chillingly, sizing up a sophisticated society for exploitation while pretending to admire it. The Taíno who paddled out to meet his ships were not extras in a European story. They were a civilization, and within a single generation of that October morning, between the diseases the Europeans carried and the forced labor they imposed, that civilization would be all but annihilated — one of the first and most total of the catastrophes this book has to record.

The first reckoning came almost at once, and it cut both ways. On Christmas night in 1492 the *Santa María* ran aground on a reef off the north coast of Hispaniola and was wrecked. Unable to carry everyone home in the two remaining small ships, Columbus had a fort built from the flagship's salvaged timbers and left thirty-nine men behind in it, naming the place **La Navidad** (lah nah-vee-DAHD, "the Nativity," for the Christmas wreck) — the first European settlement in the Americas, born from a shipwreck. He sailed home to a hero's welcome, having (he was sure) reached Asia. When he returned the next year with a far larger fleet to find his colony, La Navidad was ashes and all thirty-nine men were dead — killed by the local Taíno, whose hospitality the abandoned Spaniards had repaid by seizing women and gold until a neighboring cacique wiped them out. The very first European colony in the New World was destroyed by the very people who had first welcomed the strangers, and the destruction was a response to the strangers' abuses. It is a grimly fitting opening to the whole story of the Americas: the welcome, the abuse, the violent reckoning, and then, behind it all, the diseases already beginning their silent work. Columbus would make four voyages in all, found other colonies, govern Hispaniola so brutally that he was eventually arrested and shipped back to Spain in chains, and die in 1506 still insisting he had reached Asia, never knowing he had found a new world.

The man never knew what he had done, and that ignorance is almost the most important fact about him. He opened sustained contact between two halves of the planet that had been biologically and culturally separate for ten thousand years, and he opened it convinced he was somewhere else, looking for a Chinese emperor who wasn't there. The consequences would dwarf anything he imagined: not a trade route to Asia, but the collision of continents, the deadliest die-off in recorded human history, the silver and the slavery and the whole remade world this book is about. But before Europe could even begin to grasp that the lands across the Atlantic were *new*, the two crowns of Iberia had to decide who owned them — and they did it, with breathtaking arrogance, by drawing a single line down a globe that no one had finished mapping. That line is the next chapter.


# Chapter 6 — A Line Drawn Around the World

Here is one of the great pieces of effrontery in human history. Two small kingdoms on the edge of Europe, having between them visited a few Caribbean islands and a stretch of African coast, sat down in the 1490s and divided the *entire* non-Christian world — all of it, the parts they had seen and the overwhelming majority they had not — between just the two of them, along a line drawn straight down a globe that no one alive had finished mapping. They did it with a straight face and the blessing of the pope, and it worked well enough that you can still hear its echo today: it is why Brazil speaks Portuguese while the rest of Latin America speaks Spanish. This chapter is about that line, the arrogance behind it, and the slow, dawning, deeply uncomfortable realization — arriving years later and from an unexpected quarter — that the lands being so confidently carved up were not the edge of Asia at all, but a whole new world that changed the meaning of everything.

The carving began with the Church, because in the European mind of 1493 the pope had the authority to do exactly this. The pope was the supreme head of the Catholic Church, and under the prevailing theory of the day he could grant Christian monarchs the right to take possession of non-Christian lands, in exchange for converting the inhabitants — a doctrine that treated the souls of unbaptized people as a license to seize their countries. When Columbus came back in 1493 claiming to have reached Asia by the west, Portugal and Spain had a collision coming. Portugal had spent eighty years and a fortune creeping down Africa to reach the East the long way; now Spain was claiming to have reached the East the *short* way and wanted it recognized. They took the dispute to the pope, who happened to be **Alexander VI** (the notorious Borgia pope, Rodrigo Borgia, a Spaniard by birth and no friend of Portugal). In a series of decrees in 1493 known as the bulls of donation — the most important called *Inter caetera* (IN-ter KY-teh-rah, Latin for "among other things," from its opening words; a papal "bull" is a formal decree, named for the lead seal, the *bulla*, that authenticated it) — Alexander drew a north-south line in the Atlantic and granted Spain everything to the *west* of it and the right to the lands Columbus had found. It was a transparently pro-Spanish ruling from a Spanish pope, and Portugal was furious, because the line as the pope drew it threatened the African sea route Portugal had bled for.

So Portugal did something revealing: it ignored the pope and went straight to Spain to renegotiate, ruler to ruler, and in 1494 the two crowns cut their own deal in the Castilian town of **Tordesillas** (tor-day-SEE-yahs). The **Treaty of Tordesillas** moved the dividing line considerably west of where the pope had put it — to a meridian (a north-south line of longitude) 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, off Africa. Everything to the east of that line was Portugal's to explore and claim; everything to the west was Spain's. With a single negotiated line the two kingdoms split the unexplored world between them. The breathtaking part is not just the arrogance of two minor powers parceling out the planet while ignoring everyone who actually lived on it. It is that they drew the line in a place that, thanks to the unsolved longitude problem (Chapter 4), nobody could actually locate. There was no way in 1494 to measure longitude at sea, which meant there was no way to know, for any given spot on Earth, whether it fell on the Spanish or the Portuguese side of a line defined by east-west distance. They divided the world along a boundary they were physically incapable of finding. And yet the line had teeth, because both crowns agreed to honor it, and its single most famous consequence was about to fall, almost literally, out of the sky into Portugal's lap.

That consequence was **Brazil**, and the story of its "discovery" is the Tordesillas line's strangest gift. In 1500 Portugal sent a large fleet under **Pedro Álvares Cabral** (PEH-droo AHL-vah-rish kah-BRAHL) to follow up da Gama's route to India (Chapter 7). Recall the volta do mar (Chapter 2): to round the bottom of Africa you don't hug the African coast, you swing far out west into the Atlantic to catch the favorable winds. Cabral swung out — and either by the ordinary logic of that maneuver or by deliberate design, historians still argue which — sailed so far west that he bumped into a coastline nobody was expecting: the eastern bulge of South America, the land we call Brazil. He claimed it for Portugal, and here is the lovely accident: the Brazilian coast happened to fall on the *Portuguese* side of the Tordesillas line, drawn six years earlier through an ocean no one had crossed at that latitude. A line agreed in 1494 to settle a quarrel about Africa and the Caribbean turned out, by pure chance, to hand Portugal a vast chunk of South America. This is the literal reason a single enormous country in South America speaks Portuguese today while every nation around it speaks Spanish — a boundary drawn blind through an empty ocean, landing by luck on the one part of the continent that bulged east of it. History is rarely so tidy about showing its work.

But there was a problem with the whole grand carve-up that nobody in Tordesillas had grasped, and the man who first put his finger on it was not a king or a pope or a famous captain but a sharp-eyed Florentine named **Amerigo Vespucci** (ah-meh-REE-goh ve-SPOO-chee). Vespucci sailed on Portuguese and Spanish voyages along the coast of South America in the years around 1500, and he did something Columbus never managed: he looked at the evidence and changed his mind. Columbus had died insisting the new lands were the edge of Asia. Vespucci, examining the immense, continuous, *southern*-running coastline — a landmass that extended far below the equator, with peoples, plants, and skies that matched nothing in the known descriptions of Asia — argued in a series of letters that this could not possibly be the fringe of Asia at all. It was, he wrote, a *mundus novus*, a New World, a continent previously unknown to Europe, Africa, or Asia. This was a genuinely revolutionary mental act. For thousands of years educated Europeans had believed the world's land consisted of three connected parts — Europe, Asia, and Africa, the *orbis terrarum*, the "circle of lands." Vespucci was saying there was a fourth part, an entire additional continent, sitting in an ocean everyone had assumed was empty between Europe and Asia. The map of the world was not just incomplete; it was wrong in its basic architecture.

Vespucci's letters, helped along by the new technology of the printing press, became European bestsellers — vivid, readable, and crucially carrying the explosive claim that this was *new* land. And so we arrive at one of history's great injustices of naming. In 1507 a German cartographer named **Martin Waldseemüller** (VALT-zay-myoo-ler), working with a small scholarly circle, printed a magnificent new map of the world that, for the first time, showed the new lands across the Atlantic as a separate continent, distinct from Asia — and on that continent, down in the southern part Vespucci had explored, he printed a name. Reasoning that the other continents bore (as Europeans then thought) women's names and that this one should be named for the man who recognized it as new, he Latinized Amerigo's first name into a feminine form: **America**. The name caught on, spread to the northern continent as well, and stuck. So the two vast continents that Columbus reached, governed, and died believing were Asia ended up named not for him but for the Florentine who merely realized what they were — and named for his *first* name at that, while Columbus's name clings only to a river, a few towns, and a country in the north. It is the kind of historical accident that feels almost like a moral: the credit went not to the man who got there but to the man who understood what "there" was.

Pull back and look at what this chapter has assembled, because it is the conceptual turning point of the whole book. In the space of about fifteen years, Europeans had gone from believing the western ocean led to Asia, to drawing a line dividing a still-imaginary world between two crowns, to bumping into a continent that line happened to bisect, to finally grasping that the western lands were not Asia at all but an entirely new quarter of the Earth. The mental map of the world had been torn up and was being redrawn in real time, by people who were still profoundly unsure what they were drawing. And the consequences of that redrawn map were enormous and immediate: it meant that Spain, expecting Asia, had instead been handed two whole continents to conquer (Chapters 9 and 10), while Portugal, with the African route now proven, could finally do what it had been straining toward since Ceuta — sail around the bottom of Africa and force its way into the riches of the *actual* East. We left Bartolomeu Dias turning back at the Cape (Chapter 4). Now, with the Tordesillas world carved and the New World half-glimpsed, it is time to follow the Portuguese through the door Dias opened, into the ancient and sophisticated trading world of the Indian Ocean — a world that, unlike the Taíno or the peoples of the Americas, was more than ready to push back.


# Chapter 7 — Da Gama Forces the Indian Ocean

When the Portuguese finally sailed through the door Bartolomeu Dias had opened at the Cape (Chapter 4) and out into the Indian Ocean, they made a discovery that the triumphant version of the story usually skips: they were not discovering anything. The Indian Ocean was not an empty frontier waiting for Europeans. It was, and had been for a thousand years, the busiest, richest, most cosmopolitan maritime trading world on the planet — a vast, humming network of ports, merchants, pilots, and goods running from the Swahili city-states of East Africa to the spice islands of Indonesia, lubricated by monsoon winds that everyone but the Europeans already knew how to ride. The Portuguese did not open this world. They forced their way into it, uninvited and unimpressive, and the only thing they brought that the locals didn't already have in abundance was a willingness to use heavy cannon to take by violence what they could not get by trade. This chapter is the story of that intrusion, and it is important to keep the proportions straight: the newcomers were the crude, poor, marginal players muscling into a sophisticated economy that vastly outclassed them in almost everything except naval gunnery.

The man who led the intrusion was **Vasco da Gama** (VAHSH-koo dah GAH-mah), a hard, ruthless Portuguese nobleman whom King Manuel I sent in 1497 to do what Dias had not — go all the way to India. (Manuel I, reigned 1495–1521, was the king under whom Portugal's ocean empire reached its golden age; he liked to style himself "Lord of the conquest, navigation, and commerce of Ethiopia, Arabia, Persia, and India," which gives you a sense of the ambition.) Da Gama rounded the Cape, then sailed up the east coast of Africa — and here is where the encounter starts puncturing the myth. The Portuguese expected, on this far shore of a continent they thought of as theirs to reach, perhaps some primitive coast. Instead they found the **Swahili coast**: a string of wealthy, stone-built, Muslim trading cities — Mombasa, Malindi, Kilwa, Mozambique — whose merchants traded gold, ivory, and slaves from the African interior for Indian cloth, Chinese porcelain, and Arabian goods, who spoke Swahili (a Bantu African language richly seeded with Arabic), and who were plugged into the Indian Ocean trade network as full participants. (The arrival of da Gama and the Portuguese guns that followed is told from the coast's own side — the sack of Kilwa and Mombasa, the century of ruin — in the Swahili coast's narrative.) The Portuguese, in their small ships with their cheap trade goods, were often received with contempt or suspicion as the ragged, hostile interlopers they were. At one of these cities, Malindi, da Gama found what he most needed: a skilled pilot who knew the open-ocean route across the Arabian Sea to India and the monsoon winds that drove it. (Malindi was playing an old game — it had courted Zheng He's Chinese fleet the same way ninety years earlier, backing the powerful newcomer against its rival neighbor Mombasa; it simply did the same with the Portuguese.)

That pilot is worth pausing on, because he stands for the whole truth the triumphant story buries. The open Indian Ocean ran on the **monsoon** — the great seasonal wind system that blows steadily from the southwest for half the year and from the northeast for the other half, so reliable that for over a thousand years Arab, Persian, Indian, Swahili, and Southeast Asian sailors had timed their voyages to it, riding it out to India or Africa or Arabia on one monsoon and home on the next. This was navigational knowledge far older and in some respects more refined than anything the Portuguese had, and it was written down: there was a whole literature of Arabic navigation manuals and star tables. Tradition long named the pilot who guided da Gama across to India as **Ahmad ibn Majid** (IB-un mah-JEED), the most celebrated Arab navigator of the age, author of detailed sailing treatises — though that identification is now generally rejected by scholars (Ibn Majid was around seventy-seven by 1498 and described himself as too old to navigate, and the modern view favors an unnamed Gujarati Indian pilot instead). Whoever it was, the point is exact and important: da Gama's famous "discovery" of the sea route to India was completed by an Indian Ocean pilot using Indian Ocean knowledge to carry the Europeans across a sea the Europeans could not yet navigate on their own. The "discovery" rode on the back of a thousand years of Asian and Arab seafaring. Europe did not find the road to the East; the East's own sailors showed it the last and hardest leg.

In May 1498, guided across the Arabian Sea, da Gama reached **Calicut** (KAL-ih-kut, a major port on the Malabar coast of southwestern India, in modern Kerala — not to be confused with Calcutta on the opposite side of the country), the great pepper emporium of the Malabar coast. And the meeting that followed is almost comic in how badly it punctures European self-importance. Calicut was ruled by a Hindu monarch whose title was the **Zamorin** (ZAM-uh-rin, an anglicization of a local title meaning roughly "lord of the sea"), and the Zamorin presided over a cosmopolitan, fabulously wealthy port where Arab, Persian, Gujarati, and other merchants had traded for centuries and where pepper, ginger, cinnamon, and gems flowed through markets that made anything in Portugal look provincial. Da Gama, ushered into the presence of this rich and sophisticated ruler, presented the gifts he had carried halfway around the world: some striped cloth, a few hats, strings of coral beads, sugar, oil, honey. The Zamorin's officials reportedly laughed. These were the sort of trinkets one might give a minor African chief, not presents fit for a great king, and they made it embarrassingly clear that the Portuguese had nothing Calicut wanted and nothing remotely matching the goods the established Muslim merchants already brought. The Muslim traders, who correctly saw the Portuguese as dangerous competitors, turned the Zamorin against them. Da Gama left with a load of pepper but without a trade agreement, having learned the central, galling fact of the Indian Ocean: Europe was poor, its goods were shoddy, and it had nothing the East needed. The voyage was a commercial triumph anyway — that one cargo of pepper, bought cheap in India and sold in Lisbon, returned many times the cost of the entire expedition, proving the route's staggering profitability — but it had also revealed the problem Portugal would solve in the ugliest possible way.

The problem was simple: if you have nothing to *sell* in the richest market on Earth, how do you make money there? Portugal's answer was force. If Europe could not compete as a trader, it would dominate as a *predator* — using its one real advantage, ships built to carry heavy cannon and fight at sea, to seize control of the trade itself: to tax it, monopolize it, and shoot anyone who resisted. The shift from trading voyages to armed empire was made explicit in 1505, when Portugal appointed its first **Viceroy of India**, **Francisco de Almeida** (frahn-SEESH-koo deh ahl-MAY-dah), to govern a permanent armed presence in the Indian Ocean (the *Estado da Índia*, the "State of India"). Almeida understood the strategy as a naval one — control the sea, and the trade is yours. He proved it in 1509 at the **Battle of Diu** (DEE-oo), where a Portuguese fleet smashed a combined navy of the Egyptian Mamluk sultanate (the Muslim military regime then ruling Egypt and Syria, run by a self-perpetuating caste of slave-soldiers, whose grip on the old spice route Portugal's new sea road directly threatened), the Indian sultanate of Gujarat, and even Venice (the Venetians secretly backing Portugal's enemies because the new sea route was destroying their lucrative old Mediterranean spice business). The victory at Diu broke the Muslim and Venetian attempt to keep Portugal out and established Portuguese naval supremacy across the Indian Ocean's sea lanes. The technological gap in naval gunnery — Portuguese ships could pound an enemy from a distance their opponents couldn't match — turned a poor, marginal European kingdom into the master of an ocean it could never have competed in commercially.

The architect of the empire that supremacy built was Almeida's successor, **Afonso de Albuquerque** (ah-FON-soo deh ahl-boo-KER-keh), one of the most consequential and ruthless strategists of the age, and his insight was elegant and brutal. Portugal could never conquer or garrison the enormous lands around the Indian Ocean — it was a kingdom of a million people trying to dominate a region of tens of millions. So it wouldn't try. Instead it would seize the *choke-points*: the handful of narrow straits and key ports through which the whole ocean's trade had to pass, fortify them, and from those strongholds control and tax everything that moved. Hold the doors, and you hold the house without having to occupy a single room of it. Albuquerque executed the strategy in a swift, violent sequence. In 1510 he took **Goa** (GOH-uh) on India's west coast and made it the fortified capital of Portuguese Asia, a permanent European stronghold on the Indian mainland. In 1511 he stormed **Malacca** (muh-LAK-uh), the great entrepôt (a trading hub where goods are warehoused and re-exported) commanding the strait between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea — the single gateway through which all the spice trade of the islands beyond had to flow. And in 1515 he seized **Hormuz** (hor-MOOZ), the island fortress at the mouth of the Persian Gulf. With Goa, Malacca, and Hormuz, plus footholds on the Swahili coast and at Cochin (Portugal's first Indian fort, secured by allying with a local raja who was the Zamorin's rival), Portugal held a chain of fortified locks on the doors of the Indian Ocean. Past Malacca, in 1512, Portuguese ships finally reached the **Maluku Islands** (mah-LOO-koo), the legendary Spice Islands of eastern Indonesia — the actual, original source of the cloves, nutmeg, and mace that were worth more by weight than gold in Europe and that the entire enterprise, all the way back to Bojador, had ultimately been chasing.

What Portugal built in the East was therefore a wholly different kind of empire from the one Spain was simultaneously building in the Americas, and the contrast is worth marking because it shapes everything after. Spain, having stumbled onto two thinly defended continents whose great empires would collapse from disease and conquest (Chapters 9 and 10), built a *territorial* empire — it took the land, the people, and the silver mines, and ruled millions of subjects directly. Portugal in Asia did almost the opposite: it took no land to speak of, ruled few subjects, and built instead a thin, seaborne empire of forts and fleets, a network of fortified choke-points strung across an ocean whose societies it had no hope of conquering. It was an empire of *trade enforced at cannon-point* rather than an empire of settlement — a protection racket on a planetary scale, skimming the wealth of a vast existing economy by controlling the sea lanes through which that economy had to breathe. It made tiny Portugal, for a brilliant century, the hinge of a global commerce running from Lisbon to the Swahili coast to Goa to Malacca to the Spice Islands and on, soon, to China and Japan (Chapter 12). And it had reached, at last, the riches that had been the whole point since 1415. The other prize — the *western* route, the question of whether the New World could be sailed past or around to reach these same spice islands from the other side — was about to be answered by the most grueling voyage of the entire age. That is the next chapter, and it goes all the way around.


# Chapter 8 — The Pacific and the First Circle

By the 1510s the world map in European heads was a mess of half-truths. There was Asia, reached the long way around Africa. There were the new lands across the Atlantic, now grasped as a separate continent (Chapter 6). But the lands and the seas didn't connect into any coherent picture, and one enormous question hung over everything: what lay *between* the New World and Asia? Was the new continent a thin barrier with the spice islands just behind it, or did something vast lie in the way? The answer — that an ocean far larger than the Atlantic separated the Americas from Asia, an ocean so wide it nearly defies belief — was discovered in two stages, the first a glimpse from a mountaintop and the second the most punishing voyage of the age. This chapter is about how humans first laid eyes on the Pacific and then, at staggering cost, sailed all the way around the planet for the first time.

The glimpse came in 1513, and it came to a man who was running from his creditors. **Vasco Núñez de Balboa** (NOON-yeth deh bal-BOH-ah) was a Spanish adventurer who had fetched up in the small, struggling colonies on the Isthmus of Panama — the narrow neck of land joining North and South America. Hearing from local indigenous people of a great sea on the other side of the mountains, and chasing both gold and a way to redeem his ruined fortunes, Balboa led an expedition hacking through the dense, fever-ridden jungle and over the mountainous spine of the isthmus. In September 1513 he climbed a ridge, and from the summit saw, spread out to the south, open water reaching to the horizon — an ocean no European had ever seen from the American side. He waded into the surf in full armor, sword in hand, and claimed the entire sea and all its shores for the king of Spain, an act of breathtaking presumption toward an ocean he could not begin to imagine the size of. He called it the *Mar del Sur*, the South Sea, because from Panama it lay to the south. The discovery was momentous: it proved that the New World was not the edge of Asia but a barrier with *another whole ocean* behind it, and it instantly raised the prize question — was there a way through or around the American barrier into that southern sea, and from there to the Spice Islands? Whoever found such a passage would open a western route to the spices, on the Spanish side of the Tordesillas line. (Balboa himself came to a bad end, beheaded a few years later by a jealous rival governor — a reminder that the conquistadors were quite as lethal to each other as to anyone else.)

The man who set out to answer the question was a Portuguese nobleman named **Ferdinand Magellan** (muh-JEL-un — Fernão de Magalhães in his native Portuguese), and the politics of his voyage are themselves a sign of the age. Magellan had served Portugal in the Indian Ocean and the East, but he fell out with his own king and took his proposal to Portugal's great rival instead. So it was for *Spain*, under the young king Charles I (who in 1519 was also elected Holy Roman Emperor — the elected overlord of a sprawling patchwork of German and central European states that claimed descent from ancient Rome — and so is known to history by that grander title, Charles V; the same man, two crowns), that Magellan sailed in 1519 from Seville with five ships and a crew of around 270 men, gathered from across Europe, to find a western passage to the Spice Islands and to settle, once and for all, whether the world could be sailed clean around. The expedition was a Portuguese captain leading a mostly Spanish crew to claim for Spain the spice islands that Portugal already reached from the east — exploration as a tangle of national rivalry, defection, and overlapping claims, all chasing the same cloves.

The voyage was, from almost the first month, a catalog of horrors, and it tested the limits of what human beings could endure at sea. Crossing the Atlantic and working down the long eastern coast of South America, Magellan searched for a strait — a navigable channel through the continent into Balboa's southern sea. Down and down the coast they went, past every promising bay that dead-ended, into colder and colder latitudes, until, wintering in the bleak south, the Spanish captains who resented serving under a Portuguese broke into open **mutiny**. Magellan crushed it with cold ruthlessness — executing one ringleader, marooning others on a desolate shore — and pressed on, even after one of the five ships wrecked and another deserted and turned back for Spain. Finally, near the very bottom of the continent, he found it: a freezing, fog-bound, maze-like channel winding for over 350 miles between the mainland and the islands of Tierra del Fuego ("Land of Fire," named for the native peoples' fires the sailors saw burning on the shore). Threading that treacherous passage took weeks of terror, and it is now called, fittingly, the **Strait of Magellan**. When the battered fleet finally broke out the western end into Balboa's South Sea, the water was so unexpectedly calm after the strait's chaos that Magellan gave the ocean the name it still bears: the **Pacific** (from a word meaning "peaceful"). It was the cruelest misnomer in the history of geography, because the "peaceful" sea was about to nearly kill them all.

What followed was the part nobody had imagined, because nobody — not Balboa, not Magellan, not a single geographer in Europe — had grasped how *vast* the Pacific is. The Pacific is the largest single feature on the surface of the Earth, covering roughly a third of the entire planet, wider than all the land on Earth laid end to end. Magellan, expecting the Spice Islands to lie a manageable distance beyond the strait, instead found his ships crawling across an emptiness that simply did not end. For three months and twenty days they sailed without sighting a single inhabited, provisioned island. The food ran out. The water turned to foul slime. The men ate the leather off the rigging, soaked and roasted; they ate sawdust; they ate the rats that infested the ship, and considered themselves lucky to catch one. And they died — of starvation and above all of **scurvy**, the disease (caused, though no one then knew it, by the lack of vitamin C in a diet with no fresh food) that rotted the gums, loosened the teeth, reopened old wounds, and killed sailor after sailor in agony. Scurvy was the great unsolved killer of the entire Age of Exploration, claiming more lives across the era than storms, shipwreck, and battle combined, and on the Pacific crossing it nearly finished the expedition outright. That this starving, scurvy-ravaged remnant survived the crossing at all is a small miracle. When they finally reached land and food in early 1521, in the islands that are now the Philippines, the survivors were skeletal.

And it was in the Philippines, two-thirds of the way around the world and within reach of the Spice Islands he had crossed the planet to find, that Magellan met his end — and the manner of it deserves to be told straight, with the agency on the right side. Magellan, having reached islands whose peoples and rulers were sophisticated political actors with their own rivalries, plunged into local politics: he allied with one chieftain who accepted Christian baptism and agreed to help impose Spanish overlordship on the neighboring chiefs. One of those neighbors, a chieftain named **Lapulapu** (lah-poo-LAH-poo) on the small island of **Mactan** (mak-TAHN), refused to submit. In April 1521 Magellan, overconfident, waded ashore at the head of a small armored party to force the issue, expecting his guns and steel to overawe the islanders. Instead Lapulapu's warriors met them in the shallows and on the beach in far greater numbers, fought hard, and cut Magellan down in the surf — the great circumnavigator killed two-thirds of the way around the world by a man defending his own island. Lapulapu is today a national hero in the Philippines, the first indigenous leader to defeat the Europeans, and rightly so: at Mactan the story's usual outcome was reversed, and it is worth remembering that the famous "first man to circle the globe" did not, in fact, complete the circle — he died on a beach in the Philippines because a local ruler would not be bullied.

The circle was closed by the survivors, and barely. With Magellan dead and the crew now too few to man all the remaining ships, they burned one and pressed on under new command, eventually reaching the Spice Islands, loading a precious cargo of cloves, and facing the final problem: how to get home. One ship attempted to return back across the Pacific and was captured by the Portuguese. The other, the *Victoria*, under the Basque captain **Juan Sebastián Elcano** (el-KAH-noh), made the audacious decision to keep going *west* — to run the gauntlet across the Indian Ocean and around the Cape of Good Hope through waters Portugal controlled and would seize them in if they were caught, rather than recross the deadly Pacific. It was a desperate gamble, and it worked. In September 1522 the *Victoria* limped into Seville — leaking, half-rotten, crewed by eighteen gaunt, skeletal survivors out of the roughly 270 men who had sailed three years before, plus a handful of others who straggled home later. Eighteen men out of five ships. They were the first human beings ever to circle the entire planet, and they had proven, the hard way, with their own bodies as the cost, that the Earth was a single connected globe that could be sailed all the way around. Among the eighteen was an Italian gentleman-adventurer named **Antonio Pigafetta** (pee-gah-FET-tah), who had kept a careful journal through the whole ordeal, and whose chronicle — the storms, the mutiny, the strait, the months of starvation, the new peoples and tongues, the death of Magellan, the proof of the round world — is the astonishing eyewitness record from which we know the voyage at all. Without Pigafetta the greatest sea journey in history would be a few dry lines in a ledger; because of him it is one of the great survival stories ever written.

Step back and weigh what the circle meant. The Earth was now, for the first time, known by experience to be a single sphere of connected oceans — not a theory of geographers but a fact carried home in the bones of eighteen starving men. The Pacific, the planet's largest feature, had been crossed. And though the voyage was a commercial near-disaster (the spices in the *Victoria*'s hold barely covered the cost of one ship out of five, let alone the lives), it changed the human imagination permanently: the world was a globe, finite, sailable, and whole. The two halves of the planet that Columbus had first joined were now ringed by a single track. What remained was to grasp the full and terrible meaning of that joining — and the meaning was being written, in the same years, in blood and disease on the mainland of the Americas, where a few hundred Spaniards were about to bring down an empire of millions. That is where we turn next.


# Chapter 9 — The Fall of the Aztecs

The conquest of the Aztec Empire is the single most famous, most misunderstood, and most morally loaded episode in this entire book, so it has to be told with care. The popular version is a fairy tale of European supremacy: a few hundred Spaniards with guns, horses, and steel walk into the heart of a vast empire and topple it through sheer audacity and superior technology, the natives gaping in superstitious awe. Almost every part of that story is wrong or badly incomplete. What actually happened in central Mexico between 1519 and 1521 was a *civil war* — a revolt of subjugated peoples against a hated imperial overlord — onto which a small band of Spanish adventurers grafted themselves as the spark and the figurehead, and which was decided, in the end, less by any human army than by a microbe. To understand it you first have to understand that the empire being conquered was a genuine, sophisticated, and in many ways magnificent civilization, not the scenery of a European adventure story.

The empire belonged to the **Mexica** (meh-SHEE-kah) — the people we usually call the Aztecs, though "Aztec" is a later coinage and they called themselves the Mexica. (Their empire's own story, from its founding on the lake to this last chapter from the Mexica side, is told in the Aztec Empire's narrative; what follows here is the conquest seen from the Spanish road in.) Their capital, **Tenochtitlan** (teh-noch-TEET-lan), was one of the largest cities on Earth in 1519 — a metropolis of perhaps 200,000 people built on an island in the middle of a lake (the site of modern Mexico City, which was later built on top of its drained ruins), connected to the mainland by long raised causeways, threaded with canals, fed by floating garden-farms called *chinampas*, supplied with fresh water by an aqueduct, and kept clean by an army of sweepers. It dwarfed any city in Spain — bigger than Seville, bigger than anything most of the conquistadors had ever seen — and the Spanish who first laid eyes on it, descending toward the gleaming lake-city from the mountains, wrote that they wondered if they were dreaming. At its center rose the Templo Mayor, a great twin-shrined pyramid where the Mexica performed the human sacrifices that loom so large (and so luridly) in the European account. The sacrifices were real — the Mexica believed the sun and the cosmos had to be fed with human blood to keep existing, and they sacrificed war captives in numbers that genuinely horrified the Spanish — but they were part of a coherent and ancient Mesoamerican religious world (Mesoamerica being the band of central and southern Mexico and northern Central America where a long line of civilizations — Olmec, Maya, Mexica, and others — shared gods, calendars, ballgames, and pyramid-temples over thousands of years), not random savagery, and the Spanish, who were at that very moment burning heretics at home and would shortly slaughter Mexica by the tens of thousands, were in a poor position to be appalled. The Mexica ran a tribute empire: a dominant city-state that had, over about a century, conquered much of central Mexico and forced the subjugated peoples to send goods, treasure, and sacrificial victims to Tenochtitlan. That last fact — the resentment of the conquered — is the crack the Spanish would pry open.

The Spaniard who pried it was **Hernán Cortés** (er-NAHN kor-TES), and he was a gambler of almost insane boldness. In 1519 Cortés, a minor Spanish official on Cuba, led an expedition of about 500 men to the Mexican mainland — and did so in open *defiance* of the Cuban governor who had tried to recall him, making him a freelance adventurer with no legal authority, betting that success would retroactively make him a hero and failure would simply kill him. To make his point to his own men, after landing he reportedly had his ships scuttled or run aground — "burning his boats," the phrase we still use for cutting off retreat — so there was no going back: it was conquest or death. (The detail is famous and probably somewhat embellished in the retelling, like much of the conquest's drama, but the strategic fact of stranding his own force is real.) Cortés had a few hundred men, sixteen horses (animals the Mexica had never seen), some cannon, steel swords and armor, and a handful of guns. Against an empire of millions this was nothing. What he did with it was not win battles by technology; it was *politics* — and his political genius depended entirely on two things the empire-supremacy myth erases: an interpreter and an alliance.

The interpreter is one of the most important and most argued-over figures in the history of the Americas: an enslaved Nahua woman the Spanish baptized as Doña Marina and whom history knows as **La Malinche** (lah mah-LEEN-cheh). She had been born into a Nahuatl-speaking noble family, sold or given into slavery among the Maya, and so spoke both Nahuatl (the language of the Mexica) and Maya; given to Cortés as a captive, she quickly learned Spanish and became the indispensable hinge of the entire conquest — Cortés's interpreter, his guide to Mexican politics, his cultural translator, his strategist, and the mother of his child. Nothing Cortés accomplished diplomatically — and the conquest was won diplomatically before it was won militarily — could have happened without her ability to talk to, read, and maneuver among the peoples of Mexico. Her legacy is bitterly contested: in Mexico her name became a byword for betrayal (*malinchismo* still means selling out your own people to foreigners), yet she was an enslaved woman navigating an impossible situation with extraordinary skill, and more recent reckonings see her as a survivor and a power in her own right rather than simply a traitor. Either way, the plain fact is that the man usually credited with conquering Mexico could not have spoken a word to anyone in it without her.

The alliance is the other half of the demolished myth. As Cortés marched inland, he encountered the **Tlaxcala** (tlash-KAH-lah), a fiercely independent people who had never been conquered by the Mexica and who hated them with a century's accumulated venom — surrounded, raided for sacrificial victims, and economically strangled by the empire on every side. After first fighting the Spanish hard, the Tlaxcalans grasped the opportunity these strange newcomers represented and made an alliance: they would supply the army, and the Spanish would supply the spark. This is the single most important fact about the conquest of Mexico. Cortés's real army was *indigenous*. The force that ultimately besieged and stormed Tenochtitlan was overwhelmingly made up of tens of thousands of Tlaxcalan and other native warriors, with the few hundred Spaniards as a small armored core and a useful foreign banner. The conquest of the Aztec Empire was, in large part, a rebellion of the Aztec Empire's own oppressed subjects, who used the Spanish at least as much as the Spanish used them — and who would discover, too late, that they had traded one overlord for a far more total one.

The first phase nearly worked and then catastrophically failed. Cortés marched his Spanish-Tlaxcalan force into Tenochtitlan in late 1519 and was received, warily, by the Mexica emperor, **Moctezuma II** (mok-teh-ZOO-mah, often rendered "Montezuma"). What Moctezuma was thinking remains genuinely uncertain — the sources are all later and mostly Spanish, and the famous story that he believed Cortés might be a returning god is now widely doubted by scholars as a tale shaped after the fact. What is clear is that Moctezuma, ruler of millions, hesitated fatally, received the Spanish into his city, and was soon taken hostage in his own palace — a stunning gamble by Cortés to rule the empire through its captive emperor. It held for months and then collapsed. While Cortés was away, his deputy massacred Mexica nobles at a festival; the city rose in fury; Moctezuma died (whether killed by the enraged Mexica or by the Spanish is disputed); and in the desperate night of escape the Spanish remembered as the *Noche Triste* ("Sad Night"), the Mexica nearly annihilated the invaders as they fled the city along the causeways, drowning and cutting down hundreds. The conquest, at that moment, had failed.

What saved it was the invisible weapon, and it is the grimmest, most decisive fact in the whole story. In 1520, in the middle of this war, **smallpox** arrived in central Mexico — carried by an infected member of a later Spanish party, by tradition an enslaved African who came with it, a grim early crossing of this book's two great horrors — and tore through Tenochtitlan and the surrounding lands. The peoples of the Americas had been biologically isolated for some ten thousand years; they had never encountered smallpox, measles, influenza, or the other crowd diseases that had circulated in the densely populated, livestock-keeping Old World for millennia, and so they had no acquired or inherited resistance to any of them. The result was not an epidemic but an apocalypse. Smallpox killed perhaps a third of the population of central Mexico within a year or two, and it killed *selectively* in a way that shattered the Mexica's ability to fight — it carried off the new emperor, gutted the leadership, killed the experienced warriors, and broke the social machinery of resistance, while the Spanish, immune from childhood exposure, walked through the dying city untouched, which the survivors could only read as a terrifying sign that the foreigners' god was stronger. This is the central, brutal truth the technology-and-courage myth exists to hide: the conquest of the Americas was, above all, a biological catastrophe. Steel and horses and Spanish audacity mattered at the margins; the thing that actually emptied the cities and broke the empires was disease, against which no Mexica valor or Inca army could do anything at all.

With the Mexica reeling from plague and his Tlaxcalan alliance intact, Cortés came back. Through 1521 he laid a methodical, merciless **siege** to Tenochtitlan — cutting the aqueduct that brought the city fresh water, building small ships to control the lake and cut off food, and grinding inward causeway by causeway, block by block, with his huge indigenous army doing most of the fighting and dying. The Mexica, starving, plague-ridden, and cut off, resisted with a courage the Spanish chroniclers themselves recorded with awe, fighting from the rubble of their own temples to the end. In August 1521 the city fell. The last Mexica emperor, **Cuauhtémoc** (kwow-TEH-mok), who had taken command in the final desperate defense, was captured (and later tortured for gold and eventually executed). Tenochtitlan, one of the great cities of the world, was a smoking ruin, its population dead, fled, or enslaved. On its rubble the Spanish would build Mexico City, raising their cathedral literally on top of the Templo Mayor — a new colonial capital planted on the bones of the one it had destroyed, the architecture of conquest made literal in stone.

Much of what we know of the world that was destroyed survives by an irony almost too sharp to bear: it was recorded by the people who helped erase it. In the decades after the conquest a Franciscan friar named Bernardino de Sahagún (Chapter 12) worked with Nahua scribes and elders to compile the Florentine Codex, a vast bilingual encyclopedia of Mexica life, religion, history, and language, illustrated by indigenous artists — an attempt, by a missionary bent on stamping out the old religion, to document the very civilization he was helping to end. Through it, and through the Nahua and mixed-blood writers who used the conquerors' alphabet to set down their own people's memory, the Mexica got a voice in the record after all. They are not, in this book or in the honest history, the gaping savages of the legend. They were a civilization of city-builders, poets, astronomers, and engineers, brought down by a microbe and the rage of the subjects they had themselves oppressed, with a few hundred armored foreigners standing in the right place to inherit the wreckage. The same grim machinery — disease, native division, a tiny band of Spaniards in the right place — was about to repeat itself two thousand miles to the south, against the largest empire the Americas ever produced.


# Chapter 10 — The Fall of the Inca

A decade after Tenochtitlan fell, the whole grim pattern repeated itself on the far side of the equator, against an even bigger prize — and if anything the second conquest is more astonishing and more disturbing than the first, because the empire it destroyed was larger, more centralized, more administratively brilliant, and was brought down by an even smaller band of Spaniards exploiting an even more catastrophic stroke of luck. This is the conquest of the **Inca Empire**, and to feel the full strangeness of it you have to start by grasping how immense and sophisticated the thing being conquered was, because once again the European story shrinks it into scenery, and once again that is a lie.

The Inca Empire was the largest state ever built in the Americas and one of the largest on Earth in its day. Running for some 2,500 miles down the spine of the Andes mountains along the western edge of South America — through what is now Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia, Chile, and Argentina — it bound together perhaps ten to twelve million people of dozens of languages and peoples under a single divine emperor (the *Sapa Inca*) ruling from the mountain capital of **Cusco** (KOO-skoh). And it did this without the things you might think a great empire requires: the Inca had no writing in the usual sense (they kept records on the *quipu*, intricate knotted cords that encoded numbers and possibly more), no wheeled vehicles, no draft animals larger than the llama, and no money. What they had instead was an astonishing genius for organization. They built tens of thousands of miles of paved roads across the most vertical terrain on the planet, with rope suspension bridges over Andean gorges and relay runners who could carry a message hundreds of miles in a day. They built storehouses stocked against famine, terraced whole mountainsides into farmland, and ran a command economy that moved food, labor, and whole populations around the empire by central direction. The labor that built and fed all this came from a system called the **mita** (MEE-tah) — a rotating labor obligation under which each community owed the state a set stint of work, on the roads, the fields, the army, or the mines, in return for which the state fed them and saw them through hard times. Remember the mita; the Spanish will pervert it into something monstrous. The Inca state was, in short, not a horde but a marvel of administration — and a marvel, crucially, that ran entirely from the top down, through the person of the god-emperor. Cut off the head and the whole body could be seized.

And by the time the Spanish arrived, the head was already wounded — by the same invisible weapon that had broken the Mexica. This is the part of the story that pure luck and pure horror combine to make almost unbelievable. Smallpox, racing ahead of the Spanish themselves, had spread overland through the Americas faster than any European could travel, and it reached the Inca Empire *before any Spaniard did*. Around 1527 a great epidemic — almost certainly smallpox — swept the Andes and killed, among countless others, the reigning emperor Huayna Capac and his designated heir, both dead before they ever laid eyes on a European. (The Inca Empire's own narrative traces the same disease arriving through trade contacts a full eight years ahead of Pizarro — the same continent-spanning chain that ran south from Mexico through the Maya world.) The sudden death of the emperor and his heir threw the empire into a disputed succession, and that succession dispute became a brutal **civil war** between two of Huayna Capac's surviving sons: **Atahualpa** (ah-tah-WAHL-pah), based in the north, and his half-brother Huáscar, based in Cusco. Atahualpa won that war, bloodily, in 1532 — and the empire he had just won was exhausted, divided, full of freshly conquered enemies, and missing perhaps a third of its people to plague. It was at that exact, catastrophically vulnerable moment that the Spanish walked in. The conquest of the Inca did not happen *despite* the empire's strength; it happened because disease and civil war had already gutted that strength before the conquerors arrived. Once again, the microbe got there first.

The conqueror was **Francisco Pizarro** (pee-SAH-roh), and he was cut from even rougher cloth than Cortés — an illiterate, illegitimate, hardbitten old soldier who had been on the Isthmus of Panama since Balboa's day and had spent years chasing rumors of a rich kingdom to the south. In 1532, after earlier failed attempts, Pizarro landed on the Peruvian coast with a tiny force — fewer than 200 men, a few dozen horses, a handful of guns — and marched inland and up into the Andes toward Atahualpa, who was encamped near the mountain town of **Cajamarca** (kah-hah-MAR-kah) with an army of many thousands of seasoned, victorious troops fresh from winning the civil war. By every rational measure Pizarro and his couple of hundred men should have been annihilated. What happened instead, in the square at Cajamarca in November 1532, is one of the most shocking acts of treachery and slaughter in the age. Atahualpa, curious and contemptuous of the tiny band of strangers and feeling utterly secure in the middle of his own army, agreed to meet Pizarro and entered the great plaza of Cajamarca with a large but largely unarmed ceremonial retinue. Pizarro had hidden his men, his horses, and his cannon in the buildings around the square. At a signal, the Spanish opened fire and charged into the packed, defenseless crowd. The slaughter was total and one-sided: thousands of Atahualpa's followers were cut down in a few hours of carnage in the enclosed square, and Atahualpa himself — the god-emperor, the living head of the entire top-down empire — was seized alive. In a single afternoon, without losing a man, the Spanish had decapitated the largest empire in the Americas.

What followed is the episode that has come to stand for the whole greed of the conquest: the **ransom of Atahualpa**. The captured emperor, quickly grasping that these strangers were maddened by gold and silver above all things, made an extraordinary offer. He would fill a large room — the chroniclers describe a chamber some 22 feet long and 17 wide — once with gold and twice over with silver, as high as he could reach, in exchange for his freedom. The Spanish agreed, and for months the treasure of the empire flowed to Cajamarca: gold and silver from temples and palaces across the Andes, melted down by the Spanish into bars on the spot. It was the largest ransom in history, an almost unimaginable fortune. And when it had all been delivered, the Spanish took the treasure and **garrotted Atahualpa anyway** in 1533 — strangling him after a sham trial, having extracted everything and granting him only, as a final "mercy" for accepting baptism, death by strangling rather than burning. The betrayal was as total as it was profitable. With the emperor dead and the empire's sacred head destroyed, Pizarro marched on Cusco, installed a puppet emperor, and seemed to have completed the conquest of millions with under 200 men in barely a year. The technology-and-courage myth tells this as Spanish genius. The honest version is colder: a treacherous ambush of an unarmed retinue, an empire already broken by plague and civil war, and a god-king system so centralized that seizing one man brought the whole structure down.

But the conquest was not nearly as finished as Pizarro thought, and this is the part the triumphant story always omits: the Inca fought back for forty more years. The puppet emperor the Spanish installed, **Manco Inca Yupanqui** (MAHN-koh), watched the conquerors loot, abuse, and dishonor his people and his sisters, and in 1536 he turned on them, escaped Cusco, and raised a massive rebellion. Manco's armies besieged the Spanish in Cusco and nearly destroyed the small garrison; another Inca force assaulted the newly founded coastal capital of Lima. The sieges ultimately failed — Spanish cavalry, steel, and the constant arrival of reinforcements turned the tide, along with the help of yet more anti-Inca native allies — but Manco was not destroyed. He withdrew with his followers deep into the rugged, jungle-cloaked mountains northwest of Cusco and founded a defiant rump kingdom, the **Neo-Inca State**, at **Vilcabamba** (veel-kah-BAHM-bah), a remote redoubt the Spanish could not easily reach. From Vilcabamba a line of Inca rulers maintained an independent state-in-exile, raiding, negotiating, and holding out against the full weight of the Spanish empire for a generation. It was not until 1572 that the Spanish finally mounted the expedition that stormed Vilcabamba, captured the last Inca emperor, **Túpac Amaru** (TOO-pahk ah-MAH-roo), dragged him to Cusco, and beheaded him in the public square before a crowd of his own grieving people — the formal, brutal end of independent Inca rule, fully forty years after Cajamarca. (The name Túpac Amaru would echo down the centuries: a descendant led a huge anti-Spanish revolt in the 1780s, and the name was borrowed again by twentieth-century revolutionaries and, improbably, by an American rapper.)

The forty-year resistance matters because it corrects the deepest distortion in how the conquest of the Americas gets told. The fall of the Aztecs and the fall of the Inca are remembered as instantaneous, near-magical collapses — proof, in the racist old framing, that the native empires were hollow and the Europeans irresistible. They were nothing of the kind. They were long, brutal wars, won by the Spanish only with the help of native allies, native diseases, and native civil wars that had done most of the work before the conquerors arrived — and resisted, in the Inca case, for two generations. What the Spanish inherited at the end was not an easy prize but a wrecked and depopulated land they now had to rule and exploit, and exploit it they did, through an institution they built on the bones of that Inca labor system, the mita. For at the heart of the conquered Andes the Spanish would find something that turned the whole bloody enterprise from a one-time plunder into the engine of a global economy: a mountain made almost entirely of silver. That mountain, and the machine of forced labor and world trade built around it, is the next chapter.


# Chapter 11 — The Silver Engine

So far this book has been mostly about motion — ships creeping, oceans crossed, empires falling. This chapter is about what the motion was *for*, and the answer turns out to be a single word: silver. The conquests of Mexico and Peru looted spectacular one-time hoards of gold and silver, but a plundered treasure room runs dry. What turned the Spanish empire from a smash-and-grab into the richest enterprise the world had ever seen — and what, more than any voyage, actually created the first global economy — was the discovery that the conquered Andes contained not a treasure room but a *mountain* of silver, and the building of a vast, brutal, planet-spanning machine to dig it out and move it around the world. This is the chapter where exploration becomes economics, and where, for the first time, something that happened in a Bolivian mine could change the price of bread in a Chinese village.

The mountain was found in 1545, high in the Andes of what is now Bolivia, at a place called **Potosí** (poh-toh-SEE). It was a conical peak, the *Cerro Rico* ("Rich Hill"), and it was veined with silver of staggering richness and quantity — quite possibly the single most productive source of silver in human history. The Spanish poured in, and around the mountain, at the breathless altitude of over 13,000 feet (high enough that the thin air leaves a newcomer gasping, on a site that grows no food and where almost nothing will live), there exploded one of the most improbable cities on Earth. Within a few decades Potosí had a population of over 100,000 — bigger than London or Seville at the time — a city of churches, gambling dens, and silver barons clinging to a frozen mountainside in the middle of nowhere, existing for one reason only: to tear silver out of the hill. The phrase *vale un Potosí*, "worth a Potosí," entered Spanish to mean a priceless fortune, and it is no exaggeration to say the silver of this one mountain bankrolled the Spanish empire, funded its endless European wars, and flowed outward until it touched every economy on the planet.

The silver was dug by the bodies of Andean people, conscripted through the cruelest perversion in this book of an institution we have already met. Recall the **mita** (Chapter 10), the old Inca rotating labor draft under which communities owed the state a stint of work in return for being fed and protected. The Spanish, around 1573, seized this familiar Andean obligation and bent it into something the Inca would never have recognized: a forced rotating draft that conscripted indigenous men from across a huge swath of the Andes and marched them, sometimes hundreds of miles, to labor in the Potosí mines and the deadly mercury mines that processed the silver (mercury, a toxic liquid metal, dissolves silver out of crushed ore, letting the silver be recovered and the mercury boiled off — efficient, and slow poison to everyone who handled it). The colonial mita was, in practice, a death sentence dressed as a labor obligation. Conditions in the mines were horrific — men hauled ore up rickety ladders from suffocating depths, breathed poisonous dust, and suffered ghastly mercury poisoning in the refining process — and untold thousands of Andean workers died in the Cerro Rico and the mercury mines of Huancavelica over the centuries. The local memory was that the mountain ate men. Here is the moral arithmetic of the silver engine laid bare: an institution the Inca had used to build storehouses against famine, repurposed by the conquerors to feed human beings into a mountain so that silver could flow to Spain. The wealth that lubricated the first global economy was dug, quite literally, with conscripted lives.

Getting that silver from a mountain in the Andes to the markets of the world required bureaucracy, and Spain built a formidable one. Every ounce of New World treasure was supposed to flow through a single controlled pipeline. At the Spanish end stood the **Casa de Contratación** (KAH-sah deh kon-trah-tah-see-OHN), the "House of Trade," set up in Seville back in 1503 — a royal agency that licensed every ship, registered every cargo, trained pilots, hoarded the master charts, and tried to make sure no silver moved without the crown taking its cut. And take its cut it did: the king claimed the **royal fifth** (the *quinto real*), a flat 20 percent tax on all precious metal, which made the king of Spain personally, fabulously rich on every bar dug from Potosí. To get the treasure safely across an Atlantic now infested with pirates and rival powers (Chapter 13), Spain organized the **treasure fleets** (the *flota* system): great convoys of galleons that crossed the ocean together, heavily armed and escorted, on a fixed schedule, gathering at Caribbean ports to haul the year's silver back to Seville in one defended mass. The galleon — bigger, tougher, and more heavily gunned than the old carrack — was the workhorse of this system, the armed cargo ship that carried the world's silver and fought off the privateers who dreamed of taking it. The treasure fleet was the bloodstream of the Spanish empire, and the prize that every English and Dutch sea-raider in the next chapter spent his life trying to bleed.

And then, in 1565, the engine closed a loop that completed the encirclement of the globe, and this is the moment many historians point to as the true birth of the world economy. Spain established a regular trade route straight across the Pacific — the **Manila galleon** — running between **Acapulco**, on the Pacific coast of Mexico, and **Manila**, the Spanish base in the Philippines. The logic was beautiful and world-changing. China, the largest economy on Earth, had an almost insatiable hunger for silver, which under a sweeping tax overhaul called the Single Whip Reform it was adopting as the basis of its money and its taxes (the Chinese end of this same silver flow — and the way a later collapse in silver shipments helped bring down the Ming dynasty — is told in the Ming narrative), and silver was worth dramatically more in China than in the Americas. So the Manila galleons carried Mexican and Peruvian silver *west* across the Pacific to Manila, where Chinese merchants eagerly traded it for silk, porcelain, and other Asian luxuries, which the galleons carried *back* east to Acapulco and on to Europe. For the first time in history, there was regular, sustained trade directly linking the Americas, Asia, and (through the Atlantic fleets) Europe — a continuous loop of exchange around the entire planet. American silver, dug by conscripted Andeans, crossed two oceans to end up in Chinese vaults, and the demand of Chinese taxpayers half a world away set the price that drove men into the mines at Potosí. The world had become, economically, one interlinked system. Many historians date the real beginning of **globalization** to exactly this — around 1571, when the Manila route opened and the last gap in the global trade web was bridged.

The instrument that tied it all together, the thing that actually moved through every link in the chain, was a single coin, and it deserves to be called what it was: the first world money. The Spanish minted their American silver into a large, standardized coin worth eight *reales*, known as the **piece of eight** or the **Spanish dollar** (the *peso de ocho*). Because it was abundant, reliably pure, and uniform, it became the closest thing the early-modern world had to a universal currency — circulating not just in Spanish lands but across Europe, around the Indian Ocean, throughout China and Southeast Asia, and later in the British colonies of North America, where it remained legal tender into the 1850s and where the habit of cutting it into eight "bits" to make change is the reason Americans still occasionally call a quarter "two bits" and why the dollar sign and the very word "dollar" descend from it. A coin struck from Andean silver in a Mexican mint, spendable in Canton and London and Boston alike — that is what a single global economy looks like at the level of a thing you can hold in your hand.

But a flood of new money has consequences, and the silver engine produced the first one nobody had ever seen at this scale: inflation, across an entire continent, traceable to a cause on the other side of the world. As ever more American silver poured into Europe through the sixteenth century, the sheer quantity of money in circulation swelled far faster than the quantity of goods to buy with it, and prices rose and rose for a hundred years — a long, grinding rise that historians call the **price revolution**. The economics of *why* are still debated, but the broad picture is striking: for the first time, a glut of treasure dug out of a mountain in the Andes could push up the cost of bread in a Spanish village, of wool in England, of rent across Europe. Money itself had become global, and so had the disorders of money. (One bitter irony: Spain, which controlled the silver, ended up among the worst hurt — the treasure flowed in one side of the country and straight out the other to pay for wars and foreign goods, leaving Spain inflated, indebted, and addicted to a wealth it didn't actually produce, while the merchant economies of northwestern Europe that *sold* to Spain quietly grew rich. The kingdom that found the mountain of silver was hollowed out by it.)

Step back and look at what the silver engine had wrought. A single mountain in Bolivia, worked by the conscripted bodies of conquered Andeans, fed a bureaucratic pipeline that carried its silver across two oceans, made the king of Spain the richest monarch in Christendom, supplied China with the money it ran its empire on, minted the first coin the whole world would accept, and set off the first worldwide inflation. The voyages of the previous chapters had connected the continents; the silver engine made that connection *economic*, continuous, and self-sustaining. The world was now genuinely one world, bound together not by a single voyage but by the relentless daily flow of treasure and trade. But binding the world together also meant binding together its consciences and its cruelties, and the same century that built the silver engine and the slave ships also produced — from within the conquering society itself — the first anguished arguments about whether any of it could possibly be just. That argument, and the global missionary church that grew up alongside the empire, are the next chapter.


# Chapter 12 — Cross and Conscience

Every empire in this book sailed under a cross as well as a flag, and the religion was not decoration — it was, for many of the people involved, the entire point. The Portuguese ran their voyages under the banner of a crusading order; the Spanish conquered with a friar at the side of every captain; the whole legal fiction by which the pope had handed out continents (Chapter 6) rested on the duty to convert the people who lived on them. This chapter is about that religious empire — the disciplined new missionary order that carried Catholicism literally around the world, the dark machinery of enforced faith that traveled with it, and, set against both, the most surprising thing the age produced: a fierce, sustained, and genuinely radical argument, conducted *inside* the conquering society, over whether the whole enterprise was a sin. The Age of Exploration is the age that invented global Christianity. It is also the age that staged what may be the first formal European debate over the morality of empire itself. Both belong in the same chapter because they grew from the same root.

The spearhead of the new global Catholicism was an order founded in 1540 by a Basque ex-soldier named Ignatius of Loyola: the **Society of Jesus**, whose members were called the **Jesuits**. The Jesuits were something new — not monks withdrawn from the world in a monastery, but a disciplined, mobile, intellectually formidable corps organized almost on military lines (Loyola had been a soldier, and it shows), trained for years, sworn to obedience, and sent wherever the Church's expansion needed them. They became the elite shock troops of the Catholic missionary effort across the entire newly connected world — running schools and missions from Mexico and Brazil to India, China, and Japan, learning local languages, studying local cultures with a seriousness no previous missionaries had shown, and embedding themselves at royal courts. Where the conquistadors brought steel and the merchants brought silver, the Jesuits brought catechisms, Latin grammar, and a strategy of converting the elite from the top down. They are the reason there are old Catholic populations today scattered from the Andes to Goa to the Philippines.

The most famous of the first Jesuits carried the cross to the very far end of the world, and his journey shows just how fast the connected globe now moved ideas. **Francis Xavier** (ZAY-vee-er), one of Loyola's original companions, sailed east in the Portuguese empire's wake, preaching in India and the Spice Islands, and then in 1549 did something that would have been unimaginable a single lifetime earlier: he landed in **Japan** and launched the first sustained Christian mission in East Asia. Think about that sequence. In 1415 Europeans were too frightened to sail past a headland in Morocco; by 1549 a Spanish priest was preaching the Gospel in Japan, on the literal opposite side of the planet, reached by the chain of Portuguese trading posts strung across the Indian Ocean and on to the China coast. Xavier's mission opened what is sometimes called Japan's "Christian century," a remarkable period in which hundreds of thousands of Japanese converted to Catholicism — until the Japanese authorities, growing alarmed at the foreign faith and the foreign power behind it, turned on it with terrible violence. The first bloody sign came in 1597, when twenty-six Christians — Japanese converts and foreign missionaries — were crucified at Nagasaki, the **Twenty-Six Martyrs of Japan**, a warning of the brutal suppression and total expulsion of Christianity that Japan would impose over the following decades. The global church could reach Japan; it could not make Japan keep it.

Traveling alongside the missionary's catechism, however, was a far darker instrument, and honesty requires giving it equal weight: the **Inquisition**. The Inquisition was the Church's machinery for detecting and punishing heresy — the holding of beliefs the Church deemed wrong — and it operated through interrogation, denunciation, torture, and, for the unrepentant, execution, often by burning. The Spanish and Portuguese carried it overseas with their empires. In 1560 Portugal established the **Goa Inquisition** in its Asian capital (Chapter 7), and it became one of the most feared tribunals of the era. Its main targets were not, in fact, unconverted Hindus and Muslims but *converts* suspected of secretly keeping their old practices — newly Christian Indians accused of backsliding into Hindu rites — along with Jews and others. For two centuries it policed belief in Portuguese Asia through fear, imprisonment, and the public burning of the condemned. The Goa Inquisition is the necessary shadow of the missionary story: faith spread not only by the Jesuit's patient teaching but by the inquisitor's fire, and the same empire that produced Francis Xavier's mission produced the tribunal that tortured converts for the wrong kind of prayer. The global church was an instrument of conscience and an instrument of terror at once.

And here the two halves of this chapter turn out to be one. The missionary church and the inquisitor's fire both rested on a single claim — that the empire existed to save souls, that the duty to convert was what made the whole enterprise holy in the first place (Chapter 6). But that same claim cut both ways, because if the native peoples had souls worth saving, they were human beings, and if they were human beings, then enslaving and slaughtering them was not the saving of souls but the murder of them. The conversion mandate that licensed the conquest was the exact lever its critics would use to condemn it. And so — this is the genuinely astonishing turn, the thing that makes this chapter more than a catalog of cruelty — the conquering society generated, from within itself, out of the very mission that justified the empire, a powerful current of *conscience* that turned and condemned what the conquest was doing. It is easy and lazy to imagine that everyone in sixteenth-century Spain simply accepted the slaughter and enslavement of the Americas as obviously fine. They did not. A real and sustained argument broke out, and it was loudest among the very friars the empire sent to convert the natives, because those friars lived among the dying and could see plainly what was happening. The first public cry came early, in 1511, on Hispaniola, when a Dominican friar named **Antonio de Montesinos** (mon-teh-SEE-nohs) climbed into the pulpit and thundered at the assembled colonists that they were in mortal sin — that by enslaving, overworking, and slaughtering the Taíno (Chapter 5) they were damning their own souls, and by what right, he demanded, did they wage such cruel war on people who had done them no harm? It scandalized the colony. And it lit a fuse.

The fuse ran to the towering figure of the age's conscience: **Bartolomé de las Casas** (bar-toh-loh-MEH deh lahs KAH-sahs). Las Casas began as part of the problem — a colonist on Hispaniola who himself held an *encomienda* (the grant of indigenous labor that was the basic engine of Spanish exploitation, a legal fiction by which a colonist got the forced labor of native people in exchange for supposedly Christianizing them). Then, around 1514, he underwent a profound conversion of conscience, gave up his encomienda, became a Dominican friar, and spent the next fifty years as the most relentless, ferocious advocate for the indigenous peoples of the Americas that Europe produced. He crossed the Atlantic repeatedly to lobby the king in person; he wrote searing, deliberately horrifying accounts of Spanish atrocities (above all his *Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies*, a catalog of massacres meant to shock the crown into action); and he argued, flatly, that the indigenous peoples were fully rational human beings with souls, rights, lands, and rulers of their own, that the wars against them were unjust, and that the encomienda was a crime. Las Casas was not a lone eccentric; he was the sharp point of a real movement, and improbably, he won real victories. His campaigning pushed the crown to issue, in 1542, the **New Laws** — royal legislation that curbed the encomienda, banned the enslavement of indigenous people, and ordered protections for them. The colonists' reaction proved how little the distant crown actually controlled its conquerors: in Peru the New Laws helped trigger an outright colonist rebellion, and the crown was forced to water them down. But the principle had been asserted by the state itself, in law.

The argument reached its climax in 1550 in a genuinely remarkable event: the Spanish crown halted (in theory) the conquests and convened a formal debate at the city of **Valladolid** (vah-yah-doh-LEED) to settle the underlying question — was the conquest of the Americas just, and were its peoples rational humans with rights, or natural inferiors fit to be ruled by force? On one side stood Las Casas, arguing that the indigenous peoples were fully rational, fully human, possessed of sophisticated societies, and entitled to their freedom and their lands. On the other stood the scholar **Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda** (seh-POOL-veh-dah), who argued, leaning on the ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle's doctrine of "natural slavery" (the idea, still treated as towering authority in European universities two thousand years later, that some people are by their very nature suited only to be ruled by others), that the natives were by nature inferior beings whom it was right and even beneficial to conquer and rule. The **Valladolid debate** is sometimes called the first formal European argument over human rights and the morality of empire, and that is not too strong. Two learned men stood before a royal commission and argued, in effect, over whether an entire branch of humanity was fully human. No clear verdict was ever issued, and on the ground the exploitation rolled on regardless — Las Casas "won" the argument in the judgment of history far more than he changed the facts of the colonies. But the mere fact that the debate happened, that the conquering empire felt compelled to stop and ask whether what it was doing was a sin, and that the answer "no, this is monstrous" was argued at the highest level by the empire's own churchmen, is one of the few genuinely redeeming things in this entire dark stretch of history.

It is important not to oversell it. Las Casas, to his lasting shame, at one point suggested importing enslaved Africans to spare the indigenous Americans the labor — a proposal he later bitterly repented, but which shows how the conscience of the age had a hard limit, and how the moral concern for native Americans coexisted with, and even fed, the African slave trade that the final chapter must reckon with. The men who recorded the conquered world were often the same men helping to destroy it: the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún, whose Nahua-illustrated *Florentine Codex* (Chapter 9) preserved so much of Mexica civilization, compiled it precisely to help stamp out the religion it documented; the soldier-chroniclers Bernal Díaz del Castillo and Pedro Cieza de León left vivid, humane, indispensable accounts of the very worlds they had marched through and helped subdue. Conscience and conquest, the cross and the fire, the friar who saved a culture in ink and the inquisitor who burned heretics, the man who pleaded for the Indians and in the same breath consigned the Africans — they are all tangled together, because they all grew from the same expanding, conquering, believing society. The age that connected the world also forced that world's first arguments about what we owe the people on the other side of it. They are arguments we have not finished having.

Meanwhile, the Iberian monopoly on the connected world was not going to last. For a century Spain and Portugal had run the oceans as if Tordesillas (Chapter 6) made the planet their private property. But other Europeans — the English, the French, the Dutch — had no intention of being shut out of the richest enterprise in history, and they came not as missionaries or even, at first, as colonists, but as pirates, smugglers, and corporate raiders. The breaking of the Iberian monopoly, and the strange new kind of organization the latecomers invented to do it, is the next chapter.


# Chapter 13 — The Northern Latecomers

For nearly a century the oceans belonged, on paper, to Spain and Portugal. The Treaty of Tordesillas (Chapter 6) had split the non-Christian world between the two Iberian crowns, the pope had blessed it, and the silver fleets and spice routes ran as Iberian monopolies. The other kingdoms of Europe — England, France, the soon-to-exist Dutch Republic — looked at this arrangement, in which two middling powers had simply declared the planet their property, and reacted with the contempt it deserved. As one French king is supposed to have said, he would like to see the clause in Adam's will that left the world to the kings of Spain and Portugal. This chapter is about how the latecomers broke in — first as fishermen and explorers picking at the edges, then as out-and-out pirates plundering the silver fleets, and finally through a genuinely new and world-altering invention, the joint-stock company, which let private investors pool money to wage commerce and war on a scale no single merchant could manage. The northern Europeans entered the story late and poor and shut out. By the end of it they would own most of the future.

The earliest northern attempts were thin, cold, and aimed at a fantasy: a shortcut to Asia over the top of the Americas. England's entry was almost an afterthought. Henry VII of England had famously turned down Columbus before Spain took him on, and to make up for the miss, in 1497 he licensed another Italian, **John Cabot** (Giovanni Caboto, a Venetian), to sail west and claim new lands. Cabot made landfall somewhere on the North American coast — Newfoundland or thereabouts — and came back reporting not gold but something that proved, in the long run, almost as valuable: seas off the coast so thick with cod that, the story went, you could lower a basket and haul it up full. That cod fishery would draw European boats across the Atlantic for centuries and feed the growth of the northern colonies. France soon nosed in too: in 1524 **Giovanni da Verrazzano** (vair-ah-TSAH-noh), an Italian sailing for the French king, ran the entire eastern seaboard of North America from the Carolinas to Newfoundland, the first European to chart it and the first to sail into the great harbor of what is now New York (the bridge across its mouth bears his name today). A decade later, from 1534, the Frenchman **Jacques Cartier** (zhahk kar-tee-AY) pushed up the broad **St. Lawrence River** deep into the continent, taking from a local Iroquoian word, *kanata* ("village"), the name that would eventually attach to a whole country: Canada. None of these voyages found the hoped-for passage to Asia, and none founded a lasting colony. But they staked the northern claims and mapped the coast on which the real colonies would later rise.

While the explorers probed the cold north, a far more aggressive way of breaking the Iberian monopoly was developing: simply stealing its wealth. The Spanish treasure fleets (Chapter 11) carried the richest cargoes on Earth, and to the seamen of Protestant England — now in bitter religious and political conflict with Catholic Spain — robbing them was patriotism, profit, and holy war all in one. That religious split was new and raw. In 1517 a German monk named Martin Luther had publicly attacked the Catholic Church (the ancient, Rome-centered, pope-led Christianity under which the whole Age of Exploration had so far sailed) as corrupt, touching off the **Reformation** — a revolt that broke Western Christianity in two and produced the breakaway churches collectively called **Protestant** (from their "protest" against Rome). Within a few decades Europe was carved into Catholic powers that stayed loyal to the pope (Spain and Portugal above all) and Protestant ones that had thrown him off (much of Germany, the Dutch, and England, which had broken with Rome under Henry VIII). The division was not a quarrel over fine points of theology to the people living it; it was a matter Europeans killed and died over for the next century and a half, and it turned the rivalry between Protestant England and Catholic Spain into something closer to a holy war than an ordinary contest between kingdoms. The greatest of these sea raiders was **Francis Drake**, half explorer and half pirate (the Spanish simply called him *El Draque*, "the Dragon," and considered him a terrorist). Between 1577 and 1580 Drake sailed right around the world — the second expedition ever to circumnavigate the globe after Magellan's, and the first whose commander survived the whole trip — and he did it largely as a plundering raid, slipping through the Strait of Magellan to fall on the undefended Spanish silver ports of the Pacific coast, which had never imagined an enemy could reach them, and seizing so much treasure that when he got home the loot reportedly paid off the entire debt of the English crown with plenty left over. Queen Elizabeth I knighted him on the deck of his own ship. Drake was the spectacular face of a strategy that infuriated and bled Spain for decades.

The conflict came to a head in 1588, when the Spanish king Philip II — fed up with English piracy and English meddling, and bent on toppling the Protestant Elizabeth and returning England to Catholicism — assembled the **Spanish Armada**, an enormous invasion fleet of some 130 ships meant to escort an army across to conquer England. It was the largest naval enterprise the age had seen, and it failed catastrophically. The smaller, nimbler, more heavily gunned English ships harried it up the Channel; a night attack with fire ships scattered its formation; and then the weather, the great wild-card of the age of sail, finished the job, driving the broken fleet on a desperate flight around the storm-lashed coasts of Scotland and Ireland, where ship after ship was wrecked. Only about half the Armada limped home. The defeat did not end Spanish power overnight — Spain remained the greatest empire on Earth for decades more — but it shattered the aura of Spanish invincibility and announced, unmistakably, that the Atlantic was no longer an Iberian lake. The door the latecomers had been pushing at was now open.

Their first attempts to walk through it and actually *settle* the Americas were, however, almost comically disastrous, and one of them became an enduring mystery. In the 1580s the English courtier Walter Raleigh sponsored a colony on **Roanoke** Island, off the coast of present-day North Carolina. The settlers struggled, and when a supply ship finally returned in 1590 after a long delay (the Armada crisis had tied up England's ships), the colony was simply *gone* — the houses dismantled, the people vanished, with no bodies and no sign of struggle, and only a single word carved into a post: **CROATOAN**, the name of a nearby island and a local people. The fate of the "Lost Colony" of Roanoke has never been definitively solved; the likeliest explanation is that the abandoned settlers were absorbed into a neighboring indigenous community, but no one knows for certain. Roanoke stands as a fittingly humbling symbol of how marginal and fragile the northern foothold still was at the close of the sixteenth century: England's first attempt at an American colony didn't fail so much as evaporate.

What finally made the latecomers succeed was not a better ship or a braver captain but a better *institution*, and it is one of the most consequential inventions in this whole book: the **joint-stock company**. The problem with long-distance trade and colonization was that it was ruinously expensive and ruinously risky — a single voyage could make a fortune or sink with everything aboard, and no individual merchant could afford to lose that much. The solution was to let many investors buy *shares* in a company, pooling their money so that each risked only a slice, and sharing the profits (or losses) in proportion. Spread the risk, pool the capital, and suddenly enterprises far too large and dangerous for any one person became possible. Two of these companies would change the world. In 1600 London merchants chartered the English **East India Company** (the EIC) to break into the Asian trade. In 1602 the Dutch went a step further and fused their competing merchant ventures into the **Dutch East India Company**, known by its Dutch initials as the **VOC** — and the VOC was something genuinely unprecedented: the world's first publicly traded company, with shares anyone could buy and sell on the new Amsterdam exchange. These were not mere trading firms. Their charters gave them powers that we now think of as belonging only to states: they could raise armies and navies, build forts, mint money, sign treaties, wage war, and govern territory, all in pursuit of profit. They were corporations with the powers of governments — and they would eventually rule tens of millions of people. The modern multinational corporation, the stock market, the shareholder: they are all born here, as instruments for breaking the Spanish-Portuguese monopoly on the wealth of the world.

These companies and the crowns behind them now planted the colonies that would actually last, the seeds of the modern map. In 1607 the English Virginia Company founded **Jamestown** on a malarial riverbank in Virginia — England's first permanent American colony, which very nearly starved to death in its first years (there was a winter the survivors remembered as the "Starving Time") before the cultivation of an American plant, **tobacco**, gave it a profitable reason to exist and the first global consumer craze its cash crop. In 1608 the Frenchman **Samuel de Champlain** founded **Quebec** on the St. Lawrence, anchoring New France — an empire built less on conquest and settlement than on the fur trade (indigenous hunters trapped beaver and other animals across the northern forests and traded the pelts to the French for metal goods, cloth, and guns; the furs, above all beaver felted into the fashionable hats of Europe, were the whole reason New France existed) and on careful alliances with indigenous nations, whose canoes and knowledge the French depended on. In 1620 a group of English religious separatists, the **Pilgrims** (Protestants who had given up on reforming the official Church of England from within and wanted to break away from it entirely, a stance that made them outlaws at home), crossed on the *Mayflower* and founded **Plymouth Colony** in New England — the strain of northern colonization driven not by gold or fur but by the search for a place to worship as they pleased, a motive that would deeply mark the future of English North America. And in 1624 the Dutch West India Company founded **New Amsterdam** at the tip of Manhattan Island, a fur-trading port that, four decades later, the English would seize and rename **New York**. (Among the northern dreams that *didn't* pan out was the old fantasy of a **Northwest Passage** over the top of America to Asia, which swallowed lives and fortunes for centuries; the English navigator **Henry Hudson**, probing the river and bay that now bear his name in search of it around 1610, was set adrift to die by his own mutinous crew — the passage's obsession claiming another victim.)

It would be a serious mistake, though, to imagine the latecomers as somehow gentler than the Iberians they were displacing. The joint-stock empires could be as savage as any conquistador, and the proof is one of the coldest atrocities in this book. The VOC wanted a total monopoly on **nutmeg**, a spice then grown in only one place on Earth, the tiny **Banda Islands** of eastern Indonesia. The Bandanese, an independent trading people, kept selling to other buyers. So in 1621 the VOC's iron-willed governor-general, **Jan Pieterszoon Coen** (yahn PEE-ter-soan koon), solved the problem by effectively *exterminating* them: of a population of perhaps 15,000, the great majority were killed, starved, enslaved, or driven out in a campaign of deliberate annihilation, and the emptied islands were repopulated with enslaved laborers to work the nutmeg groves under VOC control. The Banda massacre is the spice empire's logic stripped to its bones — monopoly enforced by genocide, a corporation wiping out a people to control the price of a seasoning. The northern latecomers, with their stock exchanges and their charters and their religious refugees, were building the modern world; they were also, when the ledger demanded it, capable of murdering an entire island.

By the time the Dutch sent **Abel Tasman** (TAHS-mun) probing the far southern seas in the 1640s — skirting the island that now bears his name (Tasmania), New Zealand, and the coast of "New Holland" (Australia), sketching in the last great inhabited landmasses on the European map — and planted a resupply station at the **Cape of Good Hope** in 1652 (Jan van Riebeeck's little VOC garden for victualling the India ships, which displaced the local Khoikhoi herders and grew, over time, into a permanent European colony in southern Africa and the root of a long and bitter history there), the shape of the modern colonial world was set. The Iberian monopoly was broken for good. England, France, and the Dutch held footholds on three continents and the corporate and naval machinery to expand them. The exploring was nearly done; the maps were nearly filled. What remained was to take the full measure of what two centuries of voyages had actually done to the planet — to the foods people ate, the diseases they died of, the populations of whole continents, the millions carried across the ocean in chains, and the very idea of the world as a single place. That reckoning is the final chapter.


# Chapter 14 — The World the Voyages Made

So what did it all add up to? Two and a half centuries of voyages, from a frightened caravel creeping past a Saharan headland to a Dutch ship sketching the coast of Australia, and at the end the world was simply not the same world. For ten thousand years — ever since the rising seas at the end of the last Ice Age had cut the Americas off from Eurasia and Africa — the planet had been split into separate biological and human spheres that did not touch. The voyages welded those spheres into one. This final chapter is about the consequences of that welding: the colossal exchange of life across the oceans, the human catastrophe at the bottom of it, the trade that turned people into cargo, the new societies that grew from the collision, and the new picture of a single round Earth that the whole age finally produced. It is the chapter where exploration stops being a story about ships and becomes a story about the modern world, because nearly everything in the modern world — its dinner plates, its populations, its wealth, its wounds — begins here.

The most sweeping consequence is the one historians named the **Columbian Exchange**: the vast, two-way transfer of plants, animals, and diseases between the Old World (Europe, Asia, Africa) and the New World (the Americas) that began with Columbus's voyages and never stopped. It is almost impossible to overstate how thoroughly this remade ordinary life on every continent, because so much of what now seems eternal and native to a place actually arrived in the last five hundred years. Going *west*, from the Old World to the Americas, came horses (which transformed the Plains peoples of North America into mounted nations and which had no American ancestor walking around), cattle, pigs, sheep, chickens, wheat, sugar cane, coffee, and bananas. Going *east*, from the Americas to the Old World, came an astonishing roster of crops that would reshape the diet of the entire planet: **maize** (corn) and the **potato** above all, but also tomatoes, chili peppers, cacao (chocolate), beans, squash, vanilla, and tobacco. Try to picture Italian food without the tomato, Irish history without the potato, Indian and Thai cooking without the chili pepper, West African cuisine without maize and cassava, a Swiss café without chocolate — none of it existed before the exchange. The American crops were often more productive and more nutritious than what they joined, and they touched off population booms across the Old World: the potato helped feed the growth of northern Europe, maize and the sweet potato helped fuel an enormous population surge in China. A plant domesticated by Andean farmers ended up determining how many people Europe and Asia could feed. That is the benign face of the exchange, and it genuinely transformed humanity's prospects.

But the exchange had a second face, and it was the deadliest event in recorded human history. Along with the crops and the livestock traveled the Old World's diseases — smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus, and the rest — the crowd diseases that had brewed for millennia among Eurasia's dense, livestock-keeping populations, against which Old World peoples had built up partial resistance over countless generations. The peoples of the Americas, isolated for ten thousand years, had no such resistance to *any* of them, and when the diseases arrived they did not cause epidemics so much as a continent-wide extinction event. We met the mechanism at Tenochtitlan and in the Andes (Chapters 9 and 10): waves of disease, often running ahead of the Europeans themselves, that killed not a third or a half but, by the most credible estimates, somewhere around *ninety percent* of the entire indigenous population of the Americas over the century and more following contact — tens of millions of people, whole nations and language families simply gone, in the greatest demographic catastrophe humanity has ever recorded. (The dying was so vast that some scientists argue the regrowth of forests on the emptied farmland of the Americas pulled enough carbon dioxide out of the air to measurably cool the global climate — a death toll large enough to leave a fingerprint in the atmosphere.) This is the thing that must never be sanded out of the story of the Age of Exploration: the connecting of the world was, for the people of the Americas, an apocalypse, and it was caused not chiefly by swords but by germs that no one understood and no one could have stopped.

And the emptying of the Americas drove the age's other great crime, because the conquerors now possessed two depopulated continents full of mines and plantations and almost no one left to work them. Their solution was to import forced labor across the ocean, and so the small Atlantic slave trade we watched begin on a Portuguese dock in 1444 (Chapter 3) metastasized into an industrialized horror without parallel. In 1526 the first slave ship sailed *directly* from Africa to the Americas — bypassing Europe entirely — and over the next three centuries the **Atlantic slave trade** would forcibly carry more than **twelve million** African men, women, and children across the ocean (and well over a million more would die in the passage). It organized itself into the brutal circuit known as the **triangular trade**: manufactured goods and guns sailed from Europe to Africa and were exchanged for captives; the captives were packed into ships and carried across the Atlantic in the nightmare voyage called the Middle Passage to be sold in the Americas; and the sugar, tobacco, and silver their labor produced sailed back to Europe to start the circuit again. (The Middle Passage, the plantation complex, and the full arc of those twelve million lives are the subject of the Atlantic slave trade's own narrative, where this thread runs to its end.) Whole economies — the sugar plantations of Brazil and the Caribbean, the silver of Spanish America, eventually the cotton and tobacco of North America — were built on this traffic in human beings. The plantation prototype that had first locked together on Madeira's sugar slopes (Chapter 2) became the template for an entire hemisphere. The age that connected the world ran, in very large part, on the buying and selling of people.

It is essential, in telling this, to refuse the lie that Africa was a passive supplier with no voice of its own, because the African record of resistance and protest is real, documented, and devastating. We left the Christian king **Afonso I of Kongo** (Chapter 3) ruling a kingdom that had joined Christendom by choice. As the Portuguese slave trade tore into his realm — slavers buying and seizing his subjects faster than any law could stop, depopulating provinces, corrupting his own nobles into selling their neighbors — Afonso did something extraordinary: he *wrote about it*, in elegant Portuguese, in a series of letters to the king of Portugal in the 1520s and 1530s, begging him to halt the trade that was devouring his people, protesting that his kingdom was being emptied and his authority destroyed. Here is a literate African Christian monarch, a contemporary of Cortés, documenting in real time, from the inside, the catastrophe the slave trade was inflicting on his own kingdom — and being ignored, because the trade was too profitable to stop. A century later, in Angola, another African ruler resisted with arms and diplomacy for decades: **Queen Njinga** (also spelled Nzinga, n-ZING-ah) of the kingdoms of Ndongo and Matamba, who from the 1620s into the 1660s out-fought, out-negotiated, and out-maneuvered the Portuguese, converting and reconverting as it suited her strategy, building alliances, forming her own state, and making herself one of the most formidable rulers of the entire century — a woman holding her own in a world of European kings and male warlords, and a standing rebuke to any version of this history in which Africans only suffer and never act. Afonso's letters and Njinga's wars are not footnotes. They are the African side of the record, in African voices, insisting that this was done *to* people who saw exactly what it was and fought it.

Out of all this collision — conquest, disease, slavery, migration, conversion — grew entirely new kinds of society, and Spanish America invented an elaborate and obsessive way of trying to sort them. The conquest produced, from its first generation, large numbers of children of Spanish and indigenous parents, called **mestizos** (mes-TEE-zohs), and as enslaved Africans were added to the mix, the population of the colonies became a deeply blended one. Spanish colonial society reacted to this blending with anxiety and an almost mathematical mania for racial classification, building a hierarchy called the **casta** (KAH-stah) system that tried to assign every possible combination of Spanish, indigenous, and African ancestry its own named rank and legal status — a pyramid with people of pure Spanish blood (especially those born in Spain) at the top and everyone else slotted below by the precise proportions of their descent. The casta system was a fantasy of control over a reality far too fluid to pin down, and it never worked cleanly, but it left a deep and lasting mark: the obsession with racial hierarchy and "purity of blood" that the Age of Exploration injected into the societies of the Americas is one of its ugliest and most durable legacies, and the modern world's poisonous categories of race are in no small part its descendants. (Three centuries on, the resentments built into this hierarchy — Spanish-born rulers lording it over American-born whites, with everyone else slotted below — would help detonate the wars of Latin American independence, whose narrative opens on exactly this pressure cooker.)

And finally, the age changed how human beings *pictured* the world, because for the first time the world could be accurately pictured at all. As the voyages filled in the coastlines, mapmakers — especially in the Low Countries — assembled the new knowledge into images of a single, whole, round Earth. In 1569 the Flemish cartographer **Gerardus Mercator** (mer-KAY-tor) devised a brilliant solution to a sailor's problem: a way of projecting the curved surface of the globe onto a flat map so that a line of constant compass bearing came out as a straight line, which let a navigator plot a course with a ruler. The **Mercator projection** distorts the world badly near the poles (it is why Greenland looks as big as Africa on so many maps, though Africa is actually about fourteen times larger) — but for steering a ship it was a stroke of genius, and it is still the projection behind most of the maps and navigation apps you have ever looked at. The year after, in 1570, another Fleming, **Abraham Ortelius** (or-TEE-lee-us), bound a set of uniform maps of the whole known Earth into a single volume, the *Theatrum Orbis Terrarum* ("Theater of the World") — the first true **atlas**, a planet that could now be held in one book and turned, page by page, in your hands. (The same restless century of measuring and questioning the cosmos produced **Copernicus**, who argued in 1543 that the Earth circles the sun rather than sitting at the center of creation — part of the wider scientific upheaval that the age of global navigation both demanded and helped to fund.) The world had become, for the first time, a single object that a person could see whole.

That is the world the voyages made, and it is worth holding all of it in view at once, because the temptation is always to keep only half. The Age of Exploration gave us the global economy, the modern map, the potato and the tomato and the chili pepper on tables everywhere, the joint-stock company and the stock market, the very idea of the world as one connected place — the foundations of nearly everything we mean by "modern." It also gave us the deadliest demographic catastrophe in history, the Atlantic slave trade and its twelve million stolen lives, the colonial empires whose borders and grievances still set the world on fire, and the poisonous architecture of racial hierarchy. It was one of history's supreme achievements and one of its supreme crimes, and the unbearable, essential truth is that these were not two separate stories but a single one: the same ships, the same winds, the same restless, ingenious, ruthless little kingdoms did both at once. A few wooden ships, learning the patient trick of how to sail away from home in order to come back, stitched the whole planet into one — and we have been living, ever since, in the world they made, trying to enjoy its gifts and answer for its crimes. The voyages ended. Their consequences never have.
