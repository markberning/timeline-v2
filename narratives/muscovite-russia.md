# Muscovite Russia — How a Forest Backwater Became the Third Rome

**Goal:** Narrative for the Russian heartland from the Mongol sack of Kiev in 1240 to Peter the Great taking the imperial title in 1721 — the four and a half centuries in which "Russia" was actually invented. When this story opens, there is no Russia. There is a scatter of quarreling principalities in the forests and river valleys of the northeast, the wreckage left by the Mongol storm that had just erased the brilliant river-civilization of Kievan Rus' (the subject of the previous book). Over the chapters that follow, one of the least promising of those principalities — Moscow, a small, swampy, frontier town with no natural advantages and a reputation for nothing in particular — claws its way to the top by being the most useful collaborator the Mongol khan had: its princes collected everyone else's taxes for the Horde and skimmed the difference in land and leverage. From that grubby beginning grows everything else. Moscow gathers the Russian lands the way a snowball gathers snow, swallowing Tver, Novgorod, Pskov. Its grand prince Ivan III marries the niece of the last Byzantine emperor, adopts the double-headed eagle, faces down the Horde at a bloodless river standoff in 1480, and lets churchmen float the dizzying idea that fallen Constantinople has passed its crown to Moscow — the Third Rome, and there will be no fourth. His grandson Ivan IV is crowned the first tsar, conquers two Muslim khanates on the Volga, and then turns on his own people in a paranoid terror called the oprichnina that we are still arguing about. The dynasty burns itself out, and Russia very nearly dies in the Time of Troubles — famine, civil war, a parade of impostors all claiming to be the same dead prince, and a Polish garrison sitting in the Moscow Kremlin — until a butcher and a prince raise an army that throws the Poles out and a teenage boy named Michael Romanov is put on the throne. The early Romanovs rebuild, annex Ukraine, chain their peasants into hereditary serfdom, and split their own Church in two over the correct way to cross yourself. And then, at the very end, a seven-foot-tall young tsar named Peter, who learned to sail in a foreign quarter of Moscow and to build ships in a Dutch yard, drags the whole reluctant, bearded, holy edifice toward Europe — and at the close of a twenty-one-year war declares the Tsardom of Muscovy to be the Russian Empire. This is the book about how the Russia that the rest of the world would have to reckon with came to exist: autocratic, Orthodox, vast, suspicious of the West and hungry for it at the same time. Every later Russian state — the Empire, the Soviet Union, the one that exists now — is in some sense an argument with the Russia made in these years.

### A note on names and pronunciation

This is a story full of titles and terms that look forbidding and aren't, so settle them up front. The ruler of one of these principalities was a **knyaz** (k-NYAHZ), usually translated "prince" — not a king, just the hereditary lord of a city and its lands. The strongest of them held the title **grand prince** (*velikiy knyaz*), a first-among-the-princes who answered, for most of this book, to a foreign overlord. That overlord's title was **khan**, the Mongol word for supreme ruler, and his state on the lower Volga is the one this book calls the **Golden Horde** (the word *horde* comes from the Mongol *ordu*, a royal camp or court, and originally meant something grander and more organized than the English word's modern sense of "a disorganized mob"). The people of the Horde, and the Turkic-speaking Muslim peoples descended from them, get called **Tatars** (TAH-tarz) throughout, which is the name Russians used; it is not the same as "Mongol," exactly, but the two blur together in this period and the sources blur them too. Then comes the word the whole book is building toward: **tsar** (zar), which Russians began applying to their ruler in full force when Ivan IV took it in 1547. It is simply the Russian form of "Caesar," the Roman imperial title, carried east through Byzantium — calling yourself tsar was a claim to be an emperor, not merely a king, and that claim is half the plot. Below the ruler stood the **boyars** (BOH-yarz), the old hereditary high aristocracy, great landowning families who advised the prince, fought his wars, and resented every inch of power he took from them. A military governor of a town or province was a **voevoda** (voy-eh-VOH-dah), literally "war-leader." And the brutal personal terror-state Ivan IV carved out for himself was the **oprichnina** (oh-PREECH-nee-nah), from an old word for "apart" or "set aside" — land and men set apart for the tsar alone. One genuine tangle of names deserves flagging now: this book uses **Rus'** (roosh, with a soft final sound) for the older Kievan civilization and its people, and **Russia / Russian** for what grows out of Moscow, but the two words are the same root and the sources slide between them; the apostrophe in *Rus'* marks a soft consonant that English has no letter for. **Muscovy** is just the Latinized name outsiders gave the Moscow state, which is why this book is titled "Muscovite Russia." Spelling of Russian names in English is a minefield with no fully right answer — *Vasily* or *Basil*, *Aleksei* or *Alexis*, *Dmitri* or *Dmitry* — and this book picks the most familiar English form and sticks with it rather than chasing perfect transliteration. Dates are CE throughout. Miles come first, kilometers in parentheses.

### A note on "first" claims and on how we know any of this

As in every book in this project, "first" means "first surviving evidence of," and for Muscovite Russia the sources are a particular kind of problem, because almost all of them are working for somebody. The backbone is the **chronicles**, the *letopisi* (leh-TOH-pee-see), year-by-year records kept and recopied by monks — but a chronicle was usually compiled for a prince or a monastery, and it praised its patron, blackened his rivals, and quietly edited the past to make the present look inevitable. When a Moscow chronicle tells you Moscow was always destined to lead the Russian lands, that is the chronicle doing its job, not a neutral fact. A second major source is **hagiography**, the lives of saints — Sergius of Radonezh, Alexander Nevsky, the martyred Metropolitan Philip — written to edify the faithful, which means they freely mix the historical man with the miracle, and the reader has to keep asking which is which. A third is the **foreign envoys and travelers**, above all the Habsburg diplomat **Sigismund von Herberstein**, whose *Notes on Muscovite Affairs* (1549) is the first detailed European account of Russia and an indispensable outsider's eye — but an outsider's eye that didn't always understand what it saw and sometimes repeated court gossip as fact. All of this matters intensely for the two most dramatic figures in the book. The reputation of **Ivan IV** — "the Terrible," the child-traumatized paranoid who murdered his own son — comes to us heavily shaped by chronicles written under later regimes that had reasons to darken him, by Kurbsky's hostile letters from exile, and by foreigners retailing horror stories; the cruelty was real, but how much of the lurid detail is reliable and what his mental state actually was are open questions, and this book will flag where the colorful version may be running ahead of the evidence. The same caution applies to the **"Third Rome"** idea: a couple of lines in a monk's letter have been inflated by later writers into a grand state doctrine that supposedly drove Russian policy for centuries, and serious historians now argue it carried far less weight at the time than the legend claims. Where the sources are propaganda, where they are pious legend, and where they simply disagree, the text says so.

# Chapter 1 — The World the Mongols Broke

Most civilizations in this project begin with a founding: a first city rising on a river, a first king, a first temple. Russia begins with a funeral. When this story opens, in the winter of 1237, there is no Russia, and the thing that will eventually be called by that name is about to be born out of the wreckage of something else entirely. To understand Moscow, you first have to watch the world that came before Moscow die, because almost everything Russia would become for the next two and a half centuries was a response to a catastrophe that lasted, from first blow to final ruin, about five years.

Begin with the place that was about to fall, because if you do not know what was lost you cannot measure the loss. The civilization being smashed was **Kievan Rus'** (the word is pronounced "roosh," with a soft final sound that English has no single letter for, which is why it is written with an apostrophe). Rus' was the loose, brilliant, river-borne federation of principalities that had grown up across the forests and plains of what is now Ukraine, Belarus, and western Russia from the ninth century onward, with its mother-city at **Kiev** on the Dnieper River, and its full story is the subject of the previous book in this project. For the moment, only a few things about it need to be in your head. It was Christian, and specifically **Orthodox** Christian (the eastern branch of Christianity centered on Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire, as opposed to the western Catholic Christianity centered on Rome). It had taken that faith from Byzantium, the surviving eastern half of the old Roman Empire, around the year 988, and with the faith it had taken an alphabet, a high culture of stone churches and gold-ground icons, and a sense of itself as part of the wider Christian world. It was not a single kingdom but a sprawl of principalities, each ruled by a **knyaz** (pronounced "k-NYAHZ" and usually translated "prince," though he was not a king, just the hereditary lord of a city and its surrounding lands), all of them members of one enormous ruling clan descended from a semi-legendary Viking founder named Rurik. And by the early thirteenth century it was politically a mess: the princes feuded constantly, the senior title of grand prince passed around by a confusing rotation (a brother-to-brother system called lateral succession, explained when it nearly destroys Moscow in Chapter 5), and no single ruler commanded the whole. It was rich, cultured, fractured, and — though no one in it yet knew the word — about to meet the most effective military machine the medieval world ever produced.

That machine came out of the east, off the open grassland that runs in an almost unbroken belt from Mongolia to Hungary, the **Eurasian steppe** (a steppe is a vast, flat, treeless grassland, too dry for forest, perfect for horses and for the people who lived on horseback). The people riding out of it were the **Mongols**, and a word about who they were, because the reader is owed real scene-setting rather than a vague menace from the map's edge. A generation earlier, around 1206, a steppe warlord named **Temüjin** had united the scattered, feuding nomadic clans of Mongolia under one command and taken the title **Genghis Khan** (the word **khan** is the Mongol term for a supreme ruler, roughly "emperor of the steppe"). Under him and his sons the Mongols built, in a few astonishing decades, the largest contiguous land empire in human history, stretching eventually from Korea to the gates of Europe. Their army was almost entirely cavalry: lightly equipped horse-archers who could ride enormous distances, live off their herds, shoot accurately from the saddle of a galloping horse, and coordinate the movements of tens of thousands of riders across hundreds of miles with a discipline that settled armies could not match. They were not a disorganized mob of raiders. They were a state, with a chain of command, a postal relay system, siege engineers borrowed from conquered China, and a cold, deliberate policy toward cities that resisted: surrender and pay, or be annihilated as an example to the next city. The Rus' would learn that policy by being made the example.

One naming knot has to be cleared up right here, because it runs through the whole book. The Russians did not, on the whole, call these people Mongols. They called them **Tatars** (pronounced "TAH-tarz"), and this book will use that word throughout, because it is the word the Russian sources use. The two terms are not quite synonyms. "Mongol" properly means the steppe people from Mongolia itself; "Tatar" was originally the name of one particular tribe, which got applied loosely by outsiders to the whole confederation and especially to its largely Turkic-speaking, eventually Muslim, descendants. By the time the dust settled, the state that ruled Russia was Mongol at the top and Turkic in the mass of its population, and the sources blur the two together so thoroughly that this book will not pretend to a precision they do not have. When you read "Tatar" here, read "the Mongol-led steppe power and its successors," and do not imagine the modern people who today bear the name as a tidy continuation.

The storm broke in the winter of 1237. The Mongol commander was **Batu Khan**, a grandson of Genghis, leading the western wing of the empire's expansion. The Mongols generally preferred to campaign in winter, which seems backward until you understand the country: the Rus' lands are laced with rivers and rotted with swamp and bog, nearly impassable to a large army in the mud of spring or the wet of summer, but in deep winter the rivers and marshes freeze into smooth, hard roads that a cavalry army can ride straight down. The cold that should have protected the forest cities instead paved the way to them. The first major principality in the path was **Ryazan**, which refused to submit and was besieged, stormed, and destroyed; then the army turned on the great northeastern center.

That center was **Vladimir-Suzdal**, and its chief city, **Vladimir**, was the most important place in the northeastern Rus' lands, the seat of the grand prince and a city of stone cathedrals built in white limestone in the previous century. In February 1238 Batu's army stormed it and burned it. The grand prince, **Yuri II**, was not in the city; he had ridden north to gather an army, and the Mongols caught and destroyed that army, and Yuri with it, at the **Sit River**, a small tributary lost in the northern forests. The political head of the northeast was killed and his capital gutted in a single campaign season. Over the following months the Mongols methodically reduced city after city across the northeast, and only the spring thaw, turning the roads back to mud, spared the far north — the wealthy merchant republic of **Novgorod**, which we will meet properly later, was saved less by its walls than by the weather.

Then the Mongols withdrew east to rest and regroup, and for a year or two the surviving princes might have hoped it was over. It was not. In 1240 Batu came back, and this time the target was the heart, the mother-city itself. The Mongols took **Kiev** in December 1240 and gutted it. The destruction here is worth dwelling on because of one chilling piece of outside testimony. Six years later, in 1246, a Franciscan friar named **Giovanni da Pian del Carpine** — a papal envoy sent overland to the Mongol court to find out what this new power was and whether it could be negotiated with — passed through the ruins of Kiev on his way east, and he recorded that the ground was still littered with skulls and bones, the city shrunk to a couple of hundred houses where a great metropolis had stood. (He is the first western European to leave a detailed eyewitness account of the Mongols, and his report is one of the rare outside windows onto these years.) Kiev, the religious and cultural capital of the Rus' for two and a half centuries, the city whose conversion had made the whole civilization Christian, was finished as a center of power. The Kievan age was over. It had taken about five years.

Now sit with what was actually left, because the shape of the wreckage is the shape of everything that follows. The catastrophe did not depopulate the land or erase the Rus' as a people, but it shattered the political order completely and shifted the entire center of gravity of the surviving Rus' world. The bright, outward-facing, river-trading civilization centered on Kiev in the warm south was broken, and the people and the future drifted north and east, into a region that until now had been the backwoods: the **northeastern forest**, the densely wooded, cold, river-veined country around Vladimir, Suzdal, Rostov, Tver, and a then-utterly-obscure little town called **Moscow**. This is the geography that made Muscovite Russia what it became, so picture it concretely. It is not steppe and it is not the open Ukrainian plain. It is the southern edge of the immense **taiga**, the belt of conifer and birch forest that wraps the top of the northern hemisphere — dense woods broken by clearings, with long brutal winters, short growing seasons, thin soils, and a web of slow rivers (the upper Volga and its tributaries) for highways. It was poorer farmland than the rich black earth of the south, and it could not support the kind of dazzling urban culture Kiev had. But it had two things going for it that would matter enormously: the forest itself was a shelter, hard for steppe cavalry to operate in and easy to hide and disperse in, and the place was far from Sarai, the Mongol capital that would soon rise on the lower Volga, far enough that a clever, obedient prince could be useful to his overlords from a safe distance. The future of "Russia" would be made here, in the cold and the trees, by people whose great-grandparents had thought of this as the frontier.

And here is the thing that survived the wreck intact, the one continuous institution that bridged the old world and the new, and you must hold onto it because it is half of everything that comes later: the **Church**. The Orthodox Christian Church of the Rus' — its bishops, its monasteries, its head (a senior churchman called the **Metropolitan**, the chief bishop of all the Rus', who answered ultimately to the Patriarch of Constantinople far away in Byzantium) — was not destroyed by the conquest. Cities burned and princes died, but the Church as an institution endured, and it would soon turn out that the Mongols, for their own reasons we will come to, had no interest in destroying it and good reasons to protect it. In a world where every prince now answered to a foreign master and the political order had been smashed flat, the Church was the only structure that still spanned all the Rus' lands and still connected them to the wider Christian world. When people later asked what held "Russia" together through two centuries of foreign overlordship, the honest answer is: a shared language, a shared memory of Kiev, and above all a shared faith with an institution behind it. The Church is the thread that does not break.

Before going on, a word about how we know any of this, because the sources for Muscovite Russia are a particular kind of problem and it is best to be honest about them from the start. As in every book in this project, "first" here always means "first surviving evidence of," not necessarily the first time something actually happened. But the deeper issue is that almost every source we have is working for somebody. The backbone of the record is the **chronicles**, called in Russian the *letopisi* (pronounced "leh-TOH-pee-see"), year-by-year accounts compiled and recopied by monks. The trouble is that a chronicle was almost always written for a prince or a monastery, and it praised its patron, blackened his rivals, and quietly edited the past to make the present look inevitable. When a chronicle compiled in Moscow tells you that Moscow was always destined to lead the Russian lands, that is the chronicle doing its job, not a neutral fact, and a careful reader learns to ask, every time, *who paid for this page and what did they want it to say.* A second great source is **hagiography**, the lives of saints, written to edify the faithful, which means they freely braid the real historical man together with miracles and pious invention, and the reader has to keep separating the two. A third is the outsiders — foreign envoys and travelers like the friar Carpine, who saw real things but did not always understand what they were looking at. The catastrophe of these five years is one of the better-documented episodes precisely because so many parties had reason to record it; much of what comes after is murkier, and where the sources are propaganda, pious legend, or simply contradictory, this book will say so.

So that is the world the Mongols broke, and the world that the rest of this book grows out of. There is no Russia yet. There is a leaderless scatter of forest principalities, their old capital a field of bones, their princes about to learn that they will keep their thrones only by traveling to a foreign court and kneeling. The brilliant civilization is gone; what remains is colder, poorer, more inward, and clinging to its Church. Out of this unpromising rubble, one obscure swampy town will eventually claw its way to the top — not by heroism, at first, but by being the most useful servant the conqueror had. To understand how that was even possible, we first have to understand the conqueror's system, and what it actually meant, day to day, to live under the yoke. That is the next chapter.


# Chapter 2 — Living Under the Yoke

A conquered people can be ruled in many ways. The conqueror can move in and occupy the land directly, planting garrisons and governors in every town. He can drive the locals out and settle his own people on their fields. Or he can do what the Mongols did to the Rus': leave the locals in place, leave their own princes on their own thrones, and simply skim wealth and obedience off the top from a distance, through threat and through paperwork. The two and a half centuries that Russians later called the **Tatar yoke** (the standard name for the period of Mongol overlordship, from a word meaning the heavy wooden harness laid across the necks of oxen) were not, for most people most of the time, an occupation. They were a protection racket run on a continental scale, and learning to operate inside it — to pay, to flatter, to inform, to survive — is the school in which the future rulers of Russia got their education. This chapter is about how that system actually worked, and about the man whose career became the template for playing it.

First, the state on the other end of the leash. Around 1242, after the western campaigns wound down, Batu Khan settled the realm he had conquered into a permanent political unit on the lower **Volga River**, the huge river that drains the Russian heartland southward into the Caspian Sea. This state is the one this book calls the **Golden Horde**. The word **horde** trips modern readers, who hear "disorganized mob," so unlearn that: it comes from the Mongol *ordu*, meaning a royal camp or court, and originally signified something grander and far more organized than the English word now suggests — a mobile capital, a government on the move. (Where the "golden" comes from is genuinely uncertain; the most common explanation is that it referred to the color of the khan's great tent, but this is tradition more than documented fact.) The Horde was a Mongol-ruled, increasingly Turkic-speaking steppe empire, and over the following century its rulers and its people converted to Islam, which added a religious gulf to the political one between the Orthodox Christian Rus' and their Muslim overlords. Its capital was a real city, **Sarai**, on the lower Volga — a cosmopolitan trading metropolis of mosques, palaces, and markets that grew rich on the commerce of the steppe and the tribute of its subjects, and that has almost entirely vanished, which is its own comment on how the sources favor the settled and literate over the nomadic. The Rus' princes were now the tax-paying tributaries of this state, and they would remain so for the better part of two and a half centuries.

How did the racket actually run, mechanically? Three instruments did most of the work: the census, the tribute, and the patent. Take them in turn, because each one is a piece of the machine.

The census came first, because you cannot tax what you have not counted. In 1257 Mongol officials arrived in the Rus' lands to do something no Rus' ruler had ever done: count the population, household by household, head by head, in order to assess exactly how much each region owed. This was bureaucratic conquest, the Mongols applying to Russia the same administrative methods they had learned in China, and it was deeply hated — being counted meant being permanently fixed in the tax rolls, and resistance to the census touched off revolts, including a serious one in the northern republic of Novgorod. The galling part, and the part that tells you how the whole system worked, is that the Rus' princes were made to help *suppress* those revolts. A prince who wanted to keep his throne could not side with his own rebellious townspeople against the census; he had to help the Mongols crush them, because his authority now flowed downward from the khan, not upward from his people. That inversion is the heart of the yoke.

Out of the census came the **tribute**, the regular payment the Rus' lands owed to the Horde, known by the Russian word *dань* (transliterated *dan'*, pronounced roughly "dahn"). It was paid in silver and in goods, and in the early decades it was often collected by Mongol-appointed agents or by tax-farmers (private contractors who paid the khan a lump sum up front and then squeezed the population to make their money back, a system guaranteed to be brutal). Over time, the collection of the tribute would be handed over to the Rus' princes themselves, and — keep your eye on this, because it is the single most important fact in the rise of Moscow — whichever prince got the job of collecting everyone's tribute for the Horde was in a position to skim. Tax-collecting was not a humiliation to be endured; it was, for a clever man, an opportunity. We will watch one family turn it into an empire.

The third instrument was the **yarlyk** (pronounced "YAR-lik"), and it changed Russian politics permanently. A yarlyk was a patent, a written license to rule, issued by the khan. After the conquest, a Rus' prince could no longer simply inherit his throne and rule by right of birth; he was legitimate only if the khan granted him a yarlyk confirming him in his principality, and the most coveted yarlyk of all was the one naming a prince **grand prince** (in Russian *velikiy knyaz*, the senior prince, first among the others). This meant that the princes now had to *travel to Sarai* and compete for the khan's favor — to make the long, dangerous journey to a foreign court, bring lavish gifts, flatter Mongol officials, and outmaneuver their Rus' rivals who were doing exactly the same thing, all to secure a piece of parchment without which their rule was a usurpation. The competition was vicious and it was deadly: princes denounced each other to the khan, princes were executed at Sarai when a rival's intrigue prevailed, and the whole grubby business of currying favor at a foreign court became the central skill of Russian statecraft. A prince who could not play the game at Sarai did not keep his throne, however brave he was at home.

Which brings us to the man who played it best, and whose career fixed the pattern for everything Moscow would later do: **Alexander Nevsky** (pronounced "NYEFF-skee"). He is one of the most revered figures in Russian history, a saint of the Orthodox Church and a national hero, and his reputation rests on a genuine paradox that this chapter exists to explain. Alexander was prince of Novgorod, the great northern merchant city, in the 1240s, at the exact moment the Rus' were reeling from the Mongol catastrophe — and at that same moment a *second* threat appeared, from the opposite direction.

The threat from the west was the Catholic, German, and Scandinavian powers of the Baltic, who saw in the chaos of a shattered Orthodox Rus' an opportunity for conquest and for conversion. The Orthodox Rus' and the Catholic West had drifted into mutual hostility over the centuries (the great formal split between the Catholic and Orthodox churches dates to 1054), and to the crusading orders of the Baltic, the Orthodox were barely better than pagans, fair game for holy war. In 1240 — the very year the Mongols were finishing Kiev far to the south — a Swedish force landed at the mouth of the **Neva River**, near the future site of Saint Petersburg, and Alexander defeated it. This is the victory that earned him his surname, **Nevsky**, "of the Neva." Two years later, in 1242, he faced a more famous enemy: the **Teutonic Knights**, a German Catholic military religious order (a brotherhood of warrior-monks, sworn to celibacy and to holy war, who had carved out a crusading state along the Baltic). Alexander met them on the frozen surface of **Lake Peipus** (a large lake on the modern Estonia–Russia border) in April 1242, in a battle Russian tradition calls the **Battle on the Ice**, and routed them. The legend that the ice cracked and swallowed the armored knights is later embroidery, and the battle was probably smaller than the myth, but the outcome was real and it mattered: it checked the Catholic crusading push from the west and helped fix, for centuries, the religious frontier between the Orthodox Russian world and Catholic Europe. Alexander had, in two years, beaten both the Swedes and the Germans.

And then — this is the paradox — he turned around and *submitted to the Mongols.* Where he fought the Catholic West with the sword, he knelt to the Mongol East with gifts. From about 1252 Alexander pursued a deliberate policy of collaboration with the Horde: he traveled to Sarai, secured the yarlyk, accepted the grand-princely title from the khan's hand, paid the tribute, and even helped enforce the hated census against rebellious Russian towns, including his own Novgorod, to keep the Mongols from sending a punitive army. To modern eyes this can look like cowardice or treason, and Russians have argued about it ever since. But the logic was cold and probably correct: the Mongols were incomparably stronger than the Swedes or the Germans, resistance to them meant annihilation (the Rus' had just watched it happen), and they did not care what god you worshipped or how you ran your towns so long as the silver arrived and you stayed quiet. The Catholics, by contrast, wanted your soul as well as your land. Alexander made the bargain that the East could be appeased with money and obedience while the West had to be fought, and he chose to keep the Orthodox north intact under Mongol overlordship rather than gamble it on a hopeless war. His line — his descendants — would become the central dynasty of the next centuries, and the youngest of his sons would be handed an obscure little town called Moscow (Chapter 3). The template Alexander set was clear: survive by submitting to the strong, fight only the enemy you can beat, and never confuse heroism with wisdom. Moscow would follow that template to the throne of an empire.

There was one more pillar of the system, and it is the one that explains why the Church came through the yoke not merely intact but enriched. In 1267 the Horde issued the Orthodox Church a charter (a *yarlyk* of its own) exempting the Church's lands, monasteries, and clergy from tribute and from military service. The Mongols did this for reasons that were entirely practical, not pious: they were generally tolerant of the religions of their subjects (it cost nothing and bought goodwill), and they reckoned that a contented, privileged clergy praying for the khan's health was cheaper to keep than to suppress. But the consequence inside Russia was enormous. While princes and peasants were squeezed for silver year after year, the Church paid nothing — and so the Church grew rich. Monasteries accumulated land, peasants, and treasure; the Church became the wealthiest and most stable institution in the Russian lands precisely because it was the one exempt from the burden everyone else carried. This is the deep root of the alliance, which we will see flower in the next chapter, between the rising princes of Moscow and the head of the Russian Church. A tax exemption granted by a Muslim khan to keep his Christian subjects quiet ended up funding the institution that would help make Moscow great.

So how heavy was the yoke, really? This is a genuine and ongoing debate among historians, and it would be dishonest to give you the simple patriotic version. The traditional Russian account, shaped by later chroniclers and by nineteenth-century nationalist historians, painted the yoke as a uniform two-and-a-half-century horror that crushed the Rus', cut them off from Europe, and stamped a permanent streak of "Asiatic despotism" onto the Russian character — the idea that autocracy itself was a Mongol inheritance. More recent scholarship has pushed back hard on the lurid version. The actual Mongol presence in the Russian forest, after the initial conquest, was light: no large permanent garrisons, no settlement, mostly the tribute and the occasional punitive raid when payment lapsed or a town rebelled. For long stretches, an ordinary peasant in the northeast might go a whole life without seeing a Mongol. The economic drain of the tribute was real and the periodic raids were genuinely devastating, but the picture of relentless daily oppression is overdrawn, and the claim that the Mongols *invented* Russian autocracy is now treated with deep suspicion — Russian rulers had plenty of homegrown reasons to centralize power. What is not in doubt is the structural effect: the yoke isolated the northeast from the rest of Christendom, redirected the princes' energies toward the cynical court politics of Sarai, and rewarded exactly the qualities — calculation, collaboration, ruthlessness toward rivals, a talent for collecting other people's money — that one particular family was about to perfect. The severity is debated. The school it ran is not.

That family held a small, swampy, unremarkable town on a minor river, a place with no natural advantages and a reputation for nothing in particular. The next chapter is the story of how its princes turned the Horde's protection racket into their own ladder to the top — not by resisting the yoke, but by becoming the best collaborators it had.


# Chapter 3 — The Rise of the Moneybag

There is a kind of historical question that the chronicles are constitutionally unable to answer honestly, and the rise of Moscow is the purest example of it. *Why Moscow?* Why this town, out of a dozen roughly equal forest principalities, and not Tver or Ryazan or Suzdal or any of the others? The Moscow chronicles, written later and paid for by Moscow's rulers, give the pious answer: God chose it, the saints favored it, it was always destined to lead the Russian lands. That is propaganda, and you should distrust it on sight. The honest answer is far less flattering and far more interesting. Moscow rose because its princes were the most reliable, the most ruthless, and the most useful servants the Mongol khan had — because in a world run as a protection racket, the family that mastered the racket beat the families that tried to fight it or to stand on their dignity. This chapter is about how cynical collaboration, patient land-grabbing, and an alliance with the Church turned a backwater into the front-runner, and about the man whose nickname says it all: the Moneybag.

Start with how small Moscow really was. In the late thirteenth century Moscow was an **appanage** (an *appanage*, pronounced "AP-uh-nij," is a portion of territory handed out to a younger son of a ruling family to govern as his own — the standard way Rus' principalities subdivided among heirs). It was a minor town on the **Moskva River**, a tributary of a tributary, set in the forest with no great cathedral, no famous past, no obvious reason to matter. In 1283 it was given as his appanage to **Daniel** (in Russian Daniil), the youngest son of Alexander Nevsky, and being the youngest son of a prince was not a promising start — the best lands went to elder brothers, and a youngest son's appanage was a consolation prize. Daniel is reckoned the founder of the Moscow line, the first prince of the dynasty that would one day rule all of Russia, but nothing about his starting hand suggested it. What Daniel and his sons had instead of advantages was a strategy, and the strategy was acquisition. They began to expand the tiny principality by any means available — purchase, marriage, inheritance, conquest, and the manipulation of the khan's favor. Around 1300–1302 they grabbed the strategic towns of **Kolomna** (at the mouth of the Moskva, controlling the river's outlet to the Oka and the Volga system) and **Pereslavl-Zalessky**, the first steps in what would become Moscow's signature method over the following two centuries: the patient, relentless, opportunistic swallowing of neighbors that later Russian historians, with a certain grim admiration, called the **gathering of the Russian lands**. Moscow grew the way a snowball grows rolling downhill — slowly at first, then unstoppably, picking up everything it touched.

But Moscow did not grow into an empty field. It had a rival, and for the first half of the fourteenth century the central drama of Russian politics was the duel between Moscow and the principality of **Tver** (pronounced "tvair"). Tver was, on paper, the stronger of the two: it sat upstream on the Volga, it was older and richer, and in the early 1300s its princes more often than not held the grand-princely title. The two houses hated each other, and their feud was conducted in the characteristic style of the yoke — not primarily on the battlefield but at Sarai, where each prince raced to denounce the other to the khan, to outbid him in gifts and flattery, and to secure the all-important yarlyk for himself. (Recall from the last chapter that no prince could legitimately rule, and certainly could not hold the grand-princely title, without the khan's patent.) This was politics as a knife fight in a foreign throne room, and it was lethal: in 1318 the Moscow prince's intrigues at Sarai got the prince of Tver executed there; a few years later a Tver prince had his revenge by killing a Moscow prince at the Horde, and was executed in turn. The grand-princely title see-sawed between the two houses, paid for in flattery and blood at Sarai. Moscow did not win this contest because its cause was just. It won because one of its princes was better at the game than anyone alive.

That prince was **Ivan I**, who became prince of Moscow around 1325, and whose nickname is the most revealing in this entire book: **Kalita** (pronounced "kah-lee-TAH"), which means "Moneybag" or "Money-purse." He got the name, the tradition says, because he always carried a purse of coins to give to the poor — but the name has stuck for six hundred years because it captures something truer about how he operated. Ivan Kalita made himself, above all, the Horde's bagman. He persuaded the khan to let *him* collect the tribute not just from Moscow but from all the Russian princes, delivering the silver to Sarai on the Horde's behalf — and in that arrangement lay the whole secret of Moscow's rise. As the collector of everyone's tribute, Ivan controlled the flow of silver. He could skim it, he could time it, he could use the threat of reporting a prince as a defaulter to bend that prince to his will, and he could quietly convert the wealth that passed through his hands into land, loans, and leverage for Moscow. He bought up villages and whole estates; he extended his reach into other principalities through purchase and mortgage; he made himself simultaneously the khan's indispensable servant and the richest prince in the northeast. Collaboration was not a humiliation Ivan endured. It was the engine of his power, and he ran it shamelessly. This is the moment the book's argument comes fully into focus: in the world the Mongols made, the meek collaborator inherited the earth, and the brave resister got himself killed at Sarai.

The proof of that grim thesis is 1327, the year Moscow destroyed Tver, and it is one of the darkest and most clarifying episodes in the whole story. In that year the people of Tver rose up and killed a Mongol envoy and his retinue — a tax-collecting official of the khan's, whose presence and exactions they could no longer bear. It was a genuine popular revolt against the yoke, the kind of thing later patriots would want to admire. Ivan Kalita's response was to ride to Sarai, offer his services to the furious khan, and then *lead a Mongol army back to devastate Tver.* Moscow's prince personally helped the Horde burn his chief Russian rival, massacre or scatter its people, and break its power for a generation. It was an act of breathtaking collaboration — a Russian prince guiding the foreign overlord's punitive army against fellow Russians who had dared to resist — and it worked perfectly. The khan rewarded Ivan with the grand-princely title, which from this point essentially stayed in the Moscow line for good, and with the confirmed right to collect the tribute. Tver was crushed; Moscow was made. You can admire the result or be appalled by the means, but you cannot understand Moscow's rise without facing squarely what it was built on. The town that would one day call itself the protector of all Orthodox Christendom climbed its first decisive rung by serving as the Mongol khan's enforcer against its own.

Ivan Kalita's second great stroke had nothing to do with armies and everything to do with prestige, and it set up an alliance that would shape Russian history for centuries: he won the Church. Recall the **Metropolitan**, the chief bishop of all the Rus', the head of the Orthodox Church in the Russian lands. The Metropolitan had no fixed home of his own and his see — his official seat — had historically been at Kiev and then at Vladimir, the old centers. But around 1325 the reigning Metropolitan, **Peter**, chose to spend much of his time in Moscow, formed a close friendship with Ivan Kalita, and — crucially — was buried in Moscow when he died, in a stone cathedral Ivan had begun building to receive him. A Metropolitan's tomb made a city holy; it made Moscow a place of pilgrimage and a place the saints had chosen. Peter's successor formally fixed the metropolitanate at Moscow, and from roughly 1328 the spiritual capital of the Russian lands was the same town as its rising political capital. This was the marriage that defined Russian power: the prestige and wealth of the Church (remember, the Church was tax-exempt and therefore rich, while everyone else was being squeezed) joined to the ambition of Moscow's princes. The Church could lend Moscow legitimacy, sanctity, and the powerful argument that to oppose Moscow was to oppose God's chosen city; Moscow could lend the Church protection and patronage. Each made the other stronger, and the alliance of throne and altar in Moscow would outlast the Horde, the Rurikid dynasty, and the Tsardom itself.

The fourteenth century also brought catastrophe and renewal, and both fed Moscow's story. The catastrophe was the **Black Death**, the great plague pandemic that swept out of Asia and across Eurasia in the mid-1300s, killing perhaps a third or more of the population wherever it struck. It reached the Russian lands by about 1352, carried up the same trade routes that carried silk and silver, and it killed indiscriminately — peasants and princes alike. In 1353 it took the reigning grand prince, **Simeon the Proud** (Ivan Kalita's son), and Simeon's own sons with him, in a single sweep that nearly extinguished the senior Moscow line and threw the succession into the hands of a younger brother. The plague is a reminder that for all the politics, the deepest forces in this world were biological and beyond anyone's control; the same pandemic that was emptying the cities of Italy and the towns of England was thinning the Russian forest at the same moment, binding Russia into the one shared catastrophe of the fourteenth-century world even as the yoke cut it off from that world's commerce and ideas.

The renewal was spiritual, and its center is one of the most beloved figures in all of Russian history: **Sergius of Radonezh** (pronounced "RAH-doh-nezh"). Around 1345 Sergius, a devout young man from a displaced noble family, withdrew into the deep forest north of Moscow to live as a hermit, and the small wooden monastery that grew up around him became the **Trinity Monastery** (later the Trinity-Sergius Lavra, a *lavra* being a great senior monastery; today it is the spiritual heart of the Russian Church). Sergius is revered partly for his personal holiness — the hagiographies, which as always blend the historical man with the miraculous, paint him as humble, gentle, and unworldly, refusing high office and doing manual labor alongside his monks. But his historical importance is larger than his sainthood. Sergius sparked a great **monastic revival**: his disciples fanned out across the northern forests founding dozens of new monasteries, and those monasteries did something with deep consequences. They colonized the wilderness — clearing forest, farming, drawing peasant settlers around them — so that the spread of monasteries became, in effect, the spread of Russian settlement and Russian power northward and eastward into empty land. And because the whole movement was bound up with the Church-and-Moscow alliance, the monastic revival sanctified Moscow's cause: the holiest man in Russia and the network of monks who looked to him were, broadly, in Moscow's camp. When the moment came for Moscow to do something its collaborator-ancestors would never have dared — to stop paying the tribute and fight the Horde in the open field — it would be Sergius of Radonezh, according to the tradition, who blessed the prince before the battle. That prince was Ivan Kalita's grandson, and his name was Dmitry. The story of how the Moneybag's heir turned from collaboration to resistance, and what that change meant and did not mean, is the next chapter.


# Chapter 4 — Kulikovo and the Idea of Resistance

For four generations the princes of Moscow had risen by doing the opposite of resisting. Daniel acquired, Ivan Kalita collaborated, the whole line had prospered by paying the tribute on time, flattering the khan, collecting other princes' silver, and even leading Mongol armies against fellow Russians who dared to rebel (Chapter 3). The yoke was the water Moscow swam in, and Moscow had learned to swim better than anyone. So the most surprising thing about this chapter is that a prince of Moscow eventually stopped paying — and won a battle against a Mongol army that Russians would remember for six hundred years as the day the nation found its courage. The trick of the chapter is to hold two truths at once: that the victory was real and genuinely mattered, and that it mattered far more as a *story* than as a *fact*, because two years later the Horde burned Moscow to the ground and the tribute went right back to being paid. This is a chapter about how a battle can change what a people believe about themselves long before it changes anything on the map.

The prince was **Dmitry Ivanovich**, grandson of Ivan Kalita, who became grand prince of Moscow as a child in 1359 and grew up to be the most formidable Russian ruler the yoke had yet produced. Two things he did before the famous battle tell you that the balance of power was quietly shifting. The first was made of stone. In 1367 the young Dmitry replaced Moscow's old oak walls with ramparts of white **limestone** — the first stone fortress in the entire northeastern Russian lands, and the origin of one of Moscow's enduring poetic names, the "white-stone city." This was the first incarnation of the **Kremlin** (the word *kremlin* simply means the fortified citadel at the heart of a Russian town, its walled inner stronghold; every old Russian city had one, but *the* Kremlin means Moscow's). A stone Kremlin was not just defense; it was a statement that Moscow now had the wealth, the labor, and the confidence to build permanence, and it paid off almost at once. The second pressure on Dmitry came from the west, not the east: the **Grand Duchy of Lithuania**, a vast and rapidly expanding power (originally pagan, later Catholic) that had swallowed huge stretches of the old western Rus' lands — much of modern Ukraine and Belarus — and now rivaled Moscow as a claimant to the Russian inheritance. Its formidable ruler **Olgerd** (Algirdas) besieged Moscow more than once in the late 1360s, and the new white-stone walls held where wooden ones would have burned. Hold onto Lithuania: it will be Moscow's great western rival for centuries, the other answer to the question of who would gather the Rus' lands, and a reminder that "Russia" was not the only possible future for the people of Rus'.

What made open defiance of the Horde thinkable, though, was not Moscow's growing strength alone but the Horde's growing weakness. By the 1360s and 70s the Golden Horde — the Mongol-Turkic state on the lower Volga that had ruled the Russian lands since the conquest, with its capital at Sarai — had fallen into a long internal crisis the Russians called the *velikaya zamyatnya*, the "great troubles": a chaotic succession of coups and civil wars in which khan murdered khan and the central authority fragmented. Power fell into the hands of a strongman named **Mamai**, who was not himself of the royal blood of Genghis Khan and therefore could not legitimately become khan; he ruled through puppet khans, a usurper-general in all but name. A divided Horde led by a man of shaky legitimacy was a very different thing to defy than the unified war machine that had erased Kiev. Around 1375 Dmitry made his gamble: he stopped sending the tribute, or sent it short, and openly defied Mamai. For a prince of the Moneybag's line this was a revolution in policy — the bagman's grandson refusing to fill the bag.

The first test came in 1378 at the **Vozha River**, where Dmitry met and crushed a Mongol punitive force sent to bring him to heel. It was the first significant Russian victory over a Horde army in the open field, and it proved the unthinkable: a Russian army could beat the Tatars. But it was a skirmish next to what Mamai now prepared. Determined to break Moscow and restore the tribute, Mamai assembled a large coalition army and arranged for it to join up with allies — the king of Poland-Lithuania was to march from the west — to catch Dmitry between them. Dmitry's response was to move fast and fight Mamai before the allies could combine.

The battle they fought, on the 8th of September 1380, is the **Battle of Kulikovo** (pronounced "koo-lee-KOH-voh"), fought on **Kulikovo Field** ("Snipes' Field") near the upper reaches of the **Don River** in the open country south of Moscow — which is why Dmitry would forever after be called **Dmitry Donskoy**, "Dmitry of the Don." The traditional account, drawn from chronicles and from later epic tales written to glorify the event, is gorgeous and you have surely absorbed pieces of it without knowing the source: Dmitry visits Sergius of Radonezh (the great monastic saint of the previous chapter) and is blessed for the fight; two warrior-monks, Peresvet and Oslyabya, are sent from the monastery to fight in the ranks; the battle opens with a single combat between the monk Peresvet and a Tatar champion in which both die; the armies clash in a daylong slaughter; and at the decisive moment a hidden Russian reserve, the "ambush regiment," bursts from a wood and shatters Mamai's army, which breaks and flees. Dmitry wins. The Tatars are beaten on the open field, decisively, for the first time.

Now the honest part, because this is exactly the kind of episode where the colorful version runs far ahead of the evidence, and the writing rules of this project forbid letting it pass unflagged. The basic fact is solid: a battle was fought near the Don in 1380 and Dmitry's army defeated Mamai's. But almost every vivid detail is contested or comes from sources written long afterward with an obvious agenda. The size of the armies in the chronicles — hundreds of thousands on each side — is wildly inflated and impossible; the real numbers were a fraction of that, though we cannot pin them down. The famous single combat of the monk Peresvet, the dramatic ambush regiment, the precise choreography of the day — these come largely from later literary works, especially a poetic tale called the **Zadonshchina** (pronounced "zah-don-SHCHEE-nah"), composed around 1400, which was consciously modeled on the older epic of *The Tale of Igor's Campaign* (a celebrated, hauntingly beautiful Kievan-era poem about a doomed twelfth-century campaign against the steppe nomads, the most famous work of early East Slavic literature) and written to turn the battle into national myth. Even the exact site has been debated, and the archaeology of the field has been thinner than such a celebrated battle should leave. None of this means Kulikovo did not happen or did not matter. It means that what we *know* is "Dmitry beat Mamai near the Don in 1380," and what we have been *told* is an epic, and a careful reader keeps the two apart.

And here is the hard fact that punctures the triumph completely: Kulikovo did not end the yoke. It did not even seriously dent it. Mamai, already weakened, was soon afterward overthrown and killed by a rival — **Tokhtamysh**, a prince of the genuine Genghisid royal line who reunited the Horde under legitimate authority, the very thing Mamai had lacked. And in 1382, just two years after Kulikovo, Tokhtamysh marched on Moscow to reassert the Horde's mastery. Dmitry Donskoy, the hero of Kulikovo, was not even able to defend his capital; he left the city to raise an army elsewhere, and Tokhtamysh, unable to storm Moscow's white-stone walls by force, took it by treachery — promising the defenders mercy if they opened the gates, and then sacking the city and slaughtering thousands when they did. Moscow burned. The chronicles describe corpses choking the streets and the river. The tribute resumed; Dmitry, the conqueror of Mamai, went back to paying the khan and even sent his own son to Sarai as a hostage to guarantee his obedience. The yoke was emphatically not lifted. It would last another full century, until 1480 (Chapter 6). Anyone who tells you Kulikovo freed Russia is repeating the myth, not the history.

So if Kulikovo did not change the facts on the ground, why does it matter — why does this chapter exist at all? Because it changed the *story*, and stories are forces in history too. Before Kulikovo, the Russian self-image, insofar as the princes had one, was Ivan Kalita's: survive by serving, prosper by collaborating, never fight the master. After Kulikovo, Russians had a different thing to point to — a day when a Russian army, blessed by the holiest saint in the land and led by the prince of Moscow, met the Tatars in the open and beat them. It was proof that the yoke was not eternal and that Moscow, specifically, was the sword of Orthodox Russia against the infidel. The literary works that followed — the Zadonshchina and others — deliberately wove Kulikovo into a national legend of liberation, and it became, and remains, one of the founding myths of Russian identity, the moment the nation is said to have been born in battle. That the legend outran the reality by a century does not make it powerless; it makes it propaganda of the most effective kind, the kind people believe and act on. Moscow had discovered that it could be not just the Horde's tax-farmer but the champion of the faith, and that second role was worth more in the long run than the first.

The age also produced beauty, and it came, as so much did, from Byzantium and was made Russian. Around 1378 a Byzantine master painter known in Russia as **Theophanes the Greek** (Feofan Grek) arrived and painted the frescoes — wall paintings done on wet plaster — of churches in Novgorod and then Moscow, in the intense, dramatic, late-Byzantine style. He transplanted to Russia the living artistic tradition of the dying Byzantine Empire, and Russian painters took it and made it their own. The supreme example came a generation later: the monk **Andrei Rublev**, who had trained in Theophanes' orbit and who, around 1411, painted the **Trinity** icon (an *icon* is a sacred image, a painting of Christ, the Virgin, a saint, or a holy scene, venerated in Orthodox worship as a window onto the divine, not merely decoration). Rublev's *Trinity*, showing the three angels who visited the patriarch Abraham as a serene, weightless, infinitely gentle image of the Holy Trinity, is the acknowledged masterpiece of Russian medieval art and one of the supreme religious paintings of any tradition. It distilled a distinctly Russian style — quieter, more lyrical, more tender than its fierce Byzantine parent — out of the inherited Greek tradition, exactly as Kiswahili distilled an African language out of borrowed Arabic words, or as Russian architecture would soon distill its own forms from Italian engineers. The pattern recurs across this whole project: a younger culture takes the gift of an older one and remakes it into something unmistakably its own. That Russia could produce a Rublev in the same generation that it was being sacked by Tokhtamysh tells you the civilization the Mongols broke was, underneath the yoke, very much alive.

The chapter closes on the same hard-won balance it opened with. The Horde was weakening — within fifteen years of Kulikovo, the Central Asian world-conqueror **Timur** (also called Tamerlane, the lame warlord whose own story comes in the next chapter) would smash Tokhtamysh and devastate the Horde's cities, doing far more damage to Moscow's overlord than any Russian army had — but it was not finished, and as late as 1408 a Horde commander named **Edigu** could still ravage the Moscow lands and besiege the city to squeeze out the tribute. Dmitry Donskoy's son **Vasily I** continued the family business, buying a yarlyk from the khan to annex the principality of Nizhny Novgorod, gathering the Russian lands by purchase and patent in the old patient way even as the new language of holy resistance entered the national bloodstream. Both things were true at once: Moscow still bowed to Sarai, and Moscow had begun to imagine not bowing. The idea of resistance had been born at Kulikovo. Turning the idea into a fact would take another hundred years, a brutal civil war within Moscow's own ruling house, and a prince who would simply walk away from the Horde at a river and refuse to fight. That is where the story goes next.


# Chapter 5 — The Gathering of the Lands

The previous chapter ended on a paradox. Dmitry Donskoy (the grand prince of Moscow who beat a Mongol army at the Battle of Kulikovo in 1380 and became Russia's great hero of resistance) had won the most celebrated victory in early Russian history, and yet just two years later the khan Tokhtamysh had ridden into Moscow, burned it, and butchered its people. The lesson Moscow took from that decade was not "we can defeat the Horde" but something quieter and far more useful: that the **Golden Horde** (the Mongol-Turkic state on the lower Volga that had ruled the Russian princes as tribute-paying subjects since the 1240s) was no longer the single, unanswerable thing it had been. It could be beaten in the field. It could also still burn your capital. The right move, for a prince with patience, was neither open defiance nor abject submission but something in between: keep paying when you must, stop paying when you can, and above all keep gathering. Gather towns, gather rivals, gather the Church, gather the crown of true Christianity itself. This chapter is about a century of that gathering, and about the ugly civil war Moscow nearly destroyed itself with in the middle of it.

Start with the man who inherited Dmitry's throne. **Vasily I** (vah-SEE-lee), Dmitry Donskoy's son, ruled Moscow from 1389 to 1425, and his reign is the perfect illustration of how Moscow actually grew — not by heroic battle but by quiet acquisition, often paid for in cash. In 1392 Vasily simply *bought* the principality of **Nizhny Novgorod** (NIZH-nee NOV-guh-rud), a sizable rival state on the Volga, by purchasing a **yarlyk** for it from the khan. (A yarlyk, introduced in earlier chapters, was the Mongol patent of authority: a document from the khan granting a prince the legal right to rule a given territory. The whole point of the Horde's system was that no Russian prince ruled by his own right; he ruled because the khan said so, which meant the khan could be lobbied, bribed, and played.) So Vasily went to the Horde, outbid the local dynasty, came home with a piece of paper, and a rival principality became Moscow's. This is the land-gathering in its purest, least romantic form: a real-estate transaction backed by a foreign emperor's stamp.

What made such patient swallowing possible was a stroke of luck that came from the steppe, far outside anything Moscow had done. In 1395 the Central Asian conqueror **Timur** (tee-MOOR), known in the West as Tamerlane, the lame warlord whose empire stretched from India to Anatolia and whose campaigns were among the bloodiest of the age, fell on the Golden Horde and wrecked it. He smashed the army of the khan Tokhtamysh (the same Tokhtamysh who had burned Moscow in 1382), sacked the Horde's great trading cities on the Volga, and left its economy and its central authority in ruins. This mattered more to Moscow's future than Kulikovo had. Kulikovo was a battle Russians won and then mostly paid for; Timur's wrecking of the Horde was a wound inflicted from outside that the Horde never fully recovered from. Over the following decades the once-unified Horde began to splinter into smaller successor khanates — Kazan, Astrakhan, Crimea, the Great Horde — each a fragment of the old terror. The yoke was not lifted. But the thing holding the yoke was cracking, and Moscow was patient enough to wait it out.

It is worth pausing to be honest about how slow and uneven this was, because it is easy, looking back, to draw a smooth rising line from Kulikovo to independence. There was no smooth line. As late as 1408 the Horde commander **Edigu** (EH-dee-goo) led a fresh army into the Moscow lands, ravaged the countryside, and besieged the city to force tribute back into the pipeline. He failed to take the Kremlin but did enough damage to remind everyone that the steppe still had teeth. So the picture across Vasily I's reign is not a triumphant march toward freedom but a long, ambiguous in-between: a weakening overlord, an opportunistic and acquisitive Moscow, and recurring violent reminders that nobody was free yet.

Now the part of this century that nearly undid everything. When Vasily I died in 1425, Moscow had a problem it had never properly solved: who, exactly, was supposed to inherit the throne. The old Russian custom, inherited from the Kievan world (the brilliant river-civilization the previous book was about, smashed by the Mongols), was a system historians call **lateral** or **collateral succession** — the throne passed not from father to son but along a generation, brother to brother, and only then dropped to the next generation. The idea was that the senior surviving male of the ruling clan, the eldest uncle, had the better claim over a dead ruler's young son. This had a certain logic in a dangerous world (better an experienced grown man than a child), but it was a recipe for endless disputes, because at any given moment a reigning prince's brother and his son could each point to a different rule and each be, by some reading, correct.

That is exactly what blew up in 1425. Vasily I left the throne to his ten-year-old son, **Vasily II**. But Vasily I's brother, **Yuri of Zvenigorod**, claimed the throne for himself under the old lateral rule — he was the senior surviving male of his generation, and by the custom of the steppe and of old Rus' that gave him the better right. What followed was the **Muscovite War of Succession**, a vicious on-and-off civil war that ran for roughly twenty years and was fought, in essence, over which inheritance rule Moscow would use. It pitted Vasily II against his uncle Yuri and then, after Yuri's death, against Yuri's sons, Vasily Kosoy ("the Cross-Eyed") and Dmitry Shemyaka. It was a war of betrayals, captures, blindings, and revenge blindings, and it is one of the darkest and least heroic episodes in the whole book.

The blinding needs explaining, because it is central and it is grim. **Blinding** — deliberately destroying a rival's eyes — was a standard piece of medieval political surgery, used across the Orthodox world and especially in Byzantium (the Greek Christian empire centered on Constantinople, the source of Russia's religion and much of its political imagination). The logic was that a blinded man was disqualified from rule and from leading armies but did not have to be killed, which avoided the sin and the blood-feud of murdering a kinsman. It was mutilation as a substitute for execution. In this war it was used both ways. Vasily II's faction blinded his cousin Vasily Kosoy — which is how Kosoy got his nickname. Then, in 1446, the other side took its revenge: Dmitry Shemyaka's men captured Vasily II and put out *his* eyes. From that point on the grand prince of Moscow was **Vasily II "the Blind"** (in Russian, *Tyomny*, "the Dark").

Here is the remarkable thing, and the reason this squalid war earns a whole chapter's attention. Vasily the Blind won anyway. A blinded man, who could not see a map or a battlefield, clawed his way back from captivity, rebuilt his faction, and by the end of the 1440s had defeated and destroyed his rivals. (Shemyaka, the man who had blinded him, was eventually poisoned in exile, by tradition on Vasily's orders.) And in winning, Vasily II settled the question the whole war had been about. From his reign forward, the throne of Moscow passed **father to son**, in a direct line, every time — what historians call **primogeniture**, the rule that the ruler's eldest son inherits. This is one of the most important and least glamorous facts in Russian history. The thing that made Moscow's monarchy uniquely stable, generation after generation, while rivals tore themselves apart in succession disputes, was the bloody, ugly resolution of this civil war in favor of one simple rule. Vasily lost his eyes and won the principle that would carry his line to the conquest of all Russia. When his son Ivan III takes the throne in 1462 — the giant of the next chapter — he takes it without a fight, because his blind father had bought that certainty with his eyes.

While the dynasty was busy maiming itself, something far larger was happening to the Russian Church, and it would change how Russia thought about itself forever. To understand it, you have to understand where the Russian Church stood in the Christian world. Russia had been Orthodox Christian since the conversion of Kievan Rus' around 988, and **Orthodoxy** (the Eastern, Greek branch of Christianity, split from the Roman Catholic West in the schism of 1054 over questions of doctrine and the authority of the pope) looked for leadership not to Rome but to Constantinople. The head of the Russian Church, the **metropolitan** (the senior bishop, ranking just below a patriarch), was traditionally appointed by, or at least confirmed by, the **patriarch of Constantinople** — the senior churchman of the Orthodox world, based in the Byzantine capital. Russia, in other words, was a spiritual province of a Greek mother-church. It did not pick its own chief bishop; Constantinople did.

In the 1430s that arrangement collided with a crisis. Byzantium was dying. The Ottoman Turks (the rising Muslim empire based in Anatolia that had been swallowing Byzantine territory for a century) had reduced the once-vast empire to little more than the walls of Constantinople itself, and the Byzantine emperor was desperate for military help from the Catholic West. The price the West demanded was enormous: the reunion of the two churches, Orthodox and Catholic, under the authority of the pope. At the **Council of Florence** in 1439 — a great gathering of Western and Eastern churchmen in Italy — the Byzantine delegation, with the emperor's backing and out of sheer desperation, agreed to it. They signed a union accepting papal supremacy and Catholic doctrine, in the hope that Western armies would come and save Constantinople.

Among the men who signed at Florence was **Isidore**, the Greek who was then metropolitan of Moscow — Constantinople's appointed head of the Russian Church. He came home to Moscow in 1441 as a cardinal of the Roman Church, proclaimed the union, and prayed for the pope in the Kremlin's cathedral. Moscow's reaction was fury. Grand Prince Vasily II (yes, in the middle of his civil war) had Isidore arrested as a heretic; Isidore fled. To the Russians, the union was a betrayal: the Greeks had sold the true faith to the Latins out of fear, and a church that would do that had forfeited its authority. This is the seed of one of the most important ideas in this book, so hold onto it: in Russian eyes, after 1439, *Constantinople had abandoned Orthodoxy, and Moscow had not.*

The logic of that worked itself out fast. In 1448, with the legitimate line of Constantinople-approved metropolitans broken by the Florentine scandal, the Russian bishops simply did something they had never done: they elected their own metropolitan, **Jonah**, on their own authority, without asking Constantinople at all. This was the moment the Russian Church became, in effect, **autocephalous** (aw-toh-SEF-uh-lus) — a Greek word meaning "self-headed," the technical term in Orthodoxy for a national church that governs itself and appoints its own leaders rather than answering to a foreign patriarch. The Russian Church had, for the first time, no master abroad.

And then, five years later, came the event that seemed to confirm the whole thing was God's plan. In **1453 the Ottoman Turks took Constantinople.** After a thousand years, the Byzantine Empire — the eastern half of the Roman Empire, the mother of Orthodox Christianity, the city Russians simply called *Tsargrad*, "the city of the Caesar" — fell to the cannon of the Ottoman sultan, and the last Byzantine emperor died fighting in the breach. To Moscow, the meaning was electric and obvious. Constantinople had betrayed the true faith at Florence in 1439, and within fourteen years God had let it fall to the infidel as punishment. The Greeks had sold out; the Greeks had perished. And Moscow — which had refused the union, which had elected its own metropolitan, which still stood, free and Orthodox — was now, as far as anyone could see, the *only* major independent Orthodox Christian power left on earth. Every other center of Orthodoxy was under Muslim rule. The unbearable, intoxicating conclusion almost wrote itself, and within a couple of generations a monk would write it down: if Constantinople was the second Rome, and the second Rome had fallen, then perhaps Moscow was the third. We will examine that idea carefully, and skeptically, in the next chapter, because later ages would build far more on those few lines than the lines could bear. But you can already feel the temperature of it rising here in the 1450s.

For all the grandeur of these ideas, it is worth remembering what else this century produced, because it shows the inward, contemplative side of a culture that the politics make look only brutal. It was in these same decades that **Andrei Rublev** (introduced in the previous chapter) painted his serene *Trinity* icon, the summit of all Russian medieval art — a circle of impossible tenderness and calm, set down at the very moment the dynasty was putting out its own princes' eyes in a war over inheritance rules. That contrast is worth sitting with. The Russia of this century could blind a cousin and, in the same lifetime, produce one of the most peaceful images in the history of human art. Both are the real Russia. Hold them together, because the next two centuries will keep forcing you to.

So the gathering century ends with Moscow transformed in ways no battle could have managed. It had a stable throne with a settled rule of succession, bought at the price of a grand prince's eyes. It had a self-governing Church that answered to no foreign patriarch. It had swallowed rivals like Nizhny Novgorod by purchase and patience. And it had begun to believe, with a conviction that would outlast everything else in this book, that it was the last guardian of true Christianity on earth. The man who would turn all of that potential into an actual state — who would triple Moscow's size, marry the heiress of fallen Byzantium, and walk away from the Horde forever — was waiting in the wings. His name was Ivan, and he would be called the Great.


# Chapter 6 — Ivan the Great and the Third Rome

When Vasily II "the Blind" — the grand prince who lost his eyes and won the war that fixed father-to-son succession (Chapter 5) — died in 1462, the throne passed without a fight to his son, exactly as the bloody settlement of the civil war had been designed to ensure. The son was **Ivan III** (ee-VAHN), and over a reign of forty-three years he did more to create Russia than any ruler before him and arguably any after. He came to power as the grand prince of a principality that was the strongest of the northeastern Russian states but was still, technically, a tribute-paying subject of a Mongol khan. He died as the sovereign of a unified Russian state three times the size, recognized abroad, decorated with the symbols of a fallen empire, and answerable to no one. Historians call him Ivan the Great, and the title is earned. This chapter is about how a principality became a state, and about the most famous and most overblown idea in Russian history — that Moscow was the Third Rome.

The first thing Ivan did was finish the eating. He absorbed the remaining independent Russian principalities one after another, but the prize, and the one that shows what kind of ruler he was, was **Novgorod** (NOV-guh-rud). To understand why its fall mattered so much, you have to understand what Novgorod *was*, because it was the great exception to everything else in this book. While every other Russian state was a princedom — a city ruled by a hereditary prince — Novgorod, a vast and rich trading city in the northwest, was a **republic**. For centuries it had governed itself through an assembly of its citizens called the **veche** (VYEH-cheh), a town meeting of the free male householders that made the city's major decisions, and it hired and fired its princes like contractors, keeping them on a tight leash as military commanders rather than masters. Novgorod was fabulously wealthy — it controlled a fur-trading empire stretching toward the Arctic and the Urals and was a major partner of the **Hanseatic League** (the powerful confederation of northern German trading towns that dominated commerce across the Baltic). It was Russia's window on Europe, its richest city, and its only real tradition of self-government. It was also, by the 1470s, leaning toward putting itself under the protection of Catholic Lithuania to escape Moscow's growing shadow.

That was intolerable to Ivan on two counts: a rival Russian power slipping into the hands of a Catholic state, and a republic of free citizens that mocked the whole idea of a single God-given prince. He moved in two campaigns, and in **1478 he crushed Novgorod and annexed it outright**, abolishing its independence forever. And then he did the thing that tells you who he was. He had the **veche bell** — the great bell whose ringing summoned the citizens to their assembly, the literal voice and symbol of Novgorod's self-government — taken down and carted away to Moscow. Russia would not have another genuine experiment in republican self-rule for a very long time. Ivan did not merely conquer a city; he silenced an idea. The bell going south to Moscow is one of the quiet hinge-moments of Russian history: the point at which the centralized autocracy decisively beat the alternative, and the alternative's voice was physically removed and hung where it could be controlled.

Now the marriage that gave Ivan's growing power its grand new dress. In **1472 Ivan III married Sophia Palaiologina** (pal-eye-oh-LOG-ih-nah), and you cannot overstate who she was. Her family name, Palaiologos, was the name of the *last imperial dynasty of Byzantium* — she was the niece of Constantine XI, the very emperor who had died defending the walls of Constantinople when it fell in 1453 (Chapter 5). When that empire collapsed, its surviving princes scattered into exile in the West, and Sophia had been raised in Rome under the care of the pope, who arranged the marriage partly in the hope (a vain one, as it turned out) of pulling Russia toward Catholicism. For Moscow the meaning ran the other way entirely. By marrying the heiress of the Byzantine line, Ivan was not joining the West; he was claiming an inheritance. He was the husband of the last Roman emperor's niece, which meant — by the dizzying logic the Russians were already warming to — that the imperial dignity of fallen Byzantium had, in some sense, come to Moscow in her dowry.

The symbols followed at once. Byzantine court ceremony, far more elaborate and exalted than anything Moscow had known, began to creep into the Kremlin, surrounding the ruler with a sacred remoteness. And the **double-headed eagle**, the emblem of the Byzantine emperors — an eagle with two heads facing east and west, signifying an empire that ruled over both — began to appear on Ivan's seals (it is documented on a state seal of 1497) and would become the coat of arms of the Russian state, which it remains today. When you see the double-headed eagle on a Russian passport or a Kremlin gate now, you are looking at a claim Ivan III made in the 1470s: that Moscow is the heir of Rome through Constantinople. How much Sophia herself drove all this is debated — older histories made her the mastermind importing Byzantine grandeur, but some of that is later embroidery, and the evidence for her personal influence is thinner than the legend. What is not in doubt is that the symbols arrived in her wake.

And then, in **1480**, came the strangest and most anticlimactic turning point in this whole book: the end of more than two centuries of Mongol overlordship, accomplished without a battle. Ivan had stopped paying tribute to the **Great Horde** (the rump successor of the old Golden Horde, by now just one of several quarreling Mongol-Tatar states). Its khan, **Akhmat** (AHK-maht), determined to force the payments and the submission back into place, marched north with an army. The two forces met on opposite banks of the **Ugra River** (OO-grah), a tributary southwest of Moscow. And then — nothing. For weeks in the autumn of 1480 the two armies sat glaring at each other across the water, each waiting for the other to attempt the dangerous business of crossing a river in the teeth of the enemy. Skirmishes flared and fizzled. Akhmat waited for a Lithuanian ally who never came. The weather turned; the river began to freeze, which paradoxically made both sides more nervous, because soon there would be no river barrier at all. And then both armies simply turned around and went home. Akhmat withdrew to the steppe, where he was killed by a rival the following year, and the Great Horde disintegrated soon after.

This bloodless staring match, the **Great Stand on the Ugra River**, is conventionally taken as the end of the "Tatar yoke" — the moment Russia ceased to be a subject of the Mongol khans. It is worth being honest about how undramatic and how gradual this really was, because Russian tradition later inflated the Ugra into a glorious deliverance, and it was not that. Nobody really fought. Ivan had already effectively stopped obeying the Horde for years; the Stand on the Ugra ratified a freedom that had been quietly accumulating rather than winning it in a clean stroke. And the Tatar threat did not vanish — the Crimean Khanate would burn Moscow itself less than a century later (Chapter 8). But as a symbolic line in the sand, 1480 is the right place to mark it: after the Ugra, no Russian ruler ever again traveled to a khan's court to beg a yarlyk, and the long humiliation that opened this book was over. The most consequential event was the one where nobody charged.

A new kind of state needs a new kind of capital, and Ivan rebuilt Moscow to look the part. The old Kremlin (the fortified citadel at the heart of Moscow — the word simply means "fortress" or "citadel") had walls of white limestone dating from the previous century, and they were crumbling. Ivan imported the best engineers he could find, and the best were Italian, because Renaissance Italy then led Europe in fortification and architecture. The Bolognese engineer **Aristotele Fioravanti** (fee-or-uh-VAHN-tee) built the **Dormition Cathedral** (finished 1479), the great church in which every later tsar would be crowned — and Ivan made Fioravanti study the old churches of Vladimir first, so that the cathedral fused Italian engineering with the traditional form of Russia's own sacred architecture. Italian masters rebuilt the Kremlin's walls and towers in red brick from 1485 onward — those red walls and swallowtail battlements, which look so quintessentially Russian, were in fact built by Italians and resemble the fortress walls of Milan and Verona, because that is where the builders learned their trade. And in 1491 they completed the **Faceted Chamber**, the grand stone throne hall named for the diamond-cut stone facets of its facade, where the rulers of Russia would receive ambassadors and hold their most solemn audiences for centuries. Ivan was building a stage for an emperor.

He also gave the enlarged state its first unified law. In **1497 Ivan issued the Sudebnik** (soo-DYEB-nik), the first law code of the centralized Russian state — a single set of rules for courts and officials across all the lands Moscow now ruled, replacing the patchwork of local custom. Tucked inside it was a provision that looks small and turned out to be poisonous. It declared that a peasant could leave his landlord only during a two-week window around **St. George's Day** in the autumn, after the harvest was in, and only after settling his debts. At the time this was framed as a regulation, even a protection of a peasant's right to move at all. But it was the first crack in the door, the first legal restriction on the freedom of the Russian peasant to come and go, and over the next century and a half that crack would be widened, deliberately and step by step, until it slammed shut into full hereditary **serfdom** (the condition of peasants legally bound to the land they work and to the lord who owns it, unable to leave — closer to slavery than to medieval Western feudalism). We will watch that door close in later chapters. Ivan only opened it a finger's width, but he opened it.

Now, finally, the famous idea, and a promise to handle it honestly. Around 1510 — so technically in the reign of Ivan's son, Vasily III, but the idea belongs to the world Ivan made — a monk named **Philotheus of Pskov** (fih-loh-THEE-us; Pskov is a city in the northwest) wrote a letter to the grand prince that contained a single resonant sentence. Two Romes had fallen, he wrote: the first Rome (the original, which had lapsed into Catholic heresy in Russian eyes) and the second Rome (Constantinople, which had betrayed Orthodoxy at Florence and then fallen to the Turks). Moscow was the **third Rome**. *And a fourth there will never be* — the standard rendering of his much-paraphrased line. It is a thrilling line. It says that Moscow is the final guardian of true Christianity, the last Rome, history's terminus, with no successor possible. You can see why it has been quoted endlessly and treated as the master key to Russian history — the secret doctrine of imperial messianism that supposedly drove the tsars to conquer, the Soviets to dominate, and Russia ever after to see itself as a chosen civilization with a world-historical mission.

Now the cold water. Modern historians are quite skeptical of all that. Philotheus's "Third Rome" was a couple of lines in a monk's letter, written to urge the grand prince to be a pious protector of the Church and to stamp out heresy and corruption — it was a sermon about religious duty, not a blueprint for empire. There is little evidence that it functioned as an official state doctrine in its own time, that Ivan III or Vasily III based policy on it, or that anyone treated it as a national mission for centuries afterward. It was rediscovered and inflated by writers much later, especially in the 1800s and 1900s, who were looking for a deep explanation of Russia's imperial ambitions and found a quotable medieval slogan to hang it on. So treat the Third Rome the way the framing of this book asked you to treat all the propaganda: as a real idea that captured something genuine about how Moscow saw itself after 1453 — proud, Orthodox, singular, the last one standing — but *not* as a secret governing ideology that explains five hundred years of Russian behavior. The mood was real. The doctrine is mostly a legend that grew in the retelling, which makes it a perfect closing note for a chapter about a ruler who was genuinely great and whose greatness later ages found irresistible to exaggerate.

Ivan III died in 1505, leaving his son Vasily III to annex the last few holdout states — Pskov, Smolensk, Ryazan — and finish the gathering of the Great Russian lands. But the essential work was done. There was now a Russia: unified, sovereign, Orthodox, ruled from a Kremlin built to imperial scale by Italian hands, flying the eagle of dead Byzantium, free of the Horde, and quietly beginning to chain its peasants to the soil. Everything was in place except the title. That would come with Ivan's grandson, a three-year-old boy at his accession, who would grow up to be crowned the first tsar of Russia — and then to become the most terrifying figure in this entire book.


# Chapter 7 — The First Tsar

The Russia that Ivan the Great (Chapter 6) built passed, on his death in 1505, to his son **Vasily III** (vah-SEE-lee), who spent his reign tidying up. He annexed the last independent Russian states — the old republic of Pskov in 1510, the great fortress-city of **Smolensk** taken from Catholic Lithuania in 1514, and the principality of Ryazan — so that by the 1520s the gathering of the Great Russian lands that had begun under Ivan Kalita two centuries before was, at last, essentially complete. Moscow ruled them all. Vasily was a competent, hard, unspectacular ruler, and the most important thing he did was something he could not control: he died, in 1533, leaving as his heir a three-year-old boy. That boy was **Ivan IV**, and his reign would open like a coronation hymn and end like a horror story. This chapter is the hymn. The next is the horror.

The horror has roots in the hymn, though, and they grow out of that childhood. A three-year-old cannot rule, so power fell to a **regency** — a council governing in the child-ruler's name until he came of age. Ivan's mother, Elena Glinskaya, ran it briefly and then died in 1538, possibly poisoned (the sources suspect it but cannot prove it). After that the great **boyar** families — the old hereditary high aristocracy, the great landowning clans who had always resented the prince's growing power and now saw their chance — fell on the regency like dogs on a carcass. They fought, plotted, murdered, and looted in the boy-ruler's name, treating the little grand prince by turns as a puppet to be flattered and a nuisance to be ignored. The chronicles, written later, paint a picture of a frightened, neglected child watching armed men brawl over his throne, sometimes bursting into his bedchamber, and they claim this brutalized boyhood shaped the paranoid, vengeful man he became. That may well be true. But it is also exactly the kind of psychological origin story that later chroniclers loved to construct, working backward from the monster to invent the wound that made him, so hold it lightly: the chaotic regency is a fact, the trauma-explanation-for-the-terror is a plausible but unprovable story.

When Ivan came of age, he did something none of his ancestors had dared. In **1547, at sixteen, he was crowned not grand prince but Tsar of All Rus'** — the first Russian ruler to take the title formally and permanently. The word, introduced in this book's opening note, is worth restating here because the coronation is the moment it becomes real. **Tsar** (zar) is the Russian form of "Caesar," the title of the Roman emperors, carried east through Byzantium. To call yourself grand prince was to be the greatest of the Russian princes; to call yourself tsar was to claim to be an *emperor*, a sovereign of the same rank as the Caesars of Rome and Constantinople, answerable to God alone. It was the logical capstone of everything Ivan III had assembled — the Byzantine marriage, the double-headed eagle, the imperial Kremlin, the swagger of the "Third Rome." Ivan IV's coronation in the Dormition Cathedral, performed by the metropolitan with all the borrowed weight of Byzantine ceremony, turned the implied imperial claim into an official, sacred title. From this point on the ruler of Russia is the tsar, and the state will eventually be called the **Tsardom of Russia**.

The young tsar's first year was nearly his last. In 1547 a catastrophic fire swept Moscow — the city was overwhelmingly built of wood, and fire was its recurring nightmare — and a popular revolt followed, with a mob killing one of the tsar's relatives whom they blamed for the disaster. The chronicles say the trauma sobered the teenage Ivan and turned him toward serious reform. Whether or not the fire was really the cause, reform is what came, and for about a decade Ivan IV looked like one of the most promising monarchs in Europe.

He governed, in these good years, with the help of a small circle of trusted advisers — a priest named Sylvester, a low-born official named Alexei Adashev, and the metropolitan Macarius among them — a group that a later writer dubbed the **Chosen Council** (in Russian, *Izbrannaya Rada*). Under their guidance the reforms came fast. In **1549 Ivan summoned the first Zemsky Sobor** (ZEM-skee suh-BOR), an "assembly of the land" — a consultative body drawing together clergy, nobles, and representatives of the towns to advise the tsar and lend his decisions the weight of national consent. It was not a parliament; it had no power to compel the tsar and met only when summoned. But it was a genuine attempt to broaden the base of the state, and it would matter enormously sixty years later, when the dynasty itself collapsed and the Zemsky Sobor turned out to be the one institution capable of choosing a new tsar from the wreckage (Chapter 10).

In **1550 Ivan issued a new Sudebnik**, a revised law code that tightened central administration, curbed the judicial power of the great nobles, standardized taxes, and — continuing the long, ominous trend begun in Ivan III's code of 1497 — further restricted the peasants' right to move, turning the screw one more notch toward serfdom. In **1551 a great church council, the Stoglav** (STOH-glahf, "the Hundred Chapters," after the hundred rulings it produced), codified Russian religious practice — how services were sung, how icons were painted, how the faithful should cross themselves. That last detail looks trivial and was a time bomb: by freezing Russian ritual exactly as it then stood, the Stoglav guaranteed that when a later patriarch tried to "correct" those rituals to match Greek practice, the change would feel like an attack on the holy itself, and Russia's Church would split apart over it (Chapter 11). And the army was reformed too, with the creation of the **streltsy** (STRELT-see, "shooters"), Russia's first permanent corps of firearm-equipped infantry, who would become both a pillar of the state and, eventually, a dangerous political force in their own right.

Then came the conquests that turned Russia, for the first time in this book, from a victim of steppe empires into a conqueror of them — and the symbolism of that reversal cannot be overstated. For three centuries, Russian princes had traveled east to grovel before Tatar khans. Now the tsar marched east to destroy them. The targets were the **khanates of Kazan and Astrakhan**, two Muslim Tatar states on the Volga River that were direct successors of the old Golden Horde — the very power that had ground Russia under the yoke. **Kazan** (kah-ZAHN), upriver, was a constant menace, raiding Russian lands and carrying off captives for the slave markets. In **1552 Ivan besieged and stormed it.** His engineers (some of them foreign) dug mines under the walls and blew them open with gunpowder, and the city fell in a horrific sack. Four years later, in **1556, Russia took Astrakhan** (AHS-trah-khahn), the khanate at the mouth of the Volga where the great river empties into the Caspian Sea.

The strategic payoff was immense: with Kazan and Astrakhan in hand, Russia controlled the entire length of the Volga, Europe's longest river, from the forests of the center all the way down to the Caspian. That opened a trade route to Persia and the East, and it opened the road across the Urals into Siberia, the staggering expansion that would eventually carry Russia to the Pacific (begun, in this book, in the next chapter). But the deeper meaning was about identity. By conquering Kazan and Astrakhan, Russia became, for the first time, a **multi-ethnic, multi-faith empire** — a state ruling over large populations of Muslims and Turkic and Finno-Ugric peoples, not just Orthodox Russians. The tables had turned completely. The descendants of the conquered had become the conquerors; the former tributary now ruled Tatar Muslims as subjects. This is the hinge on which Russia stops being merely a nation and starts becoming an empire, and it happened on the Volga in the 1550s.

To celebrate the taking of Kazan, Ivan built the strangest and most beloved building in Russia. On Red Square, just outside the Kremlin walls, he raised the **Cathedral of the Intercession**, completed around 1561 and known to the world as **Saint Basil's Cathedral** (after a "holy fool," a kind of revered religious eccentric, buried there). It is unlike anything else: a riotous, asymmetrical cluster of nine chapels under a forest of onion domes, each dome a different color, shape, and pattern, swirling and spiraling like something out of a dream or a candy shop. Where Ivan III's Italian-built Kremlin cathedrals were dignified and classical, Saint Basil's is wild, native, and joyous — a thoroughly Russian explosion of color with no European model. (The much-repeated legend that Ivan had the architects blinded so they could never build anything so beautiful again is almost certainly a folktale, not history; flag it as the kind of dark story that attached itself to this tsar.) That a man who would shortly drown his own country in blood also gave it its happiest building is one more of the irreconcilable contrasts this reign keeps throwing up.

Two more things broke open in these years, both pointing toward the wider world Russia was still mostly shut off from. In **1553 an English expedition** under the navigator **Richard Chancellor**, searching for a northeast sea passage to Asia, blundered instead onto the coast of the **White Sea** in the Russian far north and made its way to Ivan's court. The result was the first direct trade link between Russia and Western Europe, formalized in the English **Muscovy Company**, which won the right to trade in Russia — a thin but real thread connecting isolated Moscow to the Atlantic world, and a sign of how much Russia wanted Western goods and expertise. And in **1564 Ivan Fyodorov** (FYO-duh-rof) printed the *Apostol*, the first dated printed book made in Russia, more than a century after the German printer Gutenberg's movable-type press (invented around 1450) had transformed Western Europe by making books cheap and plentiful. The reception tells you everything: popular hostility, including suspicion that printing was something close to sorcery, soon drove Fyodorov to flee abroad. Russia was not yet a place that welcomed the printed word, and it would stay suspicious of Western novelty for a long time to come.

So the first half of Ivan IV's reign reads as a triumph and a genuine one. A sacred coronation as the first tsar. A reforming council, a national assembly, a new law code, a reformed army. The conquest of two khanates that reversed three centuries of Russian humiliation and made Russia an empire. A new window on the West and the first printed book. Saint Basil's rising on Red Square in a blaze of color. If Ivan IV had died around 1560, he would be remembered, simply, as Ivan the Great's worthy grandson — the tsar who completed the work. He did not die in 1560. Instead, somewhere around that year, something in him turned, and the same man who built Saint Basil's set about tearing his own country apart. Why, and how, and how much of the monster is real and how much is legend, is the subject of the next chapter.


# Chapter 8 — The Terror

The previous chapter left **Ivan IV** (Chapter 7) at the height of a triumphant reign: crowned the first tsar in 1547, a reformer, the conqueror of the Volga khanates that reversed three centuries of Russian humiliation, the builder of Saint Basil's Cathedral. This chapter is about the same man becoming something the Russian language would remember him as forever: *Grozny*, usually translated **"the Terrible"** — though the word is closer to "awe-inspiring," "fearsome," "dread," the quality a thunderstorm has, not simply "wicked." Ivan the Dread. In the second half of his reign he turned the machinery of the state against his own people in a paranoid terror so strange and so savage that historians are still arguing about what it was for and whether the tsar was, in the modern sense, mad. We have to weigh all of that, and we have to do it carefully, because Ivan the Terrible is the single figure in this book whose reputation has been most worked over by hostile sources. Let us take the descent in order.

It begins with two losses, around 1560, and a war that would not end. The personal loss was the death in 1560 of Ivan's first wife, **Anastasia Romanovna**, of the **Romanov** family (remember that name — her grand-nephew will found the dynasty that rules Russia for three centuries, Chapter 10). By long tradition, Anastasia was the one person who steadied Ivan, and her death — which Ivan came to believe was murder by poisoning at the hands of the boyars (the hereditary high nobility he increasingly distrusted) — is cast as the moment his mind began to darken toward suspicion and rage. How much of this is real psychology and how much is later storytelling tidying a complicated breakdown into a single tragic cause, nobody can say; treat it as the traditional account, not a diagnosis.

The political grind was the **Livonian War**, which Ivan launched in **1558**. **Livonia** (lih-VOH-nee-uh) was the territory of roughly modern Latvia and Estonia, on the Baltic coast — and the Baltic was the thing Russia desperately lacked and Ivan wanted: a warm-water outlet to the sea, a window on Western trade and technology that did not depend on the long, frozen, roundabout White Sea route. He invaded to seize a Baltic port. What he got was a quarter-century quagmire. Early successes pulled in Russia's powerful neighbors — Poland, Lithuania (which would soon merge into the vast **Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth**), and Sweden — until Russia was fighting a coalition it could not beat, on and off, until 1583. The war drained the treasury, bled the army, and ended in exhaustion and outright failure: Russia gained no Baltic coast and lost ground it had held. The Livonian War is the slow-burning disaster running underneath this entire chapter, and it matters because a tsar growing paranoid about traitors and betrayal was, simultaneously, losing a long war — and a losing war is exactly the soil in which a hunt for internal enemies grows.

The hunt got its trigger in **1564**, when one of Ivan's most senior commanders and a former friend, **Prince Andrei Kurbsky** (KOORB-skee), defected to the enemy — fleeing across the lines to Lithuania rather than face the tsar's growing suspicion. From exile, Kurbsky did something extraordinary: he wrote the tsar an open letter, a furious indictment of Ivan as a tyrant who murdered his loyal servants, and Ivan wrote back, at length and in fury, defending his absolute, God-given right to reward and punish his subjects exactly as he saw fit. The **Ivan–Kurbsky correspondence** that followed is one of the most remarkable documents in Russian history: a real-time argument, between a tyrant and his accuser, about the nature of legitimate power — whether a ruler is bound by anything but God, or whether his servants have rights he must respect. (Some scholars have even questioned whether the letters are entirely genuine, but the mainstream view treats them as real and as a rare window into how these questions were argued.) To Ivan, Kurbsky's flight confirmed his deepest fear: that his own elite, the boyars, were riddled with traitors, and that no one could be trusted.

What he did next was political theater of a kind no one had seen. In late 1564, Ivan abruptly **left Moscow.** He packed up his treasury, his family, and his most sacred icons and withdrew to a fortified town outside the capital. Then he sent two letters back to Moscow. One, to the boyars and clergy, accused them of treason and corruption and announced that he was abdicating — giving up the throne in disgust. The other, to the common people of Moscow, assured them that his anger was not at *them* but only at the treacherous nobles. The effect was exactly calculated. A Russia without a tsar was, to the people of that age, an almost unthinkable horror, a body without a head, and the city panicked. Delegations begged him to return. And Ivan agreed to return on one condition: that he be granted absolute, unlimited power to identify, punish, and destroy "traitors" however he pleased, with no one — not the boyars, not the Church — permitted to object. They granted it. He had engineered a situation in which the nation pleaded with him to become a tyrant.

The instrument of the terror was the **oprichnina** (oh-PREECH-nee-nah), established in **1565**, and it is the strangest institution in this book. The word comes from an old Russian term for "apart" or "set aside" — it had been used for the dower lands set aside for a prince's widow. Ivan applied it to a domain he now carved out of the country for himself alone: he split Russia in two. One part, the **zemshchina**, was the ordinary land, still governed in the old way by the boyars and the regular administration. The other part, the **oprichnina**, was a vast personal domain — eventually perhaps a third of the entire country, including the richest and most strategic lands — that belonged to the tsar directly, outside all normal law, answerable to no one but him. Within it, the old landowning families were thrown off their estates, their lands handed to the tsar's new men.

Those new men were the **oprichniki** (oh-PREECH-nee-kee), Ivan's hand-picked corps of enforcers, and they became the face of the terror. They dressed in black, rode black horses, and carried as their grim emblems a dog's head and a broom — meaning, it was said, that they sniffed out and swept away the tsar's enemies. They operated as a secret police, a private army, and a death squad rolled into one, with the authority to seize, torture, and execute anyone Ivan named a traitor, confiscating their property as they went. They lived, partly, like a perverse monastic brotherhood, with Ivan himself playing abbot, alternating savage cruelty with ostentatious prayer. Across roughly seven years, from 1565 to 1572, the oprichniki gutted the old Russian aristocracy through confiscation, torture, and killing — exactly the boyar class Ivan was convinced was betraying him. The death tolls of the whole period are genuinely uncertain and the estimates vary widely; the sources are partisan and the record thin, so any figure should be treated as an educated guess rather than a count. But that it broke the independent power of the old nobility, and terrorized the country, is not in doubt.

The terror's single worst atrocity came in **1570**, against a Russian city, not a foreign enemy. Convinced — on evidence that was probably forged or imaginary — that the great old city of Novgorod (the merchant republic Ivan III had crushed and annexed in 1478, Chapter 6) was plotting to defect to Poland-Lithuania, Ivan led his oprichniki north and fell on it. For weeks they massacred the inhabitants — drowning people under the river ice, torturing and killing across every class, looting churches and monasteries. The **sack of Novgorod** of 1570 left the old city permanently broken; it never recovered its former wealth or importance. The exact number killed is, again, disputed and probably exaggerated in the lurid sources, but it was a slaughter on a horrifying scale, inflicted by a Russian tsar on a Russian city in peacetime. Novgorod 1570 is the moment the oprichnina stops looking like even a twisted political program and starts looking like something closer to murderous paranoia given an army.

There was one voice that stood up to it, and his fate tells you how total the terror had become. **Metropolitan Philip II** (the head of the Russian Church) openly condemned the oprichnina's bloodshed — to Ivan's face, in the cathedral, refusing the tsar his blessing and demanding he stop the killing. It was an act of staggering courage, the conscience of the Church against the power of the tsar. Ivan had him deposed on trumped-up charges and imprisoned in a monastery, and in 1569 Philip was strangled in his cell, by tradition by the hand of **Malyuta Skuratov**, the most notorious of the oprichnik chiefs. Philip was later made a saint and martyr. His murder settled, brutally, the question that the Ivan–Kurbsky letters had argued: in Ivan's Russia, not even the head of the Church could check the tsar. The crown stood above everything.

And then the whole thing collapsed under the weight of its own destructiveness, because a state that spends seven years terrorizing its own elite cannot defend itself. In **1571 the Crimean Tatars** — a Muslim khanate to the south, a fragment of the old Golden Horde and a client of the Ottoman Empire — broke through Russia's weakened, demoralized defenses, rode all the way to the capital, and **burned Moscow to the ground.** The oprichnina, for all its black horses and dog's heads, had been useless against a real enemy; it was good at murdering boyars and Novgorodians, not at fighting Tatars. The humiliation was total: the tsar who had conquered Kazan watched his own capital burn. The following year, in **1572**, the Crimeans came back with a far larger force, intending to finish Russia off — and this time a reunited Russian army (the oprichnina and zemshchina forces fighting together) shattered them at the **Battle of Molodi** (MOH-luh-dee), a desperate, decisive victory that saved Moscow and arguably the state. Soon after Molodi, Ivan quietly **abolished the oprichnina**, even forbidding people to mention the word. The experiment was over. It had broken the old nobility, devastated the heartland, depopulated whole regions through terror and flight, and very nearly gotten the country conquered. What exactly it had been *for* — a genuine if monstrous attempt to break the boyars' power and centralize the state, or simply the rampage of a paranoid mind dressed up as policy — historians still debate, and the honest answer is probably that it was both, inseparably.

Even amid all this ruin, the reign produced one more conquest of world-historical scale, and characteristically Ivan had little to do with it directly. In **1582**, far to the east, a band of **Cossacks** — free, semi-independent frontier warriors of the southern steppe, many of them runaway peasants who had fled the lords for the borderland (we will meet them properly in Chapter 10) — led by a chieftain named **Yermak** (yer-MAHK) and bankrolled by the **Stroganov** family (wealthy merchants and salt magnates expanding into the eastern frontier), crossed the Ural Mountains and defeated the **Khanate of Sibir**, a Tatar state in western Siberia. It was a private venture more than a royal campaign, but it cracked open the door to the unimaginable expanse beyond the Urals, and over the following century Russian fur-trappers, Cossacks, and soldiers would pour east across the whole of northern Asia, all the way to the Pacific. The conquest of Siberia — which would eventually more than double the size of the country and define Russia as a power straddling two continents — began as a sideline in the reign of the Terrible, while the heartland burned.

The reign ended in a personal catastrophe that doomed the dynasty. In **1581**, in a fit of rage — the sources say over a dispute about his pregnant daughter-in-law's dress, or over his son's criticism of the war — Ivan struck his eldest son and heir, also named Ivan, on the head with his iron-tipped staff, and the blow killed him. (The grief-stricken aftermath was immortalized centuries later, in 1885, in a famous, harrowing painting by Ilya Repin — the towering Russian realist painter of the late nineteenth century — the image through which most Russians still picture the scene.) It is impossible to overstate the consequence. The murdered Ivan Ivanovich was a capable adult, the prepared heir. With him dead, the succession fell to the tsar's surviving son **Fyodor** (FYO-dor), who was pious, gentle, and widely considered feeble and incapable of ruling — and to a much younger half-brother, Dmitry, still an infant. When Ivan IV himself died in **1584** (by tradition at a chessboard), he left the throne not to a strong successor but to a weak one, with the dynasty's future hanging by the thinnest of threads. Within fourteen years that thread would snap entirely, and Russia would fall into the catastrophe of the Time of Troubles (Chapters 9 and 10).

So how much of the monster is history, and how much is legend? This is the question the book promised to weigh, and the honest answer is layered. The cruelty was real: the oprichnina happened, the sack of Novgorod happened, Metropolitan Philip was murdered, Ivan killed his own son. None of the structural facts are in serious doubt. But the *texture* of the legend — the most lurid tortures, the exact death tolls, the precise state of Ivan's mind, the idea that he was a coherent madman or, alternatively, a cold rational state-builder — comes to us through sources with every reason to darken him: chronicles compiled under later dynasties, the hostile letters of the exile Kurbsky, and foreign travelers retailing horror stories to readers who wanted to believe the worst of barbarous Muscovy. Whether Ivan suffered from a genuine mental illness, or paranoia worsened by physical pain and possibly mercury poisoning (his exhumed remains showed high mercury levels, though that may have been from medicine), or was simply a brutal autocrat operating with a logic alien to us, remains genuinely unresolved. What is certain is that he left Russia weaker than he found it in every way that mattered except size — its nobility broken, its center depopulated, its dynasty doomed, its treasury empty from a lost war — while expanding it into Siberia almost by accident. He is the great cautionary figure of this book: proof that the sacred, absolute, God-given tsar that Russia had spent three centuries constructing could, in the wrong hands, turn the whole apparatus of the state against the people it ruled, with no one left able to say no. The next chapters are about the price Russia paid for that.


# Chapter 9 — The Dynasty Burns Out

When Ivan the Terrible died at the chessboard in March 1584, an old man worn out by his own cruelty, he left behind a throne, an empire stretching from the Baltic to the Pacific shore of Siberia, and a catastrophe waiting to happen. The catastrophe was simple arithmetic. The line of **Rurik** (ROO-rik), the half-legendary Norse warlord whom every prince of Moscow claimed as ancestor, had ruled the Russian lands for roughly seven hundred years, and it was about to run out of men. Ivan had murdered his own capable elder son in a rage three years before (Chapter 8), striking him down with an iron-tipped staff in a quarrel and then howling over the body. What remained of the dynasty was thin and getting thinner. This chapter is the story of how a seven-century-old ruling house simply stopped existing, how an able but unroyal man tried to fill the hole and could not, and how the worst famine in Russian history pulled the floor out from under everything just as the question of legitimacy turned dangerous. By the end of it Russia is poised at the edge of dissolution.

The heir was a problem from the start. Ivan's surviving son **Fyodor** (FYOH-dor), who took the throne as Fyodor I, was by every contemporary account a gentle, pious, simple-minded man with no taste or talent for ruling. He liked to ring church bells and go on pilgrimages; foreign envoys reported a small, perpetually smiling figure who shuffled through the great ceremonies of state without quite understanding them. Whether he was genuinely feeble-minded or merely uninterested and overwhelmed is impossible to recover at this distance, because the men who wrote about him had every reason to make him look helpless, but the practical result is not in doubt: Fyodor reigned and someone else ruled. That someone was his brother-in-law, a boyar (a member of the old hereditary high aristocracy, the great landowning families who staffed the court and the army) named **Boris Godunov** (boh-REES goh-doo-NOF).

Godunov is one of the genuinely interesting men in this book, and one of the most slandered, which is the same problem as Ivan in reverse: where Ivan's reputation was blackened, Godunov's was poisoned, by later writers who needed him to be a villain so that the dynasty that replaced him could look clean. Strip away the slander and what is left is a gifted, careful, ambitious administrator who had risen at Ivan's court, married his sister to the heir, and made himself indispensable. He had served in the **oprichnina**, Ivan's personal terror-state (Chapter 8), and survived it, which tells you something about his political skill. For fourteen years, through Fyodor's whole reign, Boris Godunov was the real government of Russia, and on the evidence it was a competent one. He stabilized the finances Ivan had wrecked, kept the southern frontier defended against the Crimean Tatars (the Muslim Turkic raiders of the Black Sea steppe who burned and enslaved their way north almost every year), and pulled off a genuine diplomatic coup that mattered enormously to Russian self-regard.

That coup was the creation of the **Moscow Patriarchate** in 1589. To see why it was a coup you have to understand the shape of the Orthodox Christian world. **Orthodoxy**, the eastern branch of Christianity that had split from the Catholic West centuries earlier over questions of papal authority and ritual, was organized under a handful of senior bishops called **patriarchs** (a patriarch is the highest rank of bishop, the head of a whole national or regional church). The ancient patriarchates were Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem, and after the Ottoman Turks conquered Constantinople in 1453 (Chapter 5), all of them sat inside a Muslim empire, their patriarchs reduced to tolerated subjects of the sultan. The head of the Russian Church, by contrast, was only a **metropolitan** (a senior bishop ranking just below a patriarch), even though Russia was now the largest free Orthodox state on earth and had been telling itself for a century that it was the **Third Rome**, the last guardian of true Christianity after the first Rome fell to heresy and the second, Constantinople, fell to the Turks (Chapter 6). The mismatch grated. So when the patriarch of Constantinople came to Moscow in 1588 begging for money, Godunov kept him there for the better part of a year and quietly squeezed out of him a consecration: the metropolitan of Moscow, Job, was elevated to patriarch, the fifth in the Orthodox world. It was a transaction dressed as a blessing, and it gave Moscow at last the rank its ambitions demanded.

But Godunov's other great act of state was the one that would help destroy him, and it tightened a screw that had been turning slowly for a hundred years. Russian peasants had once been legally free to move. A peasant who owed a landlord rent or labor could, at least in principle, settle his debts and leave for another lord, and the law had narrowed that freedom to a single window each year, the two weeks around **St. George's Day** in late autumn, after the harvest was in. This was already a constraint, written into the law codes of Ivan III in 1497 and Ivan IV in 1550 (Chapters 6 and 7), part of a long, grinding drift toward binding the peasant to the soil. Around 1592, under Godunov, that last window was suspended. The peasant could no longer leave at all. This is the decisive turn of the screw toward **serfdom** (SERF-dom), the system in which peasants are not slaves owned outright but are legally bound to a particular estate, unable to leave it, owing labor and dues to its lord and inherited along with the land like livestock or buildings. It is worth being precise about why a government would do something so harsh, because it was not simple cruelty. Moscow paid its cavalry not in cash, which it did not have, but in land, the **pomestie** (poh-MYES-tyeh) estate granted to a man in return for military service. An estate is worthless without peasants to work it, and if the peasants could walk away to a richer lord, the small service-gentry who formed the backbone of the army would be ruined and the army with them. Binding the peasant kept the soldier in the saddle. The state built serfdom not out of malice but out of the iron logic of how it armed itself, and Russia would live with the consequence for two and a half centuries. We will watch the screw turn a final, complete revolution under the Romanovs (Chapter 11).

Then came the death that would not stay dead. In May 1591, in the town of **Uglich** (OOG-lich) on the upper Volga, Ivan the Terrible's youngest son, the boy **Dmitry** (DMEE-tree), died. He was about eight years old, the child of Ivan's last marriage, and he had been sent away from Moscow with his mother's family to the provincial appanage of Uglich, safely out of the way of Fyodor's childless court. He died of a knife wound to the throat. A commission sent from Moscow, headed by a boyar who happened to be a rival of Godunov, investigated and returned a verdict: the boy suffered from epilepsy (the "falling sickness," a seizure disorder), had been playing with a knife, and had fallen onto it during a fit. Accident, the commission ruled. Case closed.

It was not closed, and it was never going to be, and you should sit with how genuinely uncertain this is, because Russian and later historians have argued about the death at Uglich for four hundred years without settling it. Three explanations have always been on the table. The first is the official one: a real, tragic accident, a sick child and a sharp object. The second is that Boris Godunov had the boy murdered to clear his own path to the throne, since Dmitry was the last male of the dynasty after the childless Fyodor and stood between Godunov and supreme power. This is the version the later Romanov-era chronicles pushed hard, and it is the version the great poet Pushkin and the composer Mussorgsky immortalized in the nineteenth century, so it is the version most people half-remember. The trouble is that the evidence for Godunov's guilt is thin and the motive, while real, is not proof, and serious historians have grown steadily more skeptical of it. The third possibility, the strangest and the one with the most explosive consequences, is that the boy who died at Uglich was not the real Dmitry at all, or that the real Dmitry somehow survived, a rumor that took root the instant the body was buried. What is certain is only this: a child died, the explanation satisfied almost no one, and the death of the dynasty's last spare heir would prove to be a loaded gun left lying on the table.

The gun fired in stages. In January 1598 Fyodor died, childless, and the line of Rurik that had ruled the Russian lands for seven centuries simply ended. There was no heir of the blood. This had never happened before in Russian memory, and there was no settled procedure for it, because the throne had always passed within one family by inheritance and the legitimacy of a Russian ruler rested on being of that sacred bloodline. Into the vacuum stepped the one institution capable of acting, the **Zemsky Sobor** (ZEM-skee soh-BOR), the "assembly of the land," an irregular gathering of clergy, nobles, and townsmen that Ivan IV had first summoned in 1549 (Chapter 7) and that mattered most precisely when the throne itself was empty. The Sobor, heavily managed by Godunov's allies and by the Patriarch Job whom Godunov had installed, elected Boris Godunov tsar. He had the experience, he had effectively ruled for fourteen years, and there was no obvious rival. On paper it was the sensible choice.

On the level where Russian power actually lived, it was fatally weak, and here is the mechanism that would tear the country apart. A Russian tsar's authority was not, at bottom, a thing the Zemsky Sobor could confer. It was held to come from God and from blood, the unbroken descent from Rurik that made the ruler a sacred figure, the "natural" sovereign whose right no man could question. Boris Godunov had neither. He was a boyar like any other boyar, raised above his peers by an assembly, and every great family that thought itself as good as the Godunovs, or better, looked at the new tsar and saw not God's anointed but an upstart who had grabbed a throne that was now, in principle, grabbable. The moment the crown could be won by a vote or a coup rather than inherited by blood, it could be claimed by anyone with an army and a story. Godunov had broken the spell of legitimacy simply by occupying the throne without the bloodline, and he could not put it back.

For a few years it held anyway, because Godunov governed well and the harvests were good. Then the weather turned, and turned with a savagery that no government could have survived. Beginning in the summer of 1601, Russia was struck by three consecutive years of catastrophic harvest failure. Summer frosts killed the crops in the field; it rained for weeks and then froze in August; the grain rotted or never ripened. We now understand, though no one at the time could have, that this was very likely the global climate aftershock of a colossal volcanic eruption far away, at **Huaynaputina** (WHY-nah-poo-TEE-nah) in Peru in early 1600, which threw enough ash and sulfur into the high atmosphere to dim the sun and chill the summers across the whole Northern Hemisphere for years. The effect on Russia, already a cold country farming at the edge of what grain could bear, was apocalyptic. The **Great Famine** of 1601 to 1603 may have killed something like two million people, perhaps a third of the population of the affected regions, though the figures are guesses built on the horrified accounts of survivors. Contemporaries described the roads choked with the corpses of peasants who had fled the countryside hoping for grain in the towns and died in the streets instead; they described, in the chronicles' usual lurid register, parents eating children and bread baked from bark and straw. Godunov did what he could, opening the granaries and distributing money, but charity could not conjure grain that the frost had killed, and the relief mostly drew starving crowds into the cities to die.

For a tsar whose only claim was that he governed competently, a famine that killed a third of his subjects was a verdict from heaven. To a deeply religious population, catastrophe on this scale meant one thing: God was punishing Russia, and the obvious sin to punish was the unnatural elevation of a tsar with no right to the throne, perhaps a tsar who had murdered the rightful heir to take it. The rumor of Uglich, dormant for a decade, came roaring back. The country was now full of desperate, displaced, starving people with nothing left to lose, the perfect tinder, and into that tinder, in the last year of the famine, walked a young man in Poland who said that he was the Tsarevich Dmitry, that he had not died at Uglich after all, and that he was coming home to claim what was his.

Godunov died suddenly in April 1605, of a stroke or a hemorrhage, possibly worn out, possibly poisoned, with that young man's small army already on Russian soil and Russian towns going over to him one after another. He left a teenage son to inherit a throne nobody believed in, in a country convinced it was under divine judgment. The dynasty had burned out. What came next was the closest Russia ever came, in all the centuries this book covers, to ceasing to exist.


# Chapter 10 — The Time of Troubles

The Russians have a name for the fifteen years that followed, and it is one of the bleakest a nation has ever given a stretch of its own history: the **Time of Troubles** (in Russian the *Smuta*, SMOO-tah, which means something closer to "the turmoil" or "the confusion," with a flavor of moral and cosmic disorder). It is the period, roughly 1598 to 1613, in which the Russian state effectively dissolved. There was famine, which we have met (Chapter 9); there was civil war; there was a procession of impostors all claiming to be the same dead boy; there were two foreign armies on Russian soil; and at the lowest point there was a Polish garrison sitting inside the Moscow Kremlin, the sacred fortress at the heart of the capital, while rival warlords and pretenders ruled scraps of the country and the very idea of "Russia" came apart in people's hands. This chapter is about how close it came to not surviving, and about the strange, almost accidental way it pulled itself back together, not through a tsar or an army of state but through an uprising of ordinary people who had no legitimate ruler left to fight for and decided to fight anyway.

Begin with the impostor, because the Troubles are, at their core, a story about legitimacy and the lengths to which a society starved of it will go. When Boris Godunov took the throne without the sacred bloodline (Chapter 9), he made the throne theoretically available to anyone, and the one thing that could trump an elected upstart was a genuine heir of Rurik's line. There was supposed to be no such heir left. But the boy Dmitry's death at Uglich had been murky enough that a rumor could grow in the gap, and in 1603 a young man surfaced in Poland-Lithuania claiming to be exactly that: the Tsarevich Dmitry, miraculously rescued from the assassins as a child, raised in secret, and now grown and ready to reclaim his father's crown.

Who he actually was, nobody has ever proven, and the honest answer is that we do not know. The Russian government, once it had reason to discredit him, identified him as a defrocked monk named **Grigory Otrepyev** (oh-TREP-yev), a minor nobleman's son who had taken vows, picked up an alarming amount of knowledge about the old court, and then fled to Poland with a wild story. That identification may be right; it may also be exactly the kind of thing a threatened government invents. What matters is that historians call him the **First False Dmitry** (a "pretender," meaning someone falsely claiming a throne or identity, and "false" because the claim was almost certainly fraudulent), and that a surprising number of people, including some who had known the real court, either believed him or found it convenient to. He won the backing of Polish-Lithuanian magnates, the immensely powerful regional lords of the **Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth**, the vast and aggressively Catholic state that bordered Russia to the west and had been its rival for the old lands of Kievan Rus' for centuries. He secretly converted to Catholicism, which his Polish backers wanted, and he got himself engaged to a Polish noblewoman, Marina Mniszech. With a small army of Polish adventurers and Cossacks he crossed into Russia in 1604.

The **Cossacks** (KOSS-aks) need an introduction here, because they will recur through the rest of this book as one of its wild cards. They were communities of free, fiercely independent frontiersmen living in the wide unsettled steppe grasslands south of Russia and Poland, along the great rivers, the Don and the Dnieper. Many were runaway peasants and serfs who had fled the tightening grip of lords and the state for the freedom of the borderland; they lived by raiding, fishing, hunting, and serving as mercenary cavalry for whoever paid, and they governed themselves in rough military democracies, electing their own leaders. They were superb light horsemen and a standing temptation to any rebel or pretender who could promise them loot and liberty. In a country coming apart, the Cossacks were a loaded weapon lying on the frontier, and almost every faction in the Troubles would try to pick it up.

The First False Dmitry's invasion should have failed, and at first it nearly did. But then the famine had done its work, the towns of the south, full of starving and resentful people, opened their gates to him, and in April 1605 Boris Godunov suddenly died. Godunov's teenage son was murdered within weeks, and in June 1605 the man claiming to be Dmitry rode into Moscow and was crowned tsar. A pretender, almost certainly an impostor, backed by a foreign Catholic power, sat on the throne of holy Orthodox Russia, and large numbers of Russians, desperate for a legitimate ruler of the old blood, told themselves it was a miracle.

The miracle lasted under a year. The new tsar's problem was that he behaved like what his enemies said he was. He kept his Polish entourage close, dined in Western style, did not observe Russian customs with the punctilious holiness expected of a tsar, and, worst of all, married his Polish Catholic bride in a lavish ceremony in the heart of Orthodox Moscow, crowning her tsarina. To the boyars who resented him and the Muscovites who watched a Catholic foreigner installed in the Kremlin, this was intolerable. In May 1606 a boyar conspiracy led by Prince **Vasily Shuisky** (vah-SEE-lee SHOO-skee) raised the city, and a mob hunted the false tsar through the palace and killed him. They were not gentle about it. They burned his body, loaded the ashes into a cannon, and fired them westward, back toward Poland, the most emphatic eviction notice in Russian history. It settled nothing. It made things much worse.

Vasily Shuisky now had himself proclaimed tsar, but he was simply another boyar grabbing the crown, with no more blood-right than Godunov and far less ability, and the country no longer recognized any rule as binding. The mechanism of dissolution, once started, fed on itself: every successful grab proved the throne was grabbable, which invited the next grab. Almost at once a vast revolt boiled up across the south under a former military slave named **Ivan Bolotnikov** (boh-LOT-nee-kof), sweeping together runaway serfs, Cossacks, disaffected gentry, and townsmen into an army that marched on Moscow itself in 1606 before Shuisky's forces beat it back and crushed it. The Bolotnikov revolt is sometimes called one of the great peasant risings of Russian history, a war of the bound and the poor against the lords and the bound order, and there is truth in that, but it was also simply one more faction in a country that had shattered into factions, fighting in the name of, of course, the Tsarevich Dmitry, who was supposed to be alive again somewhere.

And then he was alive again, because a second man appeared claiming to be the same prince. The **Second False Dmitry**, whose real identity is even more obscure than the first's, set up a rival court in 1607 at the village of **Tushino** (TOO-shee-noh) just outside Moscow, which earned him his contemptuous nickname, the "Brigand of Tushino" or the "Felon of Tushino." Now there were two governments, two courts, two tsars, Shuisky in the Kremlin and the pretender at Tushino, and the country split between them, with the extraordinary detail that boyars and officials shuttled back and forth between the two, taking offices and lands from each in turn, so that men called "the Tushino flitters" collected salaries from both sides of a civil war. Marina Mniszech, the Polish widow of the first false tsar, even recognized the second as her resurrected husband, lending the fraud a grotesque continuity. The state had effectively ceased to exist. Russia was a geography full of armies.

Into that vacuum the foreign powers stopped pretending. Shuisky, desperate, bought military help from Sweden, Russia's rival to the north for the Baltic coast. That gave **Sigismund III** (SIG-iz-mund), the king of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the excuse he wanted, because Sweden and Poland were at war, and in 1609 Sigismund dropped the whole charade of backing pretenders and invaded Russia outright, in his own name, to put himself or his son on the Russian throne and, ideally, drag Orthodox Russia into the Catholic fold. He laid siege to the great western fortress-city of **Smolensk** (smoh-LENSK), which would hold out for twenty months. In June 1610 a small, superbly disciplined Polish army, built around the famous **winged hussars** (heavy lance-armed cavalry who wore towering wooden frames feathered like wings rising over their backs, a terrifying and genuinely effective shock force), annihilated a much larger Russian-Swedish army at the **Battle of Klushino** (KLOO-shee-noh). The road to Moscow lay open. Shuisky was overthrown and packed off to a monastery, the Russian custom for disposing of inconvenient men by forcing them to take monastic vows, which legally ended their claim to anything worldly (though, as we will see with Filaret in the next chapter, a forced tonsure could be quietly overlooked when the politics shifted and the man was wanted back).

This is the bottom. With Klushino lost and no tsar at all, a council of leading boyars took charge of Moscow, a body history remembers as the **Seven Boyars**, and they made the calculation that the only way to end the chaos and keep their own heads and estates was to invite the enemy in. They offered the Russian throne to **Wladyslaw** (vwah-DEE-swahf), the teenage son of King Sigismund, on the condition (which the Poles never seriously meant to keep) that he convert to Orthodoxy, and in the autumn of 1610 they opened the gates of Moscow and let a Polish garrison march into the Kremlin. A foreign, Catholic army now held the sacred center of Russia. The Tsardom of Muscovy, which had spent three centuries clawing its way up from a swampy forest town to call itself the Third Rome, had a foreign king-elect and a foreign garrison in its holy fortress, and in many provinces nobody was governing at all. If there is a single moment in this entire book where you could have reasonably bet that "Russia" was about to disappear from the map the way so many medieval states did, swallowed by a stronger neighbor and remembered only as a region, this is it.

What turned it around was not a tsar, because there wasn't one, and not the boyars, who had surrendered. It was, of all things, the Church and the towns. The one institution that had survived every disaster of Russian history intact, since the Mongols first burned Kiev (Chapter 1), was the Orthodox Church, and now its head spoke. The **Patriarch Hermogenes** (her-MOH-jen-eez), imprisoned in Moscow by the Poles and their boyar collaborators, began smuggling out letters, passed hand to hand and copied and recopied, calling on Russians everywhere to rise against the Catholic occupiers and defend the true faith. The Poles starved him to death in his cell in early 1612, and in dying he became a martyr and a rallying cry. His letters did what no surviving government could: they gave the scattered, leaderless country a cause that was not a tsar, the defense of Orthodoxy and the Russian land themselves. Around the same time, the monks of the great **Trinity-Sergius Lavra** (the fortified monastery north of Moscow founded by the beloved saint Sergius of Radonezh, Chapter 3) were withstanding a sixteen-month Polish siege behind their walls, and their stubborn resistance, broadcast in letters of their own, became a second beacon.

The answer came from the provincial towns of the northeast, the ones the foreign armies had not reached. In the trading city of **Nizhny Novgorod** (NIZH-nee NOV-gor-od) on the Volga, a butcher and cattle-dealer named **Kuzma Minin** (kooz-MAH MEE-nin), who held the modest office of town elder, stood up and made the case that Russia had to be freed and that it would cost money, his own and everyone else's. What makes Minin remarkable is exactly that he was nobody, a tradesman, not a prince or a boyar, and that the rising he sparked was a grassroots, self-financed, civic affair: the townspeople taxed themselves heavily, pledged a third of their property, to pay for an army. To command it Minin recruited a competent and, crucially, honest military man, Prince **Dmitry Pozharsky** (poh-ZHAR-skee), one of the few commanders of the Troubles who had never sold himself to a pretender or a foreign king. Minin found the money; Pozharsky found the soldiers. Through 1612 this "second militia" (a first attempt the year before had fallen apart in infighting) marched on Moscow, gathering forces as it went, and in the autumn of 1612 it stormed the city and besieged the Polish garrison holed up in the Kremlin. Starving, reduced by the chronicles' account to cannibalism behind the walls, the Poles surrendered in November 1612. Moscow was free. Russia still celebrates the day as a national holiday, Unity Day, and Minin and Pozharsky stand together on a famous statue on Red Square, the butcher and the prince who saved the country when it had no tsar to save it.

A liberated capital still had no government, and the men who had freed it understood that an empty throne was the disease itself, not just a symptom. So in early 1613 they did the only thing the Russian political tradition offered: they summoned a great **Zemsky Sobor**, an assembly of the land far larger and more broadly representative than any before it, clergy, nobles, townsmen, Cossacks, even some peasants, drawn from across the country, to choose a tsar and end the interregnum by an act of the whole land. After weeks of argument and maneuvering, the Sobor settled on a compromise candidate precisely because he was a compromise: a sixteen-year-old boy named **Michael Romanov** (ROH-mah-nof). His qualifications were mostly negative, he was young, untainted by the worst factional crimes of the Troubles, and acceptable to almost everyone because he threatened almost no one, but he had one positive asset that mattered enormously to a country obsessed with the lost bloodline. His great-aunt had been Anastasia Romanovna, the beloved first wife of Ivan the Terrible (Chapter 8), which made the Romanovs connected by marriage to the extinct dynasty, the closest thing to legitimate blood anyone could offer. It was enough. In 1613 Michael Romanov was elected tsar, and the Time of Troubles was over.

The story that the new dynasty would tell about its own salvation produced its most enduring folk hero in these same months, a peasant named **Ivan Susanin** (soo-SAH-nin), who, the legend runs, was caught by a Polish search party hunting for the young tsar and deliberately led them deep into the trackless winter forest to die, perishing himself rather than betray Michael. Whether Susanin existed as described is genuinely uncertain, the story rests on a single later land grant to his family and grew in the retelling, but he became a national symbol of peasant loyalty unto death and, two centuries on, the hero of the first great Russian opera. The legend's real subject is the new dynasty's need for a founding myth of devotion, and that need was real even if Susanin may not have been.

A teenage boy now sat on a throne in a wrecked, depopulated, half-occupied country, with the Poles still holding Smolensk and the Swedes still on the Baltic coast. Russia had survived, barely, and entirely by improvisation. Putting it back together would take the rest of the century, and the work of rebuilding would, in its own quieter way, tear the country open again, this time not over who should be tsar but over how to make the sign of the cross.


# Chapter 11 — Rebuilding and the Great Schism

The first Romanovs inherited a ruin. When the boy Michael was crowned in 1613, large parts of Russia were depopulated and burned, the treasury was empty, the western fortress of Smolensk was still in Polish hands, the Swedes sat on the Baltic shore, and roving bands of Cossacks and brigands left over from the Troubles still terrorized the countryside. This chapter is about the long, grinding work of putting that ruin back together over the next seventy years, under Michael and then his son Alexis, and about the two things that work produced almost as by-products: a peasantry chained into total, hereditary serfdom forever, and a Church, the one institution that had held Russia together through every catastrophe, that split itself irreparably in two over the correct way to cross yourself and spell the name of Jesus. By the end of it Russia is whole, larger than ever, and quietly beginning to crack open toward the West, with a giant of a boy waiting in the wings to finish the job.

Recovery came first, and it came through an unusual arrangement of power. The young tsar Michael did not really govern alone, and for the crucial early years he barely governed at all. His father, **Filaret** (fee-lah-RET), had been a powerful boyar named Fyodor Romanov before the Troubles, forced into monastic vows by Boris Godunov to remove him as a rival (the standard Russian method of neutralizing a dangerous man, Chapter 10); as a monk he had risen through the Church, and he had spent years as a Polish prisoner of war. When he was finally released in 1619, he returned to Moscow, was promptly made **Patriarch** (the head of the Russian Orthodox Church, a rank Moscow had won only in 1589, Chapter 9), and became, in everything but name, co-ruler of Russia alongside his own son. Father and son even shared the formal title of "Great Sovereign." For fourteen years, until his death in 1633, Patriarch Filaret was the real government, an experienced and hard-headed man who used the towering prestige of the Church and his own paternal authority to stabilize the shattered state, rebuild the administration, restock the army, and slowly restore the crown's reach into the provinces. It was an odd thing, a patriarch running the country as the tsar's father, but it was exactly the steadying hand the wreckage needed.

The recovery had hard limits, and the bill for the Troubles came due in war. The peace deals that ended the chaos had been humiliating: the Truce of Deulino with Poland in 1618 left Smolensk and a swath of the west in Polish hands, and the earlier peace with Sweden left Russia landlocked, cut off from the Baltic Sea, with no port to trade west except the frozen White Sea in the far north. When Filaret tried to win Smolensk back in the **Smolensk War** of 1632, Russia lost, and the defeat exposed an ugly truth: the old Russian army, built around the **gentry cavalry** (the service-landholders who turned out on horseback in return for their pomestie estates, Chapter 9) and masses of poorly trained levies, was hopelessly outclassed by the disciplined, drilled, firearm-wielding infantry of Western Europe. Russia's answer was to start importing the West's military methods piecemeal. From the 1630s the government hired foreign mercenary officers, Scots, Germans, Dutchmen, to raise and drill **"regiments of the new order,"** Western-style infantry and dragoons trained in volley fire and pike-and-musket tactics. It was a halting, expensive modernization, decades from complete, but it is the thin end of a wedge that runs straight through this chapter to its end: again and again, Russia discovers that to compete with Europe it has to borrow from Europe, and the borrowing keeps widening.

Michael's son **Alexis** (ah-LEK-sis), who came to the throne in 1645, embodies the contradiction of the whole age. He is known to history as Alexis "the Quietest" (or "the Most Gentle"), a nickname that referred to his deep, sincere piety and the orderly calm he wanted around him, and it is almost perfectly misleading, because his reign was one of the most turbulent in Russian history: a wave of bloody urban revolts, the total legal enslavement of the peasantry, the annexation of Ukraine, a long war with Poland, the greatest Cossack rebellion before the eighteenth century, and the catastrophic splitting of the Church. The quiet tsar presided over an earthquake.

The revolts came first because the rebuilding had to be paid for, and the state paid for it by squeezing the people who could least bear it. In 1648 a clumsy new tax on salt, an absolute necessity for preserving food that the poorest could not do without, set off the **Salt Riot**, a violent uprising in Moscow that forced the terrified young tsar to hand over his most hated ministers to the mob to be killed and to scrap the tax. It was one of a whole wave of urban revolts across Russian towns in these years. Worse, in 1662, came the **Copper Riot**. To pay for an expensive war with Poland the government had hit on a disastrous scheme: it minted vast quantities of cheap copper coins and ordered them used at the face value of silver, while continuing to collect taxes in silver, a transparent swindle that triggered ruinous inflation as the copper money lost its worth. Crowds marched on the tsar's summer residence in fury; the rising was put down with hundreds killed and drowned, but Alexis was forced to abandon the copper coinage. These riots are not just color. They are the recurring sound of a state that has decided to become a centralized military power on the backs of a population that periodically refuses to bear the weight, and they form the constant background bass note under everything else Alexis did.

The single heaviest weight he laid on that population was the law code of 1649, the **Sobornoye Ulozhenie** (soh-BOR-noh-yeh oo-loh-ZHEN-yeh), meaning roughly "the assembly's law code," because it was drawn up and ratified by a Zemsky Sobor in the wake of the Salt Riot. The Ulozhenie was a monumental piece of legislation, the foundation of Russian law for the next two centuries, and its most fateful clause finished the long work of building serfdom that we have been tracking since Ivan III first narrowed peasant movement in 1497 (Chapters 6, 7, and 9). Earlier law had restricted when a peasant could leave his lord, then, under Godunov, suspended his leaving altogether, but there had remained a "statute of limitations," a number of years after which a runaway peasant who had escaped to a new place could no longer be hauled back. The Ulozhenie abolished it. A runaway could now be reclaimed by his lord no matter how many years or decades had passed, and the binding became hereditary, passing automatically to a peasant's children and grandchildren in perpetuity. With that single change the screw made its final, complete turn: the Russian peasant was now bound to his lord's estate forever, and his descendants with him, fixed to the soil and transferable along with it, a condition that would not be lifted until the emancipation of 1861, two centuries later. This is the moment Russian serfdom becomes total and hereditary, and it is worth pausing on the timing, because it cuts against a comfortable assumption. In Western Europe serfdom was fading away in exactly these centuries; the late medieval and early modern West was loosening the peasant's bonds. Russia did the opposite, hardening them into chains at the very moment the West was striking them off, and that divergence, free labor moving west, bound labor entrenched east, would shape the two halves of Europe for centuries.

Now the largest event of the reign, and the one with the longest shadow into our own time: the annexation of Ukraine. To understand it you have to understand the Cossacks again (Chapter 10), and specifically the **Zaporozhian Cossacks** (zah-poh-ROH-zhee-an), the free Cossack host of the Dnieper River steppe in what is now central Ukraine, living beyond the rapids (their name means roughly "those beyond the rapids"). These were Orthodox, mostly Slavic frontiersmen living under the loose, increasingly resented rule of the Catholic Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, which treated them as half-savage border guards and pressed its landlords, its Catholicism, and its Jewish estate-managers onto a hostile Orthodox peasantry. In 1648 their elected leader, the **hetman** (HET-man, the title of a Cossack supreme commander) **Bohdan Khmelnytsky** (bohg-DAHN khmel-NIT-skee), launched a massive uprising against Polish rule that exploded into a full-scale war, one of the bloodiest in the region's history, marked among other horrors by terrible massacres of the Jewish communities of Ukraine. Khmelnytsky won stunning early victories but could not, in the long run, beat Poland alone, and he looked around for a powerful Orthodox protector. The only one available was the tsar.

In 1654, at the town of **Pereyaslav** (peh-reh-YAH-slav), Khmelnytsky and his Cossack assembly swore an oath of allegiance to Tsar Alexis, placing themselves and their lands under the protection and overlordship of Moscow. This is the **Pereyaslav Agreement**, and it is one of the most fiercely contested events in all of Eastern European history, argued over by Russians and Ukrainians right down to the present day. The reason it is contested is that the two sides understood it utterly differently. To Moscow, Pereyaslav meant that Ukraine, the heartland of the old Kievan Rus' lost to the Mongols and then to Lithuania and Poland four centuries before (the subject of the previous book in this project), had finally "reunited" with Russia and submitted permanently to the tsar, a reading later Russian and Soviet governments turned into a foundational myth of brotherly union. To many Ukrainians then and since, it was a temporary military alliance between equals, a protectorate Khmelnytsky entered to win his war and never meant as the surrender of Cossack self-rule, which Moscow then steadily betrayed and swallowed. The text on which everything turns is ambiguous and the oath was sworn under wartime pressure, so the disagreement is not merely propaganda; it is genuinely built into what little survives. What is not in dispute is the consequence: Pereyaslav dragged Russia into the long, grinding **Thirteen Years' War** with Poland (1654 to 1667), and when it finally ended, Russia had at last won back Smolensk, lost since the Troubles, and secured Kiev, the ancient mother-city of all the Rus', and the left bank of the Dnieper. The annexation of Kiev was confirmed permanently in 1686. For the first time since the Mongols, the cradle of Russian Orthodox civilization was back in Russian hands, and Moscow's sense of itself as the gatherer and protector of all the Russian lands had a powerful new vindication.

And then, at the height of all this expansion and recovery, the Church tore itself apart. This is the **Great Schism** of the Russian Church, the **raskol** (rahs-KOL, "the split"), and it is one of the strangest and most revealing episodes in Russian history, because the country very nearly came to blows over what looks, from outside, like nothing at all. To see why it was not nothing, you have to grasp how Russian Orthodoxy understood ritual. For the devout Muscovite, the exact form of worship was not a changeable wrapping around the faith; it *was* the faith, the precise physical motions and words handed down from the holy ancestors, and getting them right was the difference between salvation and damnation. Russia believed itself the last bastion of pure Christianity, the Third Rome, which made the purity of its rites a matter of cosmic stakes.

The trouble was that the rites had drifted. Centuries of monks copying service books by hand had introduced small errors and divergences, and Russian practice had wandered away from the practice of the Greek Orthodox churches from which Russia had originally received its Christianity. In 1652 a forceful, ambitious man named **Nikon** (NEE-kon) became Patriarch, and he was convinced that Russian books and rituals had fallen into error and had to be "corrected" to match the contemporary Greek standard. Beginning in 1653 he ordered the changes. They were, on their face, almost unbelievably minor. The faithful were to make the sign of the cross with three fingers pressed together rather than two; the name of Jesus was to be respelled in the service books with an extra letter; processions were to circle the church in one direction rather than the other; certain chants and the number of bows were adjusted. That was essentially the whole of it.

To Nikon's opponents these were not trivia; they were a betrayal of everything holy. If the two-fingered cross their grandparents had used was now declared wrong, then either their saintly ancestors had been damned heretics, which was unthinkable, or Nikon was the heretic, throwing away the true Russian faith to grovel before the Greeks, the same Greeks who had, in living Russian memory, sold out Orthodoxy by agreeing to reunite with the Catholic pope at the Council of Florence (Chapter 5) and had then been punished by God with the fall of Constantinople. Why on earth should the Third Rome correct itself to match the rituals of a church God had so visibly abandoned? The resistance crystallized around the **Archpriest Avvakum** (ah-vah-KOOM), a fiery, eloquent provincial priest who became the great prophet and martyr of the old ways. The dissenters, who refused the new rites and clung to the old, came to be called the **Old Believers** (in Russian the *staroobryadtsy*), and in 1666 a great Church council confirmed Nikon's reforms and formally anathematized, that is, solemnly cursed and cast out, everyone who rejected them. With that, the Old Believers were no longer a faction inside the Church; they were schismatics outside it, a permanent breakaway that splintered Russian Orthodoxy for good.

The persecution that followed was savage, and so was the resistance. Avvakum was exiled, imprisoned for years in a frozen pit, and finally burned at the stake in 1682; in prison he wrote his own life story, a furious, vivid, colloquial account that is reckoned the first true autobiography in the Russian language and a masterpiece of early Russian prose. Whole communities of Old Believers, convinced that the reformed Church was the work of the Antichrist and that the end of the world was at hand, chose death over submission: thousands burned themselves alive in their own churches in mass self-immolations rather than accept the three-fingered cross. The monks of the great northern **Solovetsky Monastery** refused the new service books and held out behind their fortress walls against the tsar's own troops for eight years, from 1668 to 1676, before the place was taken and its defenders killed. The Old Believers never disappeared; they survived in the forests and the far north and the merchant towns for centuries, a stubborn underground of the old Russia, and their existence is a permanent monument to how seriously this culture took the precise shape of holiness.

There was a second drama folded inside the schism, and it settled a question that had hung over Russia since the days of the Mongols: who is supreme, the crown or the Church? Patriarch Nikon, drunk on his own authority, began to claim that the Church, and so the patriarch, stood above the tsar, that spiritual power outranked temporal, much as the popes had claimed over the kings of Catholic Europe. Alexis, the "quietest" tsar, would not have it. He froze Nikon out, and in 1666, the same council that condemned the Old Believers also deposed Nikon himself, stripping him of the patriarchate and packing him off to a distant monastery. So the council split the difference in a way that defined Russia for centuries: Nikon's *reforms* were upheld, but Nikon's *claim* was crushed. The ritual changes stood; the doctrine that the Church outranked the crown was buried. In Russia, it was settled, the tsar stood above the patriarch, and the Church was a pillar of the state, not its master, a subordination that Alexis's son Peter would soon make absolute by abolishing the patriarchate entirely (Chapter 12).

Through all of this, almost unnoticed, the West kept seeping in. It came through the new-model regiments and their foreign officers; it came through Kiev, newly Russian, whose Orthodox clergy were far better educated in Latin and Polish learning than Moscow's and who now flowed into the Russian Church; it came through men like **Simeon of Polotsk** (POH-lotsk), a learned Belarusian monk steeped in Latin-Polish Baroque culture who tutored Alexis's children and wrote the first courtly verse in Russia. It came through the court painter **Simon Ushakov**, who began softening the flat, otherworldly Russian icon with Western shading and lifelike modeling, a quiet revolution in the most sacred of Russian arts. It even reached the stage: in 1672 Alexis permitted the first theatrical performance ever staged in Russia at his court, a small Western novelty that scandalized traditionalists who saw playacting as devilish frivolity. None of this was a program; it was a slow leak, resisted at every turn, distrusted by a Church and a people who associated everything foreign with the Catholic enemy and the Antichrist. But it was happening, and it was accelerating.

When Alexis died in 1676 he left behind a far stronger, larger, more centralized Russia than the wreck Michael had inherited, and he also left a dangerous tangle. He had children by two different wives, and the two sets of heirs, the sickly sons of his first marriage and a vigorous, healthy boy from his second, would soon turn the court into a battlefield. Among the children of that second marriage was a son named Peter. The Russia he would inherit was whole again, Orthodox to its bones, suspicious of the West, chained to its serfs, and just beginning to crack open. He would take a crowbar to the crack and pry the whole thing apart.


# Chapter 12 — The Young Peter and the End of Muscovy

The last tsar of Muscovy was almost seven feet tall, could bend a silver plate with his bare hands, learned to pull a tooth and build a boat and fire a cannon, worked with his own calloused hands in foreign shipyards under a false name, and despised nearly everything about the holy, bearded, inward-looking old Russia he was born to rule. This chapter is about **Peter** (whom history calls Peter the Great), and about how, across a chaotic accession, a European apprenticeship, and a twenty-one-year war, he dragged Russia bodily toward the West, shaved its beards, moved its capital to a swamp on the Baltic, killed his own son for resisting him, and at the very end declared that the Tsardom of Muscovy this whole book has been building no longer existed, that in its place there now stood the Russian Empire. It is the chapter where Muscovy ends and the thing the rest of the world would have to reckon with for the next two centuries begins.

It began in blood, when Peter was nine. His father Alexis (Chapter 11) had left two sets of heirs by two wives, and the rival families behind them, the **Miloslavskys** (mee-loh-SLAHV-skees), kin of the first wife, and the **Naryshkins** (nah-RISH-kins), kin of the second and Peter's mother's people, went for each other's throats. Alexis's eldest surviving son, Fyodor III, reigned briefly and died in 1682, leaving the succession contested between the next Miloslavsky son, **Ivan**, who was chronically sick and possibly mentally impaired, and the bright, healthy Naryshkin boy Peter. The court first proclaimed Peter sole tsar, passing over the elder but disabled Ivan, and the Miloslavskys struck back. They turned to the **Streltsy** (STRELT-see), the hereditary corps of musketeers who garrisoned Moscow, a privileged, conservative, increasingly mutinous force, half soldiers and half a tradesmen's guild, who had grievances about pay and a deep attachment to the old ways. In May 1682 the Streltsy rose, stormed the Kremlin, and butchered Naryshkin relatives and ministers in front of the terrified nine-year-old Peter, in some accounts throwing his mother's people onto the pikes below while the boy watched. The compromise that ended the slaughter was bizarre: Ivan and Peter were made *co-tsars*, a double monarchy with a special two-seated throne, and real power went to the regent who could control the Streltsy.

That regent was Peter's formidable half-sister **Sophia** (soh-FEE-ah), a daughter of Alexis's first marriage, who became the first woman to rule Russia, governing for seven years in the name of the two boy tsars through her favorite, Prince Vasily Golitsyn. Peter, meanwhile, was pushed to the margins, allowed to grow up half-forgotten on the royal estates outside Moscow, and this neglect made him. Left to his own devices, the boy did two things that shaped everything after. He turned his childhood war-games into something real, drilling companies of **"play regiments"** out of stable boys and young nobles, regiments that he equipped with real weapons and real foreign instructors and that grew into the nucleus of a genuine, modern, loyal guard. And he began haunting the **German Quarter** (the *Nemetskaya Sloboda*), the walled suburb of Moscow where foreign merchants, soldiers, engineers, and adventurers, Dutch, German, Scots, English, were required to live, segregated from Orthodox Russians who regarded them as heretics. To the lonely, curious, neglected young tsar, the German Quarter was a window onto another world. He learned about ships and gunnery and mathematics and shipbuilding from its foreigners, drank and caroused with them, and absorbed a conviction that would define his life: that this Western world of skill and science and the sea was the future, and that holy old Moscow was hopelessly, dangerously backward.

In 1689, when Sophia's faction made a move to entrench her power permanently and perhaps push him aside for good, the seventeen-year-old Peter, warned in the night, fled to the fortified Trinity-Sergius Monastery (Chapter 3), rallied the army and the Church and his own loyal play regiments to his side, and faced Sophia down. Her support melted away. He forced her into a convent, the **Novodevichy Convent** outside Moscow that had long served as the gilded prison for inconvenient royal women, and took real power into his own hands. (His co-tsar, the harmless Ivan, lived on with the empty title until his death in 1696, after which Peter ruled alone.) The reign of Peter the Great had begun, and it began the way it would continue, with the young tsar reaching for ships and the sea.

His first great project was a war, and its first lesson was about water. In 1695 Peter attacked the Ottoman fortress of **Azov** (ah-ZOF), which commanded the mouth of the Don River and so the route to the Black Sea, and he failed, because he had no navy and the Turks simply resupplied the fortress from the water he could not control. Peter's response is the most characteristic thing about him: in a single furious winter he built a fleet, throwing up shipyards and conscripting labor and timber on a colossal scale, and in 1696 he came back and took Azov from the sea. The lesson, that Russia's landlocked, backward isolation could only be broken by ships and access to the sea, became the obsession of his life.

To learn how the West built its ships and its power, Peter did something no Russian ruler had ever done: he left the country and went to look. In 1697 and 1698 he traveled across Western Europe with a huge diplomatic mission called the **Grand Embassy**, traveling himself under the thin disguise of an ordinary member of the party, a seven-foot "private" whom no one could possibly mistake but everyone was obliged to pretend not to recognize. He worked with his own hands as a common laborer in the shipyards of the Dutch East India Company at Amsterdam and in the royal dockyards at Deptford in England, learning shipbuilding from the keel up. He toured workshops, hospitals, mints, museums, and anatomy theaters; he recruited hundreds of foreign shipwrights, officers, engineers, and craftsmen to come back and work for him; he hired the skills Russia did not have. The Grand Embassy was the hinge of his reign and arguably of Russian history: the first time a Russian sovereign went west to study the West, and he returned not merely impressed but determined to remake his country in its image, by force if necessary, and it would be necessary.

He came home in 1698 to a Streltsy mutiny, the same conservative musketeers who had traumatized his childhood, rising again in his absence. Peter's revenge was deliberately, theatrically terrible. He presided over a mass investigation under torture and then over hundreds of public executions, hanging the bodies of the Streltsy in rows through Moscow and leaving them up for months as a lesson, in places arranging them outside the windows of his sister Sophia's convent. He broke the Streltsy as a corps for good. The savagery was a message: the old Moscow, with its mutinous guards and its holy resistance to change, was finished, and the men who embodied it would die.

Then he started cutting beards. On his return Peter began, with his own hands at a reception, shearing off the long beards of his astonished noblemen, and he followed it with decrees: beards were to be shaved, and those who insisted on keeping them, for the Orthodox Russian beard was a deeply held religious symbol, a mark of the godly man made in God's image, must pay a special **beard tax** and carry a token proving it. He ordered the boyars and gentry out of their long, flowing Russian robes and into short Western "German" coats. He moved the Russian New Year from the first of September to the first of January and switched the count of years from the old reckoning since the supposed creation of the world (Russia thought it was the year 7208) to the European count from the birth of Christ (making it 1700). None of this was cosmetic to the people it was done to. The beard, the robe, the calendar were the visible skin of holy Orthodox Muscovy, and Peter was deliberately, provocatively flaying it off, announcing to a horrified, devout population that the old Russia was over and a new, European Russia was being imposed whether they liked it or not. To the traditional and the pious, especially the Old Believers (Chapter 11), Peter looked very much like the Antichrist himself, and many said so.

All of it, the beards and the calendar and the foreign experts and the conscripted fleets, was in the service of the great war that consumed the rest of his reign, the **Great Northern War** (1700 to 1721). The enemy was Sweden, then the dominant military power of northern Europe, which ringed the Baltic Sea and so blocked Russia from the western trade and the open ocean Peter craved. Peter joined a coalition with Denmark and Poland-Saxony to carve up the Swedish empire, expecting an easy win against a teenage king. He got the opposite. The Swedish king **Charles XII** (a brilliant, reckless young soldier-king, one of the great battlefield commanders of the age) knocked Denmark out of the war, then in November 1700 fell on Peter's much larger army besieging the Baltic town of **Narva** (NAR-vah) and, in a driving snowstorm, utterly routed it. The disaster at Narva should have ended Peter's ambitions. Instead it did for the army what Azov had done for the navy: it convinced him to tear down what he had and rebuild it on European lines, from scratch, with new artillery (cast, the story goes, partly from melted-down church bells), new drill, new foreign-trained officers, and a brutal new system of lifelong conscription that pressed peasant recruits into the ranks for life.

While Charles XII spent years bogged down in a war against Poland, chasing a crown across the map, Peter quietly seized the thing he actually wanted: the Baltic coast. In 1703, on a cold, marshy, half-drowned river delta newly taken from Sweden where the Neva River meets the sea, Peter founded a city, **Saint Petersburg**, named for his patron saint and built deliberately in the Western, not the Russian, style. It was his "window on the West," a deepwater port and a brand-new capital meant from the very start to replace Moscow and turn Russia's face permanently toward Europe. It was also built on bones: tens of thousands of conscripted laborers, soldiers, peasants, Swedish prisoners, died of disease, exposure, and exhaustion driving piles into the freezing mud, and the city rose, as a Russian saying had it, on a foundation of corpses. Peter loved it more than anything, and in time he moved the court, the government, and the capital itself there, abandoning holy Moscow for a European city of stone and canals.

The war's hinge came in 1709. Charles XII, having finally turned east to finish Russia off, plunged deep into Ukraine, gambling on the defection of the Ukrainian Cossack **hetman Ivan Mazepa** (mah-ZEH-pah), who abandoned Peter for the Swedes at the last moment, a betrayal that made Mazepa a national hero to later Ukrainians and the very type of the traitor to Russians (and, not coincidentally, gave Peter the pretext to crush what was left of Cossack self-rule in Ukraine). But Mazepa brought far fewer men than promised, the Russian winter and scorched-earth tactics wrecked the Swedish army before it ever reached battle, and in the summer of 1709 Peter's rebuilt, re-drilled army met Charles's starving, outnumbered Swedes at the **Battle of Poltava** (pol-TAH-vah) and destroyed them. Poltava is one of the decisive battles of European history. It broke Swedish military power, ended Sweden's century as a great power, and announced to a startled continent that Russia, the backward forest tsardom, was now a first-rank European military power. Charles XII fled into Ottoman exile; the war would drag on for another twelve years, but its outcome was settled on that field in Ukraine.

Not everything went Peter's way, and he could blunder badly. Drunk on Poltava, he marched south against the Ottomans in 1711 and walked his army straight into an Ottoman trap on the **Pruth River**, where, surrounded and out of options, he had to buy his army's escape by handing Azov, his first conquest, back to the Turks. It was a humbling reminder that he was not invincible. But it did not touch the main prize. The remaking of Russia ground on regardless. Peter abolished the office of **Patriarch** entirely: when the old patriarch died in 1700, Peter simply refused to appoint a successor and eventually replaced the headship of the Church with a state-run committee of bishops, the **Holy Synod**, run by a government official, finishing the subordination of Church to crown that Alexis had begun (Chapter 11) and making the Russian Church an arm of the state outright. He reorganized the government, founded Russia's first newspaper and simplified its alphabet, opened schools, and recast the entire nobility's relationship to the state with the **Table of Ranks** (issued in 1722, the year after the imperial proclamation that closes this book, but the capstone of everything described here), a ladder of fourteen graded service positions in which a man's status depended not on the antiquity of his family but on the rank he reached in the army, navy, or civil service, theoretically allowing a commoner who served well to rise into the nobility, and binding the entire gentry to lifelong compulsory service to the crown. Birth gave way, at least on paper, to service. The whole nobility became, in effect, servants of the state.

The cost of all this fell on the man who would have inherited it. Peter's son and heir, the **Tsarevich Alexei** (ah-lek-SAY), a soft, pious, frightened young man, hated his father's furious project and everything it stood for, longing instead for the quiet old Orthodox Russia his father was destroying. Father and son became enemies. In 1716 Alexei fled abroad, was tracked down and lured back to Russia with promises of mercy, and was then put on trial for treason, interrogated under torture, and condemned. He died in prison in 1718, almost certainly from the torture, just before his execution could be carried out. Peter had effectively killed his own son rather than let him undo the new Russia, an echo, terrible and deliberate, of Ivan the Terrible striking down his own heir a century and a half before (Chapter 8), except that where Ivan's blow doomed his dynasty, Peter's was the calculated sacrifice of a son to a project. The new Russia would have no rightful heir to inherit the old one's resentments.

The end came in 1721. After twenty-one years, the Great Northern War finally closed with the **Treaty of Nystad** (NEE-stad), signed in Finland, by which an exhausted Sweden ceded to Russia the Baltic provinces, Estonia, Livonia, Ingria, the long sea coast with its ports, the prize Peter had fought two decades to win. Russia now had its window on the West, a navy, a European army, a European capital, and a seat among the great powers. To mark the victory and the transformation, the Senate, the new governing council Peter had created, formally hailed him as **"Emperor of All Russia"** and "the Great" and "Father of the Fatherland." Peter accepted the imperial title. With that, the Tsardom of Muscovy was declared at an end, and in its place stood the **Russian Empire**.

It is worth being clear about what the title change actually meant, because it is the closing note of this whole book. *Tsar*, the word Ivan IV had taken with such pride in 1547 (Chapter 7), came from "Caesar" and claimed an emperor's standing borrowed from fallen Byzantium and the dream of the Third Rome, a sacred, Orthodox, eastward-looking claim. *Emperor* (in the Latin-derived form Peter chose, *imperator*) was a deliberately Western, secular, European title, a claim staked not on inheriting Constantinople's holy crown but on standing as an equal among the kings and emperors of Europe. The change of word was the change of the whole project in miniature: Russia would no longer define itself as the last lonely guardian of true Orthodoxy facing inward against a heretic world, but as a European great power facing outward, hungry for the West's skills and respect even as it remained autocratic to the bone, with its peasants still chained in serfdom and its ruler still answerable to no one.

That is where Muscovite Russia ends and the Russian Empire begins, and the rest of that story, two centuries of empire reaching from Poland to the Pacific until it broke in revolution, belongs to the next book (Russian Empire). What the four and a half centuries in this one had built was the thing the world would now have to deal with: a vast, centralized, autocratic, Orthodox state, made in the forests of the northeast out of a small swampy town that survived by collecting the Mongols' taxes, suspicious of Europe and desperate to master it at the same time, the Russia that every later Russian state, the empire, the Soviet Union, the one that exists now, has in some sense been arguing with ever since.
