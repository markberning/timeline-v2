# Chapter 4 — Kilwa and the Gold of Sofala

There is a particular kind of wealth that comes not from making anything or growing anything but from standing in the one place everything has to pass through and charging it to go by. Venice did it with the spice route. The Sound tolls of Denmark did it with the Baltic. For roughly two centuries a single small island off the southern Tanzanian coast did it with the gold of an entire subcontinent's interior, and the building that money paid for was, until the Europeans came, the largest stone structure anyone had raised anywhere in Africa south of the Sahara. This chapter is about how Kilwa, a town that had not even been the greatest city on its own coast a century earlier (Chapter 3), made itself the richest by seizing a gate, and about being honest, in the way this book always tries to be, about how thin and partisan our inside evidence for the story actually is.

Begin with the geography, because the monopoly is unintelligible without it, and geography is the one part of this story that does not lie.

The gold did not come from the coast. It came from far inland, mined and panned on the high plateau between the Zambezi and Limpopo rivers, in the country whose greatest monument, the stone-walled capital later called **Great Zimbabwe**, gave the whole region its modern name. That plateau was a major gold-producing zone of the medieval world, worked by African societies far in the interior, and the metal moved from there down the river and overland routes toward the Indian Ocean, reaching the sea at a single low, swampy, unglamorous funnel of a port near the mouth of the rivers: **Sofala**, on the coast of what is now Mozambique (introduced in Chapter 2 as the gate through which the interior's gold reached the ocean). Sofala mattered for one reason and it was decisive. It was the place where the gold of the interior changed hands from the people who carried it out of Africa to the people who carried it across the ocean. Whoever controlled the seaward side of that exchange controlled the single richest commodity the entire coast had to offer, and could set the price of it for half the Indian Ocean.

Here is where the geography turns into money. Sofala sits a long way south, past the comfortable monsoon zone, in difficult sailing waters, far from the main run of coastal trade. A ship coming down the coast on the northeast monsoon (Chapter 2) could reach Sofala, but the practical, regular, profitable way to work the gold was not for every Arabian or Indian merchant to sail the whole length of the coast to a malarial river-mouth and back. It was to have a relay. And the natural northern end of that relay, the last great harbor with the wealth, the ships, the warehouses, and the position to gather the ocean trade and hand it south in exchange for gold coming north, was Kilwa. Kilwa sat at the hinge: close enough to Sofala to run a dedicated shuttle down to it and back within the sailing season, far enough north to be a comfortable terminus for the main monsoon trade arriving from Arabia, the Gulf, and India. A merchant from Aden did not have to risk the long haul to Sofala; he sold his cloth and beads at Kilwa and bought gold there. A trader working the Sofala run did not have to deal with the whole ocean; he dealt with Kilwa. Kilwa inserted itself as the single obligatory middleman between the gold and the world, and that choke-point position, not any gold of its own, is the entire foundation of everything else in this chapter.

The mechanism by which it worked deserves to be spelled out rather than gestured at, because "Kilwa controlled the gold trade" is a result and the book's rule is that results need their machinery shown. The arrangement, as far as the sources and archaeology let it be reconstructed, was an exchange relationship, effectively a working contract: Sofala's gold passed up to Kilwa's merchants, and what went back down to Sofala in return was not coin but goods the interior wanted, above all imported cloth and glass beads, the very things the ocean trade brought to Kilwa from India and the Gulf. Cloth and beads went south to Sofala and inland toward the gold country; gold came north to Kilwa and out into the Indian Ocean; Kilwa took its cut in the middle of both flows. This is why the monopoly was a *relationship* and not just a tax: it depended on Kilwa being the place where the ocean's manufactured goods and the interior's gold were matched up, and on Kilwa keeping any rival from setting up the same matching service. Hold that picture precisely, because its fragility is the hinge of the whole later story: the entire structure rested on one exchange working at one funnel, and a power that could seize Sofala itself could break Kilwa at a stroke. Two centuries later Portugal would do exactly that (Chapter 6), and the contract, and Kilwa with it, would snap. But through the twelfth, thirteenth, and fourteenth centuries it held, and it made Kilwa stupendously rich.

Kilwa took control of this system over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, displacing Mogadishu's older northern primacy (Chapter 3) not by conquering it but by capturing the thing Mogadishu had never controlled: the southern gold gate. By the mid-thirteenth century Kilwa was a **sultanate**, a Muslim monarchy under a sultan, whose writ over the southern coast and the Sofala connection made it the single most powerful Swahili state and the one outsiders meant when they spoke of the coast's fabulous wealth. The towns of the coast had always been independent, competing city-states, never a single kingdom; Kilwa never ruled the whole coast and never tried to. What it ruled was the gold, and in a trading world the gold was sovereignty enough.

It is at exactly this point, Kilwa at its richest, that the story's dynasty changes hands, and the change is the clearest real-world demonstration of something Chapter 3 set up in the abstract. Around the year 1300 the old ruling line at Kilwa, the **Shirazi** house that traced itself to the legendary Persian prince Ali ibn al-Hassan (Chapter 3), was displaced by a new family, the **Mahdali** (mah-DAH-lee), also known from the title of one of its sultans as the Abuʾl-Mawahib dynasty. This was a genuine dynastic break, a real change of ruling family at the top of the richest state on the coast, and what the new line did about its pedigree is the tell. The Mahdali did not claim descent from Persia. They claimed to be **sharifs**, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad himself, through his daughter Fatima, the most prestigious lineage anywhere in the Islamic world, outranking any Persian prince. Look at what just happened with the eyes Chapter 3 trained. The old dynasty had legitimized itself with the best pedigree available when *it* rose: descent from royal Shiraz, the Shirazi claim. The new dynasty legitimized itself with an even better one: descent from the Prophet. The pedigrees track the politics. Each ruling family reached for the most prestigious Islamic ancestry it could plausibly assert at the moment it needed legitimacy, exactly as this book argued the Shirazi tradition was a claim to standing rather than a passenger manifest. The Mahdali takeover is the Shirazi argument proven on a real case: in 1300 you can watch a new African dynasty pick a more impressive Islamic genealogy off the shelf because it suited its position, not because it had just sailed in from anywhere.

Under the Mahdali, Kilwa reached its zenith, and it has a face. The sultan who presided over the height was **al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman** (al-HAH-san ib-un soo-lay-MAHN), and we know him with unusual vividness for a reason that is itself a landmark of this whole book: in 1331 the greatest traveler of the medieval world walked into his city and wrote him down. **Ibn Battuta** (the Moroccan jurist whose name and pronunciation the framing of this book has already given, the man whose three decades of travels across the Islamic world from Mali to China are among history's great eyewitness records) sailed down the coast in 1331 and reached Kilwa, and his account is the great inside-the-zenith witness, dealt with fully in the next chapter. What matters here is the portrait he left of the sultan: al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman as a ruler praised for his military raids against the unconverted of the interior, for his religious learning and humility, and above all for an almost ostentatious generosity to the poor and to visiting holy men, a king who, the traveler reports approvingly, would give away whatever was in his hands. Treat the glowing tone with the caution this book applies to all colorful single-source detail; a famous foreign jurist being lavishly hosted is not a neutral observer, and a reputation for piety and largesse is exactly what a sultan would stage for such a guest. But that the Mahdali court was rich enough, and cosmopolitan enough, to host and impress the most widely traveled man of the fourteenth century is the solid fact under the flattering anecdote, and the buildings prove the money was real.

The money became stone, and the stone is the chapter's payoff. Kilwa at its height built on a scale nothing else in sub-Saharan Africa matched, and the buildings need to be described with their motivation, not just their dimensions, because architecture in this book always gets its *why*.

The first is the **Great Mosque of Kilwa**, the city's congregational Friday mosque (Chapter 3 explained the Friday mosque and its mihrab), enlarged repeatedly over the centuries until it was among the finest medieval buildings anywhere south of the Sahara. What made it remarkable, and difficult, was its roof. An ordinary coastal mosque was a flat-roofed hall, its ceiling held up by a forest of closely spaced timber or stone posts because coral rag and lime, while strong in compression, cannot span much distance unsupported. Kilwa's builders instead roofed a large part of the Great Mosque with rows of domes and **masonry vaulting**, curved stone roofs, the dome a rounded cap and the barrel vault a continuous half-cylinder, both engineered so that the weight of the roof pushes outward and down through arches and thick walls rather than needing a post under every few feet. Vaulting in coral and lime was genuinely hard. The material is rough and weak in tension, the spans are unforgiving, and a vault that is slightly wrong does not sag; it collapses. To roof a building this way, on coral pillars, was to demonstrate a command of masonry engineering and a surplus of skilled labor and money that an ordinary town could not muster. That was the point of doing it. A domed and vaulted mosque was not just more beautiful and more open inside than a flat-roofed one; it was an unmistakable, permanent advertisement that Kilwa could afford to build what no rival could. The roof was the gold, set in stone, pointed at heaven.

The greater statement was secular. Above the harbor, on the cliff at the edge of the island, the Mahdali sultans raised **Husuni Kubwa** (hoo-SOO-nee KOO-bwah, roughly "the great fortified house"), a single sprawling complex that was palace, audience hall, and trade warehouse in one: well over a hundred rooms around courtyards, a great octagonal stepped bathing pool, terraces stepping down toward the sea, and storerooms built to hold the goods the gold trade ran on. Husuni Kubwa was, on the best current understanding, the largest single building in sub-Saharan Africa before the arrival of the Europeans. A "first" or "largest" claim in this book always comes with the world set beside it, because the size means nothing without the comparison, so here it is. When Husuni Kubwa went up, in roughly the first decades of the fourteenth century, the **Mali Empire** of West Africa was at its height under Mansa Musa, vastly richer overall than Kilwa, but its monumental tradition was the great mud-brick mosque, a different and magnificent architecture in a different material, not a single stone palace of this scale. Far to the interior, the dry-stone walls of Great Zimbabwe, the capital of the very gold country whose metal paid for Husuni Kubwa, were rising at about the same time, Africa's other great medieval stone achievement, and a reminder that the two ends of the gold chain were both building monuments out of their share of it. In Christian Europe of the early 1300s, the comparable effort was the great Gothic cathedral, taller and more elaborate but the product of far larger and richer kingdoms. The honest version of the superlative is therefore narrow and exact: Husuni Kubwa was not the grandest building in the medieval world, or even in medieval Africa. It was the largest single stone building in the whole of Africa below the Sahara before 1500, and an African coastal sultanate built it out of gold it neither mined nor owned but simply stood in the way of. Beside it stood a second great structure, **Husuni Ndogo** ("the small fortified house"), a large rectangular walled enclosure whose purpose is still genuinely unsettled, proposed as a fortified market, a barracks, a caravanserai, or a ceremonial space, with no consensus. It is honest to say we do not know what Husuni Ndogo was for; what is clear is that it belonged to the same single burst of monumental construction, the moment Kilwa's gold money turned, briefly and spectacularly, into architecture.

Kilwa made one more statement that almost no other power south of the Sahara made, and it is small enough to hold in your hand. From as early as the eleventh century, and prolifically under the later sultans, Kilwa struck its own **copper coinage**, small pieces bearing a sultan's name and a pious rhyming phrase, issued in the city's own name on the city's own authority. The cross-civ comparison this book demands at every "rare" is the whole significance of the fact. Minting coinage was extraordinarily uncommon in sub-Saharan Africa before modern times. The Mali Empire, far wealthier, was the source of much of the medieval world's gold but did not issue a coinage of its own; the gold trade of West Africa moved as dust and bars and was minted into coin elsewhere, in North Africa and beyond. Great Zimbabwe issued no coins. The Aksumite kingdom of the Ethiopian highlands had struck coins many centuries earlier, in late antiquity, and is the main earlier African exception, but by the medieval period Kilwa was one of the very few states anywhere south of the Sahara putting its own name on its own money. A coinage is a claim: it says a state is permanent enough, confident enough, and commercially developed enough that its sovereign's name is worth stamping on metal that will circulate in his name after he is dead. For a Swahili sultanate to make that claim, when the far larger empires of the African interior did not, is one more piece of evidence in this book's standing argument: the coast was not a backwater that received civilization from outside; it was a place generating its own instruments of statehood.

How far Kilwa's small change actually traveled is illustrated by one extraordinary, almost unbelievable find that this book includes precisely because it is the kind of fact that makes the scale of the Indian Ocean network suddenly visible. Kilwa copper coins have been recovered far from the coast, including, on the Marchinbar Islands off the far northern coast of Australia, a small handful of Kilwa pieces, among the very few medieval African coins ever found in Australia. How exactly they got there is debated and may never be settled. Carried by Indian Ocean traders down the Indonesian routes, lost from a much later ship, dropped by who knows whom: the chains of transmission are uncertain and the find is genuinely puzzling. But the bare fact stands, and it is the right note to measure the network by: money minted on a small coral island off Tanzania, bearing the name of a Swahili sultan, ended up on a beach on the other side of the Indian Ocean. Whatever the exact route, the coast Kilwa anchored was wired into a trading world that reached, at its furthest stretch, almost to the edge of the Pacific.

The chapter has to end where the book's introduction insisted every claim about the coast must be checked: against the evidence. Almost everything inside this story, the dynasties, the Shirazi prince, the Mahdali takeover, the founding legends, comes down to us through one internal narrative source, and that source has to be introduced honestly the way this book introduces every writer it leans on. It is the **Kilwa Chronicle**, the coast's principal inside account of its own past, and it has three problems stacked on top of each other. It is *late*: it was set down centuries after most of the events it describes, in the early sixteenth century, around the time the Portuguese arrived. It is *doubled and inconsistent*: it survives in two versions that do not agree, an Arabic manuscript and a different account preserved in Portuguese by the chronicler João de Barros, and where they diverge there is often no way to decide between them. And it is *partisan*: it is a dynastic chronicle, written for and about a ruling house, telling the past the way the rulers of its own day wanted it told, which is precisely why its Shiraz origins, its tidy royal genealogies, and its providential successions read like the legitimizing documents they are rather than like a record. None of this means the Kilwa Chronicle is worthless; it is invaluable, and much of the dynastic skeleton in this chapter rests on it. It means that where this chapter has said "the chronicle says" it has meant exactly that, and where archaeology agrees with the chronicle the ground is firmer, and where the chronicle is alone the ground is a story a dynasty told about itself. (The archaeology here means Neville Chittick's large excavations at Kilwa in the 1960s, the dig that set the city's chronology on a modern footing; Chittick was introduced in Chapter 1.) The honest position on Kilwa's golden age is that the buildings are certain and magnificent and the narrative is partial and self-serving, and a careful reader holds both of those at once.

By the middle of the fourteenth century Kilwa was the richest and most monumental city on the East African coast, its sultan a man the most famous traveler in the world had praised, its mosque vaulted in stone, its gold money found a hemisphere away. It was also, the next chapter will show, only one jewel in a whole necklace of cosmopolitan city-states then reaching their height together, and the same Ibn Battuta who left us al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman left us the rest of that world too. The zenith of the entire coast, seen through the eyes of the man who actually walked it, is Chapter 5.
