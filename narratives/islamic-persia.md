# Islamic Persia — How the Conquered Conquered Their Conquerors

**Goal:** Narrative for the 850-year hole in the Persian thread, from the murder of the last Sasanian king of kings in 651 to the coronation of Shah Ismail in 1501. This is the story of a superpower that lost everything and then won it back sideways. The Arabs erased the Persian Empire as a state in a single generation, and for a century Persia was a conquered province paying the poll tax and watching its temples close. Then the strangest thing in Persian history happened: the defeated civilization quietly took over the inside of the victorious one. Persian secretaries ran the caliph's tax registers; Persian generals (Abu Muslim) made the revolution that put the Abbasids on the throne; a new written language, New Persian, was born in Arabic letters and then used, in the Shu'ubiyya, to argue that Persians were nobody's cultural inferiors. At Bukhara the Samanids bankrolled a renaissance and Ferdowsi spent thirty years writing the Shahnameh, the national epic that almost single-handedly saved the language. The Buyids took Baghdad and kept the caliph as a pet. The Seljuk Turks arrived off the steppe and, instead of imposing Turkish, ruled in Persian — their vizier Nizam al-Mulk built the madrasa system and wrote the great manual of kingship, while Omar Khayyam fixed the calendar, al-Ghazali reconciled law with mysticism, and the Assassins killed from their mountain castles. Then Persian Sufism flowered into the most beloved poetry the language ever produced — Attar, Rumi, Saadi, Hafez. And then it was all smashed. The Mongols came in 1219 and committed the worst destruction in Persian memory, sacking Baghdad and killing the caliph in 1258. But within forty years the Mongol Ilkhans had converted to Islam and become patrons of Persian art and the first world history. Timur smashed it again and his heirs built the dazzling courts of Samarkand and Herat — Ulugh Beg's observatory, Bihzad's miniatures. And in 1501 a teenage warlord-mystic named Ismail reunited the country and made it Shia, setting the stage for the Safavids and modern Iran. Twice militarily annihilated, twice the absorber of its annihilators: that is the through-line, and it is the answer to why there is still such a thing as Persia.

A note on names and pronunciation. This is a story full of Persian, Arabic, and Turkish names that don't look the way they sound, so a few signposts before we start. **Sasanian** (suh-SAH-nee-un) is the pre-Islamic Persian empire the Arabs conquered. The man who wrote the national epic is **Ferdowsi** (fer-DOW-see), and the epic is the **Shahnameh** (shah-nah-MEH), the "Book of Kings." The Persian dynasty at Bukhara is the **Samanid** (sah-MAH-nid); the Shia dynasty that took Baghdad is the **Buyid** (BOO-yid); the Turkish dynasty that ruled in Persian is the **Seljuk** (SEL-jook). A **vizier** (vih-ZEER) is a chief minister, effectively a prime minister — the great one here is **Nizam al-Mulk** (nih-ZAHM al-MULK). A **madrasa** (muh-DRAH-suh) is a religious college. The Mongol state in Persia is the **Ilkhanate** (ILL-kah-nayt), "il" meaning a subordinate khanate. The brittle empire the Mongols destroyed was **Khwarazm** (kwah-RAZM), ruled by the Khwarazm-Shahs. A **qanat** (kuh-NAHT) is an underground irrigation channel. The Sufi mystics are **Rumi** (ROO-mee), **Attar** (ah-TAR), **Saadi** (sah-DEE), and **Hafez** (HAH-fez). The militant Turkmen warriors of the final chapter are the **Qizilbash** (kih-zil-BAHSH), the "red-heads," named for their caps. Place names: the great cities are **Bukhara** (boo-KHA-rah), **Samarkand** (SAM-ar-kand), **Nishapur** (nee-shah-POOR), **Isfahan** (iss-fah-HAHN), **Tabriz** (tah-BREEZ), **Shiraz** (shee-RAHZ), and **Herat** (heh-RAHT). Dates are CE throughout; this whole story is post-conquest, so there are no BCE dates to worry about. Miles come first, kilometers in parentheses. Two words deserve care from the outset because they are easy to throw around carelessly: **Persian** here means the language and the culture, not necessarily an ethnic group — much of the "Persianate" world was ruled by Turks and Mongols who adopted Persian; and **Iranian** is the broader family the language belongs to. We'll keep "Persia" for the place and "Persian" for the culture, and flag where the people doing the ruling were not ethnically Persian at all, which is most of the time.

A note on "first" claims and on how we know any of this. As in every timeline in this project, "first" means "first surviving evidence of," and for Islamic Persia the sources have their own strong slants the reader should keep in view. The single most important Persian text, Ferdowsi's **Shahnameh**, is a poem, not a chronicle — it preserves national memory and self-image, and it is priceless for that, but it is mythology and dynastic legend as much as history, especially for the deep past it claims to record. The Persian chroniclers who matter most for the medieval centuries often wrote for the very regimes that had conquered them: **Ata-Malik Juvayni** wrote his vivid history of the Mongol conquests while serving the Mongols, and **Rashid al-Din**, whose Jami al-Tawarikh is the first attempt at a true world history, was the chief minister of the converted Mongol Ilkhans and wrote to glorify them. The death tolls those chroniclers give for the Mongol sack of cities — hundreds of thousands, even over a million at a single city — are almost certainly inflated, products of a literary tradition of catastrophe; we will use them to convey the scale of the trauma in Persian memory while flagging that the real numbers, though still appalling, were smaller. The Arab geographers and the Shu'ubiyya writers each had an axe to grind about whether Arabs or Persians were superior. And several of the most colorful stories in this book — Ferdowsi cheated of his reward, the "Old Man of the Mountain" drugging assassins in a secret garden — are legends that grew up later, sometimes in European retellings like Marco Polo's, and the text will say so each time rather than passing them off as fact. Religiously contested matters get the same care: the Sunni-Shia split, the Ismailis, and the exact moment "Persian Islam" became "Shia Persia" are handled with the hedging they deserve, because confident storytelling about contested religion is exactly how history goes wrong.

# Chapter 1 — The Empire That Vanished

In the year 651, somewhere near the city of Merv in what is now Turkmenistan, a man was murdered in a miller's hut for the jewels and fine clothes he was wearing. The miller did not know who his guest was, or perhaps did not care. The dead man was **Yazdegerd III** (yaz-deh-GERD), and he was the **shahanshah** (shah-an-SHAH), the "king of kings," the title the rulers of Persia had carried for over a thousand years. He was the last of the **Sasanians** (suh-SAH-nee-uns), the dynasty that had ruled Persia for four hundred years and had spent most of those four hundred years fighting the Roman Empire to a standstill. He was running east, away from the Arab armies that had taken everything else, hoping to raise troops somewhere beyond the next river. He died penniless in a stranger's house, and with him died not just a man but an institution. The Persian Empire, the superpower of the ancient Near East, the thing that had stood opposite Rome as one of the two great powers of the known world, simply ended. There would not be a Persian empire again, ruled by Persians, in the old imperial style, for a very long time. And that is where this book begins: not with a rise, but with the most complete fall a great civilization has ever suffered and survived.

To feel how strange that fall was, you have to know what had just collapsed. The **Sasanian Empire** (the last pre-Islamic Persian state, ruling from roughly 224 to 651 from a capital on the Tigris) was not some fragile thing waiting to topple. It ran from Mesopotamia (the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, roughly modern Iraq) eastward across the Iranian plateau to the edges of Central Asia and down into the Persian Gulf. Its religion was **Zoroastrianism** (zor-oh-AS-tree-an-ism), one of the oldest organized faiths in the world, built around a cosmic struggle between a god of light and truth, **Ahura Mazda**, and a principle of darkness and lies, with fire kept burning in temples as the symbol of the divine. Its kings hunted lions, sat on jeweled thrones, and presided over a court so elaborate that its ceremonies, its titles, and its very table manners would be copied by everyone who conquered it for the next eight hundred years. Just a generation before the end, the Sasanians and the **Byzantines** (the eastern, Greek-speaking continuation of the Roman Empire, ruled from Constantinople) had fought a war so total, so exhausting, that it nearly destroyed them both. The Persians had reached the walls of Constantinople; the Byzantines had marched into the Persian heartland. When the dust settled around 628, both empires were bled white, broke, and full of resentful, unpaid soldiers. Neither of them was watching the desert to the south.

Out of that desert came something no one had planned for. In the Arabian Peninsula, the merchant-prophet **Muhammad** had founded a new religion, **Islam** (a monotheist faith centered on submission to a single God, *Allah*, and on the revelations Muhammad delivered as God's final messenger), and by his death in 632 he had united the fractious Arab tribes under it for the first time in their history. Those tribes, newly welded into a single fighting force and lit up with religious conviction, looked north at the two great empires and found them both wrecked. What followed is one of the fastest conquests in recorded history. The Arabs did not slowly nibble at Persia's edges. They broke it open in a handful of battles inside a single decade.

The first hammer-blow fell in 636 at **al-Qadisiyyah** (al-kah-dih-SEE-yah), in the flat country of southern Iraq. An Arab army under the general **Saad ibn Abi Waqqas** met the main Sasanian field force — the empire's central army, the one that was supposed to be unbeatable — and over several days of brutal fighting, including a stretch later remembered as the "night of clamor," the Persian line broke. The Sasanian commander was killed, and with the field army shattered, the road to the imperial heartland lay open. In 637 the Arabs took **Ctesiphon** (TES-ih-fon), the great Sasanian capital on the Tigris, the richest city of the empire. The plunder they carried off became legendary: the chronicles describe a single ceremonial carpet so enormous and so encrusted with jewels that it represented an entire garden, paths and streams and flowers worked in gold thread and gemstones, and the Arab soldiers, having no use for it and no way to keep it whole, cut it into pieces and shared it out. Whether the carpet was quite that fabulous we can't say for certain — the story comes down through writers who loved a good treasure — but the point it carries is sound: the conquerors had walked into a level of wealth they had never imagined, and they took it apart with their swords.

The empire was wounded but not yet dead. The killing blow came in 642 at **Nahavand** (nah-hah-VAND), in the Zagros mountains (the long rugged range that walls off the Mesopotamian plain from the high Iranian plateau). The Sasanians scraped together one last great army for a stand, and the Arabs destroyed it. The conquerors themselves called this battle the "Victory of Victories," and the name was earned: after Nahavand there was no Sasanian field army left to speak of, no central force that could meet the invaders in open battle. From here the conquest became a long mopping-up, province by province, city by city, as Yazdegerd fled eastward ahead of it, always raising one more local army, always losing, until the miller's hut near Merv. His son **Peroz III** (peh-ROZE) fled all the way to the court of **Tang China** (the great Chinese empire of the day, the other superpower at the far end of the Silk Road) in 661, where he was given a grand-sounding title and the fiction of a "Persian governorate" in exile, and spent his life lobbying a Chinese emperor for the troops to take his kingdom back. They never came. The Sasanian state was finished, and the dream of restoring it died on the far side of Asia, an empire-in-exile that consisted of a few homesick aristocrats at a foreign court.

Now comes the first of the two great surprises that organize this entire book, and it is worth slowing down for, because everything else builds on it. You would expect that when a thousand-year-old civilization is conquered this completely, it disappears — absorbed, erased, turned into a memory and a ruin like so many others. That is roughly what happened to a dozen peoples the Arabs conquered. It is not what happened to Persia. Instead, the conquered began, almost immediately, to make themselves indispensable to the conquerors, and over the next century they would quietly take over the inside of the empire that had defeated them. The mechanism for that takeover started with the most boring thing imaginable: paperwork.

Here is the problem the conquerors faced, and it is a problem every fast conqueror faces. The Arabs had a brilliant army and a powerful new faith, but they did not have a state. They had no bureaucracy, no system of tax registers, no chanceries full of trained clerks, no institutional machinery for governing millions of farmers across thousands of miles. They had conquered one of the most sophisticated administrative states on earth, and they had no idea how to run it. The Persians, on the other hand, had been running exactly this kind of state for centuries. The empire was full of **katibs** (KAH-tibs), the secretaries and scribes — a hereditary professional class who knew how to assess a harvest, calculate what a province owed, keep the books in the old Sasanian way, and make the revenue flow. The conquerors needed them, and so, in the practical bargain that defines this whole early period, the Persian secretaries kept their jobs. For decades after the conquest the tax registers of the new empire were still being kept in Middle Persian, the language of the people who had lost the war, because the people who had won it could not read their own accounts. This is the first thread, the small one that becomes the large one: the conquered ran the conqueror's state because the conqueror could not.

Religion was a slower, stranger story, and it is one the popular imagination usually gets backward. The Arab conquest did not turn Persia Muslim overnight, or anything close to it. For the first century and more, the overwhelming majority of Persians stayed Zoroastrian, and they were allowed to. Islam, like the other monotheist faiths it grew alongside, made a category for the conquered: a non-Muslim who belonged to a recognized religion of scripture could keep his faith, his temples, and his community, provided he accepted Muslim rule, kept his head down politically, and paid a special tax. That tax was the **jizya** (JIZ-yah), a poll tax (a tax levied per head, on each adult non-Muslim man) that was the price of practicing another religion under Islamic rule. The arrangement was, for the conquerors, an excellent deal: the non-Muslim majority paid more than Muslims did, so a province full of Zoroastrians paying the jizya was a province full of revenue, and there was no rush to convert anyone. Conversion, when it came, was a slow drift over many generations, driven less by preaching than by economics and social pressure — a convert stopped paying the extra tax, gained access to the privileged ruling community, and over a century or two the balance of the population tipped. There was no single moment when Persia "became Muslim." There was a long, grinding, undramatic shift, and it is exactly the kind of mechanism this book cares about: not a flip but a tide.

The Zoroastrians, watching that tide come in, did something defensive and very human. As their numbers slipped and their position weakened, the Zoroastrian priests, who for centuries had preserved their scriptures largely by memory and oral recitation, began to write everything down. Through the later seventh and eighth centuries and beyond, they compiled and codified their religious texts, their law, and their cosmology in Middle Persian, an act of preservation under pressure — getting the faith onto the page before the people who carried it in their heads were gone. This is one of the few places where we can watch a religion bracing for its own decline in real time. The faith never vanished — there are Zoroastrians today, in Iran and especially among the **Parsis** of India, descended from communities that emigrated to escape exactly this slow squeeze — but it shrank from the religion of an empire to the religion of a minority, and it did so over centuries, not years. One piece of it, though, refused to shrink at all. The ancient Persian spring festival of **Nowruz** (no-ROOZ), the New Year celebrated at the spring equinox, a Zoroastrian holiday older than Islam itself, was simply never suppressed. It survived the conquest, survived conversion, and is still the great national holiday of Iran and the Persian-speaking world today — an unbroken thread running straight back through the Islamic centuries to the empire that vanished.

The smooth bargain — Persians run the books, everyone pays their tax, conversion drifts along — held up only so long before it cracked, and the crack ran along a fault line built into the system from the start. The problem was the converts. Islam, in principle, taught that all believers were equal before God. But the early empire was run by and for Arabs, and a non-Arab who converted to Islam did not become an equal Arab Muslim. He became a **mawla** (MAW-lah; plural **mawali**, maw-AH-lee), a "client" — a non-Arab Muslim attached as a junior dependent to an Arab tribe, a second-class member of the faith he had just joined. The mawali were taxed when they were not supposed to be, kept out of the best positions, paid less in the army, and generally treated as the religious inferiors of people whose only qualification was being born Arab. For a proud Persian who had embraced Islam in good faith, expecting the equality the religion promised, this was an intolerable insult, and the resentment piled up fastest exactly where you would expect: in **Khurasan** (khoo-rah-SAHN), the great eastern province of Persia (covering parts of modern northeastern Iran, Afghanistan, and Central Asia), far from the Arab heartland, heavily settled by both Arab colonists and Persian converts, and full of men with grievances and weapons.

Khurasan became the seedbed of a revolution, and the man who lit it was a Persian of mysterious origins named **Abu Muslim** (ah-boo MUS-lim). In 747 he raised the **black banners** in Khurasan — black being the chosen color of the rebellion — and gathered an army of the disaffected: mawali Persians who wanted the equality they had been promised, and Arabs who had their own quarrels with the ruling dynasty. That dynasty was the **Umayyads** (oo-MY-yads), the family that had held the caliphate (the office of **caliph**, kay-LIF — the successor to Muhammad as the political and religious leader of the whole Muslim community) since the 660s, ruling from Damascus in Syria as a frankly Arab aristocracy. Abu Muslim's army marched west under its black flags, and in 750 it won. The Umayyads were overthrown, hunted down, and very nearly exterminated, and a new family seized the caliphate: the **Abbasids** (uh-BASS-ids), who claimed descent from an uncle of the Prophet. This was the **Abbasid Revolution**, and it is the hinge on which the whole early story turns. The center of gravity of the Islamic world swung east, away from Arab Syria and toward Persian Iraq and Khurasan. The new empire would be more cosmopolitan, more Persian-flavored, more open to the conquered peoples — and it had been put on its throne by an army raised in Persia, led by a Persian, under a Persian grievance.

The Abbasids understood exactly where their power had come from, and they planted their capital to show it. In 762 the caliph **al-Mansur** (al-man-SOOR) founded a brand-new city on the Tigris and called it **Baghdad**. He built it as a perfect circle, a "round city" with the caliph's palace and the great mosque at the dead center and the walls and gates radiating out around them — a design that was itself an old Persian and Mesopotamian idea about kingship, the ruler at the hub of a cosmic wheel. And he built it just a few miles up the Tigris from the ruins of Ctesiphon, the sacked Sasanian capital. This was not an accident of geography. The Abbasids were deliberately planting their throne in the old Persian imperial landscape, on the same river, in sight of the old capital, claiming the inheritance of the kings of kings. They quarried the ruins of Ctesiphon for building material. They adopted Sasanian court ceremony, Sasanian titles, the Persian institution of the chief minister. The empire that had conquered Persia was, within a century, dressing itself in Persian clothes.

And the man who had made it all possible got a knife for his trouble. Abu Muslim, the general who had won the revolution, had become far too powerful and far too beloved in Persia for the new caliphs to tolerate — a man who could raise an army out of Khurasan once could do it again. So in 755 al-Mansur lured him to court and had him killed. The revolution's chief engineer became its first famous victim, and his murder did not end the trouble; it inflamed it. Across the newly Islamized plateau, the following decades saw a string of strange and violent Persian uprisings, religious-political revolts that fused the new faith with old Persian ideas and local grievances. There was **Sunpadh**, who rose partly to avenge Abu Muslim; there was **Ustadh Sis**; and most lurid of all there was **al-Muqanna** (al-moo-KAH-nah), "the Veiled Prophet," who wore a veil or a mask, said by his enemies to hide a face too radiant — or too hideous — to look upon, claimed to be divine, and held out against the caliph's armies in a fortress in Central Asia until his movement was crushed. These revolts failed, every one of them, but they tell you how unsettled the conquered land still was, a century after the conquest, how much of old Persia was still simmering under the new order, looking for a way to be itself again.

It would find that way, and not through revolt. The lesson of Abu Muslim was that you could win the war and still lose your head. The deeper Persian victory was already underway, and it was quieter and far more durable than any rebellion: it was happening in the tax registers, in the court ceremonies, in the slow conversion that made Persians full members of the faith, and, above all, it was about to happen in language. A people had lost its empire and kept its skin. Now it would lose its old written language and grow a new one, and use that new language to argue, in the heart of the conqueror's own capital, that it had never been anyone's inferior at all. That is the next chapter.


# Chapter 2 — A Language Refuses to Die

A civilization can survive losing its army. The Persians had just proved that (Chapter 1): the Sasanian Empire was gone, but the Persians were still there, running the conqueror's accounts, paying the conqueror's tax, drifting slowly into the conqueror's religion. The harder question, the one that decides whether a conquered people stays a people or dissolves into the victors, is whether it can survive losing its *voice* — its language, its literature, the words in which it remembers who it is. Most conquered peoples don't. The Egyptians, conquered in the same wave, eventually stopped speaking Egyptian and spoke Arabic; so did the Syrians, the Iraqis, the North Africans. Across the whole vast sweep of the Arab conquests, the local languages of the conquered mostly faded into Arabic over a few centuries, the way Latin had once swallowed the languages of the Roman provinces. Persia was the great exception. It is essentially the only major land conquered by the early Arabs that kept its own language and made that language a vehicle of high culture. This chapter is about how that happened — and it happened not by resisting Arabic but by absorbing it, the same sideways move that had let Persians run the state.

Start with what "Persian" even meant at the moment of conquest, because the language did not survive unchanged; it transformed, and the transformation is the whole story. The Persians of the Sasanian Empire spoke and wrote **Middle Persian**, also called **Pahlavi** (PAH-luh-vee), written in a difficult, ambiguous script descended ultimately from the alphabet of the ancient Aramaeans (an old Semitic people of the Near East whose language and alphabet had spread across the region centuries before). Pahlavi was the language of the Zoroastrian priesthood, the old court, and the tax registers — and it was bound up with the losing side of history. It was the language of fire-temples and a defeated dynasty, written in a script that fewer and fewer people could read. Under the new order, the prestige language, the language of God and government and serious scholarship, was **Arabic**, the language of the Quran (the holy book of Islam, believed to be God's own words revealed to Muhammad in Arabic) and of the conquerors. A Persian who wanted to get ahead — in administration, in religion, in learning — learned Arabic. So Persia became, for a while, a bilingual world: Arabic on top, for the high and holy and official; the spoken Persian of ordinary life underneath, carrying on in homes and markets and fields but not, for the moment, writing much down.

Then something genuinely new grew up in between, and it is one of the quiet miracles of cultural history. Out of the spoken Persian of the eastern provinces, especially Khurasan, a new written language emerged — not Pahlavi reborn, but a fresh thing. It was **New Persian**: the everyday spoken Persian of the post-conquest world, written down in a modified version of the **Arabic alphabet** (the conquerors' script, far easier to learn than the old Pahlavi one) and salted thoroughly with Arabic loanwords for all the new concepts — religious, legal, scientific, administrative — that came in with Islam. This is the language that is still spoken in Iran, Afghanistan, and Tajikistan today; it is the direct ancestor of modern Persian, and it was being written by around the year 800. The mechanism here is the thing to hold onto, because it is the book's whole argument in miniature. New Persian did not survive by keeping Arabic out. It survived by taking Arabic in — borrowing the script, borrowing thousands of words, and remaining, underneath all the borrowing, unmistakably Persian in its grammar, its bones, its sound. The closest analogy a reader already knows is English: English is a Germanic language at its core, in its grammar and its commonest words, but it borrowed an enormous French vocabulary after the Norman conquest of 1066, and nobody thinks English is therefore French. New Persian did exactly the same thing with Arabic. It is Persian to the bone with a borrowed Arabic wardrobe, and the wardrobe never made it any less Persian.

While the new language was being born, the old Persian inheritance was being smuggled into the new culture through a side door, and the smuggler has a name. **Ibn al-Muqaffa** (IB-n al-moo-KAF-fah) was a Persian convert to Islam, a brilliant prose stylist, who around 750 translated into Arabic a book of animal fables called **Kalila wa-Dimna** (kah-LEE-lah wah-DIM-nah). The book had come a long way to reach him — it descended from an Indian collection of fables, the *Panchatantra*, that had been translated into Middle Persian in Sasanian times — and Ibn al-Muqaffa rendered it into an elegant Arabic that became one of the most beloved and most copied books in the entire Islamic world. The fables, in which talking animals teach hard lessons about power, loyalty, and how to survive at a dangerous court, were on their surface charming children's stories. Underneath, they were a manual of statecraft, Persian political wisdom about how kings should rule and how courtiers should stay alive, dressed up as talking jackals. Ibn al-Muqaffa was doing, in literature, exactly what the katib secretaries were doing in the tax office: pouring Persian content into the conqueror's vessels, so that the substance of the old civilization flowed on inside the forms of the new one. (He came, fittingly and grimly, to a courtier's end — executed, gruesomely, by a governor he had offended, proof that the world of his fables was the world he actually lived in.)

This pouring-in was not a one-man effort; it was a whole movement, and the Abbasids backed it with money and prestige. The early Abbasid caliphs sponsored what historians call the **translation movement**, a sustained, lavishly funded effort, centered in Baghdad, to translate the accumulated learning of the ancient world into Arabic. Greek philosophy and medicine, Indian mathematics and astronomy, and a great body of Persian material — works on astronomy, ethics, court etiquette, and the art of government inherited from Sasanian times — were all rendered into Arabic by teams of scholars, often Christian or Persian, working in a milieu later romanticized as the **House of Wisdom**. The result was that the science and philosophy of half the ancient world were gathered into a single language, Arabic, just as that language was reaching the height of its prestige. And Persia's share of that inheritance — its astronomy, its statecraft, its idea of the just king — entered the bloodstream of Islamic civilization at the very center, in Baghdad, carried in by Persian translators. The conquered were not just keeping their culture alive; they were injecting it into the conqueror's.

At the political center of all this stood the most spectacular Persian success story of the early empire: the **Barmakids** (BAR-muh-kids). The Barmakids were a family from **Balkh** (a great city in what is now northern Afghanistan), originally not Muslim at all but the hereditary guardians of a famous Buddhist temple there, who had converted to Islam and risen, with astonishing speed, to the very top of the Abbasid state. By the 780s, under the caliph **Harun al-Rashid** (hah-ROON al-rah-SHEED) — the caliph of the *Thousand and One Nights*, presiding over Baghdad at its glittering height — the Barmakids effectively *ran the empire*. They held the office of **vizier** (vih-ZEER), the chief minister, the head of the entire administration, the man who actually governed in the caliph's name (think of it as a prime minister with the powers of a chief of staff and a treasurer rolled together). For a generation, a family of Persian converts from a Buddhist temple in Afghanistan governed the Islamic world. This was the high-water mark of Persian power at the caliph's court, and like Abu Muslim before them (Chapter 1), they flew too close to the sun: around 803 Harun al-Rashid turned on the Barmakids with terrifying suddenness, executing the head of the family and imprisoning the rest, for reasons the sources never quite agree on — too rich, too powerful, too independent, too much a state within the state. The pattern from the last chapter holds with grim regularity. A Persian could rise to run the empire, and the rising itself made him a target.

But running the empire and quietly winning it were never quite the same thing, and the deeper Persian victory of this period was not the Barmakids' political power, which proved fragile, but a literary argument that proved permanent. It is called the **Shu'ubiyya** (shoo-oo-BEE-yah), and it is one of the most fascinating intellectual movements in the whole period. The word comes from a Quranic verse that speaks of God creating humanity in "peoples and tribes" (*shu'ub* being the peoples), and the Shu'ubiyya was, in essence, a generations-long literary quarrel over a single explosive question: were the Arabs actually better than everyone else? The Arab ruling culture had taken for granted that they were — that Arabic was the finest language, Arab lineage the noblest blood, Arab custom the standard of civilization. Starting around the early ninth century, Persian writers, working in Arabic, the language of their opponents, began to push back, and hard. They argued that the Persians had an ancient empire, sophisticated arts of government, refined manners, and a glorious history when the Arabs were still illiterate desert nomads. They mocked Arab pretensions, catalogued Persian achievements, and insisted that non-Arabs — above all Persians — were the cultural equals or the betters of their conquerors. This was not a call to revolt or to leave Islam. The Shu'ubiyya writers were Muslims arguing inside Islam, accepting the faith but denying that the faith made Arabs superior people. It was an assertion of cultural pride, a refusal to be looked down on, and it did something no battle could have done: it gave the conquered a coherent, articulate, intellectually respectable case for their own worth, made in the conqueror's own language and on the conqueror's own terms. The army was gone, but the argument was won.

The argument needed a state to stand on, though, and during these same decades the political ground was shifting in a way that would, within a few generations, give Persia exactly that. The Abbasid caliphate, vast and unwieldy, could not really govern its eastern provinces directly from Baghdad. So the caliphs did what overstretched empires always do: they delegated, handing real power to trusted governors and hoping the tribute kept coming. In 821 the caliph rewarded a loyal Persian general, **Tahir ibn Husayn** (TAH-hir), with the governorship of Khurasan, and Tahir promptly began behaving less like a governor and more like a king. He and his descendants, the **Tahirids** (TAH-hir-ids), ruled Khurasan as a near-independent dynasty, paying lip service to the caliph in Baghdad, sending some tribute, mentioning his name in the Friday prayers — but actually running their own state, raising their own troops, and passing power from father to son. This was the first real crack in the caliphate's control of Persia: a Persian dynasty ruling Persian land, in fact if not yet in name. The caliph was still the nominal sovereign, the spiritual head of the community, but the day-to-day reality of power in the east had quietly gone local, and gone Persian.

If the Tahirids were the polite crack, the next dynasty was the violent one, and it produced one of the great rags-to-riches stories of Persian history. In the rough frontier province of **Sistan** (sis-TAHN), in southeastern Persia, a man named **Ya'qub ibn al-Layth** (YAH-koob) started out as a **coppersmith** — literally a metalworker, a man who hammered pots — which earned him the nickname **al-Saffar**, "the Coppersmith." He drifted into the world of armed frontier bands, fighting bandits and heretics, was good at it, and rose to lead his own army. By 861 he had carved out a kingdom for himself, founding the **Saffarid** (SAF-fa-rid) dynasty, and unlike the deferential Tahirids he had no patience for caliphal pretensions at all. He conquered his way across the Persian east and then marched on Baghdad itself, getting within striking distance of the caliph before being turned back. Here was a coppersmith from the back of beyond who nearly toppled the commander of the faithful — a vivid measure of how far real power had already drained away from the center. And Ya'qub did one more thing that matters enormously for this book: tradition holds that when a court poet praised him in Arabic, Ya'qub, who did not know Arabic well, told the man to compose in a language he could understand. So poets at his court began composing in **New Persian**. Ya'qub the Coppersmith is remembered as the first ruler to make New Persian a language of court poetry — the moment the new written language stepped out of the tax office and the market and onto the dais beside the throne. The language had refused to die. Now it was about to be handed a kingdom rich enough to make it sing, and that kingdom is the next chapter.


# Chapter 3 — The Samanid Renaissance

By the early 900s the pieces were all in place for something extraordinary, and for once in this story they fell together rather than apart. A people had kept its identity through conquest (Chapter 1). It had grown a new written language out of the wreckage and won the intellectual argument for its own worth (Chapter 2). It had produced the first dynasties to rule Persian land in something close to Persia's own name, and the first ruler to put New Persian poetry on the dais. What it had not yet had was a single rich, stable, confident Persian court that would take all of that and *pour money into it* — bankroll the poets, the scientists, the libraries, the buildings — for long enough to produce a golden age. That court arose in the far northeast, in the lands beyond the Amu Darya river that the Arabs called **Transoxiana** (trans-ox-ee-AH-nah, "the land beyond the Oxus," the Oxus being the old name for the Amu Darya), and the dynasty that built it was the **Samanid** (sah-MAH-nid). What happened under the Samanids was, by any reasonable measure, a renaissance — a deliberate flowering of Persian high culture under an Islamic skin — and it is the payoff of everything the first two chapters set up.

The Samanids were a Persian dynasty, and they were proud of it. Their family traced itself back to a Persian noble named Saman, and they claimed descent from the old Sasanian kings — a claim that may or may not have been literally true, but that tells you exactly how they wished to be seen: as the legitimate heirs of pre-Islamic Persian royalty, restoring the glory of the kings of kings under the new faith. Beginning around 819 they were granted governorships in Transoxiana and Khurasan, and like the Tahirids before them (Chapter 2) they turned governorship into something close to independent kingship, nodding to the distant Abbasid caliph while ruling a wide, wealthy realm themselves. Their capital was **Bukhara** (boo-KHA-rah), in what is now Uzbekistan, and under their patronage Bukhara grew into something the chroniclers reached for grand words to describe. It was called, admiringly, "the dome of Islam in the East" — a glittering city of mosques, palaces, bazaars, and, crucially, libraries and gardens of scholars. The Samanid court made a conscious project of reviving Persian high culture: gathering poets, funding scholars, building a famous library that the young Avicenna would later describe as a wonder. This was not an accident of prosperity. It was a policy. The Samanids set out to make Bukhara the place where the Persian language and Persian learning would have their renaissance, and they had the money to do it.

That money came, more than anything, from silver, and the story of where it went is one of the great surprises of medieval economics. Central Asia, the Samanid heartland, was rich in silver mines, and the Samanids minted that silver into coins called **dirhams** (DIR-hams; a dirham being the standard silver coin of the Islamic world). What is astonishing is where those coins ended up. Samanid dirhams have been dug up by the hundreds of thousands far to the north — in Russia, in the Baltic, in Scandinavia, buried in the hoards of **Viking** traders (the Norse seafarers and merchants of the far north). The connection ran up the great rivers of Russia: Viking and Rus' traders (the Norse and Slavic river-merchants who plied the great rivers of what is now Russia and Ukraine) came down from the north carrying furs, amber, and enslaved people, and went home with Samanid silver, so much of it that the medieval Scandinavian economy ran in part on coins minted in Bukhara. Picture it for the scale: a Persian dynasty in Uzbekistan was, through a chain of river-borne middlemen, helping to fund the world of the Vikings. The same silver that paid Rudaki to write poetry in Bukhara ended up in a hoard under a Swedish farm. That is how plugged-in the Samanid economy was — Bukhara sat on the overland Silk Road and at the head of a river-trade network reaching to the Arctic, and the wealth that flowed through it is what paid for the renaissance.

The renaissance began, as renaissances often do, with a poet, and the Persians give him a title that says everything: **Rudaki** (roo-DAH-kee), the "father of Persian poetry." Rudaki was the great court poet of the Samanids in the early tenth century, blind (by tradition, though the sources are unsure whether from birth) and famous, the man who took the young New Persian language and showed that it could do everything the most refined poetry demanded — lyric grace, wit, melancholy, praise, philosophy. Only fragments of his enormous output survive, but the fragments are enough: they are the first great literary voice of the new written language, the proof that New Persian was not just for tax registers and rough frontier courts but could be a vehicle for poetry as sophisticated as anything in Arabic. To call him the "father of Persian poetry" is to say that the entire towering tradition that follows — the tradition that will produce Ferdowsi, Khayyam, Rumi, Saadi, and Hafez over the next five centuries — starts, as a written art, with him. The language had been saved (Chapter 2); Rudaki was the first to prove it could fly.

But the supreme literary achievement of the age, the work that more than any other secured the Persian language forever, was not a lyric. It was an epic, and it consumed one man's entire adult life. To understand it you first have to understand the strange gap it filled. The Persians had a deep, glorious national past — the old kings, the legendary heroes, the half-mythical history of Iran stretching back before the Sasanians into pure legend. But that past lived mostly in scattered Pahlavi texts and oral tradition, and it was in danger, in a Persia now writing in Arabic and reading the Quran, of being forgotten. A poet named **Daqiqi** (dah-KEE-kee) set out, around 977, to gather the whole sweep of Persia's kings into a single great verse epic in New Persian — and got perhaps a thousand lines into it before he was murdered, supposedly by his own slave. The project passed to a greater man.

That man was **Ferdowsi** (fer-DOW-see), a landowner of modest means from near the city of Tus in Khurasan, and what he did is almost without parallel in world literature. He took up the abandoned national epic — folding Daqiqi's thousand lines into it as a tribute — and he spent roughly thirty years finishing it, completing the work around 1010. The result was the **Shahnameh** (shah-nah-MEH), the "Book of Kings," an epic of around fifty thousand rhyming couplets (so, on the order of a hundred thousand lines — by line count, several times the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* put together, which run to roughly twenty-eight thousand lines between them) that told the entire mythical and historical past of Persia, from the creation of the world and the first king, through the great legendary heroes, down to the fall of the Sasanians and the Arab conquest. It is the Persian national epic in the fullest sense, the single most important text in the Persian language, and Ferdowsi did something deliberate and remarkable with the language itself: he wrote it in a Persian as free of Arabic loanwords as he could manage, reaching for the old, pure Persian vocabulary, as if to prove that the language could carry the whole weight of the nation's memory on its own. It is widely said that Ferdowsi, more than any other single person, saved the Persian language — not from extinction exactly, but from becoming a junior dialect under Arabic, by giving it a monument so great that the language could never again be dismissed as merely the speech of the conquered. Whether one man can "save a language" is the kind of grand claim worth flagging as a simplification of a process that involved many hands and two centuries (Chapter 2). But the Shahnameh's central place in Persian identity is no exaggeration at all: a thousand years later, Iranians still recite it, still name their children after its heroes, still know its stories the way an English speaker half-knows Shakespeare.

What were those stories? The epic's beating heart is its heroes, and one towers over the rest: **Rustam** (ROOS-tam), the mighty champion, impossibly strong, fiercely loyal to kings who rarely deserve him, the Persian Hercules and Achilles rolled into one. His most famous story is also its most heartbreaking: the tragedy of **Sohrab** (so-RAHB), in which Rustam, not knowing he has a son, meets that son in single combat and kills him, recognizing him only as the boy lies dying — a tale of fate and unbearable loss that Persians have wept over for a thousand years. The epic's great villain is **Zahhak** (zah-HAHK), a tyrant with serpents growing from his shoulders that must be fed human brains, eventually overthrown by a blacksmith named Kaveh who raises his leather apron as a banner of revolt. These figures — Rustam, Sohrab, Zahhak, Kaveh and his apron — became cultural touchstones recited in courts and bazaars, painted in manuscripts, and woven into the self-image of the entire Persian-speaking world for the next thousand years. The Shahnameh did for Persia what Homer did for Greece, except that Ferdowsi was one identifiable man, working within living memory, in a language he was helping to make.

There is a famous legend about Ferdowsi's reward, and it is worth telling precisely because the honest version is more interesting than the myth. By the time he finished, the Samanids had fallen, and Ferdowsi presented his epic to their successor in the east, the Turkic **sultan** (the title of a Muslim sovereign ruler) **Mahmud of Ghazni** (later in this chapter). Tradition says Mahmud had promised Ferdowsi a gold coin for every couplet — a fortune — but paid him instead in silver, a fraction of the value, whether out of stinginess or because a court rival had poisoned the sultan against the poet. The story goes that Ferdowsi, insulted, gave the silver away on the spot to a bath attendant and a beer-seller, wrote a stinging satire of the sultan, and died poor and embittered, the gold arriving (in the most romantic version) just as his funeral was leaving the city. How much of this is true is anyone's guess — it is exactly the kind of tale that grows up around a great artist cheated by a powerful patron, and the details vary from telling to telling. But its survival tells you something real: the Persians needed the story of the Shahnameh to be a story of Persian genius unappreciated by a Turkish king, because that, too, was part of how they understood themselves.

The renaissance was not only literary. Bukhara and the wider Samanid world produced, in the same decades, some of the greatest scientific minds the medieval world would ever see, and they were so good that European universities were still teaching their work five hundred years later. The towering figure is **Ibn Sina** (IB-n SEE-nah), known in the Latin West as **Avicenna** (av-ih-SEN-nah), born near Bukhara around 980, a prodigy who claimed to have mastered all the available learning by his late teens and who became the most influential physician and philosopher of the entire medieval period. His medical encyclopedia, the **Canon of Medicine** (in Arabic, *al-Qanun*), organized all the medical knowledge of his world into a single systematic reference, and it was used as a standard textbook in European medical schools for *six hundred years*. His philosophical masterwork, the *Book of Healing*, synthesized Greek philosophy (above all Aristotle) with Islamic theology so powerfully that he set the agenda for centuries of debate — including the great pushback we will meet later, when the theologian al-Ghazali sets out to refute exactly the kind of rationalist philosophy Avicenna built (Chapter 7).

He was not alone. **Al-Biruni** (al-bee-ROO-nee), a Persian polymath and a contemporary of Avicenna, was one of the most extraordinary scientific minds of any age. He calculated the radius of the Earth with remarkable accuracy using a clever method involving the angle of the horizon from a mountaintop, getting a figure strikingly close to the modern value. He traveled to India in the train of conquering armies and, instead of writing propaganda, wrote a careful, fair-minded, genuinely curious ethnography of Indian religion, science, and custom — a work of comparative scholarship so even-handed that it stands almost alone in its century. He ranged across astronomy, geology, pharmacology, and mathematics with the same restless precision. Slightly earlier, **al-Razi** (al-RAH-zee), known in the West as **Rhazes**, a physician from the city of Rayy (near modern Tehran), had been the most original clinician of the age, the first to clearly distinguish smallpox from measles by their symptoms and the author of a vast medical compendium drawn from his own bedside observation. And earlier still, working in Baghdad in the ninth century, the Persian mathematician **al-Khwarizmi** (al-khwa-RIZ-mee) had written the foundational treatise that gave us the very word **algebra** (from the Arabic *al-jabr* in his title), and whose own Latinized name, *Algoritmi*, became the word **algorithm**. Two words at the absolute core of modern mathematics and computing are the fossilized name and book-title of a Persian scholar from a thousand years ago — about as concrete a measure as you could ask for of how deep this inheritance runs. Put the cluster together — Avicenna, al-Biruni, al-Razi, al-Khwarizmi — and the Persian east of the ninth, tenth, and eleventh centuries was producing science at a level that Latin Europe, then in the depths of its early medieval slump, could not approach and would spend centuries catching up to.

The Samanids did not last. In the late tenth and early eleventh centuries their state was squeezed out of existence by rising powers on its frontiers, and the most important of those powers tells us where the story is going next. The **Ghaznavids** (GHAZ-nuh-vids) began as Turkish military slaves in Samanid service — a crucial detail, because it introduces the engine of the next several centuries. The early Islamic states had taken to buying or capturing **Turks**, the nomadic horse-peoples of the Central Asian steppe (the dry grasslands north and east of Persia), and training them as elite soldiers, because they were superb mounted warriors; over time, inevitably, some of these soldier-slaves seized power for themselves. That is what the Ghaznavids did, establishing an independent kingdom centered on **Ghazni** in what is now Afghanistan. Their greatest ruler, **Mahmud of Ghazni** (the same sultan of the Ferdowsi legend), was a Turk by blood and a ferocious warrior who launched some seventeen plundering raids into the rich temple-cities of northern India, carrying off staggering amounts of gold and earning a reputation as a holy warrior and a sacker of idols.

And here is the thing that makes Mahmud the perfect bridge to the rest of this book. This Turkish warlord, raiding Hindu India for plunder, ran a court that was thoroughly *Persian*. He patronized Persian poets (Ferdowsi among them), kept his administration in Persian, hosted al-Biruni, and modeled his kingship on Persian ideas of royal magnificence. The Ghaznavids made Persian the language of government and high culture across their realm, from Afghanistan down into the Punjab — which means they carried the Persianate world *into India*, planting the seed of a Persian cultural presence in the subcontinent that would last, through the later Delhi sultans and the Mughals, for the better part of a thousand years. So the Samanid renaissance ended not with a collapse of Persian culture but with its *export*: the language and the courtly model that Bukhara had perfected were now riding east on the backs of Turkish conquerors. That pattern — Turks supplying the swords, Persians supplying the culture and the state — is about to become the dominant fact of Persian history for the next four hundred years. But before the Turks took over completely, the Persians had one more act of their own to play, right at the dead center of the Islamic world, in Baghdad itself. That is the next chapter.


# Chapter 4 — The Persian Intermezzo

So far the Persian comeback has happened at the edges. Persians ran the conqueror's tax office (Chapter 1) and won the conqueror's argument (Chapter 2); they built a glorious court at Bukhara, far out in the northeast, and saved their language there (Chapter 3). But all of that left one thing untouched, and it was the thing that mattered most symbolically: the center. Baghdad — the round city the Abbasids had planted in the old Persian landscape (Chapter 1) — was still the seat of the **caliph** (kay-LIF, the successor to Muhammad as supreme leader of the Muslim community), still the spiritual and theoretical capital of the Islamic world. The caliph might have lost real power to provincial dynasties, but he was still the caliph, the commander of the faithful, the sun the whole system was supposed to orbit. This chapter is about the astonishing century when a Persian dynasty marched into Baghdad, took the caliph prisoner in his own palace, and kept him on as a powerless figurehead — a religious mascot to be displayed and obeyed in the prayers and ignored in everything that mattered. Historians have a name for this episode, and it is a lovely one: the **Iranian Intermezzo**, or the **Buyid Intermezzo** — an "intermezzo" being a short interlude between the main acts of a play. For about a hundred years, in the very heart of the Arab-founded caliphate, the Persians ran the show.

The dynasty that did it came from an unlikely corner of Persia, and where it came from matters. The **Buyids** (BOO-yids; also spelled Buwayhids) were not from the great cities or the old aristocracy. They came from **Daylam** (DY-lam), the rugged, rain-soaked highlands south of the Caspian Sea — a remote mountain region that had been one of the very last parts of Persia to be conquered and converted, a stubborn pocket of fierce hill-people who had held onto their independence and their old ways long after the lowlands had submitted. The **Daylamites** (DY-luh-mites) were famous as tough infantry, mountain fighters who hired themselves out as mercenaries across the region. Three brothers of a Daylamite family named Buya rose through this mercenary world in the early tenth century, and like so many soldiers before them in this story, they parlayed military skill into political power, carving out territories across western and southern Persia. Then, in 945, one of them, **Ahmad**, did the thing nobody from the mountains was supposed to be able to do: he marched into Baghdad and took it.

What happened next was a careful, deliberate humiliation dressed up as a rescue, and the mechanism is worth spelling out because it became a model. The Buyids did not depose the caliph. That would have been a step too far — the caliph's religious authority was real, and abolishing the office would have united the whole Sunni world against them. Instead they kept him, and hollowed him out. The caliph remained on his throne, his name was still read in the Friday prayers, his face was still on the coins, he still performed the religious functions of his office. But the Buyid ruler took the title **amir al-umara**, "commander of commanders," and with it all actual power: the army, the treasury, the appointment of officials, the running of the state. The caliph became, in effect, a sacred rubber stamp — fed, housed, displayed, and obeyed in ritual, but allowed no real decision. If a caliph got difficult, the Buyids simply deposed him and installed a more cooperative relative; several caliphs of this period were blinded (a traditional way of disqualifying a man from rule without quite killing him) and shuffled aside. The Persians had found a way to rule the Islamic world without destroying the institution that legitimized it: keep the caliph as a holy figurehead, and govern around him.

There is a delicious irony at the center of this arrangement, and it points forward to the very end of the book, so hold onto it. The Buyids were **Shia** (SHEE-ah), and the caliph they were keeping as a pet was **Sunni** (SOON-nee). This is the great division of Islam, and since it runs through the rest of this story it has to be set out carefully and even-handedly, because confident storytelling about contested religion is exactly how history goes wrong. The split goes back to a question that arose the moment the Prophet Muhammad died in 632 without clearly naming a successor: who had the right to lead the Muslim community after him? One group held that the leader, the caliph, should be the ablest and most respected man available, chosen by the consensus of the community's elders — these became the **Sunnis**, who are and have always been the majority of Muslims. The other group held that leadership belonged by right to the Prophet's own family, descending through his cousin and son-in-law **Ali** (ah-LEE) and Ali's sons, and could not be a matter of election — these became the **Shia** (the name comes from *shi'at Ali*, "the party of Ali"). The disagreement, which began as a dispute about *who should rule* rather than about basic doctrine, hardened over the centuries into two distinct branches of the faith with their own law, their own scholars, their own devotional life, and it still divides Islam today. The point for this chapter is the irony: a Shia dynasty held real power over a Sunni caliph and chose, for cold political reasons, to prop him up rather than replace him with a Shia leader of their own. They needed his Sunni legitimacy more than they needed a co-religionist on the throne.

The greatest of the Buyid rulers shows what the dynasty was really reaching for, and it was something older and grander than the caliphate. **Adud al-Dawla** (ah-DUD ad-DOW-lah), who ruled in the 970s, was the most powerful and accomplished of the line, and he did something no Muslim ruler had dared since the conquest: he revived the ancient Persian imperial title **shahanshah**, "king of kings." This was a stunning gesture. The shahanshah was the title of Yazdegerd III, murdered in the miller's hut three centuries earlier (Chapter 1); it was the title of the Sasanian kings the Arabs had destroyed. To take it up again, in Baghdad, while keeping the Arab caliph as a figurehead, was to announce that the old Persian imperial idea was not dead at all — that it had come back, in Islamic dress, to the very capital that had been built on the bones of Ctesiphon. Adud al-Dawla acted the part of a Persian great king. He built hospitals (including a famous one in Baghdad), repaired the irrigation works and dams that kept the land fertile, patronized poets and scholars, and presided over a court of conscious magnificence. For a moment the wheel had come full circle: a Persian king of kings, ruling from the heart of the empire that had once erased Persia, with the caliph as his ceremonial ornament.

The Buyid century mattered for something quieter than titles, though, and it is the thread that will run all the way to the last chapter. Because the Buyids were themselves Shia, they were tolerant patrons of Shia scholarship in a way no Sunni regime would have been, and under their protection the intellectual life of **Twelver Shiism** — the largest branch of Shia Islam, which holds that there were twelve rightful imams (divinely guided leaders) descending from Ali, the last of whom did not die but went into a miraculous hidden state from which he will one day return — came of age. Under the tolerant Buyid umbrella, Shia scholars in Baghdad were able to work, teach, and write openly. The great theologian **Shaykh al-Mufid** (al-moo-FEED) systematized Shia theology; the canonical collections of Shia **hadith** (the recorded sayings and deeds of the Prophet and the imams, the second great source of Islamic law and practice after the Quran) were compiled in this period; the public mourning rituals for the martyred imams that are still central to Shia devotion today were practiced more openly than ever before. None of this made Persia as a whole Shia — it would stay overwhelmingly Sunni for another five centuries. But it built the scholarly and institutional foundations of Twelver Shiism, the very tradition that a teenage warlord named Ismail would one day impose on the whole country and make the permanent identity of Persia (Chapter 12). The seed of modern Shia Iran was planted, intellectually, under Buyid patronage, half a millennium before it flowered. That is the kind of long fuse this book is built to trace.

It is worth being honest about the limits of the Persian intermezzo, in the spirit the book keeps. It was not a unified Persian empire reborn. The Buyids were a family confederation, and the three brothers and their descendants ruled their territories semi-independently, quarreling among themselves as often as they cooperated, and Daylamite infantry increasingly had to share the army with Turkish cavalry whose interests pulled the other way. They were a Shia minority ruling a Sunni majority, dependent on a legitimacy they did not themselves possess, which is precisely why they kept the Sunni caliph alive. And the "Iranian" or "Buyid" intermezzo is partly a label historians apply in hindsight — a useful frame for a real phenomenon (the resurgence of Persian language, titles, court ceremony, and political power across the tenth century, the Buyids being its most spectacular instance) rather than a tidy unified period anyone living through it would have named. What is real, and what matters, is the pattern: across that century, Persian dynasts dominated the political center of the Islamic world, the old Persian imperial vocabulary came back into fashion, Persian ceremony was revived, and the caliph-the-Arabs-had-founded sat powerless in his palace while Persians governed around him. The comeback that began in the tax office had reached the throne room.

But it did not hold, and the reason it did not hold is the reason for everything that follows. The Buyids' great weakness was military: their Daylamite foot-soldiers, however tough, were being eclipsed by the mounted archers of the steppe, the Turkish cavalry that every state in the region was coming to depend on. The future of war belonged to the horse-peoples of the grasslands, and a new wave of them was already gathering on the northeastern frontier — not slave-soldiers this time, like the Ghaznavids (Chapter 3), but a whole migrating nation of free Turkish nomads, recently converted to Sunni Islam, looking for land and plunder and led by an ambitious clan. In 1055 their chief would ride into Baghdad, brush the Buyids aside, "free" the Sunni caliph from his Shia keepers, and take a new title for himself — and the long age of Persians ruling in Persia's own name would give way to the long age of Turks ruling Persian lands in Persian dress. It was the central bargain of the whole civilization, about to be struck at full volume. That is the next chapter.


# Chapter 5 — Turks Who Ruled in Persian

For four chapters now this book has been making the same strange argument from different angles. A superpower was militarily erased (Chapter 1), and instead of vanishing, the conquered people made themselves indispensable to the conquerors — running the tax registers, saving the language, writing the national epic at Bukhara, and finally, under the Buyids, taking the caliphate's own capital and keeping the caliph as a figurehead (Chapter 4). That is the conquered absorbing the conqueror, played at gradually rising volume. This chapter is the argument at full volume, and it comes from the last direction anyone would expect: not from Persians at all, but from a band of horse-archers off the open grassland of Central Asia who, having conquered Persia, proceeded to govern it more Persian than the Persians.

To understand why that is astonishing, you have to understand who was arriving. The **Seljuks** (SEL-jook) were a clan of **Oghuz** (OH-gooz) Turks, and the word *Turk* here needs unpacking before anything else, because it does not mean what a modern map suggests. The Turkic peoples were a family of nomadic herders who originated far to the northeast, on the steppe — the vast belt of open, treeless grassland that runs across the top of Asia from Mongolia toward the Black Sea. Their languages belong to the **Turkic** family, which is no relation at all to Persian. (Persian is an Indo-European language, the same broad family as English, Latin, and Sanskrit; Turkic is its own separate family entirely, as distant from Persian as Hungarian is from Italian.) These were not city people. They lived in felt tents, moved with their flocks across enormous distances, and fought from horseback with the composite bow — the steppe's signature weapon, a short, powerful recurved bow a rider could shoot at full gallop, the technology that made nomad armies the terror of every settled civilization on the edge of the grassland for two thousand years. Persia sat on exactly such an edge. To its northeast lay the steppe, and off the steppe, again and again across this whole book, came the people who would conquer it: the Seljuks here, the Mongols in Chapter 9, Timur in Chapter 11.

So in the early eleventh century a clan of these Oghuz Turks, recently converted to **Sunni Islam** (the majority branch of the faith, of which more shortly), drifted south off the steppe under leaders descended from a chief named Seljuk, and into the rich, settled, irrigated lands of Persia and Central Asia. They were not the first Turks to do this. For two centuries the Islamic world had been hiring Turkish nomads as soldiers — the steppe bred the best cavalry on earth, and a caliph or a sultan who could buy or recruit them got an army that outclassed anything a settled population could field. The **Ghaznavids** (GHAZ-nuh-vidz), the Turkish slave-soldier dynasty centered on Ghazni in Afghanistan, and their great raider-king **Mahmud of Ghazni** (mah-MOOD), had already shown the way (Chapter 3): Mahmud was a Turk who sacked Hindu temples by day and patronized Persian poets by night. His court at Ghazni spoke and wrote in Persian, and it was a Ghaznavid sultan (by tradition Mahmud himself) to whom Ferdowsi presented the *Shahnameh* around 1010. The Ghaznavids carried Persian as the language of administration and high culture east into the Punjab, planting the seed of a Persian-speaking high culture in India that would last, through the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals, for the better part of eight hundred years. The Turks were already ruling in Persian before the Seljuks ever showed up. The Seljuks just did it on a far grander scale.

The Seljuks announced themselves by smashing the Ghaznavids. In 1037 they swept into the eastern Persian lands, and over the following years they took the great cities of the northeast. By the 1040s the new dynasty had effectively replaced both the Ghaznavids in the east and the crumbling remnants of older powers across the plateau. Their leader was **Tughril Beg** (too-GHRIL beg), grandson of the clan's founder, and in 1055 he did something that crystallized the new order. He marched into **Baghdad**.

Recall what Baghdad was at that moment. It was the seat of the **Abbasid caliph** — in theory the supreme leader of all Sunni Muslims, the successor to the Prophet Muhammad as head of the community, an office founded in 750 (Chapter 1). In practice the caliph had been a prisoner of fashion for over a century: since 945 the city had been controlled by the **Buyids**, a dynasty of Shia warlords from the Caspian highlands who kept the Sunni caliph as a powerless religious mascot (Chapter 4). Tughril ended that. He drove out the Buyids, restored the caliph's dignity (the caliph was Sunni, the Seljuks were Sunni, and so the new arrangement could be dressed up as a liberation rather than another captivity), and in return had himself proclaimed **sultan** (SUL-tan) — a title meaning, roughly, "holder of power" or "authority." This was the deal that would define the next several centuries of Islamic history. The caliph kept his religious prestige, the spiritual headship of Sunni Islam. The sultan held the actual armies, the actual taxes, the actual government. Religious legitimacy in one set of hands, brute political power in another. The Seljuks had invented, or at least perfected, the template: a Turkish warrior-king ruling in the caliph's name.

But the Seljuks did not bring a government with them. This is the crux of the entire chapter. A clan of nomads who counted their wealth in horses and sheep had no machinery for running tax registers, paying salaried bureaucrats, surveying farmland, building roads, or administering cities of a hundred thousand people. They had an army; they did not have a state. And lying ready to hand in the very lands they had just conquered was the most experienced administrative class in the Islamic world — the Persian secretaries, the *katibs*, who had been running the paperwork of empires since Sasanian times and through the whole caliphate (Chapter 1). So the Seljuks did the obvious and the consequential thing. They hired them. The Turks supplied the cavalry; the Persians supplied the chancery. And because the chancery's working language, its prestige, its whole inherited model of how a kingdom ought to be run, was Persian, the empire the Turks built was a Persian empire with a Turkish army on top.

The man who embodies that partnership — who is, in a sense, its living definition — is **Nizam al-Mulk** (nih-ZAHM al-MULK). The name is a title, meaning "Order of the Realm," and he earned it. Born a Persian of modest background near the eastern city of Tus around 1018, he rose through the administrative service to become the **vizier** (the chief minister, Chapter 2) — and then served in that office for some thirty years under two Seljuk sultans, **Alp Arslan** (ALP ar-SLAHN) and his son **Malik-Shah** (MAH-lik shah). For most of that time, by general agreement, it was Nizam al-Mulk and not the sultan who actually ran the empire. The sultans led armies and hunted and held court; the vizier governed. He is the answer to the riddle of how a nomad confederation became a sophisticated state almost overnight: it didn't, really. A Persian bureaucrat ran a Persian state and let the Turks think they were in charge of it.

Two of his achievements outlasted him by a thousand years. The first was a book. Late in his life, at the sultan's request, Nizam al-Mulk wrote the **Siyasatnama** (see-yah-saht-NAH-mah), the "Book of Government" — a manual of statecraft for kings, the genre scholars call a "mirror for princes" (a book that holds up the image of the ideal ruler for an actual ruler to study). The *Siyasatnama* is a remarkable fusion. Its bones are old Persian: the inherited Sasanian ideal of the just king at the center of an ordered, hierarchical realm, the same image of kingship the *Shahnameh* celebrated. Onto that frame Nizam al-Mulk grafted Islamic religious duty and a great deal of hard practical advice, much of it illustrated with stories of earlier kings and viziers. Watch your spies. Keep your soldiers paid so they don't plunder. Hear petitioners directly so corrupt officials can't trap you behind a wall of flatterers. Be suspicious of heretics — a theme with a sharp personal edge, as the next chapter will show. The book became a classic, copied and studied across the Persianate world for centuries, and it is one of the clearest single pieces of evidence for the book's whole thesis: when a Turkish dynasty wanted to learn how to be a proper monarchy, the textbook it reached for was written in Persian and drew its model of kingship from the empire the Arabs had destroyed four hundred years before.

His second monument was a school system. Nizam al-Mulk founded a network of state-funded religious colleges called the **Nizamiyya** (nih-zah-MEE-yah) — named after himself — the grandest of them established in Baghdad in 1067. A college of this kind is a **madrasa** (muh-DRAH-suh), an institution for advanced religious and legal study, where students lodged, were fed, and learned Islamic law, theology, and the religious sciences from salaried teachers. Madrasas had existed before, often as private or charitable foundations. What Nizam al-Mulk did was make them a deliberate instrument of the state, endowed and funded on a large scale across many cities, with a standardized purpose. The purpose was partly educational and partly political: to train a reliable class of Sunni scholars and judges loyal to the regime, and to push back against the religious propaganda of the regime's enemies (above all the Ismailis of Chapter 6, and the rival Shia caliphate in Egypt — the Fatimids, a Shia dynasty that claimed the caliphate against Baghdad, introduced in Chapter 6). It worked. The Nizamiyya colleges helped institutionalize the madrasa as the central organ of higher learning in the Sunni Islamic world, a model that spread far beyond Persia and shaped Islamic education for the rest of the period. The most famous teacher the Baghdad Nizamiyya ever hired was the theologian **al-Ghazali** — whose spiritual crisis, when he walked away from that very professorship, is the heart of Chapter 7.

While Nizam al-Mulk governed, the army did what steppe armies do, and in 1071 it changed the map of the Near East permanently. Sultan Alp Arslan, campaigning in the northwest, ran into the army of the **Byzantine Empire** — the Greek-speaking, Christian, eastern continuation of the Roman Empire, ruled from Constantinople, which had been the great power of Anatolia and the eastern Mediterranean for seven hundred years. The two armies met at **Manzikert** (MAN-zih-kert), in what is now eastern Turkey, in August 1071. The Seljuks won, decisively, and in the rout they captured the Byzantine emperor himself, Romanos IV — a humiliation almost without precedent. The battle mattered less for the casualties than for what it opened. With the Byzantine field army shattered and the empire thrown into civil war over the captured emperor, the Anatolian interior lay open, and Turkish nomads poured into it over the following decades, settling the high plateau that is the heartland of modern Turkey. Manzikert is, in a real sense, why there is a country called Turkey today: it began the long process by which Greek-speaking Christian Anatolia became Turkish-speaking Muslim Anatolia. It also, by exposing the weakness of Byzantium and the Turkish threat to the Christian holy places, helped trigger the call that launched the First Crusade a generation later in 1095 — so a Seljuk victory in eastern Turkey is one of the threads leading to Western European knights marching on Jerusalem. The Seljuks themselves spun off a western branch, the Sultanate of Rum (the name simply means "Rome," i.e. former Byzantine land), to rule the new Anatolian conquests.

The Seljuk high noon under Malik-Shah and Nizam al-Mulk, roughly the 1070s and 1080s, was also a high noon for Persian culture, and the regime bankrolled it. The most celebrated case is **Omar Khayyam** (OH-mar khy-YAHM), who is one of those rare figures famous in the modern West for entirely the wrong reason. In his own time and place Khayyam was renowned as a mathematician and astronomer. Around 1079, under Malik-Shah's patronage, he led a team of astronomers who produced a reformed solar calendar — the **Jalali calendar**, named for the sultan — that fixed the length of the year with extraordinary precision, in fact more accurately than the **Gregorian calendar** that Europe would adopt five centuries later and that the world uses now. He also did original work on the geometry of cubic equations. None of that is what made him famous abroad. Khayyam is best known in the English-speaking world for the **Rubaiyat** (ROO-by-yaht) — a collection of *rubaiyat*, four-line verses (quatrains), on wine, doubt, fate, and the shortness of life. It is genuinely uncertain how many of the quatrains attributed to him he actually wrote; the corpus grew over centuries, and "a poem by Omar Khayyam" became something of a category that later, anonymous, skeptical verses got filed under. What is certain is that in 1859 a Victorian Englishman named Edward FitzGerald published a loose, beautiful English version of them, and that version became a runaway sensation — for generations of English speakers, the *Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam* was the only Persian poetry they had ever heard of. A man Persians remember as a great scientist, the West remembers as a melancholy poet, largely on the strength of a Victorian translation of verses he may only partly have written.

The age built in stone as well as in verse, and here too the Seljuks set a template Persia would follow ever after. Their builders perfected the **four-iwan** (EE-wahn) plan for the great congregational mosque. An *iwan* is a large vaulted hall, open on one side, facing onto a courtyard — a feature with deep roots in pre-Islamic Persian palace architecture, now adapted to a house of prayer. The four-iwan plan put one such hall at the center of each of the four sides of a rectangular open courtyard, with the whole bound together by arcades, often crowned with domes, and increasingly clad in patterned tile and that honeycomb of stacked niches called **muqarnas** (moo-KAR-nas). The supreme surviving example is the great **Friday Mosque of Isfahan** (iss-fah-HAHN), which the Seljuks rebuilt in this form in the early twelfth century and which became the model for Persian mosques for the next eight hundred years; nearly every classic Persian mosque you can picture, right through the Safavid masterpieces of the next era, is a descendant of that plan. The motivation was not only beauty. The four iwans organized the huge crowds of a major city's congregation, gave the building a monumental face on every side of its court, and announced, in expensive permanent material, the piety and power of the ruler who paid for it — the same triple logic (practical, expressive, and a statement of rank) that drives almost all premodern monumental building.

Underneath the poetry and the mosques ran the unglamorous machinery that actually held the empire together, and it is worth one paragraph because it shaped Persian society for centuries. To pay an army of expensive cavalry without a reliable cash treasury, the Seljuks systematized a practice called the **iqta** (ik-TAH) — a grant by which a soldier or officer was assigned the tax revenue of a piece of land, in place of a salary in coin. Instead of the state collecting taxes and paying troops, the troops collected (a defined portion of) the taxes themselves, directly, from the farmers on their assigned land. It was an elegant solution to a cash-poor empire's payroll problem, and it had a long afterlife: variants of it organized Persian and wider Islamic society's fiscal-military arrangements for generations. It also carried an obvious danger, the same one that haunted feudal Europe — a soldier with his own land and his own income is a soldier who may decide he does not need the sultan, and the slow fraying of central control along exactly these lines is part of how the empire eventually came apart.

Because come apart it did. The structural weakness was built into the foundations. The Seljuk realm was, in the steppe tradition, regarded as a kind of family property to be shared among the ruling clan rather than a single indivisible state passed intact to one heir, so it was forever splintering into the appanages of competing princes, uncles, and cousins, each with his own Turkish soldiers and his own Persian vizier. The system held together only as long as a strong sultan and a strong vizier could dominate it. In 1092 both pillars fell within weeks of each other: Nizam al-Mulk was assassinated (by whom, and why, is the subject of the very next chapter), and Sultan Malik-Shah died soon after. The empire promptly fractured into a tangle of warring Seljuk princes. There was one last broad flourishing under **Sultan Sanjar** (SAN-jar), whose long reign over the eastern lands stretched into the 1150s and whose enormous tomb at Merv was among the dynasty's grandest monuments — but Sanjar's reign was the sunset, not the noon. By the time he died, real power on the plateau was passing to the local strongmen and provincial governors who had grown up under the iqta system, and one such governor's family, on the eastern frontier, was quietly building the empire that becomes Chapter 8.

The Seljuk political empire, then, was a brilliant, brittle, two-generation thing. But its cultural achievement was permanent, and it is the reason this is the central chapter of the book. Under the Seljuks the **Persian language** completed its conquest of the conquerors. Persian, not Arabic, became the prestige language of administration, of poetry, of the cultured court, across an enormous zone — what historians call the **Persianate** world, meaning the vast region, far larger than Persia itself, that took Persian as the language of high culture and government. Arabic remained the language of religion and law, the sacred tongue of the Quran; but the language a king wrote his letters in, the language a poet sought patronage in, the language a bureaucrat kept his registers in, was now Persian, from Anatolia in the west to Central Asia in the east, and soon, through the Turkish conquests, into India. The astonishing thing is who carried it. The men spreading Persian as the universal court language across half of Asia were, overwhelmingly, not ethnic Persians. They were Turks — Seljuks, Ghaznavids, and their successors — who had adopted Persian high culture so completely that they exported it as their own. A civilization that had been militarily annihilated four hundred years earlier was now the cultural operating system of the entire eastern Islamic world, installed and propagated by the descendants of the nomads who had most recently conquered it. That is the conquered conquering the conqueror, stated at full volume. Everything in the next six chapters — the Assassins, the Sufi poets, the Mongol catastrophe and its strange afterlife — happens on the stage the Seljuks built.


# Chapter 6 — The Castles in the Mountains

The Seljuk empire of the last chapter was vast, Sunni, and confident, with a Persian bureaucracy, a state-funded college system designed in part to stamp out religious dissent, and the most feared cavalry in the world. This chapter is about a tiny religious minority that should have been crushed flat by all of that, and instead survived inside it for a hundred and sixty-six years by inventing a new kind of power — and about the legend that grew up around them, which is one of the most famous stories ever told about the medieval Islamic world and is mostly false. Untangling the record from the legend is the whole task here, because almost everything a Western reader thinks they know about the **Assassins** is a thirteenth-century rumor that hardened into a fairy tale.

Start with who they actually were, which requires a short detour into the most important fault line in Islamic history. From very early on, Muslims divided over who had the right to lead the community after the Prophet Muhammad died in 632. The majority, the **Sunnis**, accepted the line of caliphs who actually held power (the Abbasids in Baghdad, Chapter 1). A minority, the **Shia** (SHEE-ah), held that leadership belonged by right to the Prophet's family — specifically to his cousin and son-in-law Ali and to Ali's descendants, called the **Imams**, whom the Shia regarded as the divinely-guided rightful leaders of all Muslims. (This is the same split that has run quietly under the whole book: the Buyids of Chapter 4 were Shia, and the question of when Persia itself became Shia is the payoff of Chapter 12.) But the Shia themselves split, repeatedly, over which descendant of Ali was the true Imam. One such split produced the **Ismailis** (iss-MY-ee-leez), a branch named for an Imam called Ismail, who developed an elaborate, intellectually sophisticated religious system with an esoteric inner doctrine — a belief that scripture and law carry hidden spiritual meanings accessible only through the teaching of the rightful, living Imam.

By the eleventh century the Ismailis had their own caliphate: the **Fatimids** (FAH-tih-midz), a Shia dynasty ruling from Egypt, who were the great religious and political rivals of the Sunni Abbasids and the Seljuks. The Fatimids ran a network of missionaries (the Arabic term is *da'i*, a "summoner" or propagandist) who spread Ismaili belief in secret across the Sunni-ruled lands to the east, including Persia. And it was as one of these Ismaili missionaries that the central figure of this chapter enters the story.

**Hasan-i Sabbah** (HAH-san ee sah-BAH) was a Persian, born in the city of Qom around the middle of the eleventh century, who converted to Ismaili Shiism as a young man, traveled to Fatimid Egypt to study, and returned to Persia as a *da'i* — a missionary for the cause, operating underground in the heart of the hostile Seljuk empire. He was, by every account, brilliant, austere, disciplined, and utterly committed. And he understood something about his situation with perfect clarity: a scattered, secret religious minority could never win a conventional war against the Seljuk state, which had more soldiers, more money, and more cities than the Ismailis could ever dream of. So he did not try to win that war. He found a different way to be unconquerable.

In 1090 Hasan-i Sabbah seized, by a mix of infiltration and persuasion rather than open assault, a remote mountain fortress called **Alamut** (AH-lah-moot) — the name is often glossed "the eagle's nest" — perched on a high crag in the Elburz mountains (the steep range walling off the southern shore of the Caspian Sea in northern Iran), in a tangle of valleys so steep and defensible that a small garrison could hold it against an army. Alamut became his capital, and from it he built up a network of similar mountain strongholds across northern Persia and, later, in the mountains of Syria. This was the genius of the strategy: a fortress on an inaccessible crag, well-provisioned and loyal, simply could not be taken by the methods of the day except by a siege so long and costly that it was rarely worth mounting. The Ismailis could not hold cities or open country against the Seljuks, but they could hold a constellation of eagle's nests indefinitely — a state with no territory to speak of, just a string of impregnable rocks, embedded inside the empire that wanted them dead.

Around the same time the wider Ismaili world fractured again, and Hasan's community went its own way. When the Fatimid caliph in Egypt died in 1094, the succession was disputed; Hasan-i Sabbah and his followers backed the claim of a son named **Nizar**, who lost the contest in Cairo and was killed. Hasan's community kept its loyalty to Nizar's line, broke decisively with Fatimid Egypt, and became the **Nizari Ismailis** (nih-ZAR-ee) — an independent movement, taking its religious lead from Alamut rather than Cairo, that scholars sometimes call the "New Preaching." They were now genuinely alone: a Shia splinter of a Shia splinter, surrounded on every side by enemies, holding nothing but their mountains.

This is the situation that produced the thing they became famous for. Cut off from any hope of fielding an army, the Nizaris developed targeted political murder into a deliberate, disciplined instrument of state — the calculated killing of a specific powerful enemy, a general or a vizier or a prince, usually with a dagger, usually in public, often at the cost of the killer's own life. The logic was coldly rational. A community that cannot defeat an empire's army can still kill the one man holding a campaign together, and the threat of that — the knowledge that any commander who moved against Alamut might be stabbed in his own mosque by a man who had patiently worked his way into his household — bought a kind of deterrence that no number of soldiers could. It was terror used by the weak against the strong, the politics of the dagger as a substitute for the politics of the battlefield.

By tradition, their first and most famous victim was Nizam al-Mulk himself — the great Seljuk vizier of Chapter 5, the architect of the madrasa system explicitly built in part to combat Ismaili propaganda — struck down by a Nizari killer disguised as a Sufi (a Muslim mystic; the inward, devotional current of Islam is the subject of Chapter 7) in 1092. Here a note of caution is required, because this is exactly the kind of vivid, satisfying story the rules of this book demand we examine rather than simply repeat. That the Nizaris claimed Nizam al-Mulk's killing, and that a long campaign of such assassinations is real and well-attested in the sources, is not in serious doubt. But the political background of any single famous murder is often murky — viziers had many enemies, including inside their own courts — and the neat tableau of the disguised Sufi assassin should be held as the tradition's version rather than as forensically certain fact. The pattern of Nizari assassination is solid history. The cinematic details of particular killings are frequently legend.

Which brings us to the legend proper — the "Assassins" of the European imagination — and it has to be dismantled carefully, because it is famous, it is lurid, and it is mostly invented. Start with the word. The English word *assassin* derives, through medieval European writers, from an Arabic term, **Hashishin** (hah-shih-SHEEN), apparently applied to the Nizaris by their enemies, which seems to mean something like "hashish-users" (hashish being the cannabis preparation). It was almost certainly a slur — a term of contempt thrown at a hated heretical sect, the medieval equivalent of calling someone a deranged drug fiend — not a literal description of their habits. There is no good evidence the Nizaris actually drugged their agents. But European writers, hearing the insult and the stories, built a fantasy on top of it.

The fullest version is the tale of the "Old Man of the Mountain," and its most influential teller is **Marco Polo**, the Venetian merchant who passed through Persia around 1271 on his way to China and recorded what he heard (decades after Alamut had already fallen, so he is repeating campfire legend, not eyewitness report). In Polo's telling, the lord of Alamut kept a hidden garden of earthly delights — flowing wine, fountains, beautiful companions, every pleasure — and would drug a young recruit, carry him unconscious into this secret paradise, let him wake and revel in it, then drug him again and remove him; the youth, convinced he had been to heaven and could be sent back by his master's command, would then murder anyone, fearlessly, to earn his return. It is a wonderful story. It is also, by the judgment of modern scholarship, a romance — a tissue of rumor, slur, and Orientalist fantasy assembled by outsiders, with no credible support in the Nizaris' own records or in the more sober contemporary sources. The reality was both less colorful and more impressive: the Nizari agents were not drugged dupes but, as far as we can tell, devoted believers, disciplined and motivated by genuine conviction and loyalty to their Imam, which is a far more unsettling thing than a hallucinated paradise.

The truth about Alamut points the same way. Far from being a den of intoxicated killers, the fortress housed a famous **library** and was a serious center of Ismaili learning and philosophy. The most striking proof of its intellectual seriousness is a single biography. **Nasir al-Din al-Tusi** (NAH-sir al-DEEN al-TOO-see), one of the greatest scientists and philosophers of the entire medieval world — astronomer, mathematician, author of the planetary models that some scholars believe influenced Copernicus (the sixteenth-century Polish astronomer who put the sun at the center of the cosmos) two centuries later — spent decades at the Nizari fortresses, doing his work in their libraries, before the end came. (His later, second career, building the great observatory for the Mongols who destroyed Alamut, is Chapter 10.) A community that could shelter and support a mind like al-Tusi's was not a drug cult. It was a small, embattled, intellectually ambitious religious state that had solved the problem of survival in a hostile world more ingeniously than anyone before it.

For a century and a half the strategy worked. The Nizaris outlasted the Seljuk empire that had first tried to destroy them; they survived the fracturing of the twelfth century; they negotiated, fought, assassinated, and endured, a permanent fixture of Persian and Syrian politics, the state that everyone feared and no one could conquer. What finally killed them was the thing that killed almost everything in this book: the Mongols. When **Hulagu Khan** (HOO-lah-goo), grandson of Genghis Khan, led a fresh Mongol army into Persia in the 1250s, one of his explicit objectives was to wipe out the Nizaris. The Mongols brought something the Seljuks never had — the patience, the engineering, the siege machinery, and the sheer overwhelming force to take even an eagle's nest. In 1256 Alamut surrendered to Hulagu, and the Mongols methodically reduced the other fortresses one by one. The famous library was burned, or at least dispersed and largely lost (al-Tusi, by tradition, salvaged some of it on his way into Mongol service). The Nizari Ismailis as a political and military power simply ended.

But — and this is the recurring note of the whole book — they did not vanish as a community. The Nizari Ismailis survived as a religious tradition, scattered and quiet, through the centuries that followed, and they exist today as a worldwide community of millions, still led by a hereditary Imam in the line they have always claimed, now styled the Aga Khan (the title borne by the present-day spiritual leader of the Nizari Ismailis). The mountain state died at Alamut in 1256. The faith that had built it did not. That distinction — between the destruction of a polity and the survival of a people and their culture underneath it — is the exact pattern that Chapter 9's catastrophe and Chapter 10's afterlife will play out on the grandest possible scale. The Assassins are the small, strange rehearsal for the book's biggest theme.


# Chapter 7 — The Inner Path

The last two chapters were about power: Turkish sultans, Persian viziers, mountain fortresses, the dagger and the madrasa. This chapter is about something that ran underneath all of it and outlasted all of it, and that turns out to be the most durable Persian achievement of the entire age. While the politics churned and the dynasties rose and fell and the Mongols loomed, Persians were building an interior world — a tradition of religious experience and, above all, of poetry, so deeply woven into the language that when the cities were smashed and the kings were killed, this was the thing that could not be destroyed, because it had gone inward, into the heart and onto the page. The poetry of this chapter is what the Persian language is still loved for today, a thousand years later. To understand why it could survive the coming catastrophe, you first have to understand what it was, and that means starting with a word.

The word is **Sufism** (SOO-fizm), the name for the mystical, inward dimension of Islam. Where the religious law (the *sharia*) and the theologians concerned themselves with correct belief and correct behavior — what to do, what to avoid, how the community should be ordered — Sufism concerned itself with the inner experience of God: the purification of the self, the disciplines of devotion, and above all the longing for, and the possibility of direct, loving, even ecstatic nearness to, the divine. The name probably comes from *suf*, the Arabic word for wool, after the simple woolen garments early ascetics wore. A practitioner is a **Sufi**. Sufism was not a separate religion or even, properly, a separate sect; it was a current within Islam, a way of living the faith from the inside out, and it existed in both Sunni and Shia settings. For much of early Islamic history it sat in some tension with the legal scholars, who were suspicious of mystics claiming private, unmediated knowledge of God, and of the wilder things some of them said in the grip of religious ecstasy. The great problem of the age was how to reconcile the two — the outer law and the inner path, the mosque and the heart. The man who did it, more than anyone, is the first major figure of this chapter.

**Al-Ghazali** (al-ghah-ZAH-lee) was a Persian, born around 1058 in the region of Tus in the northeast (the same region that produced Ferdowsi and Nizam al-Mulk), and he was, by his thirties, the most celebrated and successful religious scholar in the entire Islamic world. He held the most prestigious teaching chair there was — the professorship at the **Nizamiyya** college in Baghdad, the flagship of the state-funded madrasa system Nizam al-Mulk had built (Chapter 5). He was a master of law, theology, and philosophy, showered with honor, at the absolute summit of the intellectual establishment. And around 1095, at the height of that career, he suffered what we would now call a breakdown. He became convinced that all his learning and all his honors were hollow — that he was teaching religion for fame and salary rather than for God, and that he did not actually *know*, in his heart, the things he so brilliantly argued about. By his own later account he was physically unable to speak or lecture, his health collapsed under the strain, and he walked away from everything: the chair, the fame, the security. He left Baghdad and spent roughly a decade wandering, in poverty and obscurity, as a Sufi seeker, before eventually returning to teaching a changed man.

Out of that crisis came his masterwork, the **Ihya ulum al-din** (ih-YAH), "The Revival of the Religious Sciences" — an enormous, systematic book that did the thing the age most needed done. It reconciled the outer and the inner. Al-Ghazali argued, with all the authority of the establishment's own greatest scholar, that orthodox law and theology were necessary but not sufficient — that they were the outer shell, and that the inner, experiential, devotional path of Sufism was the kernel they existed to protect, and that a properly disciplined, law-abiding Sufism was not a threat to mainstream Sunni Islam but its living heart. Because this argument came from *him* — not from some marginal mystic the scholars could dismiss, but from the most impeccably orthodox authority alive — it carried enormous weight. Al-Ghazali is generally credited with making Sufism respectable, with bringing the inner path in from the suspicious margins and setting it near the center of mainstream Sunni piety, where it would remain for the rest of the period. The reconciliation was never total and never uncontested, but he shifted the whole balance.

He fought a second battle that shaped Islamic thought for centuries, and it cut the other way. In a sharp polemic called **The Incoherence of the Philosophers**, al-Ghazali attacked the Islamic philosophical tradition that had grown up around the work of thinkers like **Avicenna** (Ibn Sina, the great Persian polymath of Chapter 3) — a tradition that leaned heavily on Greek philosophy, especially Aristotle, and reached conclusions that al-Ghazali judged incompatible with Islamic doctrine (such as the eternity of the world, or limits on God's knowledge of particular things). His critique was rigorous and devastating, and it is often credited with checking the prestige of pure rationalist philosophy in the eastern Islamic world. (The book got a famous rebuttal a century later from the Spanish-Arab philosopher Averroes, titled *The Incoherence of the Incoherence* — a debate that, carried into Latin Europe, would help shape Western thought as well.) Historians still argue about how decisive al-Ghazali's blow really was, and whether the later relative decline of a certain kind of philosophy in the region can fairly be laid at his door; that is a genuine scholarly debate, not a settled fact. But that he framed the question of where reason ends and revelation begins, for the whole tradition after him, is not in doubt.

While al-Ghazali was making the inner path respectable in prose, Persian poets were making it immortal in verse, and this is where the chapter, and arguably the whole civilization, reaches its emotional core. Persian had been a poetic language since Rudaki at the Samanid court (Chapter 3); what happened now was that the mystical, the Sufi, the longing-for-God current flooded into it and produced poetry of a depth and beauty the language is still defined by. The pioneer was **Sanai** (sah-NY-ee) of Ghazni, a court poet who, around the early twelfth century, turned away from writing praise-poems for princes and toward mystical themes, and who pioneered the long Sufi narrative poem — the **masnavi** (mahs-nah-VEE), a poem in rhyming couplets that could run to enormous length, the verse form in which the greatest Persian mystical works would be written.

The form reached an early summit with **Attar** (ah-TAR), Farid al-Din Attar of Nishapur, a druggist and pharmacist by trade (the name means "the perfumer" or "apothecary") who wrote, around 1177, one of the most beloved allegories in any language: the **Conference of the Birds** (in Persian, *Mantiq al-Tayr*). In it, the birds of the world set out on a journey to seek their king, a mythical bird called the Simurgh, crossing seven perilous valleys, dropping away one by one through fear and weakness, until only thirty birds complete the journey — and discover, in a sublime pun, that the Simurgh they sought (*si murgh* means "thirty birds" in Persian) is nothing other than themselves, that the divine they searched the world for was within them all along. It is a Sufi map of the soul's journey toward God, told as a bird-fable, and it has never stopped being read.

Working in a different key was **Nizami Ganjavi** (nih-ZAH-mee gan-jah-VEE) of Ganja, in the Caucasus, who around the late twelfth century composed the **Khamsa** (KHAM-sah), the "Quintet" — five long narrative poems that defined the Persian romance, the idea of love in the language, for everyone after him. Two are still household names across the Persianate world: *Layla and Majnun*, the tragedy of a man driven literally mad (the name Majnun means "the possessed one") by an unattainable love, a story sometimes called the Romeo and Juliet of the East; and *Khosrow and Shirin*, the great romance of a Sasanian king and his beloved. Nizami's gorgeous, intricate storytelling became the template that painters illustrated and poets imitated for centuries.

A more philosophical strand of the inner path produced **Suhrawardi** (soo-hrah-WAR-dee), a Persian thinker who, in the 1180s, founded a whole school of mystical philosophy he called the "Philosophy of Illumination" — an attempt to fuse Avicenna's logic with Platonic and ancient Persian imagery of light as the substance of reality and the divine. He was brilliant, daring, and short-lived: his ideas were judged heretical, and he was executed at Aleppo around 1191, still in his thirties, which earned him the somber title "the Murdered." His light-mysticism nonetheless became a lasting current in later Persian thought.

But the towering figure — the poet who is, by wide agreement, the supreme voice of Persian mysticism and one of the most translated poets in the world today — is **Rumi** (ROO-mee), Jalal al-Din Rumi. And the circumstances of his life fold this chapter directly into the catastrophe of the next, which is why he belongs here at the end of it. Rumi was born around 1207 in or near **Balkh**, in the far northeast of the Persian world (in modern Afghanistan). While he was a boy, his family fled west — and what they were fleeing was the Mongol advance, the storm that is Chapter 9 (the family's flight ahead of Genghis Khan's invasion put them on the road around the 1210s–1220s). They settled, after long wandering, in **Konya** in Anatolia — former Byzantine territory recently turned Turkish and Persianate, the land the Seljuks had opened at Manzikert (Chapter 5) — where the place-name "Rum" (Rome) gave Rumi his name. So the greatest of all Persian poets wrote his Persian masterpieces not in Persia at all, but in a Turkish sultanate in Anatolia, as a refugee from the destruction of his homeland. That fact is the whole thesis of the book in one biography: the culture had become so portable, so interior, that it could be carried in a refugee's head across a thousand miles and flower more brilliantly than ever in exile.

Rumi was a respected but conventional scholar and preacher until, around 1244, he met a wandering, electrifying mystic named **Shams of Tabriz** (shams), and the encounter detonated something in him. The intense, transformative friendship with Shams turned the sober teacher into an ecstatic poet, and out of that transformation poured an astonishing torrent of verse: a vast collection of passionate lyric poems (the *Divan*), and, above all, the **Masnavi** — a sprawling six-volume mystical poem of some twenty-five thousand couplets, a teeming compendium of stories, parables, and meditations on the soul's love for and journey toward God, so revered that it is sometimes called "the Quran in the Persian tongue." Rumi's central image is love as the engine of all reality, the ache of separation from the divine and the longing for reunion, and he wrote it with a directness and warmth that has made him, eight centuries later, one of the best-selling poets in the modern English-speaking world (often, it must be said, in loose adaptations that quietly strip out the deeply Islamic context of the original — a modern echo of the FitzGerald treatment of Khayyam in Chapter 5).

Two more developments complete the inner world. After Rumi's death in 1273, his followers organized themselves into a formal brotherhood, the **Mevlevi** (MEV-leh-vee) order — the "whirling dervishes" of the modern tourist's image, who developed the meditative spinning dance called the *sama* as a form of worship, the body turning while the soul reaches for God. The Mevlevis are one instance of a much larger phenomenon that reshaped Persian religious life in exactly these centuries: the rise of organized Sufi brotherhoods, called **tariqas** (tah-REE-kahs). A *tariqa* (the word means "path" or "way") was a formal order built around a chain of spiritual masters reaching back to a founding saint, with lodges where members gathered, a master to guide each disciple, and distinctive practices and devotions. From around the twelfth century on, these brotherhoods spread across Persia and the wider Islamic world and became the dominant form of popular, lived religion for ordinary people — the place where most Persians actually experienced their faith, far more than the law schools or the theologians' debates. This matters enormously for what is coming. When the political and urban order was about to be smashed by the Mongols, religion in Persia was no longer concentrated in caliphs and capitals that could be destroyed. It had decentralized into thousands of lodges and into a body of poetry held in millions of memories.

That is the deep answer to the question the next three chapters raise — how, after a catastrophe that genuinely seemed to end the civilization, was there a culture left to revive? The answer is partly in this chapter. By the early thirteenth century the most precious things Persia had made were no longer only in its cities and its courts, which could be burned, but in its language, its poetry, and its Sufi lodges, which could not. The story's final survivor of the Mongol age, the poet **Saadi** (sah-DEE) of Shiraz, makes the point with his own life: he survived the catastrophe by spending its worst decades wandering far from home, and only after the Mongols had done their worst did he return to Shiraz and write the **Gulistan** ("The Rose Garden") and the **Bustan** ("The Orchard"), collections of moral tales and verse that became, for the next several centuries, the most quoted and most-taught books in the entire Persian language. The catastrophe is the subject of Chapter 9. That it did not, in the end, win, is the subject of Chapter 10 — and the reason it could not win is in this chapter.


# Chapter 8 — The Brittle Colossus

This is a short hinge of a chapter, and it has one job: to explain how the dazzling Persianate world of the last four chapters — the Seljuk synthesis, the mountain Assassins, the Sufi poets, the four-iwan mosques — came to be destroyed. The destroyer was the Mongols, and they are Chapter 9. But the Mongols did not come unprovoked, and they did not come for Persia first. They were dragged west by a single catastrophic blunder committed by an empire that had risen too fast and rested on too little, an empire most people have never heard of, which for one brief generation was the largest Muslim power on earth and then evaporated almost overnight. To understand the worst disaster in Persian history, you first have to understand the brittle giant that invited it in.

The giant was **Khwarazm** (kwah-RAZM), and the place itself is the first thing to fix, because the name is unfamiliar. Khwarazm was a region centered on the delta of the **Amu Darya** (AH-moo DAR-yah) — the great river the ancient Greeks called the Oxus — where it empties into the Aral Sea (a large inland lake of Central Asia, northeast of Persia), in what is now Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan, on the northern edge of the Persianate world where the settled lands met the steppe. It was an old, rich, irrigated land of trading cities, and its rulers carried the title **Khwarazm-Shah** ("Shah of Khwarazm"). Here, again, the book's central machinery is at work: the Khwarazm-Shahs began the period not as independent kings but as provincial governors serving the Seljuks (Chapter 5), Turkic in origin, ruling a Persian land with a Persian administration. As the Seljuk empire fractured after the death of Sultan Sanjar in the mid-twelfth century, these governors did what governors all over the crumbling empire were doing: they made themselves independent. But the Khwarazm-Shahs did it bigger than anyone.

In the space of a few decades, roughly the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, they expanded with stunning speed out of their delta base to swallow most of Persia and Central Asia. They conquered the last remnants of the eastern Seljuks, they took the great cities — Bukhara, Samarkand, the cities of Khurasan — they pushed into northwestern Persia and toward Baghdad, and by around 1210 they had assembled, in one or two generations, the largest empire in the Muslim world. On a map it looked colossal. It looked like a worthy successor to the Seljuks, a new Persianate superpower stretching from the edge of India to the edge of Iraq.

It was, in reality, a shell. And the reasons it was a shell are the whole point of the chapter, because an empire built this fast had no time to grow the things that hold an empire together. Consider the difference from the Seljuks. The Seljuk synthesis took its strength from a genuine partnership cured over generations — Turkish soldiers and Persian administrators who had, by Nizam al-Mulk's day, learned to work as one machine, bound by a shared interest and a worked-out system (Chapter 5). The Khwarazmian empire had no such depth. Its army was a heterogeneous mass of recently-recruited Turkic nomad warriors whose loyalty ran to their own tribal commanders rather than to the throne, and whom the Shah did not really trust. Its conquered cities had been swallowed too recently to be digested; their populations had no love for their new masters. And at the top sat a ruler who personified the brittleness.

**Ala al-Din Muhammad II** (the Khwarazm-Shah from around 1200) was an ambitious and capable conqueror, but his realm was held together by force and fear rather than loyalty, and he ruled it badly in the specific way that would prove fatal. He distrusted his own generals (with some reason — they were tribal warlords who might turn on him), and he quarreled bitterly with the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad, whom he tried to depose, alienating the one source of religious legitimacy that might have rallied the Muslim world behind him. Crucially, he had no real strategy for defending such an enormous, recently-conquered, internally distrustful territory against a serious external threat. His empire was wide and shallow — vast in extent, thin in the bonds that actually make a state hold, a structure that looked like a great power and would shatter at the first hard blow. The blow was about to come from a direction Muhammad barely understood.

Far to the east, on the Mongolian steppe, a new power had arisen that dwarfed anything in the nomadic world's previous experience. **Genghis Khan** (GENG-gis KHAN, also spelled Chinggis) had, over the preceding decades, united the warring Mongol and Turkic tribes of the eastern steppe into a single disciplined military machine of terrifying effectiveness — the subject, in full, of this project's Mongol Empire timeline. By the 1210s Genghis had conquered northern China and turned his attention to the rich lands of Central Asia, where his expanding domain now bordered the western edge of Muhammad's empire. And here is the thing the legend often gets wrong, and that the record makes clear: Genghis Khan did not initially want to invade the Muslim world. He wanted to trade with it. The two empires were neighbors; the Silk Road ran between them; Genghis sent a large commercial caravan and, with it, envoys, to open peaceful trade relations with the Khwarazm-Shah.

What happened next is one of the most consequential blunders in human history, and it happened in 1218 at a frontier city called **Otrar** (oh-TRAR), on the Syr Darya river near the border of the two empires. The governor of Otrar — a relative of the Shah's named Inalchuq — seized the entire Mongol caravan, called the merchants spies, and had them killed. (The next chapter tells the seizure and its aftermath in full, from the Mongol side.) Genghis Khan, who by the practice of the steppe held envoys and the security of trade to be sacred, demanded the governor be handed over. Muhammad refused, and compounded the refusal by abusing the embassy Genghis sent. That sealed his empire's fate.

That was the fatal insult. Genghis Khan had been facing east, toward China, with no particular designs on Persia; the murder of his merchants and the killing of his ambassadors turned him west, with the full weight of the Mongol war machine and a cold determination to make an example of the man who had wronged him. The Mongol armies that crossed into Khwarazmian territory in 1219 were not coming for plunder or for the ordinary objectives of conquest. They were coming for revenge, and they came in a mood to annihilate.

What that unleashed on Persia — the brittle colossus shattering on contact, the systematic destruction of the great cities, the wrecking of the irrigation that made the plateau livable, the death tolls that Persian memory has never let go of — is the subject of the next chapter, and it is the nadir of this entire book. The largest Muslim empire on earth would last barely a generation, and it would die because its ruler could not control a greedy frontier governor and would not swallow his pride before the most dangerous man alive. But the lesson of this one should be carried into it. The catastrophe was not inevitable, and it was not, in its origin, even really about Persia. It came down on the Persianate world because a brittle, overreaching empire built on fear and speed rather than on the slow, deep partnership that had made the Seljuks strong, committed a single act of arrogance at a frontier town that almost no one remembers — and turned the deadliest army in the world from facing east to facing west. Persia would pay for Otrar for a hundred years.


# Chapter 9 — The End of the World

The previous chapter ended with a diplomatic incident. A governor in a frontier town called **Otrar** (oh-TRAR), on the Syr Darya river in what is now southern Kazakhstan, seized a Mongol trade caravan, accused its merchants of spying, and had them killed. When the man who had sent that caravan asked for the governor to be handed over, the shah of Khwarazm (the brittle, overgrown empire that had swallowed most of Persia in a single generation) refused, and for good measure executed the senior envoy and singed the beards of the other two before sending them home. The man who had sent the caravan was **Genghis Khan** (GEN-giss kahn), and he took it personally.

That is the small hinge on which the largest catastrophe in Persian memory turns, and it is worth pausing on how small it was. Genghis Khan, the founder and supreme ruler of the **Mongol Empire** (a confederation of Turkic and Mongolic nomad tribes from the grasslands north of China, which he had welded into a single terrifying war machine and then aimed at his neighbors), had not been planning to invade the Islamic world. His attention was on China. He had sent the caravan, by every surviving account, as a genuine trade overture, because Khwarazm sat astride the western end of the **Silk Road** (the chain of overland caravan routes linking China to the Mediterranean) and an open trade relationship was worth more than a war. The Otrar massacre changed the calculation. A *khan* (the Mongol and Turkic word for a ruler or chief; Genghis held the grander title of *khagan*, "khan of khans") who let an insult like that stand would not stay khan for long, because his authority rested on the certainty that crossing him was suicide. So in 1219 he turned the war machine west.

A word about that machine, because the destruction it caused makes no sense otherwise. The Mongol army was not a horde in the sense of a disorganized mob; the word "horde" comes from the Mongol *ordu*, a royal camp, and the reality was the most disciplined and mobile military force in the world. It was built around mounted archers riding small, tough steppe ponies, every man with a string of remounts so the army could cover ground at a speed settled peoples found literally unbelievable, sometimes a hundred miles (160 km) in a day. It used feigned retreats to draw enemies out of formation, coordinated its movements across vast distances with a relay of signal flags and fast riders, and — crucially for a story about cities — it learned siege warfare fast, capturing Chinese engineers and their catapults and using them against the next set of walls. And it operated by a logic of terror that was deliberate, not incidental. A city that surrendered at once was spared and taxed. A city that resisted was made into an example so horrifying that the next city would not dare resist. The massacres were not the war getting out of hand. They were the strategy.

Khwarazm should have been able to fight. On paper the shah, **Muhammad II** (Chapter 8), had the larger army and the home ground. But his empire was held together by fear of him rather than loyalty to him, his Turkic commanders distrusted each other and the shah distrusted them all, and he made the fatal decision to garrison his cities separately rather than mass his forces and meet the Mongols in one great battle. That handed Genghis Khan exactly the war he was best at: a sequence of isolated sieges, each city left to face the whole Mongol army alone while the others waited their turn. The shah himself, instead of leading a defense, fled west across his own collapsing empire and died sick and abandoned on a tiny island in the Caspian Sea in 1220, which is a fitting epitaph for a man who built an empire on the assumption that nothing could ever go truly wrong.

Otrar fell first, after a long siege, and the governor whose greed had started everything was — by a much-repeated tradition that may well be more symbol than fact — executed by having molten metal poured into his eyes and ears, a death fitted to a man who had killed for silver. Then came the great cities, and here the chapter has to slow down and tell the reader something about its sources, because what happened next has been recorded mostly by men with strong reasons to make it sound either better or worse than it was. The two indispensable Persian chroniclers of the Mongol conquest both wrote while serving the Mongols. **Ata-Malik Juvayni** (ah-tah-MAH-lik joo-VY-nee), who composed his *History of the World Conqueror* around 1260, was a Persian administrator employed by the very regime that had ruined his homeland, and he wrote a vivid, anguished, but ultimately accommodationist account. **Rashid al-Din** (rah-SHEED ad-DEEN), whose great history we will meet properly in the next chapter, was the chief minister of the converted Mongol rulers and wrote to glorify their dynasty. Neither was an impartial witness, and both belonged to a literary tradition that reached for catastrophe at full volume. So when the chronicles give body counts in the hundreds of thousands, or even over a million for a single city, the honest thing to say is that these figures are almost certainly inflated — they are the language of trauma and warning, not a census — and that the real toll, while still appalling, was very probably a good deal smaller. The destruction was genuine. The numbers are propaganda for genuineness.

With that caveat in hand: **Bukhara** (boo-KHA-rah), the old Samanid capital that had been called "the dome of Islam in the East" (Chapter 3), fell in early 1220. The garrison tried to break out and was hunted down; the city surrendered. Genghis Khan rode into the great congregational mosque, and the surviving story has him asking whether it was the sultan's palace, being told it was the house of God, and ordering the grain chests stored there and the holy Qur'ans trampled under the hooves of the cavalry's horses while the city's notables held the bridles. Whether the scene is literally accurate or a chronicler's image of the world turned upside down, it captures what Bukhara felt to Persian memory: the unthinkable, the sacred itself dishonored. The population was driven out, the useful craftsmen sorted from the doomed, and the city burned. Juvayni's famous line about the catastrophe, often rendered as the Mongols having "come, uprooted, burned, slain, carried off, and departed," is as close as medieval Persian prose comes to a scream.

**Samarkand** (SAM-ar-kand) fell within weeks, betrayed in part by its own garrison, its craftsmen deported east to serve Mongol needs, its survivors taxed. Then the army turned south into **Khurasan** (khoo-rah-SAHN), the great northeastern Persian province that had been the seedbed of the Abbasid Revolution four centuries earlier (Chapter 1) and the cultural heart of the Samanid and Seljuk worlds. Khurasan held three of the largest cities on earth — **Merv** (merv), **Nishapur** (nee-shah-POOR), and **Herat** (heh-RAHT) — and in 1221 the Mongols destroyed them in a campaign of such thoroughness that it changed the demography of the region for centuries. Merv, possibly the most populous city in the world at the time, surrendered and was massacred anyway. Nishapur, where a Mongol prince and son-in-law of Genghis had been killed by a crossbow bolt during the assault, was singled out for a vengeance so total that the chroniclers say the heads of the dead were stacked in pyramids, men, women, and children sorted into separate heaps. Herat was destroyed, repopulated, and destroyed again when it rebelled. Even allowing fully for inflated figures, this was the deliberate erasure of an entire urban civilization, the densest concentration of Persian learning and wealth in the world, in the space of about two years.

And then there was a kind of damage that no chronicler dramatized because it killed slowly and out of sight, but that may have done more lasting harm than any massacre: the wrecking of the **qanats** (kuh-NAHTS). A qanat is an underground irrigation channel, a gently sloping tunnel dug from a water source in the hills down to the fields, sometimes for miles, with vertical shafts to the surface for digging and maintenance. The qanat is the technology that makes the dry Iranian plateau farmable at all; it carries water without the evaporation losses of an open canal, and it represents centuries of accumulated, skilled labor underground. When the Mongols killed or scattered the populations who maintained them, the qanats silted up and collapsed, and a collapsed qanat is not a thing you simply switch back on — it has to be re-dug by people who know how, and the people who knew how were dead or gone. Whole districts of cultivated land reverted to desert and stayed that way. Some regions of northeastern Persia did not recover their pre-Mongol population for centuries, and some, arguably, never have. This is the unromantic core of the catastrophe: not the heaped skulls the chronicles love, but the empty fields nobody could refill.

There were flickers of resistance, and they only underline how total the defeat was. The shah's son, **Jalal al-Din Mangburni** (jah-LAHL ad-DEEN MANG-bur-nee), turned out to be everything his father was not — a genuinely brave and capable soldier — and he led a decade-long fugitive war against the Mongols, winning the occasional battle, fleeing across the Indus into India when he had to, raiding the Caucasus, and generally making himself a nuisance from one end of the wreckage to the other. But he commanded the last loyal scraps of a dead empire, he made enemies of nearly every Muslim power that might have helped him, and in 1231 he was killed in obscure circumstances in the mountains of Anatolia, reportedly by a Kurdish bandit (the Kurds being a mountain people of the highlands where modern Iraq, Iran, and Turkey meet) who had no idea who he was. With him died the last claim to a pre-Mongol Persian sovereignty. Genghis Khan himself had died in 1227, in China, on a separate campaign; the western conquest he had launched out of personal affront was now simply a fact of the map, an inheritance to be divided among his heirs.

The destruction had a strange side effect that mattered enormously for the rest of this book: it scattered Persia's poets and scholars westward, out of the kill zone and into the courts of Anatolia and the lands the Mongols had not yet reached, and there, in exile, Persian culture did some of its most enduring work. The single greatest example fled from Khurasan itself. A family from **Balkh** (balkh), near the Mongol path of advance, packed up and traveled west, settling eventually in **Konya** (KOHN-yah) in Anatolia, then ruled by a branch of the Seljuks. The family's young son would grow up to be **Rumi** (ROO-mee), full name Jalal al-Din, the most beloved mystical poet the Persian language has ever produced — a man whose ecstatic verse about divine love would, seven centuries later, make him a perennial bestseller in American bookshops. We will come to his poetry in its own place (Chapter 7 covers the Sufi flowering that produced it). For now the point is the irony: the Mongol terror that seemed to end Persian civilization was, at the very same moment, dispersing the seeds of its most famous flowering.

But the catastrophe was not finished. A generation after Genghis Khan, the Mongols came back to Persia not to raid but to rule, and the man who came was his grandson. In 1256 **Hulagu Khan** (hoo-LAH-goo kahn) arrived at the head of a fresh and enormous army, sent west by the **Great Khan** in Mongolia (then Hulagu's brother) with two specific assignments: destroy the **Assassins** in their mountain fortresses, and bring the Islamic caliphate to heel. The Assassins were the **Nizari Ismailis**, a revolutionary Shia minority (a branch of Islam that, unlike the Sunni majority, held that authority belonged to the line of the Prophet's family) who for a century and a half had survived inside far stronger empires by perfecting the targeted political murder, striking from a network of nearly impregnable castles (Chapter 6 tells their whole story). Their crown fortress was **Alamut** (ah-lah-MOOT), an eagle's-nest of a castle in the Elburz mountains of northern Persia that no army had ever taken by force. Hulagu took it. The Nizari leader, faced with the Mongol siege engines and the hopelessness of his position, surrendered the castles one by one in 1256, and the Mongols methodically destroyed them, including Alamut's celebrated library, burning or scattering a collection of philosophy and science that had been gathered over generations. The dagger that had terrorized the Seljuk world for a hundred and fifty years was simply outmatched by an empire that could not be intimidated and could not be reached by a single well-placed knife.

Then Hulagu turned to the second assignment, and it produced the moment that, more than any massacre, marks the symbolic end of the old order. In 1258 he marched on **Baghdad**, the round city the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur had founded five centuries earlier beside the ruins of the old Persian capital (Chapter 1), and the seat of the **caliph** — the supposed successor to the Prophet Muhammad and the nominal sovereign of all Sunni Islam, an office that by now held little real power but immense religious prestige, the institutional center of the Muslim world for half a millennium. The reigning caliph, **al-Musta'sim** (al-moos-TAH-sim), was weak, badly advised, and catastrophically slow to grasp that the Mongols meant exactly what they said. He neither submitted in time nor prepared a serious defense. The city fell after a short siege. The Mongols sacked it for days, slaughtered a large part of the population, and burned the libraries of the greatest center of learning in the Islamic world — there is a much-repeated tradition that the Tigris ran black with the ink of drowned books and red with blood, which is exactly the kind of literary image the reader should now know to treat as memory rather than measurement, true to the horror if not to the chemistry. And the caliph himself was executed. The sources differ on exactly how he died, but the most famous and most-repeated tradition turns on a Mongol custom forbidding the spilling of royal blood on the ground: al-Musta'sim, it holds, was rolled in a carpet and trampled to death by horses, so that no drop of his blood touched the earth.

It is difficult to overstate what that meant to the contemporary Muslim world. The caliphate as a continuous institution running back to the death of the Prophet — the thing that, however hollowed out, had defined the political shape of Sunni Islam for five hundred years — was simply ended, in an afternoon, by a non-Muslim warlord from the grasslands of Mongolia. To many it looked like the end of the world in the most literal sense available to a medieval mind: the divinely ordained order of things had been overturned, and there was no obvious reason the conquerors should stop.

They were stopped, but not by anyone in Persia. In 1260, an Egyptian army of **Mamluks** (MAM-looks) — a regime of military slave-soldiers, mostly of Turkic and Caucasian origin, who had seized power in Egypt and made themselves its rulers — met a Mongol force at **Ayn Jalut** (ine jah-LOOT) in Palestine and defeated it. The victory was helped by the fact that Hulagu had pulled most of his army back east on news of the Great Khan's death, leaving a smaller force behind, but it was a real defeat all the same, the first major check the Mongols had suffered in the west, and it fixed the boundary of Mongol Persia roughly where it would stay. Beyond that line lay the Mamluks and the surviving Islamic world; behind it lay a Persia that had now been conquered twice — once by Arabs six centuries before, now by Mongols — and that lay, at the end of this chapter, in what looked like terminal ruin: its cities gutted, its fields gone to desert, its caliph dead, its scholars in exile or dead, its civilization seemingly extinguished by a power that did not even share its religion.

Seemingly. Because the most extraordinary thing about this whole story is what the conquerors did next, and it is the same impossible thing the Arabs had done before them. They lost.

# Chapter 10 — The Conquerors Convert

Here is the pattern this whole book is built around, stated once more so the reader can watch it happen at the largest scale it ever reaches. Six centuries before this chapter, the Arabs annihilated the Persian Empire and then, within a few generations, found themselves running their new state with Persian secretaries, writing their poetry to Persian models, and building their capital in the old Persian imperial landscape — the conquered quietly taking over the inside of the conqueror (Chapters 1 through 5). Now it is the Mongols' turn. They came as the most destructive force in Persian history, men who did not share the religion, the language, the cities, or the values of the people they had crushed. And within forty years of the sack of Baghdad, the grandsons of the destroyers were Muslims, wrote and patronized in Persian, sponsored the finest Persian painting the world had yet seen, and underwrote the first attempt anyone had ever made to write the history of the whole world. The pattern did not just repeat. It repeated faster, and on a grander stage.

Start with the state they built, because the absorption happened through its machinery. Hulagu's realm became the **Ilkhanate** (ILL-kah-nayt), and the name itself tells you something. *Il* in Mongol means subordinate or obedient; an *ilkhan* was a khan who acknowledged the supremacy of the **Great Khan**, the supreme ruler of the whole Mongol Empire back in the east (the office Genghis had held, now held by his descendants ruling China). So the Ilkhanate began as a frankly colonial arrangement: a Mongol military aristocracy ruling Persia and Iraq as a far-western province of a Mongol world-empire, its center in the northwest of Iran around **Tabriz** (tah-BREEZ) and the summer pastures of Azerbaijan, where the grass suited the horses. The early Ilkhans were not Muslims. Hulagu himself leaned toward Buddhism and had a Christian wife, and the early Mongol regime in Persia was strikingly indifferent to Islam — it taxed mosque and church and temple alike, employed Christians and Jews and Buddhists in high office, and treated the religion of the conquered majority as simply one option among several. For a Muslim population that had just watched its caliph trampled in a carpet, this was its own kind of trauma: not only conquered, but ruled by infidels who did not even pretend to convert.

And yet the machinery of rule pulled the conquerors in. The Mongols were brilliant at destroying states and hopeless at running them; a nomad aristocracy that counted its wealth in horses and herds had no idea how to assess and collect the land-tax of a complex agricultural empire, and so, exactly as the Arabs had before them, they reached for the people who did know — the Persian **secretaries** and administrators, the *katibs* and viziers whose families had been running Persian bureaucracies under one dynasty after another for half a millennium. The conqueror needs the conquered to make the conquest pay. That single fiscal fact is the lever by which Persian civilization got back inside the empire that had wrecked it, and it worked the same way it had under the caliphs, only faster, because the Mongols were starting from even greater ignorance.

The first great figure of the recovery had, fittingly, served everyone. **Nasir al-Din al-Tusi** (nah-SEER ad-DEEN at-TOO-see) was a Persian polymath — astronomer, mathematician, philosopher, theologian — of staggering range, who had spent years inside the Assassin fortresses, possibly as a willing guest and possibly as a kind of honored hostage, writing for the Nizari Ismailis. When Hulagu took Alamut, al-Tusi smoothly transferred his services to the conqueror, and rather than being killed he became Hulagu's trusted advisor. What he talked the Mongol into is one of the more wonderful turns in the whole story: he persuaded Hulagu, fresh from burning the libraries of Baghdad, to fund a magnificent new center of learning. The **Maragha observatory** (mah-RAH-gah), built from 1259 in northwestern Persia, was a research institution on a scale the world had not seen, equipped with large precision instruments, staffed by astronomers gathered from across the Mongol domains (including, through the open road to the east, Chinese astronomers), and stocked with a library said to hold hundreds of thousands of volumes salvaged from the wreckage. The Maragha school refined the planetary models inherited from the Greeks, and devised a mathematical device now called the **Tusi couple** — a clever way of producing straight-line motion from two rotating circles — that turns up, two and a half centuries later, in the work of the Polish astronomer Copernicus, the man who put the sun at the center of the cosmos. Whether Copernicus borrowed it directly from the Maragha tradition or arrived at it independently is genuinely debated by historians and cannot be settled from the surviving evidence; what is not in doubt is that the device was first worked out at a Persian observatory built with money from the man who had just sacked Baghdad. The conqueror was already paying for the conquered's science.

But the decisive turn was religious, and it came in 1295 with the seventh Ilkhan, **Ghazan** (gah-ZAHN). Ghazan was a great-great-grandson of Genghis Khan, born and raised in Persia, and he had been brought up a Buddhist. When he seized the throne in 1295 he converted to Islam, declared it the state religion, and brought a large part of the Mongol military aristocracy with him. This was the hinge. The descendants of the men who had trampled the caliph now prayed in the direction of Mecca. We should be careful about why, because it is easy to tell a sentimental story here and the honest one is more interesting. Ghazan's conversion was partly genuine conviction and partly hard politics — converting aligned him with the overwhelming Muslim majority he was trying to tax and govern, secured the loyalty of the Persian administrative class he depended on, and distinguished his realm from the rival Mongol states he was fighting. Conversion and self-interest pointed the same way, which is usually when conversions happen. However it is weighted, the outcome was the same: within two generations of Baghdad, the Mongol regime in Persia was a Muslim, increasingly Persianized state, ruling in the old Persian idiom of kingship, its rulers taking Islamic names and Persian titles, indistinguishable in many respects from the dynasties they had destroyed.

The man who managed this transformation, and who embodies the whole pattern better than anyone, was Ghazan's chief minister: **Rashid al-Din** (rah-SHEED ad-DEEN). Rashid al-Din was a Persian physician, born Jewish and converted to Islam, who rose to be the most powerful vizier of the Ilkhanate and ran its government for two reigns. Under him and Ghazan, the Mongol state was rebuilt on Persian administrative lines: the coinage was reformed and standardized, the tax system was overhauled to stop the ruinous arbitrary exactions that had been bleeding the peasantry, the postal relay system was regularized, and ruined agricultural land was deliberately resettled. This was the conscious repair of the very damage the Mongols had done — a Mongol king and his Persian minister rebuilding the qanats and the tax registers their own grandfathers had wrecked.

And Rashid al-Din did something even more remarkable than govern well. He wrote a book, or rather supervised the writing of one, called the **Jami al-Tawarikh** (jah-MEE at-tah-WAH-reekh), the "Compendium of Chronicles," and it has a fair claim to be the first true world history ever attempted. Earlier histories, in every culture, had been the history of *us* — one dynasty, one people, one faith, with everyone else appearing only as enemies or barbarians at the edges. Rashid al-Din set out, with the Mongol empire's unique reach giving him access to informants from across Eurasia, to write the history of *everyone*: the Mongols, of course, but also the Chinese, the ancient Persians, the Jews, the Arabs and the rise of Islam, the popes and emperors of the Christian Franks, and the Indians and their religions. He had the empire's pan-Eurasian connections, and he used them — drawing on Chinese scholars for Chinese history, on a Buddhist monk for the history of India, on European sources for the Franks. It is a flawed and uneven book, and it was written, the reader should remember, to glorify the Mongol dynasty that employed its author. But its ambition is genuinely without precedent. The first time anyone tried to write the history of the whole human world, it was a Persian minister doing it, for a Mongol king, in the Persian language, in a state founded on the ruins the Mongols had made.

The same Mongol-opened roads that let Rashid al-Din gather Chinese history brought something else west: Chinese art. The unified Mongol world, sometimes called the *Pax Mongolica* (the "Mongol peace," the period when a single Mongol-dominated political order made the overland routes across Asia unusually safe for merchants and travelers), connected Ilkhanid Persia directly to **Yuan China** (the Mongol dynasty then ruling China) for the first time. Persian merchants, diplomats, and artists traveled the whole breadth of Asia, and Chinese motifs traveled back with them — the dragon, the phoenix, the curling cloud-band, the particular way Chinese painters rendered rocks and water and mountains. These poured into Persian art and were absorbed and transformed, and out of that fusion of Chinese style, older Persian and Mesopotamian traditions, and a touch of Byzantine influence from the west, something new crystallized: the **Persian miniature** (mih-nee-uh-TYOOR or MIN-ee-uh-chur), the tradition of small, jewel-bright, intensely detailed paintings made to illustrate books. This is one of the supreme achievements of Persian, indeed of all Islamic, visual art, and it was born in the Ilkhanid workshops, midwifed by the Mongol conquest that had opened the road to China.

The grandest surviving fruit of those workshops is a manuscript, and it ties the whole chapter together with a beautiful irony. Late in the Ilkhanate, the court commissioned a monumental illustrated copy of the **Shahnameh** (shah-nah-MEH) — Ferdowsi's national epic, the "Book of Kings," the 50,000-couplet poem of Persia's mythic and royal past that had almost single-handedly saved the Persian language three centuries earlier (Chapter 3). This manuscript, known today as the **Great Mongol Shahnameh** (also called the Demotte Shahnameh after a later dealer who, infamously, broke it up to sell the pages separately), is among the most powerful works of painting the Islamic world ever produced. Its illustrations are large, dark, emotionally charged scenes of kings and battles and mourning. And consider what it is: the Persian national epic, the great monument of Persian cultural survival, lavishly illustrated by Mongol-employed painters for a Mongol court that had converted to Islam and was now patronizing the very identity its founders had tried to erase. The Mongols did not merely tolerate the Shahnameh. They paid for its most magnificent edition. The conquerors had not just converted; they had adopted the conquered's deepest story as their own.

They built, too. The Ilkhan **Oljeitu** (ohl-JY-too), Ghazan's brother and successor, raised at his new capital of **Soltaniyeh** (sohl-tah-NEE-yeh) an enormous domed mausoleum for himself, one of the largest brick domes ever built anywhere up to that time, its surface a skin of turquoise tile. It still stands, a vast empty shell on the Iranian plateau, and it is a landmark of Persian architecture — a Mongol king's tomb in the full Persian-Islamic idiom of dome and tile and *muqarnas* (the honeycomb of stacked niches that became Persian architecture's signature ornament). The men who had burned the cities now competed to build the most beautiful ones.

It did not last, of course; nothing in this book does. The Ilkhanate ran on the energy of strong individual khans, and when the last effective one, **Abu Said** (ah-BOO sah-EED), died in 1335 without a clear heir, the state simply came apart. There was no institution underneath it strong enough to hold it together — the Mongol succession had always been a fight, and now there was no obvious fighter. Persia fractured into a patchwork of squabbling successor dynasties: the **Jalayirids** (jah-LY-ihr-idz) in Iraq and the northwest, the **Muzaffarids** (moo-ZAH-fah-ridz) in the south around Shiraz and Isfahan, the populist **Sarbadars** (sar-bah-DARZ) in Khurasan, and others. We will pick up those fragments at the end of this book. The point for now is the shape of the thing. In a little over a century, the Mongols had conquered Persia, very nearly destroyed it, ruled it, converted to its religion, adopted its language and its art and its idea of kingship, repaired some of the damage they had done, sponsored its first world history and its finest painting — and then dissolved back into the ground, leaving Persia, battered but unmistakably itself, to carry on.

The reader who has been paying attention will sense what is coming. A pattern that has now happened twice, on this scale, with this much detail, is not an accident. It is a law. And in the very next chapter, history is going to test that law by running the entire experiment again — a new world-conqueror, a new wave of devastation, and a new brilliant court rising out of the wreckage — as if to prove that this is simply what happens when somebody conquers Persia.

# Chapter 11 — Skulls and Tilework

Before the conqueror, the poet, because he was already alive and writing when the storm broke, and because he is the proof that the thing the Mongols and their successors kept failing to destroy had by now retreated somewhere no army could reach.

In the city of **Shiraz** (shee-RAHZ), in the soft southern uplands of Persia, in the broken decades after the Ilkhanate fell apart, lived the greatest lyric poet the Persian language ever produced: **Hafez** (HAH-fez). His name is a title — it means one who has memorized the entire Qur'an by heart — and he spent most of his life in Shiraz under the local **Muzaffarid** dynasty (one of the splinter states left by the Ilkhanate's collapse, Chapter 10), writing in a form called the **ghazal** (guh-ZAHL): a short lyric poem of rhyming couplets, traditionally about love and wine and longing, which in Persian hands became something far stranger and deeper, because the love and the wine are always also, and never only, about the love of God and the intoxication of the divine. Hafez brought the ghazal to a pitch of compression and ambiguity that has never been equaled. A single couplet can be read at once as a drinking song, a love poem, a piece of mystical theology, and a sly satire on the hypocrite preacher — all four meanings live in the same words, and you cannot pin the poem down to one. To this day Iranians keep a copy of his collected poems, the *Divan*, in the house and consult it as an oracle, opening it at random and reading the first lines they land on as a kind of fortune, a practice called *fal-e Hafez*. Goethe, the towering figure of German literature, fell so hard for Hafez two centuries ago that he wrote a whole collection of poems in homage. That is the measure of the man: a provincial poet in a minor splinter-state, writing during one of the most violent and chaotic stretches of Persian history, produced verse that became, and remains, the most loved poetry in the language.

Hold that image — the supreme refinement of a high culture, produced in a small inland city by a man writing about wine and God — against what was coming, because the contrast is the whole point of the chapter.

What was coming was **Timur** (tee-MOOR), known in the West as **Tamerlane** (a corruption of *Timur-e Lang*, "Timur the Lame," after a leg wound that left him limping for life). Timur was born around 1336 near Samarkand, in the region the geographers called **Transoxiana** (tranz-ox-ee-AH-nah), "the land beyond the Oxus," meaning the rich, settled territory beyond the Amu Darya river, the old Oxus, in what is now Uzbekistan. He was a Turco-Mongol — a man from the Turkic-speaking, partly Mongol-descended military aristocracy that the Mongol conquests had left scattered across Central Asia — and he was a Muslim, which is the first thing that distinguishes him from Genghis Khan. But he modeled himself on Genghis with open and deliberate ambition. He could not claim the title of khan himself, because by the rigid steppe law of legitimacy only a direct male descendant of Genghis could be a khan, so Timur ruled formally as an *amir* (a commander) in the name of a puppet khan of Genghisid blood whom he kept as a figurehead, while marrying into the Genghisid line to borrow its prestige. From the 1360s he fought his way to mastery of Transoxiana, took Samarkand as his capital around 1370, and from that base launched thirty-five years of almost ceaseless conquest in every direction.

And here the pattern of the previous two chapters comes back with a vengeance, because Timur did to Persia and the surrounding world more or less what the Mongols had done, only this time the conqueror was a Muslim doing it to fellow Muslims, which makes the savagery somehow harder to fit into any frame of holy war. His armies overran Persia in the 1380s and 1390s. They sacked Baghdad — twice — and after the second revolt the chronicles say his soldiers were each ordered to bring back a severed head, and that the heads were built into towers, a signature horror Timur repeated at city after city. He sacked Isfahan and reportedly massacred tens of thousands when it rose against his tax collectors, building, again, towers of skulls outside its walls. He stormed Delhi in India in 1398 and left it a charnel house. He took Damascus and Aleppo in Syria. The body counts in the sources are, once again, the language of catastrophe and should be read as such, but the towers of skulls are too consistently and too widely attested, across hostile and friendly sources alike, to be dismissed; this was a man who used systematic, theatrical massacre as an instrument of policy, exactly as Genghis had, and who seems to have genuinely believed it was his destiny to do so. Persia, only beginning to recover from the Mongols a century and a half before, was devastated a second time.

His most famous single victory came at the very end of his life and against the rising power that would, much later, define the Near East. In 1402, at the **Battle of Ankara** (ANG-kah-rah) in central Anatolia, Timur met the army of the **Ottoman Empire** (the Turkish state, founded around 1300, that was in the process of conquering the old Byzantine and Balkan lands and would go on to take Constantinople in 1453). The Ottoman sultan, **Bayezid I** (BY-uh-zeed), was a formidable conqueror in his own right, nicknamed "the Thunderbolt." Timur shattered his army and took him prisoner. There is a persistent legend that Timur kept the captured sultan in a cage and used him as a footstool; the cage story is probably a later embellishment and historians treat it with caution, but the capture itself is solid fact, and its consequences were enormous. Bayezid died in captivity within a year, the Ottoman state fell into a decade of civil war among his sons, and the rise of the empire that would dominate the eastern Mediterranean for centuries was set back by a generation. Timur, having humbled the Ottomans, turned east to invade China itself — the one prize that would have made him Genghis's true equal — and died on the road in 1405, in the winter, before he had gone far. He was about sixty-eight.

Now the pattern completes itself, and the reader who watched the Ilkhanate convert in the last chapter will recognize every beat. Timur was not only a destroyer. He was, simultaneously and without any apparent sense of contradiction, the patron of one of the most beautiful courts in the history of the world. He poured the plunder of half of Asia — and, more to the point, the *craftsmen* of half of Asia, deported by the thousands from the cities he sacked — into rebuilding **Samarkand** (SAM-ar-kand) as the showpiece capital of his empire. He gathered the finest tile-workers, masons, architects, calligraphers, and gardeners that conquest could supply and set them to raising buildings on a scale meant to stun. The towering **Bibi-Khanym Mosque**, the great congregational mosque he built in honor of a wife, was so vast and so hastily over-ambitious that parts of it began collapsing almost at once — a fitting monument to a man who wanted everything bigger and faster than was wise. His own tomb, the **Gur-e-Amir** (goor-eh-ah-MEER, "tomb of the king"), is a jewel: a fluted, ribbed dome glazed a deep turquoise blue, sitting on a drum of tile, and it became the direct ancestor of a whole line of domed tombs across the Persianate world — the architectural grandfather, through his descendants the Mughals (the dynasty Timur's line would later found in India), of the Taj Mahal itself. The man who built towers of skulls also built the prototype for the most beautiful tomb on earth. Skulls and tilework, in the same hands.

And his heirs, like Hulagu's, turned out to be patrons more than warriors. Timur's son **Shah Rukh** (shah ROOKH) inherited a calmer empire and ruled it for forty years from **Herat** (heh-RAHT), the old Khurasan city the Mongols had twice destroyed, now rebuilt into the cultural capital of what historians call the **Timurid renaissance** — a flowering of Persian art, architecture, book-making, and science under Timur's descendants that consciously echoes, almost beat for beat, the Ilkhanid flowering of the previous century. The Timurids were a Turkic dynasty patronizing Persian culture with total devotion, which is the central thesis of this entire book stated one more time: the conquerors off the steppe did not impose their own culture; they adopted, funded, and perfected the culture of the conquered.

The brightest figure of that renaissance was a king who would rather have been an astronomer. **Ulugh Beg** (oo-LOOG beg), Timur's grandson, ruled Samarkand and was by temperament and gift a serious scientist. Around 1424 he built a great **observatory** at Samarkand, equipped with an enormous stone sextant — a curved measuring instrument set into a trench in the ground, tens of feet across — for tracking the positions of the stars with a precision that earlier instruments could not match. The star catalogue produced there, the *Zij-i-Sultani*, listing the positions of nearly a thousand stars, was the most accurate compiled anywhere between the ancient Greek astronomer Ptolemy and the European astronomers of the sixteenth century, and it was still being consulted in Europe two hundred years later. Working alongside Ulugh Beg was the mathematician **Jamshid al-Kashi** (jam-SHEED al-KAH-shee), who computed the value of *pi* to sixteen decimal places — a record that would stand for nearly two hundred years — and pioneered the use of decimal fractions. A scholar-king and a brilliant mathematician, doing world-class science at the court of Timur's grandson, in a city Timur had rebuilt with deported slaves. Ulugh Beg's end, sadly, was the steppe end: he was a poor politician, lost his throne, and was murdered in 1449 on the orders of his own son. The observatory was abandoned and, eventually, demolished by the pious as a nest of impious star-gazing, its very foundations lost until archaeologists dug them up again in the twentieth century.

The other glory of the Timurid renaissance was the book, and specifically the painted book, because the Persian miniature tradition that had been born under the Mongol Ilkhans (Chapter 10) reached its absolute summit in the Herat workshops at the end of the fifteenth century. Under the last great Timurid ruler of Herat, **Husayn Bayqara** (hoo-SANE by-KAH-rah), and his remarkable minister and friend **Ali-Shir Nava'i** (ah-LEE-sheer nah-vah-EE), Herat became a hothouse of manuscript production, and out of it came the most celebrated painter in the history of the Persian miniature: **Bihzad** (bee-ZAHD), full name Kamal al-Din Bihzad. Bihzad's paintings broke from the static, symbolic compositions of earlier miniatures toward something more alive — figures caught in real, individual movement, faces with character, scenes of building and harvesting and feasting observed with a new attention to ordinary human gesture, all of it set in compositions of dazzling color and balance. He became so famous that later collectors forged his signature onto lesser works to raise their value, the surest sign of an artist's stature. Bihzad is to Persian painting roughly what his rough contemporaries Leonardo and Botticelli were to Italian painting, working at the exact same moment on the other side of the world, and the comparison is worth pausing on: while the Italian Renaissance was reaching its height in Florence, a Persian renaissance was reaching its own height in Herat, the two great late-medieval flowerings of book and image happening simultaneously and in near-total ignorance of each other.

Two final figures close the great age of classical Persian culture, both at Herat, both at the end of the fifteenth century. The poet **Jami** (jah-MEE) — a Sufi master and prodigiously productive writer of verse and prose — is conventionally counted the last of the great classical Persian poets, the man who closes the tradition that had begun with Rudaki at the Samanid court five centuries before (Chapter 3). After Jami, Persian poetry continues, but the canon of the giants — Ferdowsi, Khayyam, Nizami, Attar, Rumi, Saadi, Hafez, Jami — is essentially complete; the language had produced its monuments. And Ali-Shir Nava'i, the minister-patron who made the Herat flowering possible, did something quietly historic of his own: steeped though he was in Persian culture, he argued passionately that his own native tongue, **Chagatai Turkish** (a Central Asian Turkic literary language), was a worthy vehicle for high literature, and he proved it by writing major works in it. In doing so he marked the moment when the Turkic literary tradition, for centuries the junior partner that wrote in Persian, began to stand on its own — a small but telling sign that the absolute dominance of Persian as the only prestige language of the eastern Islamic world had a horizon after all.

By 1500, then, the situation in Persia was, in its broad shape, eerily familiar. A second world-conqueror had devastated the land and then dissolved; his cultured descendants had built a second dazzling renaissance out of the wreckage; and now that renaissance, like the Ilkhanate before it, was fragmenting as the Timurid line ran out of strong rulers and the empire splintered among quarreling princes and the Turkmen tribes pressing in from the west. The pattern had held, exactly, for the second time. Persia had been destroyed and had absorbed its destroyer, again.

But the splintered Persia of 1500 was missing something it had not had to find before. After the Arabs, Persia had reassembled itself around a saved language and a national epic. After the Mongols, it had reassembled itself around the converted Ilkhans. Now, with the Timurids fading, the question was who, or what, would gather the fragments this time — and the answer, when it came, would not be a poet or a dynasty of administrators or a converted khan. It would be a teenage boy with a red-capped army of fanatical Turkmen tribesmen and a claim to be something close to divine. He is the subject of the last chapter.

# Chapter 12 — The Red-Heads and the Boy King

We left Persia, at the end of the last chapter, as a patchwork. So the final chapter of this book has to begin by laying out the pieces on the board, because the founding of modern Persia is the story of how a single improbable player swept all of them off it at once.

After the Ilkhanate dissolved in 1335 (Chapter 10), and again after the Timurid empire began to fragment in the later fifteenth century (Chapter 11), Persia had no single ruler. It was divided among rival powers, and the reader needs four of them in mind. First, the lingering **successor dynasties** of the fourteenth century — the Jalayirids in Iraq, the Muzaffarids in the south who had patronized Hafez, and the populist, partly Shia **Sarbadars** (sar-bah-DARZ) in Khurasan, whose name means roughly "the gallows-headed," from a defiant boast that they would rather have their heads on the gallows than submit. The Sarbadars are worth a flag in passing, because their blend of militant Shiism with a tribal following is a small early rehearsal of the much larger thing this chapter is about. Second, and most important by 1500, two great **Turkmen confederations** — and a word is owed on what those were.

A **Turkmen** (TURK-men) in this context was a member of one of the Oghuz Turkic nomadic tribes — the same broad stock as the Seljuks and the Ottomans, pastoral herders who lived on horseback and made their living by their bows. The phrase "Turkmen confederation" means a loose, mobile alliance of such tribes held together by a ruling clan, the kind of organization that could put a formidable cavalry army in the field but could also fly apart the moment its leader died. Two of them dominated western Persia and eastern Anatolia after the Timurids weakened: the **Kara Koyunlu** (KAH-rah koy-OON-loo), the "Black Sheep" Turkmen, and the **Aq Qoyunlu** (ahk koy-OON-loo), the "White Sheep" Turkmen — the names come from the tribal emblems on their banners. (The two are easy to confuse, so a tag to hold onto: *kara* is black, *aq* is white; the White Sheep are the ones who matter most for this chapter, because they win.) The White Sheep eventually beat the Black Sheep, and under their greatest leader, **Uzun Hasan** (oo-ZOON hah-SAHN, "Hasan the Tall"), who ruled from the 1450s to 1478, the Aq Qoyunlu became the dominant power of western Persia. Uzun Hasan was ambitious enough to negotiate an alliance with the merchant republic of Venice against their common enemy the Ottomans, which tells you that western Persia was now a player in Mediterranean diplomacy. But the Aq Qoyunlu were a Turkmen confederation like any other, and when Uzun Hasan died, the White Sheep fractured into the usual succession wars and began to come apart. By the 1490s western Persia was up for grabs, contested by quarreling White Sheep princes who had inherited a powerful army and no unity.

That is the board. Now the player who swept it, and he came from the one direction nobody was watching: a Sufi lodge.

Go back to the city of **Ardabil** (ar-dah-BEEL), in the cold northwestern highlands of Persia near the Caspian, around the year 1300, at the very moment the Ilkhans were converting to Islam. There a holy man named **Shaykh Safi al-Din** (shaykh SAH-fee ad-DEEN) founded a **Sufi order** — and the reader needs this term firmly in hand, because the whole chapter turns on it. Sufism is the mystical, inward dimension of Islam, the pursuit of direct personal experience of God through devotion, discipline, and love rather than through law and ritual alone (Chapter 7 covers its flowering in Persia). A **Sufi order**, or *tariqa* (tah-REE-kah, literally "the path"), is an organized brotherhood gathered around a master and his teachings: disciples swear loyalty to a living spiritual guide, the guidance passes down a chain from master to successor, and the order spreads through a network of lodges. Most such orders were peaceful, pious, and politically quiet, devoted to prayer and the inner life. The order Shaykh Safi founded at Ardabil — the **Safaviyya** (sah-fah-VEE-yah), named for him — began exactly that way: a respectable, quietist brotherhood that attracted donations and pilgrims and stayed out of politics for generations.

Two things then transformed it from a prayer-circle into an army, and both took about two centuries to mature. The first is that the Safaviyya's appeal shifted east and onto the steppe frontier, where it began to attract a following among exactly those rootless, militarized **Turkmen** tribes of eastern Anatolia and northwestern Persia. And the second is that the message itself radicalized. Over the fifteenth century the Safavid sheikhs — by now a hereditary line, passing leadership from father to son — moved from ordinary Sunni Sufism toward a militant, intensely devotional form of **Shia Islam** (the branch holding that leadership of the community rightfully belonged to the family and descendants of the Prophet, the same branch the Buyids and the Sarbadars had championed earlier in this book), and they encouraged their tribal followers to regard the sheikh not merely as a spiritual teacher but as something far more — a near-divine figure, the rightful lord, a manifestation of the holy, worth dying for. This is the explosive combination: a tribal cavalry army that worshipped its commander as a saint and believed that to die fighting for him was to die for God.

These followers had a name, and it is the name in the chapter title. They were the **Qizilbash** (kih-zil-BAHSH), which is Turkish for "red-heads" — not a description of their hair but of their headgear, a distinctive tall red cap, traditionally said to have twelve folds or gores in honor of the twelve Imams — the twelve rightful leaders recognized by **Twelver Shiism** (the largest branch of Shia Islam, Chapter 4). The Qizilbash wore the red cap as a badge of devotion to the Safavid house, and their fanatical loyalty made them, by the late fifteenth century, one of the most dangerous fighting forces in the region — dangerous enough that the established powers, the Aq Qoyunlu among them, came to see the Safavid family as a mortal threat and set about hunting it.

And hunt it they did, which is how this book's final hero comes to be a hunted child. The Safavid leadership fell into a vendetta with the Aq Qoyunlu and the local rulers of the Caspian shore. The boy's grandfather and father, the militant sheikhs who had built the Qizilbash following, were both killed in battle against these enemies. The family was nearly exterminated; the surviving children were marked for death. And so the last viable heir of the Safavid line, a small boy named **Ismail** (iss-mah-EEL), spent years of his childhood in hiding, sheltered and smuggled from place to place by loyal Qizilbash, kept alive by men who believed he was the living embodiment of their faith and the rightful lord of Persia. He was born in 1487. He had lost his father and grandfather to the cause before he was old enough to remember them. He was, to his followers, not a refugee but a messiah in waiting.

In 1499, at the age of twelve, Ismail came out of hiding. He gathered the Qizilbash tribes to him, and they came — because for years they had been waiting for exactly this, for the boy in whom their murdered masters lived again to call them to war. With this red-capped army, a child commanded the reconquest of Persia. The fractured Aq Qoyunlu, still tearing themselves apart in their own succession wars, could not unite against him in time. In 1501, at the **Battle of Sharur** (shah-ROOR) in the northwest, Ismail's Qizilbash defeated the main White Sheep army and cleared the road to the great northwestern city of **Tabriz** (tah-BREEZ), the old Ilkhanid and Aq Qoyunlu capital.

He took Tabriz, and there, still only fourteen years old, he did two things that founded a country. He had himself proclaimed **Shah** (shah), the ancient Persian word for king — reaching back past the Mongols and the Arabs to the title of the pre-Islamic king of kings, the imperial Persian word that the Shahnameh had kept alive for five hundred years (Chapter 3). And he proclaimed **Twelver Shiism** the official religion of his realm, by force, in a land that was at the time overwhelmingly Sunni.

The reader should sit with how radical, and how violent, that second act was. Persia in 1501 was mostly Sunni; the great Shia future of Iran did not yet exist. Ismail decreed it into being, ordering the public cursing of the first caliphs revered by Sunnis, importing Shia scholars to staff a clergy he largely had to build from scratch, and backing the whole program with the swords of the Qizilbash. The conversion of Persia to Shiism was not a gentle drift like the long conversion to Islam eight centuries earlier (Chapter 1); it was a state policy enforced over the following century with considerable bloodshed, and it would take generations to complete. But it took, and it is the single most consequential thing in this final chapter, because it is the origin of the fact that Iran today is the great Shia power of the Muslim world, religiously distinct from its overwhelmingly Sunni neighbors. That distinction, which shapes Middle Eastern politics to this day, was decreed by a fourteen-year-old warlord-mystic in Tabriz in 1501.

Step back, now, to the whole arc of the book, because Ismail's coronation closes it with a precision that is almost too neat to be true. This story opened in 651 with the murder of the last pre-Islamic Persian king of kings, a man fleeing for his life into a miller's hut, the Persian Empire annihilated by the Arabs and seemingly gone forever. It closes, 850 years later, with a new king of kings crowned in Tabriz, reuniting a Persia that had been conquered twice over and absorbed both its conquerors — taking the old imperial title *Shah*, ruling in the Persian language that the Samanids and Ferdowsi had saved, drawing on a militant Shiism whose roots reach back to the tolerant Buyid patronage of the tenth century, and riding to power on a fusion of Sufi devotion and Turkmen cavalry that only the strange Persianate world of the previous chapters could have produced. Every thread the book has followed is gathered into that one teenage figure on the throne at Tabriz: the saved language, the imperial memory, the Shia undercurrent, the Sufi orders, the Turkic armies ruling in a Persian idiom.

Twice militarily annihilated, twice the quiet absorber of its annihilators, Persia had not merely survived. It had reconstituted itself as an empire under a king who called himself by the oldest Persian name for kingship, and it had given itself, in the process, the religious identity it carries still. That is the answer to the question this whole book was built to answer: why there is still, after everything, such a thing as Persia.

What Ismail did next — the empire he built, the long duel with the Ottomans to the west, the glittering capital his descendants raised at Isfahan, and the dynasty's eventual fall — belongs to the story that follows, in safavid-persia. This book ends where it should, on the eve, with a boy king on the throne and the country whole again.
