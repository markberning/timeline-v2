# Latin American Independence — How a Hemisphere Tore Itself Free

**Goal:** Narrative for the breaking-apart of the Spanish and Portuguese empires in the New World, from the moment in 1808 when Napoleon kidnapped the king of Spain to the moment in 1903 when the United States helped pull Panama away from Colombia. When this story opens, the largest empire on earth has held together for three centuries on a single thread: the crown. Spain rules from Mexico City to the southern tip of South America through a chain of viceroys, all of them legitimate only because a king in Madrid says so. Then Napoleon throws that king into a French dungeon, and the thread snaps. Suddenly a hemisphere has to answer a question nobody had been allowed to ask: who rules when the king is gone? The answer takes fifteen years of war and a parade of impossibly large personalities to work out. A failed Venezuelan aristocrat named Simón Bolívar marches an army up over the frozen Andes to ambush an empire from behind. A modest Argentine general named José de San Martín pulls off the same insane mountain crossing from the south, frees Chile and Peru, then meets Bolívar behind a closed door and quietly hands him the entire war rather than fight him for the credit. A village priest in Mexico rings his church bell and starts a peasant uprising so terrifying that the people it was meant to free finish independence themselves, on the opposite, conservative side. And in Brazil, the heir to the Portuguese throne looks at the chaos, shrugs, and declares independence against his own father — the only revolution in the hemisphere led by a prince, and the only new monarchy that lasts. Behind all of it stands Haiti, where enslaved people had already done the unthinkable and built, in 1804, the first nation in human history born from a successful slave revolt — and then armed and funded Bolívar on one condition: that he free the slaves. This is the story of how roughly twenty new nations came into being almost at once, how formal monarchy was swept off an entire hemisphere, and how the modern map of Latin America was drawn in blood. And it is the story of the long, bitter morning after — the broken dream of a united Spanish America, the strongmen who inherited the republics, the catastrophic wars the new nations fought against each other, and the slavery and racial caste the revolution had promised but failed to end. Winning a war, it turns out, is the easy part.

### A note on names, places, and pronunciation

This is a story that sprawls across two continents and a dozen future countries, so a few words are worth settling up front. The two empires breaking apart here are **Spanish America** (the vast bulk of it, from Mexico to Argentina) and **Portuguese America** (which is just Brazil, and behaves completely differently, for reasons this book spends a whole chapter on). Spain did not govern its empire as one block. It carved the Americas into a handful of giant provinces called **viceroyalties**, each run from a capital by a **viceroy** (vye-SROY), a stand-in for the king with near-royal powers; the four big ones were New Spain (run from Mexico City), Peru (from Lima), New Granada (from Bogotá), and the Río de la Plata (from Buenos Aires, created late, in 1776). Those four administrative boxes are the rough ancestors of the future nations, which is why the map of independent Latin America looks the way it does. The single most important social fact to carry through the whole book is the word **casta** (KAH-stah), the colonial system that sorted everyone by ancestry into a fixed racial ladder — it is explained in full in Chapter 1, and it is the social fuel under everything that burns. A handful of personal names recur so often they are worth pre-loading: **Bolívar** (boh-LEE-var), the Liberator of the north; **San Martín** (sahn mar-TEEN), the liberator of the south; **Sucre** (SOO-kray), Bolívar's best general; **Iturbide** (ee-toor-BEE-day), who freed Mexico from the conservative side; and **Pedro** (PAY-droh), the Portuguese prince who freed Brazil. Spanish and Portuguese spelling keeps its accents here because the accents tell you where the stress falls. A *junta* (HOON-tah) is a governing committee, defined when it matters in Chapter 3. A *caudillo* (kow-DEE-yoh) is a personalist strongman who rules by charisma and force, and a *gaucho* (GOW-choh) is one of the cattle-herding horsemen of the South American plains; both are defined at first use and given fuller treatment in Chapter 10. Miles come first, kilometers in parentheses; dates are CE throughout.

### A note on "first" claims and on how we know any of this

As in every book in this project, "first" means "first surviving evidence of," and for this period the sources are unusually rich but unusually slippery, because almost everyone who recorded these events had a side. The independence wars produced a flood of writing — proclamations, manifestos, constitutions, the famous letters of Bolívar — but a revolutionary's proclamation is an argument, not a transcript, and the founders of nations spend their later years editing their own legends. Two specific cautions run through the book. First, the **great-man legends**: Bolívar, San Martín, Hidalgo, and the rest were turned into marble statues by the nations they founded, and the patriotic version of any given scene (the priest ringing his bell, the prince drawing his sword by the brook, the liberator pointing across the mountains) is often a later national myth polished smooth — where the colorful story outruns the evidence, this book says so. The famous closed-door meeting between San Martín and Bolívar at Guayaquil is the sharpest case: no reliable record of what was said survives, and anyone who tells you exactly what passed between them is guessing. Second, the **numbers**, especially the casualty and population figures of the regional wars — the claim that Paraguay lost more than half its people in the 1860s, for instance, is genuinely debated, and the book flags the disputed counts rather than picking the most dramatic one. Where the sources are propaganda, where they are national legend, and where they simply disagree, the text will tell you.

# Chapter 1 — The Colonial Pressure Cooker

To understand why an empire that had stood for three hundred years came apart in fifteen, you have to understand what kind of empire it was, and why so many of the people living inside it had learned to hate it without ever quite imagining they could leave it. That is the strange thing about Spanish America in the year 1800. It was not seething with independence plots. Most of the people who would soon lead the revolutions had no intention of becoming revolutionaries; they wanted, at most, a better deal inside the system. The empire did not fall because its subjects rose up to overthrow it. It fell because its head was suddenly cut off in Europe (Chapter 3), and the people left holding the pieces discovered, to their own surprise, how much resentment had been packed into those pieces over three centuries. This chapter is about the packing.

Begin with the sheer scale of the thing, because it is hard to overstate. Starting with Columbus in 1492 and the conquests of the Aztec empire of central Mexico and the Inca empire of the Andes in the following decades (the side of the conquered is told in the Aztec and Inca narratives), Spain had assembled the largest empire the world had yet seen, a possession that ran the length of two continents, from the deserts of what is now the southwestern United States down through Mexico and Central America, across the Caribbean, and the entire western spine of South America to the cold tip of Patagonia. Portugal, meanwhile, held the enormous bulge of Brazil on the Atlantic side. For three centuries this whole expanse was, in theory, the personal property of the Spanish (and Portuguese) crowns, and the genius of the system — the thing that held it together across an ocean and a four-month sailing voyage — was that everyone in it derived their authority from one distant person: the king.

Spain governed this empire through the **viceroyalty** system. A viceroyalty was a giant administrative province ruled by a **viceroy** (the word means "vice-king," a man standing in for the monarch with near-royal powers), who governed from a colonial capital in the king's name. There were originally two, New Spain (governed from Mexico City and covering Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean) and Peru (governed from Lima and covering most of South America). As the empire grew the crown split it further, carving out the Viceroyalty of New Granada in the north of South America (capital Bogotá) and, in 1776, the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata in the southeast (capital Buenos Aires). Hold onto those four boxes, because they are not just bureaucratic trivia: when the empire shattered, it tended to shatter along these administrative lines, and the rough outlines of New Spain, Peru, New Granada, and the Río de la Plata are the ancestors of Mexico, Peru, Colombia, and Argentina. The empire drew the dotted lines along which it would one day be torn.

Now the part that turned a functioning empire into a pressure cooker: the way it sorted its people. Colonial Spanish America ran on an elaborate racial hierarchy that historians call the **casta** system (from the Spanish word for "lineage" or "breed," the root of the English "caste"). It was a ladder, and where you stood on it determined what you could own, what offices you could hold, what taxes you paid, and whom you could marry. At the very top sat the **peninsulares** (peh-neen-soo-LAH-rays), the Spanish-born — people who had been born in Spain itself, on the Iberian peninsula, and had crossed the ocean to govern, trade, and command. Just below them, and this distinction is the hinge of the entire story, sat the **creoles** (in Spanish, *criollos*, kree-OH-yohs), people of pure or nearly pure Spanish blood who had been born in the Americas. Below the creoles came the great mixed and non-European majority: the **mestizos** (mes-TEE-zohs, people of mixed European and Indigenous ancestry), the **mulattoes** (of mixed European and African ancestry), the Indigenous peoples, and at the very bottom, with no rung of their own at all, the enslaved Africans. Colonial painters actually produced *casta paintings*, careful illustrated charts naming the supposed product of every racial combination — a society so obsessed with ancestry that it tried to diagram it.

The grievance that mattered most for independence was the one closest to the top, between the peninsulares and the creoles. On paper they were the same race; in practice they were rivals separated by an ocean and a chip on the shoulder. The creoles were the American-born aristocracy — they owned the great estates, the mines, and the plantations, they were educated and often wealthy, and they thought of America as their home. But the best jobs in the empire, the viceroyalties and the bishoprics and the high courts, went almost always to peninsulares freshly shipped from Spain, on the theory that a man born in the colonies could not be fully trusted to put the crown's interests over his own. A creole could be the richest man in his province and still watch some newly arrived Spaniard with half his fortune be handed the governorship. That sting — of being treated as a second-class subject in your own land, of being told you were not quite Spanish enough to rule the place you were born — is the resentment that, when the crown vanished, the creoles would turn into a war. It is worth being precise here, because it shapes everything: this was, at its core, a quarrel between elites. The men who led independence were overwhelmingly creoles, fighting for the right to run their own countries; the liberation of the enslaved and the equality of the castes were promises made along the way, sometimes sincerely, often reluctantly, and mostly left unkept (Chapter 11).

Underneath the racial machinery ran an economic one, and its engine was silver. For two centuries the single most valuable thing in the Americas was the mountain of silver at **Potosí** (poh-toh-SEE), in the high Andes of what is now Bolivia — a peak so rich that "vale un Potosí," "worth a Potosí," became Spanish for unimaginable wealth. The silver of Potosí funded the Spanish crown's wars across Europe for two hundred years, and it was dug out under a brutal labor system called the **mita** (MEE-tah), a rotating draft the Spanish inherited and twisted from the old Inca empire — the Inca had used the same kind of rotating community labor levy, the *mit'a*, to build roads and farm state land, but the Spanish redirected it to send a quota of men to work and die in the silver mines (the Inca side of this system is told in the Inca narrative). This is the structural fact that independence leaders would later attack as theft: the colonies existed, in the crown's design, to be drained. Wealth flowed out, to Spain; manufactured goods flowed in, from Spain, which forbade the colonies from trading freely with anyone else or making things that might compete with the mother country. An empire arranged for extraction breeds, in the people being extracted from, a very specific kind of anger.

For most of three centuries, that anger stayed banked. What stirred it, in the second half of the 1700s, was a well-meaning attempt at reform that backfired spectacularly. In 1700 the Spanish throne had passed to a new royal family, the **Bourbons** (the same dynasty that ruled France, which mattered because it brought French centralizing habits of government to a Spanish empire long run on local autonomy), and the Bourbon kings, looking at their sprawling, leaky, under-taxed empire, decided to modernize it. The result, rolled out across the middle and late 1700s, is known as the **Bourbon Reforms**. The idea was sensible from Madrid's point of view: tighten administration, collect more revenue, reassert royal control, and squeeze more profit out of the colonies to fund Spain's competition with rising rivals like Britain. In practice the reforms did three things that enraged exactly the people the crown could least afford to enrage. They raised taxes and tightened monopolies, hitting colonial merchants and consumers. They created new layers of royal officials to enforce all this, and staffed them — pointedly — with peninsulares, freezing creoles further out of power at the very moment they expected to be brought in. And they cracked down on the comfortable old corruption and local autonomy that creole elites had quietly enjoyed for generations. The Bourbon Reforms were meant to bind the empire tighter to the crown. Instead they convinced the colonial elite that the crown saw them as a cow to be milked, and they are the slow-building pressure under everything that follows. Resentment, by 1800, had become combustible.

It had also, here and there, already caught fire. Three sparks before the great explosion are worth knowing, because they show how much tinder was lying around. The largest by far came in 1780, high in the Peruvian Andes, when a leader named José Gabriel Condorcanqui — a Quechua (the language and people of the former Inca heartland) — took the name **Túpac Amaru II** (TOO-pahk ah-MAH-roo), claiming descent from the last independent Inca ruler, Túpac Amaru I, whom the Spanish had beheaded in Cusco in 1572 (the Inca narrative tells that ending), and led a massive Indigenous uprising against the abuses of the colonial system: the forced labor, the crushing tribute, the corrupt officials. For a few months it convulsed the southern Andes and terrified the authorities. It was crushed with extraordinary cruelty; Túpac Amaru was forced to watch his family killed and was then publicly torn apart in the main square of Cusco in 1781. The revolt failed, but it left two lasting marks: it proved how deep the rage ran in the Indigenous majority, and it terrified the creole elite about what might happen if the social order ever truly broke — a fear that would make many of them hesitate, decades later, to unleash the masses.

Across the continent, in Portuguese Brazil, the sparks were smaller but pointed in a new direction, toward the radical ideas crossing the Atlantic from the American and French Revolutions. In 1789, in the gold-mining province of Minas Gerais, a group of well-read colonial elites hatched a plot called the **Inconfidência Mineira** (een-kohn-fee-DEN-see-ah mee-NAY-rah, roughly "the Minas Conspiracy"), inspired directly by the recent American Revolution (the founding of the United States, the model they were copying, is told in its own narrative), to break Brazil away from Portugal and found a republic. They were betrayed before they could act. The crown hanged their most expendable member, a low-ranking militia officer and part-time tooth-puller named Joaquim José da Silva Xavier, nicknamed **Tiradentes** (tee-rah-DEN-chayss, "the tooth-puller"), in 1792 — drawn, quartered, and displayed in pieces as a warning. A century later, the Brazilian republic would dig him up as a national martyr, the patron saint of independence; in his own moment he was simply an example. Then, in 1798, came a sharper-edged movement in the Brazilian port of Salvador: the **Tailors' Revolt** (also called the Revolt of the Tailors, after the trade of several leaders), a plot not of rich elites but of mixed-race artisans, soldiers, and free people of color, calling for a republic, racial equality, and an end to the color line. This one frightened the authorities far more than the genteel Minas conspiracy, precisely because it came from the bottom rather than the top, and it too was suppressed, its leaders hanged. The pattern across these episodes is the pattern of the whole coming century: revolt from the top wanted self-rule and kept the social order; revolt from the bottom wanted to overturn the social order itself, and that was the thing the elites feared most.

Finally, the ideas. Long before the wars, the arguments that would justify them were already seeping into the colonies, carried in smuggled books and the conversations of the educated creole class. The European **Enlightenment** — the eighteenth-century intellectual movement that prized reason, natural rights, and the idea that legitimate government rests on the consent of the governed rather than the will of God-appointed kings — gave colonial intellectuals a vocabulary for their grievances. The Prussian naturalist **Alexander von Humboldt** spent five years, beginning in 1799, traveling Spanish America and producing the first great scientific portrait of its geography, peoples, and staggering inequalities; his published data became ammunition, a measured European confirming that the colonies were rich, capable, and badly misgoverned. Creole scientists in places like Bogotá, gathered around the priest-botanist José Celestino Mutis, were learning to classify their own world and, in the process, to think of it as theirs. And the dangerous documents were already circulating: in 1794 a Bogotá printer named Antonio Nariño secretly translated and published the French Revolution's *Declaration of the Rights of Man* — and was thrown in prison for it. The pamphlet was contraband; the idea inside it, that men have rights no king can take away, was the real smuggled cargo.

So picture the pressure cooker in 1807, the year before the lid blows off. A vast empire, three centuries old, held together by loyalty to a distant crown. A wealthy, educated, resentful creole elite, locked out of the top jobs and squeezed by reformist taxes, who think of themselves as the natural lords of America and are treated as junior partners. A huge mixed and Indigenous and enslaved majority with grievances far deeper than the creoles', whose occasional eruptions terrify everyone above them. An economy built to drain wealth toward Europe. And a set of new, dangerous ideas about rights and consent, already loose in the conversation. Everything needed for an explosion is present except a trigger — and crucially, no one inside the system is reaching for one. The creoles want reform, not rupture. What breaks the empire will not come from inside it at all. It will come from two directions the colonists are barely watching: a slave revolt on a small Caribbean island that proves the unthinkable is possible (Chapter 2), and a French army marching into Spain that removes, in a single stroke, the king on whom the entire three-hundred-year structure rested (Chapter 3).


# Chapter 2 — Haiti Lights the Fuse

The first nation in the Americas to break free of European rule was not founded by creole aristocrats quoting the Enlightenment. It was founded by enslaved people who picked up cane knives and refused to be slaves anymore, and it terrified every other founder in this book. To understand why the liberators of Spanish America were haunted by a small French sugar island most of them never visited, you have to understand what happened there between 1791 and 1804 — and you have to start with what slavery in that place actually was, because the casual modern word "slavery" does not begin to convey it.

The island in question was **Saint-Domingue** (san doh-MANG), the western third of the Caribbean island of Hispaniola, the rest of which was a sleepy Spanish colony called Santo Domingo. (The whole island sits between Cuba and Puerto Rico; Saint-Domingue is the part that became Haiti, Santo Domingo the part that became the Dominican Republic — two countries, one island, and they will not be confused again here.) In 1791 Saint-Domingue was the single richest colony on earth. It produced something like a third of the world's sugar and more than half its coffee, and it made France stupendously wealthy. It did this by working human beings to death on an industrial scale. Of the roughly half a million people on the island, the overwhelming majority — close to ninety percent — were enslaved Africans, against a few tens of thousands of white colonists and a sizable class of free people of color (people of mixed African and European descent, or freed slaves, who had won or bought their liberty — many of them property-owners and themselves slaveholders, occupying an uneasy middle rung between master and slave). The arithmetic of the sugar economy was that it was cheaper to work an enslaved person to death within a few years and buy a replacement off a slave ship than to keep one alive — so the plantations consumed people, importing tens of thousands of fresh captives a year to replace the ones the work and the cruelty killed. This was not the relatively self-reproducing slavery of the North American mainland. It was a death machine, and the enslaved majority knew exactly what it was.

In August 1791 that majority rose. The **Haitian Revolution** — the largest and the only fully successful slave revolt in recorded history — began with a coordinated uprising in the rich plantation country of the north, in which the enslaved burned the cane fields, killed the planters, and turned the engine of French wealth into a war zone. What makes it the hinge event of the whole hemisphere is not just that it started but that it *won*, and won against everyone. Over the next thirteen years the formerly enslaved armies of Saint-Domingue defeated, in turn, the local French colonists, a Spanish invasion, a British expeditionary force that lost tens of thousands of men to combat and yellow fever trying to seize the rich prize, and finally the army of Napoleon himself. No comparable thing had ever happened. The reader should sit with how genuinely unimaginable this was to contemporaries: the entire economic and racial order of the Atlantic world rested on the unspoken assumption that the enslaved could not, would not, and structurally were not able to free themselves by force. Saint-Domingue proved the assumption false, on the most valuable real estate in the empire of France.

The revolution found its great leader in a man born into slavery on the island named **Toussaint Louverture** (too-SAN loo-vair-TYUR). He was, by any measure, one of the most remarkable figures of his age: a man who had been enslaved into middle age, who taught himself to read, who turned out to possess a genius for both war and politics, and who rose to command the revolution's armies and then to govern the colony in everything but name. Louverture was no simple firebrand. He played the French, Spanish, and British against one another with cold skill, abolished slavery in the territory he controlled, kept the plantations running to fund the war, and by 1801 ruled Saint-Domingue as its effective master while still, technically, professing loyalty to a France that had (briefly) abolished slavery in its 1794 revolutionary fervor. His tragedy is one of the cruelest in the period. When Napoleon came to power in France, he resolved to crush the black general and restore slavery, and in 1802 he sent a massive expedition for the purpose. Louverture was lured to a parley under a flag of truce, seized, and shipped to France, where he was thrown into a freezing prison high in the Jura mountains of eastern France (the Fort de Joux, near the Swiss border) and left to die of cold, hunger, and neglect in 1803. Napoleon thought removing the leader would end the revolution. It did the opposite.

Because by then the revolution no longer needed Louverture, and Napoleon's intentions had become impossible to hide. When the formerly enslaved understood that the French had come to put the chains back on — Napoleon had already restored slavery in France's other colonies — the war became total and merciless. Leadership passed to one of Louverture's hardened lieutenants, **Jean-Jacques Dessalines** (zhahn-zhahk deh-sah-LEEN), a former field slave with the scars of the whip on his back and no illusions left about coexistence with France. Under Dessalines the rebel armies and the climate did the rest: yellow fever — a deadly mosquito-borne tropical disease — scythed through the French ranks, killing tens of thousands of European soldiers who had no immunity to it, while the Haitian forces, many of whom had survived the disease in childhood and so were immune, fought on. The decisive blow came in November 1803 at the **Battle of Vertières** (vair-tee-AIR), outside the northern city of Cap-Français, where Dessalines's army shattered the last effective French force on the island. Within weeks the broken French expedition evacuated. Napoleon's dream of a restored Caribbean sugar empire was finished — and, in a consequence that rippled all the way to the United States, the loss of Saint-Domingue helped convince him to give up on the Americas entirely and sell the enormous Louisiana Territory to the young United States in 1803 (the U.S. narrative tells this purchase from the other side, as the bargain that doubled the new republic's size).

On the first day of 1804, Dessalines proclaimed independence. The new nation took the name **Haiti** (HAY-tee), from an old word for the island used by the Taíno (the Indigenous people who had lived across the Caribbean before Columbus and had been all but wiped out by Spanish conquest and disease within a generation), deliberately erasing the French colonial name and reaching back past the Europeans to the people who had been there before them. This was a cascade of firsts, and each one deserves its weight. Haiti was the first independent nation in Latin America, beating the entire Spanish-American mainland by a decade and a half. It was the first independent nation in the world founded by formerly enslaved people who had liberated themselves by force of arms. It was the second independent republic in the Western Hemisphere, after the United States — but where the United States had been founded by free colonists who kept slavery firmly in place, Haiti was founded on the wreckage of slavery, by the enslaved themselves, with abolition as its first principle. There is no real precedent for it anywhere. (The cost was savage and the new nation's birth was not clean: Dessalines, who crowned himself emperor and was assassinated in 1806, ordered the massacre of most of the remaining white French population in 1804, an atrocity that horrified Europe and gave Haiti's enemies a permanent propaganda weapon. The revolution that ended one of history's worst crimes ended in a crime of its own.)

Now the part that makes Haiti load-bearing for the rest of this book: what it *meant* to everyone watching, which was almost everyone. To the enslaved across the hemisphere — in the American South, in Cuba, in Brazil — Haiti was proof and inspiration, a beacon that said the thing could be done. To the slaveholding elites of those same places, Haiti was the nightmare, the specific horror that kept them awake: the burning plantation, the master killed, the world turned upside down. Historians sometimes call this the "specter of Haiti," and it is impossible to overstate how it shaped the calculations of the creole revolutionaries you are about to meet. Many of them owned slaves. All of them lived in societies with large enslaved or mixed-race populations. When a creole aristocrat in Venezuela or Mexico thought about overthrowing the crown, the example of Haiti cut two ways at once: it showed that European empire could be beaten, and it warned, in letters of fire, what could happen if the people at the bottom were ever fully mobilized and then betrayed. This is the deep reason the Spanish-American revolutions were so cautious about race and slavery — why their leaders made and broke promises of emancipation, why they feared their own armies, why men like Venezuela's elite hesitated to arm the very masses they needed. They had all seen what happened on Saint-Domingue.

And Haiti was not merely a symbol. It became, in the most literal and material way, the secret backer of the Spanish-American revolution. When the cause in the north was at its lowest ebb and Simón Bolívar was a defeated exile with nothing, it was Haiti — the one place in the hemisphere where a black-led republic had everything to gain from the spread of abolition — that gave him ships, weapons, a printing press, and men to try again. The price was a promise, and it is one of the great hinges of this whole story: free the slaves wherever you win (Chapter 5). The first nation born of a slave revolt would arm the man who freed half a continent, on the condition that he carry abolition with him. The fuse that Napoleon would light in Europe in 1808 ran toward a hemisphere already primed — and the most powerful proof that the powder could actually catch had been delivered, four years earlier, by people the rest of the Americas had been told could never light anything at all.


# Chapter 3 — The Empire Decapitates Itself

Here is the thing that has to be understood about the Spanish empire in 1808, the single fact on which everything turns: it was held together by a person, not an idea. There was no Spanish "nation" in the modern sense that the colonies belonged to, no constitution, no parliament whose authority spanned the ocean. There was the king. Every viceroy, every governor, every judge, every officer held his power because the king in Madrid had granted it, and the colonists' loyalty was, quite literally, personal loyalty to the monarch. A vast structure resting on a single point. And in 1808, Napoleon Bonaparte reached out and removed the point.

The man at the center of the catastrophe was the emperor of the French, **Napoleon** Bonaparte, who by 1807 had conquered most of continental Europe and was trying to strangle his last great enemy, Britain, by closing every European port to British trade. Two ports remained stubbornly open: those of Portugal, Britain's old ally, and, complicatedly, Spain. Napoleon's solution was to send his armies into the Iberian Peninsula (the southwestern corner of Europe that Spain and Portugal share), beginning the long, ruinous conflict the British and Spanish would call the **Peninsular War**. He came first for Portugal, in late 1807, and that produced the first of the two great shocks that detonate this book.

Faced with French troops marching on Lisbon, the Portuguese royal family did something no European dynasty had ever done: rather than be captured or fight a hopeless defense, the entire **Portuguese court** — the prince regent (the future King **John VI**, ruling on behalf of his mother, Queen Maria I, who had been declared mentally incapacitated), the royal family, the government, the treasury, thousands of nobles and officials, a whole transplanted state — packed onto ships and, under the protection of the British navy, fled across the Atlantic to their colony of Brazil (Portuguese ever since the 1494 line that split the New World between Spain and Portugal — see the age-of-exploration narrative). In early 1808 the monarchy of Portugal reestablished itself in Rio de Janeiro. Stop and absorb how strange this was: for the first and only time in the history of European colonialism, an empire was now ruled *from* its colony. The king of Portugal sat in the Americas. This single decision is the reason Brazil's road to independence would look nothing like Spanish America's — there was no decapitation here, no vanished king, but rather a king who showed up in person (Chapter 9). Portugal's empire bent in the storm. Spain's snapped.

Because Napoleon did to Spain what he could not be bothered to do gently. In the spring of 1808, by a mix of trickery and force, he lured the Spanish royal family across the border to the French town of Bayonne, just inside France, and there orchestrated one of the most consequential humiliations in European history: the **Abdications of Bayonne**. The situation in the Spanish royal house was already a mess — the weak King **Charles IV** had just been forced to abdicate in favor of his son **Ferdinand VII** by a palace uprising (the Tumult of Aranjuez, a revolt of courtiers, soldiers, and townspeople against the king's hated chief minister that spilled over into toppling the king himself), and father and son were openly feuding. Napoleon summoned both to Bayonne, played them against each other, and bullied each in turn into renouncing the throne. Then he handed the crown of Spain to his own brother, **Joseph Bonaparte**, and installed him as king. In a few weeks, with barely a shot fired at the top, Spain's legitimate king had become a prisoner in France and a Frenchman sat on his throne.

The young, deposed Ferdinand VII now became something he had done nothing to earn: a symbol. To Spaniards and colonists alike, who refused to accept the French usurper Joseph, Ferdinand was the rightful king, the captive monarch around whom all loyalty crystallized. They began to call him **"el Deseado," the Desired One** — the longed-for king who would one day return and set everything right. It is one of history's grim ironies, because when Ferdinand finally did return in 1814 he turned out to be a vicious, vengeful absolutist who would betray everyone who had fought in his name (Chapter 4). But in 1808, the Desired One in chains was the empty space at the center of the empire, and a whole hemisphere now had to decide what to do about a throne that was occupied by a fraud and a true king who was a prisoner.

This is the moment to introduce the word that organizes the next several years: the **junta** (in Spanish, HOON-tah; it just means a "committee" or "council," and gives English the modern sense of a group that seizes governing power). When the French installed Joseph, ordinary Spaniards across the unoccupied parts of Spain refused to obey him, and in town after town they spontaneously formed local governing committees — juntas — to rule in the name of the captive Ferdinand and to organize resistance to the French. The logic they reached for was an old one, dusted off from medieval Spanish political theory and now suddenly explosive: if the king is unable to rule, sovereignty — the right to govern — reverts to the people, who may exercise it through their own assemblies until the king is restored. These Spanish juntas eventually banded together under a central junta, and then, with most of Spain under French boots, into something more ambitious.

In 1810 the dwindling free remnant of patriotic Spain, holed up behind British naval protection in the southern port city of Cádiz, convened a national assembly called the **Cortes of Cádiz** (KOR-tays; *cortes* is the old Spanish word for a representative parliament). Besieged by the French, the Cortes did something remarkable: it set out to reinvent Spain as a modern liberal nation, and it invited delegates from the American colonies to take part as equals. In 1812 it produced the **Spanish Constitution of 1812**, nicknamed "La Pepa" because it was promulgated on March 19, the feast day of St. Joseph (whose Spanish nickname is Pepe), a genuinely radical document for its day — it proclaimed that sovereignty resided in the nation rather than the king, established a constitutional monarchy with the king's powers limited by an elected assembly, and granted rights of citizenship and representation to Spaniards on both sides of the Atlantic. For colonial liberals this was electric. It seemed to promise that they might remain within a Spanish world reborn as a partnership of equals rather than an extraction colony. The promise would be torn up two years later by the returning Ferdinand, and that betrayal would push many of the hesitant toward independence (Chapter 4). But the Cortes mattered for a longer reason: in its debates over rights, representation, and who counted as a citizen, it taught the political vocabulary — sovereignty, nation, constitution, the consent of the governed — that the revolutions would speak.

Now follow the logic across the ocean, because here is where the trap springs. When news of the Bayonne abdications and the French usurpation reached the Americas in 1808 and 1809, the colonists faced the same dilemma as the Spaniards, but with a twist that nobody could quite contain. They, too, refused to recognize the French puppet Joseph. They, too, declared their loyalty to "the Desired One," the captive Ferdinand. And they, too, reasoned that with the legitimate king imprisoned, sovereignty had reverted to the people — so they, too, were entitled to form their own juntas to govern in his name until he returned. On its face this was a profoundly *loyalist* argument. The first colonial juntas insisted, sincerely or strategically, that they were not rebelling at all; they were merely safeguarding the king's authority in his absence, exactly as the Spaniards in Cádiz were doing.

But the argument contained a question it could not stop itself from asking, and the question was radioactive. If sovereignty had reverted to the people of America in the king's absence — if the colonists had the right to govern themselves while the throne was empty — then *why obey the juntas in Spain at all?* Why should the committee in Cádiz, claiming to speak for the captive king, have any more authority over Mexico or Venezuela or the Río de la Plata than the committee in Caracas or Buenos Aires? On what principle was a Spaniard in Seville more entitled to rule Americans than the Americans themselves? The colonists had been handed, by the collapse of the crown, the intellectual tools to justify self-government — and once those tools were in their hands, the distance between "we govern ourselves temporarily, for the king" and "we govern ourselves, full stop" was a distance that events, and a few bold men, could close very fast.

That is the decapitation. Spain did not lose its empire because the colonies rose up against a hated king. It lost the empire because the king was taken away, and the colonists, forced to improvise a government in the vacuum, talked themselves — at first reluctantly, then irreversibly — into the realization that they could do it on their own. The peninsulares who ran the colonial governments understood the danger perfectly and tried to hold the line for the old order; the creoles saw, in the king's absence, the opening of a lifetime. Everything was now in place for the explosion that Chapter 1 said only lacked a trigger. The trigger had been pulled, four thousand miles away, by a French emperor who never gave the Americas a second thought. In 1810, the juntas of America began to declare themselves — and the wars began.


# Chapter 4 — The Juntas and the First Republics

The year 1810 is the year the dam broke, and it broke nearly everywhere at once. Across Spanish America, in a span of months, the creole elites of one capital after another deposed their Spanish-appointed governors and set up their own governing **juntas** — committees claiming, at first, to rule in the name of the captive king Ferdinand VII (Chapter 3) while the throne was usurped by the French. But this chapter is not a triumph. It is mostly a chapter of failure: of fragile first republics that rose with enormous hope and were crushed within a year or two, of revolutionaries who turned out to disagree violently about what they were even fighting for, and of a savage royalist counterattack that, by 1815, had nearly wiped the whole cause off the map. Understanding why the first attempt collapsed is the key to understanding why the second attempt, the one that won, had to be so ruthless.

Start in the far south, in the spring of 1810, in the prosperous port of **Buenos Aires**, capital of the youngest viceroyalty, the Río de la Plata (the future Argentina). Over the course of a week in May, the creole leaders of the city, taking advantage of the news that the central junta back in Spain had collapsed before the French, deposed the Spanish viceroy and installed a junta of their own. This was the **May Revolution**, and it is the founding event of Argentine independence — though, characteristically, the men of Buenos Aires were careful not to declare full independence for years, governing under the old loyalist cover of acting for Ferdinand. (Argentina would not formally declare its independence until 1816, at the Congress of Tucumán — Chapter 6.) What matters about the May Revolution is that it stuck. The royalists never managed to retake Buenos Aires itself, which gave the southern revolution something the north would desperately lack: a secure home base from which to fight. That single fact will explain a great deal about why San Martín's southern campaign succeeded where the early northern republics drowned.

Now go to the north coast of South America, to **Venezuela**, where the revolution began with the most fervor and ended in the most spectacular collapse. In April 1810 the cabildo (the town council) of **Caracas**, the Venezuelan capital, deposed the Spanish captain-general and formed its junta. The young men of the Caracas elite were in a hurry, and one of them, a passionate, wealthy, French-educated aristocrat in his twenties named **Simón Bolívar**, was sent to London to seek British support. There he found and recruited an old exile, the figure the histories call the "Precursor": **Francisco de Miranda** (mee-RAHN-dah). Miranda was a Venezuelan adventurer with one of the most extraordinary résumés of the age: he had left Spanish service decades earlier, fought for the rebels in the American Revolution, and then, washing up in revolutionary France, risen to command an army as a general of the French Republic — before spending years lobbying any power that would listen to back the independence of Spanish America, and dreaming, long before anyone else, of uniting the whole of it into a single nation he wanted to call "Colombia." Now, at last, the dream seemed real. In 1811 Venezuela became the first Spanish-American colony to formally declare its independence, and Miranda was made the leader of the **First Venezuelan Republic**.

It lasted barely a year, and its destruction is a case study in everything that would go wrong with the first generation of republics. The new republic was idealistic and divided: it adopted a loose federal structure (power spread among the provinces rather than concentrated at the center) at the exact moment it needed unity to fight a war, and the regions squabbled while the royalists organized. It alienated the very people it needed — the masses of mixed-race and enslaved Venezuelans had little reason to fight for a republic run by the same creole landowners who had always lorded over them, and the Spanish were happy to recruit them against it. And then nature intervened with cruel timing: in March 1812 a catastrophic earthquake struck, devastating patriot-held cities while sparing some royalist ones, and the Church and royalists seized on it as the visible wrath of God against rebellion. The combination of military weakness, social division, and bad luck finished the republic. Miranda, judging the cause lost, signed a capitulation to the royalists in mid-1812. In one of the period's ugliest episodes, his own officers — Bolívar among them — regarded the surrender as a betrayal and handed the old Precursor over to the Spanish, who shipped him to a dungeon in Cádiz, where he died in 1816. The man who had dreamed of a free Colombia for thirty years was delivered to his enemies by the man who would build it.

That man, Bolívar, fled into exile, and here we meet the first turning point in his transformation from idealistic young aristocrat into the hardest political mind of the revolution. In 1812, sheltering in the New Granadan port of **Cartagena**, he wrote the **Cartagena Manifesto**, a furious, clear-eyed autopsy of why the First Republic had died. His verdict was blunt and it became his lifelong creed: the republic had killed itself with idealism. Too much federalism, too much faith in fine constitutions copied from abroad, too little central authority, too little willingness to wage war as war. The fledgling nations of America, Bolívar argued, were not the disciplined citizenry of the United States; they needed strong, centralized, unsentimental leadership to survive their birth. This conviction — that liberty in Spanish America required a firm and even authoritarian hand — would drive every triumph and every tragedy of his career, all the way to the dictatorship and the bitter death (Chapter 10). The romantic was becoming the realist.

He proved it almost at once. In 1813 Bolívar led a swift, brilliant campaign out of New Granada into Venezuela, retaking Caracas and earning the title by which he would be known forever: **El Libertador**, the Liberator. This is celebrated as the **Admirable Campaign**, and it established a Second Venezuelan Republic. But to wage it, Bolívar issued one of the most chilling documents of the war, the **Decree of War to the Death**. Frustrated that so many American-born creoles were sitting on the fence or actively fighting for Spain, he declared that the conflict was now a war between nations, not parties: any American who joined the patriot cause would be spared, but any Spaniard, or anyone supporting Spain, faced extermination. The decree was meant to force the undecided off the fence by making neutrality impossible and to brand the war as American-against-Spaniard rather than a civil quarrel. What it actually did was license atrocity on both sides and turn the conflict into a cycle of massacre and reprisal that would scar Venezuela for a generation.

And it did not work, because the assumption underneath it — that the American masses would naturally side with the American-born patriots — turned out to be dead wrong, as the destruction of the Second Republic proved with horrifying force. The republic was brought down not by a Spanish army from across the sea but by Venezuelans, led by a royalist commander named **José Tomás Boves** (BOH-vess). Boves had grasped what the genteel creole patriots had missed: the poor, mixed-race horsemen of the Venezuelan plains — the **llaneros** (yah-NAY-rohs, the cowboys of the *llanos*, the vast tropical grasslands of the Orinoco basin) — had no love for the creole aristocrats who ran the republic, and could be turned into a ferocious fighting force in the king's name with promises of plunder and a settling of scores against their social betters. Boves rallied the llaneros into a terrifying cavalry that tore the Second Republic apart in 1814 with extraordinary savagery, slaughtering patriots and driving Bolívar once more into exile. The lesson was brutal and it was the same lesson Haiti taught (Chapter 2) from the other direction: the social war and the independence war were tangled together, and the masses were not automatically anyone's allies. Whoever could enlist the llaneros would win Venezuela. For now, the king had them.

The pattern repeated, with local variations, across the continent. In the Río de la Plata, the revolutionary general **Manuel Belgrano** — a lawyer and economist turned reluctant soldier, who in 1812 improvised a sky-blue-and-white banner that is still Argentina's flag — won hard-fought victories at **Tucumán** and **Salta** in 1812, blunting royalist invasions coming down from **Upper Peru** (the high silver country of modern Bolivia, directly north of Argentina and a fortress of Spanish royalist power because its wealth and its garrisons made it the hardest region in the south to dislodge). But his subsequent attempts to push north into the highlands failed disastrously, teaching the southern patriots a strategic lesson they would not forget: the royalist heartland of Peru could not be conquered by marching overland from the south through the mountains. There had to be another way in (Chapter 6). Meanwhile, in the disputed grasslands east of Buenos Aires called the **Banda Oriental** (the future Uruguay), a charismatic **gaucho** leader named **José Gervasio Artigas** (ar-TEE-gahs) — *gauchos* being the cattle-herding horsemen of the South American plains — raised a different kind of revolution, fighting not only Spain but also the centralizing elite of Buenos Aires, demanding a loose federation of self-governing provinces and pushing radical land reforms that gave him a devoted following among the rural poor. Artigas points toward a tension that will outlast the war itself: the fight between those who wanted power concentrated in the capital and those who wanted it spread among the provinces (Chapter 10).

By 1814 and into 1815, the whole edifice of the first revolutions was caving in, and the reason was that the king came back. With Napoleon defeated in Europe, **Ferdinand VII** — "the Desired One" — was restored to the Spanish throne in 1814. The colonial liberals who had fought in his name and pinned their hopes on the liberal Constitution of 1812 expected a grateful, reforming monarch. What they got was a vengeful absolutist who immediately tore up the constitution, restored the old order in its harshest form, and — now that he had a free hand and a peacetime army — resolved to crush the American rebellions by force. In 1815 he dispatched the largest military expedition Spain had ever sent to the Americas, roughly ten thousand veteran troops under a tough, capable general named **Pablo Morillo**, to reconquer the rebellious colonies. This is the **royalist reconquest**, and it nearly ended everything.

Morillo's expedition rolled over the weakened patriot strongholds of the north. New Granada and Venezuela fell back under firm Spanish control; the second Venezuelan republic was already gone, and now Bogotá and the New Granadan interior were retaken. But Morillo made the mistake that would, in the long run, doom the reconquest: he was harsh. His pacification relied on executions of captured patriot leaders, confiscations of creole property, and a heavy-handed terror meant to break the will to resist. Among those he had shot was **Camilo Torres Tenorio**, the New Granadan lawyer who years earlier had written the *Memorial of Grievances* arguing that American-born subjects deserved equal representation with Spaniards — a moderate, now a martyr. And that was the flaw in the whole strategy: Morillo's cruelty fell on exactly the wealthy, hesitant creoles whom Spain most needed to win back, and instead of cowing them it convinced many that there was no future for them inside the empire, that the only safety lay in driving Spain out completely. The reconquest crushed the first republics, but in its brutality it manufactured the recruits for the second wave.

So by 1815 the situation looked, on the map, like total royalist victory. Venezuela and New Granada were reconquered. The Venezuelan republics were rubble. Mexico's great rising had been beheaded (Chapter 8). The patriot cause survived in only two places: in the secure south around Buenos Aires, where a quiet professional was beginning to build something new (Chapter 6), and in scattered guerrilla bands hiding in the hills. The Liberator himself was a defeated exile yet again, washed up with nothing. The first attempt had failed almost completely. But it had also taught its survivors three lessons written in blood — that idealism without central authority kills republics, that the war could not be won without the masses on your side, and that Spanish brutality was the revolution's best recruiting officer. The man who had learned those lessons most thoroughly was about to wash ashore in Jamaica, sit down, and write the future of a continent (Chapter 5).


# Chapter 5 — Bolívar's Northern Campaign

In 1815 Simón Bolívar was, by any reasonable accounting, a failure. He had helped destroy two Venezuelan republics, handed the revolution's grand old man over to the Spanish, watched a llanero army shred his cause, and been driven from his homeland twice. He was a defeated exile, broke and without an army, sheltering on the British island of **Jamaica**. Eleven years later he would be the master of an area larger than the conquests of Napoleon, the liberator of six future nations, the most powerful man on the continent. The story of how a beaten exile became that is the story of this chapter, and it begins not with a battle but with a letter and a bargain.

First, the man, because the legend has buffed him so smooth it is worth recovering the human being. **Simón Bolívar** was born in 1783 into one of the richest creole families in Venezuela — old money, slave-worked cacao plantations, the colonial aristocracy at its most golden. He was orphaned young, raised by an Enlightenment tutor, **Andrés Bello** (who reappears as the great intellectual founder of independent Spanish America — Chapter 10), and sent to Europe to be finished as a gentleman. There, the story goes, standing on a hill in Rome in 1805, he swore an oath to liberate his homeland. He was short, intense, restless, vain, brilliant, and physically tireless, able to ride and march in conditions that destroyed younger men; he could be a visionary statesman one month and a petty, jealous intriguer the next. He was also, and this matters, a slaveholder by birth who became a genuine abolitionist by conviction — partly through principle, partly through a bargain he was about to make. The point of recovering the messy man is that the revolution was won by a person, not a saint, and his flaws are as load-bearing as his genius.

In exile in Jamaica in 1815, with the reconquest (Chapter 4) triumphant and the cause in ruins, Bolívar sat down and wrote the document that made him the prophet of the revolution as well as its general: the **Jamaica Letter**. It is a sweeping, astonishing piece of political writing, and it is the founding text of Latin American political thought. In it Bolívar surveyed the whole of Spanish America, predicted with eerie accuracy that it would win its independence, and then asked the harder question of what kind of nations it would become afterward. His answer was pessimistic and prescient. Spanish America, he argued, had been kept in such ignorance and servitude under colonial rule — denied any real experience of self-government — that its peoples were not prepared for the kind of liberal democracy that had worked in the United States (which loomed in his thought as both the proof that a colony could become a self-governing republic and the cautionary counter-model his own societies were not ready to copy — the U.S. founding has its own narrative). He feared the new nations would fracture, fall to demagogues and strongmen, and need strong, centralized governments to hold them together. Reading it now, knowing what the rest of the century held (Chapters 10 and 11), the Jamaica Letter is uncanny: the man predicted his own disappointment fifteen years early.

But prophecy does not free a continent; guns and men do, and Bolívar had neither. He got them from the one place in the hemisphere that wanted the Spanish empire destroyed even more than he did: **Haiti** (Chapter 2). In 1816 Bolívar sailed to the black republic and was received by its president, **Alexandre Pétion** (peh-tee-OWN), a free-born man of color who had risen in the revolution and now governed the south of Haiti after the assassinated Dessalines (Chapter 2). Pétion gave him what no one else would — ships, weapons, ammunition, a printing press, and volunteers — and asked for almost nothing in return. Almost. The one condition Pétion attached is one of the great hinges of this story: that Bolívar proclaim the abolition of slavery wherever his armies triumphed. The leader of the world's only nation born of a slave revolt would arm the liberation of the Spanish mainland, on the price of carrying emancipation with it. Bolívar agreed, and he largely meant it; from this point he was committed, at least in principle, to freeing the enslaved as he advanced. It was both conviction and necessity — he needed soldiers, and an offer of freedom was the strongest recruiting tool he had. The debt was real and Bolívar acknowledged it. The richest revolutionary in Venezuela went to war on the credit of formerly enslaved Haitians.

The other thing Bolívar had finally learned was where to find his army, and the answer was the same place his enemy Boves had found one (Chapter 4): the plains. After his early defeats, Bolívar based himself not in the cities of the coast but on the wild, flooded grasslands of the Orinoco basin, around the river town of Angostura (modern Ciudad Bolívar). And he won over the **llaneros**, the fearsome plains horsemen who had destroyed the Second Republic for the king, by allying with the one man who could lead them: **José Antonio Páez** (PAH-ess), an illiterate, charismatic llanero chieftain of staggering physical courage who was the finest cavalry commander of the war. With Páez's lancers — and with a growing legion of foreign volunteers, especially the **British Legion**, thousands of veteran soldiers thrown out of work by the end of the Napoleonic Wars in Europe and hired or inspired to fight in America — Bolívar at last had the instrument he needed. The social calculus had flipped: the plainsmen who had ridden for Spain now rode for the republic.

What he did with that instrument in 1819 is one of the boldest gambles in military history. The Spanish held the wealthy New Granadan highlands (the future Colombia), confident they were protected by geography: between the patriot base on the Orinoco plains and the royalist heartland around Bogotá lay hundreds of miles of flooded grassland and then the high, cold wall of the eastern **Andes**, the immense mountain range that runs the length of South America. No army, the Spanish reasoned, would attempt that crossing — least of all in the rainy season, when the plains were a sheet of water. So Bolívar did exactly that. In the middle of 1819 he marched his army — llaneros bred to the tropical lowlands, plus the British Legion — across the flooded llanos, swimming rivers and wading for days, and then up and over a frozen Andean pass more than 13,000 feet high. The cold and altitude killed men and horses by the hundreds; whole units of plainsmen who had never seen snow simply died on the mountain. The army that staggered down the far side into New Granada was ragged and depleted. It was also exactly where the Spanish were certain no army could be.

Three days after coming off the mountains, on August 7, 1819, that exhausted army fell on the surprised royalist forces at a bridge over the **Boyacá** (boh-yah-KAH) river. The **Battle of Boyacá** was over in about two hours. Bolívar's troops surrounded and captured nearly the entire royalist army — some 1,600 men and their commander — at the cost of fewer than a hundred patriot casualties. It was one of the most lopsided decisive victories of the age, and it broke Spanish power in New Granada in a single afternoon. Three days later Bolívar rode into **Bogotá** in triumph; the Spanish viceroy had fled in such a panic that he left the colonial treasury behind. The gamble had worked completely. The man who had been a penniless exile four years earlier now controlled the heart of northern South America.

With New Granada in hand, Bolívar moved fast to make his lifelong dream real. Late in 1819, at a congress meeting in **Angostura**, he delivered a famous address laying out his political philosophy — the need for a strong executive, a hereditary-style senate (an upper house whose seats would pass down within a class of distinguished families, like a home-grown aristocracy, rather than be elected), moral education for citizens — and persuaded the delegates to proclaim the union of Venezuela and New Granada (and, in aspiration, Ecuador) into a single great state: the **Republic of Colombia**, which historians call **Gran Colombia** ("Great Colombia") to distinguish it from the later, smaller country of the same name. This was the realization of Miranda's old vision and Bolívar's own: not a scatter of small, weak republics but one powerful Spanish-American nation that could stand up to the great powers of the world. On paper it was magnificent, sprawling across the top of South America. In reality its regions — the Venezuelan plains, the New Granadan highlands, the Ecuadorian sierra — barely knew one another, were separated by brutal terrain, and had little in common but the war. The dream and its fatal flaw were born in the same breath (Chapter 10).

But first the war had to be finished, and over the next three years Bolívar's generals completed the liberation of the north. In 1821, at the **Battle of Carabobo** (kah-rah-BOH-boh) on the Venezuelan plains, Bolívar's army — with Páez's llanero lancers and the British Legion bearing the brunt — broke the main Spanish field force and opened the road to Caracas; this was the battle that effectively secured Venezuelan independence at last, in the very country where the revolution had failed twice before. The naval side was finished in 1823 when a patriot flotilla destroyed the last Spanish fleet on **Lake Maracaibo**, ending any hope of a seaborne reconquest. And to the southwest, Bolívar's most gifted lieutenant carried the war into Ecuador. **Antonio José de Sucre** (SOO-kray) — young, disciplined, humane, and devastatingly competent, the model professional soldier of the whole war and Bolívar's clear heir — won the **Battle of Pichincha** (pee-CHEEN-chah) in 1822 on the slopes of a volcano above the city of Quito, liberating the future Ecuador and bringing it into Gran Colombia. Sucre will be the one who delivers the final blow of the entire continental war (Chapter 7), and whose murder will break Bolívar's heart (Chapter 10). Remember the name.

One more figure belongs to this campaign, because the marble statues left her out for a century and she earned her place. **Manuela Sáenz** (SAH-enz), a fierce, politically sharp woman from Quito, became Bolívar's lover, confidante, and political ally — and far more than a mistress in the footnotes. She was an active revolutionary in her own right, decorated for her service, trusted with his correspondence and his secrets, and she would one night save his life from assassins by holding them off long enough for him to escape, earning the title posterity gave her: "the Liberator of the Liberator" (Chapter 10). She stands for the many women — spies, couriers, financiers, soldiers, organizers — whose work the official histories of great men erased.

So by 1822 the north was free. Venezuela, New Granada, and Ecuador had been wrenched from Spain and bolted together into Gran Colombia, and Bolívar stood at the height of his power and fame, the Liberator in the fullest sense. But the revolution was not finished. The richest and most stubborn royalist stronghold on the continent still held: the old Viceroyalty of Peru, with its capital at Lima and its powerful Spanish armies dug into the high Andes. Bolívar was coming for it from the north. And from the south, having pulled off a mountain crossing every bit as audacious as Boyacá, another army was coming for it too — led by a man who was, in almost every way, Bolívar's opposite. The two liberators were about to converge on the same prize, and on the same town (Chapter 6).


# Chapter 6 — San Martín and the Southern Crossing

If Bolívar was the revolution's fire — passionate, vain, prophetic, hungry for glory and power — then the man who freed the southern half of Spanish South America was its ice. **José de San Martín** (sahn mar-TEEN) is the strangest great figure in this whole story, because he did everything Bolívar did — built an army, crossed the Andes, won the decisive battles, liberated nations — and then, at the moment of supreme triumph, when the whole continent was within his grasp, he simply handed it all to his rival, turned around, and walked away from public life forever, dying decades later in obscurity in France. He is the liberator who refused to become a ruler, and to understand both his triumph and his renunciation you have to start with the fact that he was, by temperament, not a revolutionary romantic at all. He was a professional soldier.

San Martín was born in 1778 in a remote corner of the Río de la Plata, the son of a Spanish officer, and as a boy he was taken to Spain and put into the Spanish army, where he spent more than twenty years as a competent, disciplined career officer, fighting against the French in the Peninsular War (Chapter 3). He learned war from the inside of the most professional army he would ever face. Then, in 1812, in his mid-thirties, he made a decision that still puzzles historians — he abandoned his Spanish career and sailed back across the Atlantic to offer his sword to the revolution in the land of his birth, a place he barely knew as an adult. He arrived not as a firebrand but as a technician of war, contemptuous of amateur heroics, obsessed with training, logistics, and discipline. His first contribution was small and characteristic: in 1813, at **San Lorenzo** on the banks of the Paraná river, he led a brief, sharp action with a unit of mounted grenadiers he had personally trained, wiping out a royalist raiding party. It was a minor engagement, but it announced a new kind of soldier in the southern war — methodical, professional, unsentimental.

San Martín had also worked out the strategic problem that had defeated everyone else in the south, and his solution is the intellectual heart of his campaign. The patriots of Buenos Aires kept trying to destroy the royalist power on the continent by marching their armies north, overland, through Upper Peru (modern Bolivia) toward the great prize: **Lima**, the capital of the Viceroyalty of Peru, the oldest, richest, and most stubbornly royalist city in South America, the very citadel of Spanish power. Belgrano's victories and then disasters on that northern front (Chapter 4) had proved it was a dead end — the terrain was murderous, the supply lines impossible, and the royalists always held the high ground. San Martín's insight was that Lima could not be taken by land from the south at all. It had to be taken from the sea. And to attack it from the sea, the patriots first needed a base on the Pacific coast — which meant they first had to liberate **Chile**, the long ribbon of land on the far side of the Andes, currently back in royalist hands. The whole grand plan followed from that geography: free Chile, build a fleet, sail north, take Lima.

The obstacle was the **Andes** themselves — here, between Argentina and Chile, some of the highest and most forbidding mountains on the planet, with passes well over 12,000 feet. So San Martín did the patient, unglamorous thing that defined him: he got himself appointed governor of the western Argentine frontier province of **Cuyo**, around the town of Mendoza at the foot of the mountains, and he spent two years quietly turning the whole province into a war machine. He raised and drilled an army from scratch — the **Army of the Andes** — and he equipped it by mobilizing the entire local economy: workshops cast cannon, women wove uniforms, the province was taxed and organized for a single purpose. He recruited Chilean exiles who had fled the royalist reconquest of their country, chief among them a stocky, determined man named **Bernardo O'Higgins** (oh-HIG-inz), the illegitimate son of Ambrosio O'Higgins, an Irish-born emigrant who had taken service with Spain and climbed all the way to viceroy of Peru (Spain recruited capable foreigners, especially Catholic Irishmen with no love for England, into its colonial administration), and who would become the founding leader of independent Chile. This methodical, two-year, province-wide preparation is the opposite of Bolívar's improvised gambles, and it is exactly why San Martín's campaign, once launched, would unfold with almost machine-like success.

In January and February of 1817 came the **Crossing of the Andes**, the feat that ranks with Bolívar's Boyacá march as one of the great military operations of the age — and in some ways exceeded it for sheer planning. San Martín took roughly 5,000 men, with thousands of mules and horses and herds of cattle for food, and pushed them over the high passes in separate columns, coordinated to converge on the Chilean side at the same time, having deliberately spread misinformation so the royalists could not guess where the blow would fall. The crossing was an ordeal of altitude, cold, and exhaustion that killed a third of the animals, but the army came down out of the mountains intact and, crucially, where it was least expected. Surprise was total. On February 12, 1817, just two days after descending, San Martín's army caught the royalist defenders at **Chacabuco** (chah-kah-BOO-koh) and smashed them, and two days after that the patriots entered the Chilean capital, **Santiago**. O'Higgins was made Supreme Director of a Chile that declared its independence the following year.

The royalists were not finished in Chile, and the campaign nearly came undone in early 1818. The Spanish gathered a fresh army and, in a night attack at **Cancha Rayada**, surprised and badly mauled the patriot forces — for a few days it looked as if the whole liberation of Chile might collapse, and panic gripped Santiago. But San Martín, helped by O'Higgins, rallied the broken army with astonishing speed, and just weeks later, on April 5, 1818, he met the Spanish in open battle at **Maipú** (my-POO), outside Santiago, and destroyed them. Maipú annihilated the last royalist field army in Chile and secured the country's independence for good. The first half of San Martín's grand plan was complete. Chile was free, and it would now serve as the launching pad for the assault on Lima.

The second half came in 1820 and 1821. With Chile secured, the patriots built and bought a navy — commanded, in a vivid detail of the age, by a flamboyant and brilliant Scottish ex-Royal Navy officer named Thomas Cochrane — a famous British naval captain who had been drummed out of his own service over a financial scandal and hired himself out to liberation movements abroad, one of many foreign professionals who sold their skills to the revolution — and San Martín's army sailed north up the Pacific coast and landed in Peru. Rather than storm the royalist strongholds head-on, San Martín moved cautiously, cutting Lima off, waiting, maneuvering, hoping the royalist cause would crumble from within rather than be smashed in a bloodbath (his caution here would later be criticized as fatal slowness). In July 1821 he entered Lima and proclaimed the independence of **Peru**, taking for himself the deliberately modest title of "Protector" — a temporary guardian-of-the-nation role rather than a head of state, chosen precisely because it claimed no permanent power and no crown. But he knew the job was only half done. The Spanish had pulled their main armies back into the high Andean interior, where they remained powerful and undefeated, dug into the mountains around Cusco. San Martín had taken the capital but not broken the enemy, and he did not have the strength to finish the war alone. He needed Bolívar's army from the north. And so the two halves of the continental revolution, which had been marching toward Lima from opposite ends of South America for years, finally had to meet.

They met once, and only once, in July 1822, in the steaming Pacific port of **Guayaquil** (gwah-yah-KEEL), in modern Ecuador — a city both of them, awkwardly, wanted to claim for their respective nations. The **Guayaquil Conference** is the most famous closed-door meeting in Latin American history, and it is famous precisely because nobody knows what was said in it. The two liberators talked privately, with no reliable record kept, over the course of a day or two. We know only the result: San Martín emerged having yielded completely. He gave up his claim to Guayaquil, he declined Bolívar's chilly offer to serve under him, and — most stunning of all — he arranged to hand over his army, his resources, and the whole unfinished liberation of Peru to Bolívar, and then he withdrew from the war and from public life entirely. He sailed away, resigned his command, and left South America. What passed between them? Historians have argued for two centuries. Did Bolívar refuse to share the glory or the command? Did San Martín, the cold professional, simply judge that two ambitious leaders could not coexist and that the cause was better served by one man finishing it than by the two of them fighting over it? Did he see in Bolívar a hunger for power he himself did not have and want no part of the squabbles to come? The honest answer is that we do not know, and anyone who tells you the exact words is filling a silence with invention. What is certain is the choice San Martín made, and it is almost without parallel: at the summit of his achievement, with half a continent at his back, he stepped aside and gave it all to his rival rather than fight for the credit.

It cost him everything, in his own lifetime. San Martín went into a self-imposed exile in Europe and spent nearly thirty years living quietly and mostly forgotten near Boulogne, in France, where he died in 1850 (Chapter 10), while lesser men inherited the nations he had freed. Only long afterward would Argentina and Chile reclaim him as the supreme national hero, the liberator whose greatness was measured precisely by his refusal to grab power — the anti-*caudillo*, in a century that would be ruined by *caudillos*, the personalist strongmen who would rule by charisma and force rather than law (Chapter 10). For now, in 1822, the consequence of Guayaquil was simple and enormous: there was now only one Liberator left in the field, and the entire remaining task of destroying Spanish power on the continent fell to Bolívar and his lieutenant Sucre. The Spanish armies were still up in the Peruvian Andes, undefeated and dangerous. The final reckoning was at hand (Chapter 7).


# Chapter 7 — The Final Reckoning

By the end of 1822 the Spanish empire in South America had been reduced to a single, stubborn, well-defended fortress: the highlands of Peru. The north was free under Bolívar; Chile and the coast of Peru were free under San Martín, who had then walked away (Chapter 6) and handed Bolívar the rest of the job. But possessing Lima and the coast was not the same as winning, because the Spanish had done something shrewd: they had pulled their main armies up out of the lowlands and into the high interior Andes around Cusco, the old Inca capital, where the thin air, the brutal terrain, and the loyalty of much of the highland population made them very hard to reach and very dangerous to attack. These were not demoralized garrison troops. They were the largest, most experienced royalist field armies left on the continent, and as long as they survived intact, the independence of all of South America was provisional. This short chapter is about how that last army was hunted down and destroyed, ending three centuries of Spanish rule on the continent in two battles fought above 11,000 feet.

Bolívar arrived in Peru in 1823 to take charge, but the situation he found was a snake pit. The Peruvian patriots were divided and quarrelsome, the treasury was empty, and the royalist forces in the mountains were formidable and, briefly, even pushing back toward the coast. For a moment the whole enterprise wobbled. Bolívar, granted near-dictatorial authority by a desperate Peruvian congress, spent much of 1823 and early 1824 doing the patient work San Martín had done before Chile — rebuilding, supplying, and training an army capable of going up into the high country and beating the Spanish on their own ground. By the middle of 1824 he was ready, and he took the war up into the Andes.

The first clash came in August 1824 on the high tableland at **Junín** (hoo-NEEN), and it was one of the strangest battles of the entire era. It was fought almost entirely by cavalry, at over 13,000 feet, and — in a detail that sounds invented but is well attested — it was fought in near silence, with the lance and the saber rather than the musket. Not a shot was fired. For about an hour the patriot and royalist horsemen hacked at each other on the cold plateau until the royalist cavalry broke and fled. Junín was not a huge battle in numbers, but it was decisive in morale: it shattered the confidence of the royalist command and forced the main Spanish army into a long, exhausting retreat through the mountains, with the patriots in pursuit. The endgame was now in motion.

It ended on December 9, 1824, on a high plain near the town of **Ayacucho** (eye-ah-KOO-choh), and the **Battle of Ayacucho** is the single most important battle in the history of South American independence — the one that finished it. Bolívar himself was not present; he had returned to the coast, and command of the patriot army fell to his finest general, **Antonio José de Sucre** (Chapter 5). Sucre had perhaps 6,000 men against a larger and well-positioned royalist army of around 9,000 under the Spanish viceroy himself. In a sharp, brilliantly handled action, Sucre's army broke the royalist line, routed the larger force, and captured the **Spanish viceroy** of Peru, José de la Serna, on the field. The viceroy, seeing the situation was hopeless, signed a capitulation that day surrendering all remaining Spanish forces in Peru. With the stroke of that pen, the Spanish empire on the continent of South America — which had begun with the conquest of the Inca three centuries before, almost in these same mountains (the Inca narrative tells that beginning) — was finished. There would be mopping up; the great fortress of **Real Felipe** at Callao, guarding Lima's port, held out under siege into 1826, the very last royalist stronghold to fall, a reminder that the fortifications built to defend the empire became the final pockets of its resistance. But the war on the continent was effectively over at Ayacucho. The continent was free.

There was one more nation to make, and it was made out of the ground the last battles had been fought over. The region of **Upper Peru** — the high silver country around Potosí (Chapter 1), long governed as part of one viceroyalty or another and claimed by both Peru and the Río de la Plata — now had to decide its future. Rather than be absorbed by either of its large neighbors, it chose, with Sucre's encouragement, to become an independent nation of its own. In 1825 it declared its independence and, in a gesture of homage, named itself after the Liberator: **Bolivia**. Sucre became its first president. Bolívar, asked to write the new country a constitution, produced a document that revealed how far his thinking had hardened since the idealistic early days — most strikingly, it provided for a **president-for-life**, an executive who would serve until death and effectively choose his successor, a near-monarchy in republican dress. To Bolívar this was the logical conclusion of the argument he had been making since the Cartagena Manifesto (Chapter 4) and the Jamaica Letter (Chapter 5): the new nations were too fractured and inexperienced for ordinary democracy and needed a strong, permanent hand to hold them together and save them from chaos. To his critics — and they were multiplying, especially the constitutionalists who wanted civilian rule and law (Chapter 10) — the Bolivian Constitution's president-for-life looked exactly like what they had just fought a fifteen-year war to abolish: a king by another name. The argument over whether liberty needed a strong man or feared one would, within five years, tear apart everything Bolívar had built.

So stand back and measure what had been accomplished by 1826. In a little over fifteen years, an empire that had governed half a hemisphere for three centuries had been driven entirely out of mainland Spanish America. From Mexico to the tip of South America, the viceroyalties were gone, replaced by a cluster of new independent republics: Mexico, the Central American states, Gran Colombia (Venezuela, New Granada, Ecuador), Peru, Bolivia, Chile, the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata, Paraguay, the Banda Oriental. Spain, which had ruled an empire on which the sun famously never set, now held in the entire Americas only two Caribbean islands: Cuba and Puerto Rico (and even those it would lose by the end of the century — Chapter 11). It is worth pausing on the sheer speed and scale of it, because nothing quite like it had happened before: roughly twenty future nations came into being in less than two decades, an entire continental empire dissolved, formal European monarchy swept off most of a hemisphere. The military achievement of Bolívar, San Martín, Sucre, and the armies of llaneros, gauchos, freed slaves, conscripts, and foreign volunteers who did the actual fighting and dying was staggering.

And yet the men who had won it were already, in their private moments, uneasy. They had answered the question Chapter 3 posed — who rules when the king is gone? — by saying: we do, the people of America, through republics of our own. But they had not answered the harder questions underneath it. What were the borders of these new nations, and who decided? Who counted as a citizen in societies still riven by the casta hierarchy (Chapter 1) and still, in most places, holding human beings in slavery? Would the promise of emancipation Bolívar had made to Haiti (Chapter 5) actually be kept? And the deepest question of all, the one that would dominate the rest of the century: in nations with no experience of self-rule, no settled institutions, and a continent full of victorious generals with armies at their backs, what would stop those generals from simply taking power for themselves? Ayacucho had ended the war. It had not begun the peace. The hard part — building the nations the war had merely conceived — was about to break the dreamers who had fought for it (Chapter 10). But before that reckoning, two great parts of the hemisphere had been winning their independence on entirely different terms, by entirely different paths: Mexico, where the revolution ran backward (Chapter 8), and Brazil, where a prince did the whole thing almost without firing a shot (Chapter 9).


# Chapter 8 — Mexico's Two-Stage Revolution

Mexico won its independence twice, and the two times were aimed in opposite directions. The first revolution, beginning in 1810, was a vast, furious uprising of the Indigenous and mixed-race poor against the whole colonial order — against Spain, yes, but also against the wealthy creole and Spanish elites who ran the place — and it so terrified those elites that they crushed it and clung to Spain for protection. The second "revolution," in 1821, was led by those very same elites, who flipped and declared independence precisely in order to *preserve* the conservative order against a threat coming, of all places, from a newly liberal Spain. Mexico is the great paradox of Latin American independence: the country broke from Spain not because radicals overthrew conservatives, but because the conservatives finally decided that staying with Spain had become more dangerous than leaving. To understand how a revolution can run backward, you have to follow both stages.

The first stage began with a sound: a church bell. In the small town of **Dolores**, in the rich farming region of the Bajío north of Mexico City, the parish priest was a sixty-year-old creole named **Miguel Hidalgo** (ee-DAHL-goh) — an educated, unconventional, somewhat heterodox priest who had read his Enlightenment and chafed at the colonial system. In the early hours of September 16, 1810, with a creole conspiracy he was part of about to be exposed, Hidalgo rang his church bell, gathered his largely Indigenous and mestizo parishioners, and issued a call to rise up — the famous **Cry of Dolores** (the *Grito de Dolores*), Mexico's founding patriotic moment, still reenacted every year on its independence eve. What exactly he cried is itself a bit of national myth-making, but the gist was a call to rebel against bad government and the hated Spanish-born *gachupines* (the Mexican slang for peninsulares), in the name of the king, the faith, and the Virgin of Guadalupe, Mexico's beloved brown-skinned image of the Virgin Mary, whose banner the rebels carried.

What answered Hidalgo's call was not an army but a flood — and it terrified everyone, including the people who might have been its allies. Tens of thousands of Indigenous farmers, mine workers, and the rural poor rose up behind him, and the rising became a vast, vengeful explosion of accumulated rage against three centuries of the casta system (Chapter 1). Hidalgo's host, swelling toward 80,000 people, swept across central Mexico, and when it took towns it sometimes massacred the Spanish and creole inhabitants and plundered their property. This is the crucial point about the first Mexican revolution: it was as much a race-and-class war as a war for independence, and the creole elites — the people who in Venezuela or Argentina led the independence movement — looked at Hidalgo's mob and saw their own throats. They saw, in fact, exactly the nightmare of Haiti (Chapter 2): the masses risen, the social order overturned, the propertied classes slaughtered. And so in Mexico the wealthy creoles mostly threw in with the royalists, and the colonial authorities were able to raise effective armies against the insurgency with creole support. Hidalgo, despite his enormous numbers, commanded an untrained, poorly armed multitude, and in January 1811 a far smaller, disciplined royalist force shattered it at the **Battle of Calderón Bridge** near Guadalajara. Hidalgo fled north, was captured, stripped of his priesthood, and executed; the Spanish hung his severed head in an iron cage on a public building in Guanajuato, where it stayed for a decade as a warning.

But the insurgency did not die with him, because a far more capable leader took it up: another priest, **José María Morelos** (moh-RAY-lohs), who had been one of Hidalgo's followers and who turned out to be a natural military and political talent of a high order. Where Hidalgo had led an uncontrollable mob, Morelos waged a disciplined guerrilla war (a war of mobile, irregular forces using ambush and raid rather than set-piece battles) across the rugged south of Mexico, and he proved nearly impossible to catch. His defense of the besieged town of **Cuautla** (KWOW-tlah) in 1812, where he and his men held out through months of starvation before breaking through the royalist lines in a daring escape, made him a legend. And Morelos was a genuine political radical with a clear program, which Hidalgo had never really had. In 1813 and 1814 he convened a congress that declared Mexican independence outright and produced a remarkable document calling for an end to slavery, the abolition of the caste system and of legal racial distinction, and a sovereign Mexican nation. For a moment, the insurgency had both a brilliant general and a real vision of a new, egalitarian Mexico.

It was not enough. The same forces that had doomed Hidalgo — the hostility of the creole elite, the superior organization of the royalist armies — eventually closed in on Morelos. His military fortunes turned, the congress he protected was driven into flight, and in 1815 Morelos was captured, tried by the Inquisition (the Catholic Church's tribunal for rooting out heresy, which operated in colonial Mexico and claimed jurisdiction over Morelos because he was a priest), defrocked, and shot. With his death the first Mexican revolution effectively collapsed. For the next five years it survived only as scattered guerrilla bands holding out in the mountains, most stubbornly under **Vicente Guerrero** (geh-RAY-roh) in the rugged south — a commander of mixed African, Indigenous, and Spanish descent who refused every offer of pardon and famously answered a plea to surrender with "*la patria es primero*," the homeland comes first. (Guerrero would live to become, briefly, Mexico's first president of African descent, and as president he would abolish slavery outright in 1829 — early by hemispheric standards, and a decision that, by alarming the slaveholding American settlers in Mexico's northern province of Texas, helped set off the chain of events that cost Mexico half its territory to the United States; the antebellum-America narrative tells that side.) But by 1820 the independence cause in Mexico looked dead. The popular revolution had been beaten. The country was firmly in royalist hands. And then it won — from the other side.

The thing that flipped Mexico had nothing to do with Mexico. In 1820, back in Spain, the army revolted and forced the restored king Ferdinand VII (Chapters 3 and 4) to reinstate the liberal Constitution of 1812. Suddenly Spain itself had a liberal, anticlerical, reformist government — one that threatened the privileges of the Church and the special status of the elites in the colonies. And this horrified Mexico's conservatives: the wealthy creoles, the high clergy, the great landowners, the men who had spent a decade fighting *against* independence to protect the old order. They now faced the prospect of that old order being dismantled not by a Mexican mob but by the legitimate government in Madrid. For the first time, independence began to look, to the Mexican right, like the way to *preserve* their world rather than lose it. Better to break from a dangerously liberal Spain and run a conservative Mexico themselves.

The man who turned that calculation into a country was **Agustín de Iturbide** (ee-toor-BEE-day), and his story is one of the great about-faces of the era. Iturbide was a creole royalist officer — and not just any royalist, but one of the more ruthless and effective commanders who had spent years fighting and killing the insurgents of Hidalgo and Morelos. In 1820, sent south ostensibly to finish off the last rebel holdout Guerrero, Iturbide instead opened secret negotiations with him, and the two former enemies struck an extraordinary bargain: they would join forces and declare independence together, on terms designed to reassure absolutely everyone. In February 1821 they proclaimed the **Plan of Iguala** (ee-GWAH-lah) — a "Plan," in the Mexican political tradition that was just being born, meant a published manifesto declaring a program and calling others to rally to it, a form that would be used again and again across the century — built around three promises that became known as the **Three Guarantees**: independence from Spain; the supremacy and protection of the Catholic Church (reassuring the conservatives and clergy); and full legal equality between Spaniards and Americans, with protection for the persons and property of all (reassuring the Spanish-born and quieting the race question). It was a masterpiece of having-it-both-ways. By promising the old elite that independence would *protect* the Church and their property rather than threaten them, while promising the insurgents the independence they had bled for, Iguala assembled a coalition broad enough to win. What a decade of bloody warfare had failed to achieve, this conservative compromise achieved in a matter of months: in September 1821, with almost no fighting, the Spanish authorities gave way and Mexico was independent.

Then it went, very quickly, wrong. Having freed Mexico, the vain and ambitious Iturbide was not content to be its first citizen. In 1822 he had himself proclaimed **Emperor of Mexico**, founding the **First Mexican Empire** with himself as Agustín I — a homegrown monarchy with a brand-new aristocracy, crowns, and court ritual, presiding over a country that was broke, politically divided, and utterly unconvinced. The empire was a fiasco from the start: the treasury was empty, the congress turned against him, and Iturbide responded by dissolving it, which only deepened the crisis. Within a year the army turned on him. A military revolt under the **Plan of Casa Mata** in early 1823 demanded a new congress and forced Iturbide to abdicate and go into exile. (He made the fatal mistake of returning to Mexico in 1824, apparently unaware that the congress had declared him a traitor to be executed on sight; he was arrested on landing and shot.) The First Mexican Empire had lasted less than a year, and its collapse dragged in the regions to the south: the Central American provinces, which had been attached to the Mexican empire, now broke away to form their own short-lived federation, the root of the future states of Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica.

With the empire gone, Mexico became a republic, and in 1824 it inaugurated its first president, **Guadalupe Victoria** — an old insurgent who had fought on through the dark years and taken a *nom de guerre* honoring the Virgin of Guadalupe and the cause of victory. He was one of the very few early Mexican leaders to actually complete his term in office, a rare patch of stability that would not last; the rest of the century would bring coups, foreign invasions, the loss of half the national territory to the United States (the antebellum-America narrative tells that war), and the long, destabilizing dominance of strongmen like Antonio López de Santa Anna — the mercurial general who would seize and lose the Mexican presidency some eleven times — in the same caudillo pattern that was gripping the rest of the continent (Chapter 10). The Catholic Church, which had stood on every side of the long struggle — village priests like Hidalgo and Morelos leading the revolt while bishops and the Inquisition condemned it, and then the Plan of Iguala enshrining Catholicism as a pillar of the new nation — emerged as one of the most powerful institutions in independent Mexico, and the fight over its enormous wealth and influence would define Mexican politics for fifty years.

So Mexico arrived at independence having traveled a road that inverted everyone else's. Elsewhere, idealistic creole republicans led the break and dragged reluctant masses behind them; in Mexico, the masses rose first and the creole elite led the break specifically to contain them. The radicals — Hidalgo, Morelos, with their visions of abolition and racial equality — lost, and were martyred. The conservatives won, and got their compromise nation with the Church and the social hierarchy intact. The deep promise of the first Mexican revolution, the egalitarian Mexico that Morelos's congress had declared, was deferred for generations. It is the same pattern as the rest of this book, just sharpened to a point: the war could change who ruled without changing the order they ruled over. And it is the mirror image of what was happening at the same moment on the other side of South America, in Brazil, where independence would also be led from the top and also leave the old order intact — except that there, the man at the top was an actual prince, and he would do it without a war at all (Chapter 9).


# Chapter 9 — Brazil's Bloodless Break

Everything about Brazil's independence is the opposite of Spanish America's, and the contrast is the most illuminating in this whole book — because it shows that the way an empire breaks apart depends enormously on the accidents of how it was held together in the first place. Spanish America was decapitated when its king was kidnapped, plunged into a leaderless crisis, and shattered through fifteen years of war into a dozen quarreling republics. Brazil's king came to live there in person, the colony grew comfortably into a kingdom, and when the break finally came it was declared by the heir to the throne himself, produced almost no bloodshed, and resulted in a single, vast, unified monarchy that held together as one country. Same continent, same era, same Iberian crisis at the root — and a completely different outcome. This chapter is about why, and the answer begins with the flight that opened Chapter 3.

Recall that in 1808, fleeing Napoleon's invasion of Portugal, the entire **Portuguese royal court** sailed to Brazil and set up the monarchy in **Rio de Janeiro** (Chapter 3). This single fact rearranged everything. For Spanish America, the king's removal created a vacuum; for Brazil, the king's arrival created a capital. Suddenly the colony that had been a backwater of plantations and gold mines was the seat of a European empire, and the prince regent — the future **John VI** (in Portuguese, João VI) — set about turning Rio into a real royal capital. He opened Brazil's ports to free trade with friendly nations (above all Britain), ending the old colonial monopoly at a stroke. He founded a bank, a printing press, a military academy, libraries, botanical gardens, a court. Brazil filled up with the apparatus of statehood. And then, in 1815, John formally elevated Brazil from a colony to a co-equal **kingdom**, joined with Portugal in a "United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil, and the Algarves" (the Algarve being the southernmost province of Portugal, traditionally named alongside Portugal proper in the crown's full title). Read that again: Brazil had been promoted out of colonial status without firing a shot, simply because the king happened to be living there. The economic and political grievances that drove Spanish creoles to war — the closed trade, the second-class status, the rule by distant officials — had largely been dissolved in Brazil by the mere presence of the crown. There was far less to rebel against.

But the arrangement could not last, because Portugal still existed across the ocean and it wanted its king and its old order back. After Napoleon's final defeat, the Portuguese back home grew increasingly resentful at being governed, in effect, as the colony of their own colony — Rio outranked Lisbon, and Portugal's economy suffered from Brazil's open ports. In 1820 a liberal revolution broke out in Portugal (part of the same wave that flipped Spain and triggered Mexican independence — Chapter 8), and the revolutionaries demanded that the king return to Lisbon and that Brazil be put back in its place as a subordinate colony. In 1821 John VI, under pressure, sailed home to Portugal — but he did something shrewd before he left. He installed his young adult son, **Pedro**, as prince regent to govern Brazil in his absence, and, according to the famous account, he gave Pedro a piece of advice that turned out to be the hinge of Brazilian history: if Brazil moves to break away, put the crown on your own head before some adventurer grabs it. In other words, if independence is coming anyway, lead it yourself and keep it in the family.

The Portuguese parliament, meanwhile, kept pushing to dismantle Brazil's new status, stripping away its institutions and ordering Prince Pedro himself to come home to Europe like a child being recalled from a privilege. Brazilians who had grown used to running their own affairs — and who feared being shoved back into colonial subordination — urged Pedro to refuse. In January 1822 he did. Ordered by Lisbon to return to Portugal, **Pedro** announced that he would stay: "*Se é para o bem de todos e felicidade geral da nação, diga ao povo que fico*" — "if it is for the good of all, tell the people that I stay." The day is remembered as the **Dia do Fico**, "the Day of 'I Stay.'" It was the first open defiance, an heir to the Portuguese throne siding with his Brazilian subjects against the Portuguese government. From there the break unrolled quickly and, remarkably, mostly peacefully.

The decisive moment came that September. Pedro was traveling near São Paulo when fresh dispatches arrived from Lisbon, full of new demands to subordinate Brazil and rein him in. According to the patriotic tradition, on September 7, 1822, beside a small stream called the **Ipiranga**, Pedro read the letters, lost his temper, drew his sword, and proclaimed Brazil's independence with the cry "*Independência ou morte!*" — "Independence or death!" This is the **Cry of Ipiranga**, Brazil's founding moment, and like the Cry of Dolores in Mexico (Chapter 8) it has been polished by a century of national art into something grander and cleaner than the messy reality almost certainly was. But the substance is real: in late 1822 Brazil declared its independence, and Pedro was crowned **Emperor Pedro I** of a new and enormous Brazilian Empire. The political brain behind the break was a distinguished elder statesman and naturalist, **José Bonifácio de Andrada e Silva**, often called the "Patriarch of Independence" — Pedro's chief minister and the man who did much of the work of organizing the new state, even as he argued (without success) for abolishing slavery and integrating the Indigenous peoples into the nation, reforms the conservative new empire would not accept.

The independence was not literally without violence — there was fighting, especially in the north, to expel garrisons loyal to Portugal, and people died. But compared to the fifteen-year bloodbath next door in Spanish America, Brazil's break was astonishingly cheap, and it preserved rather than smashed the existing order. And here is the great structural contrast, the thing worth slowing down for. Spanish America fragmented into many republics; Brazil stayed one empire. Why?

Several reasons converge. The first is the monarchy itself. Brazil kept the existing royal dynasty — the legitimate heir to the Portuguese throne became its emperor — which gave the new nation an instant, unquestioned source of legitimacy and a single focus of loyalty. Spanish America had no such figure; it had a kidnapped king it could not reach and then a vicious one it came to hate, and into that vacuum stepped a dozen rival generals and regions with no agreed authority above them. The presence of a crowned emperor at the center gave Brazil a centripetal force — pulling inward toward a single point — that Spanish America entirely lacked. The second reason is that Brazil never went through the shattering fifteen-year war that, in Spanish America, empowered regional military strongmen and let far-flung provinces drift into separate armed orbits. Brazil's relatively quick, top-led transition meant the central authority in Rio was never broken. A third reason is structural: Spanish America had been administratively divided into separate viceroyalties and captaincies for centuries (Chapter 1), each with its own capital and identity, so it had natural fault lines to fragment along — Brazil had been a single Portuguese domain. A fourth is the elites' shared fear: Brazil's ruling class, sitting atop a society where enslaved Africans were an enormous share of the population, had every reason to value a strong central monarchy that could keep the social order — and the slave system — intact, rather than risk the chaos and radicalism that fragmentation might unleash (the specter of Haiti, Chapter 2, loomed even larger in slave-heavy Brazil than elsewhere).

It was not automatic, and Pedro had to fight to keep it one country. The republican and federalist impulse that fragmented Spanish America existed in Brazil too, especially in the resentful northeast, which had a long tradition of regional revolt (a short-lived republican rising had flared in 1817 in Pernambuco, the sugar-growing region of the far northeast, where resentment of rule from distant Rio ran deep). In 1824, provinces of the northeast, angry at Pedro's centralizing new constitution and his autocratic style, rose in revolt and proclaimed a breakaway republic, the **Confederation of the Equator**. Pedro crushed it hard, executing its leaders. The message was unmistakable: independent Brazil would be a strong, centralized empire ruled from Rio, not a loose federation of provinces — the opposite of the road Spanish America had taken. Centralization won in Brazil where it had failed almost everywhere in Spanish America.

Pedro I himself, it must be said, was a restless and quarrelsome man not really suited to the patient work of building a nation. He feuded with his parliament, governed high-handedly, and was distracted by European dynastic politics — when his father John VI died in 1826, Pedro inherited the Portuguese throne as well — but a Brazilian emperor could not also rule Portugal, so within weeks he abdicated it in favor of his young daughter, and then spent years obsessed with securing it for her against a usurping younger brother. He also lost a war: the **Cisplatine War** against the United Provinces over the disputed Banda Oriental ended, under British mediation, with that territory becoming the independent buffer state of **Uruguay** in 1828 — the first new nation carved not from the Spanish empire but from the rivalries of the newly independent powers themselves. Worn down by political conflict and the Portuguese distraction, Pedro I abdicated the Brazilian throne in 1831 and sailed back to Europe to pursue his Portuguese ambitions, leaving behind his five-year-old son.

Through the 1830s, while that son was a child, Brazil was governed by a series of appointed regents ruling in his name — turbulent years of regional revolts that nearly tore the young empire apart, which is partly why the political class eventually rushed to crown him early. Raised in Brazil and crowned in 1840 as a bookish teenager, that son became **Emperor Pedro II**, and under his long, stable reign of nearly half a century Brazil became something genuinely exceptional in the hemisphere: a durable constitutional monarchy in a continent of fractious republics and caudillos. While Spanish America churned through coups and strongmen and civil wars (Chapter 10), imperial Brazil — for all its deep injustices, above all the slavery it clung to longer than any other country in the Americas — enjoyed a remarkable continuity of government. The monarchy that began with a prince's defiance by a brook gave Brazil the one thing the Spanish-American republics most conspicuously lacked: a stable center that held. It would last until 1889, when abolition finally cost it the support of the slaveholding elite and a military coup swept it away (Chapter 11). But for two-thirds of the nineteenth century, Brazil's bloodless, top-down, monarchical break delivered exactly what Bolívar had wanted and failed to build in Spanish America — a single, large, stable nation. The prince had succeeded where the Liberator was about to fail.


# Chapter 10 — The Broken Dream and the Caudillos

The wars were won. And then the winners discovered the cruelest truth of the whole story: that defeating an empire is far easier than building a nation. The same fifteen-year struggle that had freed Spanish America had also hollowed it out — wrecked its economies, killed a generation, militarized its politics, and left behind a continent of victorious generals with armies at their backs and no settled authority above them. The grand republics that the liberators had imagined dissolved almost as soon as the fighting stopped, the greatest of the liberators died heartbroken and disowned, and into the vacuum stepped a new kind of ruler who would dominate Latin America for the rest of the century: the strongman. This chapter is the morning after, and it is mostly a story of disappointment — but understanding the disappointment is essential, because it is the answer to the question of why a hemisphere that won its freedom so dramatically spent the following century in such turmoil.

Start with the collapse of the biggest dream of all: **Gran Colombia**, Bolívar's great union of Venezuela, New Granada, and Ecuador (Chapter 5). On paper it was the foundation of the powerful, unified Spanish America Bolívar had fought his whole life for. In practice it was ungovernable almost from birth. Its three regions were separated by jungle and mountains, had distinct economies and identities, and had been bolted together by war rather than grown together by common life. And at the top, a fundamental disagreement about what the new nations should be hardened into open enmity between the two most important men in the republic. On one side stood Bolívar himself, who by the late 1820s had become convinced — through the Jamaica Letter (Chapter 5), the Bolivian president-for-life constitution (Chapter 7), and bitter experience — that only a strong, centralized, near-dictatorial executive could hold these fragile nations together and save them from anarchy. On the other stood his vice-president, **Francisco de Paula Santander** (sahn-tahn-DAIR), the brilliant administrator who had governed New Granada while Bolívar campaigned and who was known as "the Man of Laws" for his insistence on constitutional, civilian, federal government, with power checked and spread rather than concentrated in one man.

This was not a personality clash, or not only one; it was the founding argument of Latin American politics, and it has never entirely stopped: should the new nations be held together by a strong central hand, even at the cost of liberty, or governed by law and divided power, even at the cost of stability? Bolívar, watching his union fray, increasingly chose the strong hand. In 1828, convinced that only force could save Gran Colombia from disintegration, he assumed dictatorial powers. The constitutionalists were horrified; to many it looked as if the Liberator had become the very thing they had fought to destroy. Later that year his enemies tried to assassinate him in Bogotá, and his life was saved only because **Manuela Sáenz** (Chapter 5) held off the attackers and helped him escape out a window — the night that earned her the title "Liberator of the Liberator." But dictatorship could not hold together regions that no longer wished to be one country. In 1830 Venezuela, under the llanero hero **José Antonio Páez** (Chapter 5), broke away; Ecuador broke away under another general; and Gran Colombia simply dissolved into the three separate nations it had been all along. The single Spanish-American superstate had lasted barely a decade.

For Bolívar it was the end of everything. Stripped of power, his union in fragments, reviled in the very countries he had liberated, and then struck by the news that his beloved heir Sucre (Chapters 5 and 7) had been ambushed and shot dead in the mountains of southern Colombia at the age of thirty-five — a political assassination, almost certainly arranged by regional rivals who feared he would hold Gran Colombia together, that removed the one man who might have held things together and that Bolívar felt as the death of the revolution's best hope — the Liberator's spirit broke. Sick with tuberculosis, exhausted, and bitterly disillusioned, he died near Santa Marta on the Caribbean coast in December 1830, at forty-seven, while preparing to sail into exile in Europe. The epitaph he left on his own life's work has echoed through Latin American history ever since. Surveying the wreckage of his dream, he is reported to have said that those who had served the revolution had "plowed the sea" — labored immensely and changed nothing, their efforts dissolving behind them like a furrow cut in water. Whether or not he said the exact words, they captured the mood of the disappointed continent perfectly, and they have been quoted ever since as the keynote of independence's bitter aftermath.

The other great liberator met a quieter version of the same fate. **San Martín** (Chapter 6), who had walked away from power at Guayaquil in 1822, spent the rest of his life in self-imposed exile in Europe, watching from a distance as the nations he had freed tore themselves apart, and died near Boulogne, in France, in 1850 — largely forgotten, honored by almost no one, a foreign pensioner in a country not his own. The two men who had freed a continent both died abroad or about to go abroad, embittered or ignored, their grand visions in ruins. It was only later, much later, that their nations dug them up, literally and figuratively, and rebuilt them into the marble heroes of national mythology — Bolívar as the patron of half a dozen countries, San Martín as Argentina's secular saint. In their own final years, both knew mainly disappointment.

Into the vacuum left by the broken republics and the fallen liberators stepped the figure who would define nineteenth-century Latin America: the **caudillo** (kow-DEE-yoh). The word means something like "chief" or "leader," and it names a specific and recurring type — the personalist strongman who seized power not through institutions or elections but through force of arms and force of personality, ruling through networks of personal loyalty, patronage, and charisma rather than through laws. The caudillo was the natural product of the world the wars had made. When the war ended, the most powerful things in any new nation were its armies and its victorious generals; the institutions that might have checked them — settled constitutions, courts, a tradition of civilian rule — barely existed, because there had never been any practice of self-government under Spain (exactly as Bolívar had predicted in the Jamaica Letter). A charismatic general with loyal troops and a personal following could simply take power, and so, again and again, one did. The prototype was Páez in Venezuela, the illiterate llanero hero who led his country out of Gran Colombia and then dominated its politics for two decades through sheer personal authority.

The most formidable of them all ruled in the south. In Argentina — still not really a unified country, but a loose, quarreling cluster of provinces locked in the long struggle between the centralizing liberals of Buenos Aires (the **unitarians**, who wanted power concentrated in the capital) and the provincial strongmen (the **federalists**, who wanted the provinces left autonomous) — power fell to a cattle baron named **Juan Manuel de Rosas** (ROH-sahs). From 1835 Rosas dominated Argentina for nearly two decades as the "Restorer of the Laws," ruling through a potent mix of genuine popular support among the rural gauchos (the Argentine cowboys of the pampas grasslands), federalist ideology, the cult of his own person, and a secret-police terror against his enemies, the *mazorca* (a network of armed loyalist enforcers who intimidated, beat, and murdered suspected opponents), that made open opposition deadly. Rosas is the archetypal caudillo at full power: a man who held a fractured country together by personal will and fear, who governed without real institutions, and who would finally be overthrown by force in 1852, leaving Argentina to resume the slow, violent work of becoming a single nation, which it did not really complete until the 1860s.

Not everywhere went the caudillo way, and the great exception is worth noting because it proves the pattern. In **Chile**, a conservative minister named **Diego Portales** (por-TAH-less) engineered, in the early 1830s, something almost no other Spanish-American nation managed in this period: a stable, impersonal *state*, rather than a strongman's personal regime. Portales's authoritarian conservative constitution of 1833 built strong, durable institutions — an order that did not depend on any one man's life — and gave Chile decades of unusual political stability and continuity, even after Portales himself was assassinated in 1837. Chile became the outlier, the Spanish-American republic that managed to build a functioning state early, and it is no coincidence that it also became one of the most stable and (later) prosperous. The contrast with Rosas's Argentina or caudillo Venezuela is the whole lesson: where institutions were built, stability followed; where only strongmen filled the void, instability became the rule.

The disappointment of the era found its great voice in a book. In 1845 an exiled Argentine writer and future president named **Domingo Faustino Sarmiento** (sar-mee-EN-toh) published *Facundo*, a furious, brilliant, and hugely influential book that framed the whole tragedy of the post-independence nations as a war between two principles he called "civilization and barbarism." Civilization, for Sarmiento, was the city, Europe, law, education, and order; barbarism was the countryside, the gaucho, the caudillo, violence, and the vast empty interior that bred strongmen like Rosas. *Facundo* was unfair, elitist, and contemptuous of the rural and Indigenous masses it blamed for the continent's troubles, but it named the central anxiety of the liberal nation-builders with unforgettable force, and the "civilization versus barbarism" framing dominated Latin American intellectual life for generations. Sarmiento and men like him — including the great Venezuelan-born scholar **Andrés Bello**, once Bolívar's tutor (Chapter 5), who wrote the definitive grammar of Spanish, drafted Chile's civil law code, and founded its university — represented the other half of independence's unfinished work: the effort to build, after the soldiers had failed, a *cultural* and *institutional* independence to match the political one. The republics had been won on the battlefield; now they had to be made in schools, law codes, and universities, and that work would occupy the rest of the century.

So the broken dream had a shape. The single great Spanish America was gone, fragmented into the rival republics whose borders still map the continent. The liberators were dead and disillusioned. The strongmen ruled. And the deep promises of the revolution — equality across the casta lines (Chapter 1), the emancipation of the enslaved (Chapter 2), genuine self-government — remained, for the most part, unkept. The nineteenth century in Latin America would be spent grinding through the consequences: building the institutions that had been missing, fighting savage wars among the new nations, and at long, shameful last finishing the one piece of business the wars of independence had most conspicuously left undone — the abolition of slavery (Chapter 11).


# Chapter 11 — The Unfinished Century

The wars of independence ended around 1825 (Chapter 7), but the *era* of independence — the long process of working out what these new nations would actually be — ran on for the rest of the century, and it did not end gently. Having torn itself free of Europe, Latin America spent the following decades turning its weapons on itself in some of the bloodiest wars in the hemisphere's history, finally getting around to dismantling slavery a half-century late, watching its one durable monarchy fall, and then discovering that the European empire it had thrown off was being quietly replaced by a new and closer hegemon to the north. This last chapter follows independence to its real conclusion — not the triumphant battles of the 1820s, but the messy, violent, unfinished settling-up that stretched all the way to 1903.

Begin with the wars, because the most shocking fact about post-independence Latin America is how savagely the new nations fought *each other*. The Spanish enemy was gone; the rivalries the empire had suppressed now had room to explode, often over the export commodities that the new economies lived on. The worst of them was the **War of the Triple Alliance** (also called the Paraguayan War), fought from 1864 to 1870, in which the small, isolated, inland nation of **Paraguay**, under its dictator **Francisco Solano López**, somehow ended up at war simultaneously with all three of its larger neighbors — Brazil, Argentina, and Uruguay. It was a catastrophe almost without parallel. Solano López, a grandiose strongman in the caudillo mold (Chapter 10), waged a hopeless total war and refused every chance to surrender, fighting on until he was hunted down and killed in 1870. By the war's end Paraguay had been crushed and depopulated to a horrifying degree — by the most-cited estimates it lost well over half its entire population, and the great majority of its adult men, though historians genuinely dispute the exact figures and some put the losses lower. Either way it was demographic catastrophe, the bloodiest war in South American history, and a brutal demonstration of how completely the dream of a fraternal, united Spanish America (Chapter 5) had curdled. Solano López remains contested to this day: a megalomaniac tyrant who destroyed his own country to his enemies, a heroic national martyr who defended its sovereignty to later Paraguayans.

The second great war redrew the western coast. The **War of the Pacific** (1879–1883) pitted **Chile** against **Peru** and **Bolivia** over the **Atacama** desert, a barren but fabulously valuable stretch of coast rich in nitrates and guano — nitrates being the mineral salts (used both as crop fertilizer and to make gunpowder and explosives) and guano the accumulated seabird droppings, two of the most valuable export commodities in the world in the age before synthetic fertilizer, and worth fighting a war over. (Peru had already enjoyed a "guano boom" in the mid-century, decades of state revenue from exporting seabird droppings, a vivid illustration of how thoroughly the new nations had settled into the role of raw-commodity exporters to the industrial world.) Chile, with its stable institutions and effective state (Chapter 10), won decisively, seizing the whole disputed coastline. Peru lost territory and was left devastated; Bolivia lost its entire coastline and became the landlocked country it remains to this day, still nursing the grievance more than a century later. These wars — Paraguay, the Pacific, and the long Argentine civil wars (Chapter 10) — are the hard evidence for the central irony of the whole story: independence created the nations, and then the nations spent the century bleeding one another.

Now the great unfinished business, the promise the wars of independence had most shamefully failed to keep: the end of slavery. Across most of Spanish America, slavery had been abolished in the decades after independence, often gradually and grudgingly — Bolívar's bargain with Haiti (Chapter 5) and the egalitarian visions of men like Morelos (Chapter 8) had committed the cause to abolition in principle, and the relatively small enslaved populations of most Spanish-American republics were freed by mid-century. But the great exception was the giant of the south. **Brazil**, the hemisphere's largest slave society, had built its entire economy on enslaved African labor and clung to the institution longer than anyone. Through the imperial decades under Pedro II (Chapter 9), Brazil dismantled slavery only with agonizing slowness, dragged by a growing abolitionist movement and by British pressure. It came in stages: an 1850 law finally ended the Atlantic slave trade into Brazil; the **Rio Branco Law** of 1871 (the "Law of the Free Womb") declared that children born to enslaved mothers would be free; and finally, on May 13, 1888, the Princess Regent **Isabel**, governing while her father Pedro II was abroad, signed the **Lei Áurea** (LAY-ee OW-ray-ah, the "Golden Law"), abolishing slavery in Brazil outright and without compensation to slaveholders. Brazil was the last country in the entire Western Hemisphere to abolish slavery — nearly a century after Haiti had ended it by revolution in 1804 (Chapter 2), and a generation after the United States had ended it by civil war (both endings are told in the Atlantic slave-trade narrative, which also weighs how many of the roughly twelve million captives carried across the Atlantic went to Brazil — far more than to any other single destination). The institution that independence had promised but failed to destroy finally died, on the continent, in 1888.

And it took a throne down with it. The Brazilian monarchy — the durable, stabilizing empire that had set Brazil so far apart from its republican neighbors (Chapter 9) — had rested, in large part, on the support of the great slaveholding landowners. When the Lei Áurea freed the slaves without compensating their owners, that powerful class abruptly withdrew its loyalty from the crown. Barely a year later, in 1889, a military coup deposed the aging, popular, but now politically isolated **Pedro II** and proclaimed the **Brazilian Republic**, under the army marshal **Deodoro da Fonseca** as its first president. The hemisphere's last monarchy fell with almost no bloodshed, much as it had been born (Chapter 9) — and Brazil joined the rest of Latin America as a republic dominated, in the familiar pattern, by the military and the landowning oligarchy. With the fall of imperial Brazil in 1889, formal monarchy was at last entirely extinguished across the Americas. The work begun when Napoleon emptied the Spanish throne in 1808 (Chapter 3) was, in this sense, finished: no king ruled anywhere in the New World.

Two final bookends close the long independence century, and both point to the future rather than the past — because both mark the arrival of a new dominant power in the hemisphere. The Spanish empire still held its last two scraps of the Americas, the Caribbean islands of **Cuba** and **Puerto Rico**, which had never joined the mainland revolutions. In Cuba a long independence struggle was relaunched in 1895 by the poet and revolutionary **José Martí** — relaunched because Cubans had already fought a grinding, failed war of independence against Spain from 1868 to 1878 (the Ten Years' War). Martí became the soul of the renewed cause and died in battle that same year. The Cuban war for independence might have run its own course — except that in 1898 the **United States**, citing the war and the still-unexplained explosion that sank its battleship USS *Maine* in Havana harbor (an event American newspapers blamed on Spain, though its true cause was never established), intervened, crushed Spain in a few months, and stripped it of Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines in the **Spanish–American War**. The Spanish empire in the Americas, born with Columbus in 1492, was over. But Cuba's "independence" came hedged with heavy U.S. control, and Puerto Rico simply passed from Spanish to American hands. The empire that replaced Spain in the Caribbean was not European at all.

The very last new nation of the long independence era made the new reality unmistakable. In 1903 the province of **Panama**, the narrow isthmus connecting the two Americas, broke away from **Colombia** — but not in a war against a European empire. Panama separated because the United States wanted to build a canal across the isthmus, Colombia had balked at the American terms, and so the U.S. backed and protected a Panamanian secession that gave it the canal zone it wanted. The last nation of the independence century was born not from the old struggle against Europe but from the ambitions of the rising power to the north, foreshadowed eighty years earlier by the **Monroe Doctrine** of 1823, in which the United States had declared that Europe must keep its hands off the Americas — a doctrine that had meant little when it was issued — in 1823 the United States was a minor power with no navy to enforce it, and it rode entirely on British sea power instead — but that now, with the United States grown into an industrial giant with warships in the Caribbean and money behind a new republic, had teeth at last (the U.S. narrative tells the doctrine's own story). One era of foreign domination was closing as another opened.

So end where the wars left their longest shadow. Latin American independence accomplished something genuinely world-historical: it created some twenty nations almost at once, swept formal monarchy off a hemisphere, ended three centuries of European empire across two continents, and drew the modern map of Central and South America. That much the liberators won, and it was enormous. But the deeper revolution — the one the war's rhetoric had promised — was left unfinished, and that is the truest legacy of the period. The racial caste of the colonial order (Chapter 1) outlived the wars by generations and in many places mutated rather than died. Slavery, the institution the cause had pledged to destroy, survived in Brazil until 1888. The dream of a united, fraternal Spanish America (Chapter 5) shattered into rival states that warred on each other catastrophically. Genuine self-government gave way, across most of the continent, to the rule of strongmen (Chapter 10). And the foreign domination the wars had thrown off began, by the century's end, to be replaced by a new one. Bolívar, dying disillusioned in 1830, had said the men who served the revolution had plowed the sea (Chapter 10). He was too harsh — the nations were real, and they endured. But he had grasped the thing this whole book has been circling: that winning a war of independence is the beginning of the work, not the end of it, and that the hardest fights — over who counts as equal, over who actually rules, over what a free nation owes the people inside it — were the ones the glorious battles of the 1820s had only postponed. The century that followed was the long, unfinished argument over the freedom the wars had declared but not yet delivered.
