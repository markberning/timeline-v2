# The Uyghur & Qara-Khitai Steppe — The Forgotten Half-Millennium

**Goal:** Narrative for the long gap between the Göktürks and Genghis Khan (744–1218 CE), the four and a half centuries when the Central Asian steppe and the oasis cities of the Tarim were neither Turkic-pagan nor Mongol. Across this span the Uyghurs overthrew the Türks and built a literate, city-dwelling, Manichaean steppe empire that lived off the silk of a China it had rescued and then bled; were destroyed in a single year by the Kyrgyz in 840; and reinvented themselves as settled Buddhist merchant-kingdoms of the Silk Road. In the same centuries their Karluk cousins became the first Turkic Muslims under the Kara-Khanids and gave Islam its first Turkic literature; and a Buddhist refugee dynasty from China, the Qara-Khitai, fled west and ended up ruling a Muslim Central Asia with conspicuous tolerance until the Mongols swallowed all of it. This is the bridge the reader is missing: how the Turkic world became literate, urban, and Muslim, and why the administrative machinery of the Mongol Empire (Mongol Empire) was, almost to the loanword, Uyghur.

**Status:** First draft — Chapter 1 (voice-calibration sample). Chapters 2–8 follow after the register is locked.

A note on names and pronunciation: the people in this book wrote little about themselves, and what they did write used scripts and titles that need flagging up front. A **qaghan** (pronounced "KHA-gahn," the same word that later wears down to "khan") is a steppe emperor, the ruler of rulers; lesser kings under him are merely khans. The hardest knot is the word *Turk* itself. **Türk** with the umlaut, in this book, means the specific ruling people — the Ashina clan and their Göktürk Khaganate (Göktürk Khaganate) — whom the Uyghurs overthrew. **Turkic** is the much larger language family those Türks belonged to: a family as internally varied as the Romance languages, spoken by dozens of peoples (Uyghurs, Karluks, Kyrgyz, Naimans, Pechenegs) who were no more "the same people" than Portuguese and Romanians are. The Uyghurs were Turkic but not Türk, kin to the dynasty they destroyed the way a Spaniard is kin to a Frenchman. One more caution: the medieval Uyghurs of this book are connected to the modern people of the same name in Xinjiang by a long, broken, and politically charged thread; this is the story of the medieval khaganate and its oasis successors, not a charter for anyone's present claims, and where the link is real or strained it is said so plainly. Dates are CE unless marked BCE. Miles come first, kilometers in parentheses.

A note on "first" claims and on knowing anything at all: as in every TL in this project, "first" means "first surviving evidence of." For the steppe this caveat is not boilerplate — it is the whole problem. Nomads built few archives and fewer libraries; almost everything here is seen through the eyes of the people they fought, traded with, or frightened. The Chinese dynastic histories watched the Uyghurs as a creditor watches a debtor; Muslim geographers wrote them up from Baghdad and Bukhara; a Persian official under the Mongols, Ata-Malik Juvayni (a thirteenth-century administrator-historian who had the Qara-Khitai and Mongol records in front of him), preserves much of the late story. Against all that outside testimony the Uyghurs left a thin, precious counter-voice: a few great inscribed stones in their own runiform Old Turkic, raised on the Orkhon, and later a flood of Buddhist manuscripts from the dry sands of Turfan. When this book says the Uyghurs "did" or "believed" something, it usually means a Chinese clerk or a Muslim traveler said they did, sometimes confirmed by a stone the Uyghurs cut themselves. Where the outside and inside voices disagree, or where there is only one of them, the text says so.

# Chapter 1 — The Khaganate That Replaced the Turks

The empire this book opens with was built by a people who took someone else's throne, someone else's capital region, and very nearly someone else's name — and then made all three into something the previous owners would not have recognized.

Start with the ground. The **Eurasian steppe** is a belt of open grassland that runs, almost unbroken, from the plains north of the Black Sea to the edge of Manchuria, several thousand miles of wind-combed grass with too little rain for farming and exactly enough for grazing. A people who could turn that grass into horses, sheep, and mounted archers could field armies that no settled kingdom could catch and few could beat. The catch was that grass is not silk, iron, grain, or books: a steppe power that wanted those things had to take them from, or trade them with, the farming civilizations on its rim, above all China. Every empire in this book is some answer to that single problem — how a grass economy gets the products of a grain economy — and the answers run from extortion to conquest to, eventually, simply moving into the oasis towns and becoming the farmers and merchants themselves.

Within that vast belt, one stretch of it was the steppe's throne room: the valley of the **Orkhon** (OR-khon) River, in the heart of what is now Mongolia, sheltered by a sacred wooded massif the Turkic peoples called the **Ötüken** (UH-tuh-ken). Whoever held the Orkhon was understood, by steppe people themselves, to hold the legitimate center of nomadic power, the way "Rome" meant more than a city. The Xiongnu had ruled from near here; so had the Türks (Xiongnu & Huns; Göktürk Khaganate). To take the Orkhon was to claim the steppe's equivalent of a crown, and that is precisely what the Uyghurs did in 744.

The people who held it before them were the **Türks** — specifically the Second Türk Khaganate, ruled by the **Ashina** clan, the dynasty that had given the world the very word *Turk* and the first empire to use it as a name for itself (Göktürk Khaganate). By the 730s the Second Türk Khaganate was the steppe's reigning power but a brittle one, its later qaghans weak, its subject tribes resentful and over-taxed in the only currency nomads have, which is loyalty. The largest block of those resentful subjects was a loose grouping of Turkic-speaking tribes the Chinese sources lump together as the **Tiele** (TYEH-luh) — kin to the ruling Türks in language and way of life, as a Dane is kin to a German, but politically a conquered underclass. Among the Tiele, one tribal confederation was rising: the **Uyghurs** (OO-ee-gur, also written Uighur), led by a clan called the Yaglakar.

It is worth being honest about how little of the Uyghurs' own early story survives in their own words, and how much in their enemies'. The Chinese knew them as a Tiele tribe that had supplied cavalry, sometimes to the Türks, sometimes against them, for over a century. The Uyghurs' own account, cut into stone a generation later, naturally tells it as destiny. The plain version both can agree on is this: around 742 three of the Türks' subject peoples — the Uyghurs, the **Karluks** (KAR-luk), and the **Basmyl** (BAHS-mil) — rose together, and the Second Türk Khaganate, already rotten, could not put them all down at once.

What followed is a small masterclass in steppe politics. The three rebel peoples won, and then immediately turned on each other to decide who would actually inherit the Orkhon. The Basmyl took the title first; within two years the Uyghurs and Karluks destroyed the Basmyl. The Uyghurs and Karluks then divided the spoils, the Karluks taking the western steppe (file that away — it is the seed of Chapter 6) and the Uyghurs taking the center. In 744 the Uyghur leader **Qutlugh Bilge Köl** (koot-LOOG BILL-geh KUL) proclaimed himself qaghan on the Orkhon, and in 745 his forces killed the last Ashina qaghan, **Özmiş** (UHZ-mish), and sent his head to the Tang court in China as both a trophy and a diplomatic announcement: the management has changed; deal with us now.

That head mattered more than it seems. By delivering the last Türk ruler's head to **Tang** China — the long, brilliant Chinese dynasty that ran from 618 to 907 and was, in the 740s, at the height of its wealth and confidence (Tang–Song China) — the new Uyghur qaghan was performing a careful piece of theater. He was telling the richest empire on earth that the Uyghurs were the Türks' legitimate successors, a stable address China could trade with and hire, not just another raiding nuisance. The relationship that opened with that severed head would, within fifteen years, make the Uyghurs the creditors of the Tang and is the whole subject of Chapter 2.

Qutlugh Bilge Köl died in 747, and the empire's real builder took over: his son, who reigned as **Bayanchur Khan** (BUY-an-chur), titled El Etmish Bilge Qaghan, "the state-organizing wise qaghan," and the title was earned. Over roughly the next dozen years Bayanchur turned a victorious tribal coalition into an actual state, and he did it with two instruments that tell you immediately this was not going to be an ordinary nomad empire: a city, and an alphabet's worth of writing.

The city was **Ordu-Baliq** (OR-doo BAH-lik), "the city of the court," raised about 200 miles (320 km) west of modern Ulaanbaatar on the Orkhon and known to later ages by its Mongol name, **Karabalgasun**, "the black ruins." A walled, gridded, permanent capital with a citadel, markets, and craftsmen's quarters was a genuinely strange thing for a steppe empire to build. The whole logic of nomad power was mobility: you could not besiege a people who had no fixed place to defend. By planting a capital, Bayanchur was trading away some of that invulnerability for something he wanted more — a fixed point where tribute could be collected, foreign merchants and ambassadors received, scribes housed, and the qaghan's wealth and prestige put permanently on display. It was a bet that the Uyghur empire would make its money less from raiding than from being a stable hub that taxed and protected trade, and that bet shaped everything that followed, including the religion the court would adopt (Chapter 3) and, ultimately, the speed with which the whole thing could be destroyed when an enemy finally did have something to burn (Chapter 4).

The writing is the other tell. The Türks before them had cut monumental inscriptions in a runiform Old Turkic script, and the Uyghurs continued the practice on a grand scale. Bayanchur left at least two great inscribed stelae — known today, from where they were found, as the **Shine Usu** and **Tariat** inscriptions — that narrate his campaigns and the founding of the khaganate in the Uyghurs' own language and their own voice. These stones are the thin inside-voice the introduction promised: almost everything else in this chapter is Chinese testimony, but here, for a few hundred lines, the Uyghurs speak for themselves, in the formulaic, boastful, surprisingly moving register of steppe royal memory ("I had walls built there; I had the eternal stone inscribed"). It is the difference between a credit report and a diary, and the historian needs both.

The state Bayanchur organized rested on a tribal skeleton the sources call the **Toquz Oghuz** (toh-KOOZ oh-GOOZ), "the Nine Tribes" — the nine-clan core of the confederation, with the royal Yaglakar at its head and a fixed order of precedence beneath. This is the answer to a question steppe history constantly raises and rarely answers cleanly: when a nomad "empire" appears overnight, what actually holds it together between campaigns? For the Uyghurs the honest answer, as far as the inscriptions and Chinese reports let us reconstruct it, is the Toquz Oghuz hierarchy plus the qaghan's ability to keep delivering two things — military success and a cut of the China trade. Remove either and the confederation could come apart as fast as it had come together, which is exactly what Chapter 4 records.

It is worth pausing, as this project does at every "first" or "only," to place the Uyghur khaganate in the world of its own moment, because the steppe is usually told as if it ran on a separate clock from everywhere else, and it did not. The year Qutlugh Bilge Köl took the Orkhon, 744, sits in an unusually crowded decade. Six years later, in 750, the **Abbasid Revolution** would overthrow the Umayyad caliphs and found the Baghdad-centered empire whose Persianized, Silk Road–wired world the western half of this book eventually joins (Islamic Golden Age). The next year, 751, an Abbasid army and a Tang army collided at the **Battle of Talas**, in the Karluk country west of the Uyghurs, and the Karluks' decision to change sides mid-battle helped hand Central Asia's future to Islam rather than China — the single clearest hinge in this whole book, and it happens offstage in Chapter 1 to people who will not matter until Chapter 6. In China, the Tang dynasty was four years from the catastrophe (Chapter 2) that would nearly destroy it and make the Uyghurs rich. In Japan, the court at Nara was casting the Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji. In Europe, the Frankish mayor Pepin the Short was about to depose the last Merovingian king and found the dynasty that produced his son Charlemagne. The Uyghur Khaganate was born into the same fifteen years as Abbasid Baghdad and Carolingian France, and it would outlast neither in fame, only in consequence.

By around 760, then, the picture was set. A Turkic people who were not the Türks held the Orkhon, the steppe's throne. They ruled from a walled city, kept written records of themselves, organized power through the Nine Tribes, and had announced themselves to China by couriering it the head of the last emperor they had replaced. Everything that made this empire unusual — that it was urban, literate, mercantile, and would soon be the only steppe empire in history to adopt a scriptural missionary religion of the settled world — is already visible in embryo in what Bayanchur built.

None of it yet explains how a grass empire got rich enough to pay for stone cities and a state church. That money did not come from the grass. It came from a disaster four years downriver, in the heart of Tang China, where a general named An Lushan was about to set the richest empire on earth on fire — and the Uyghurs were about to charge an extraordinary price to help put it out.
