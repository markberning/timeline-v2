# The Uyghur & Qara-Khitai Steppe — The Forgotten Half-Millennium

**Goal:** Narrative for the long gap between the Göktürks and Genghis Khan (744–1218 CE), the four and a half centuries when the Central Asian steppe and the oasis cities of the Tarim were neither Turkic-pagan nor Mongol. Across this span the Uyghurs overthrew the Türks and built a literate, city-dwelling, Manichaean steppe empire that lived off the silk of a China it had rescued and then bled; were destroyed in a single year by the Kyrgyz in 840; and reinvented themselves as settled Buddhist merchant-kingdoms of the Silk Road. In the same centuries their Karluk cousins became the first Turkic Muslims under the Kara-Khanids and gave Islam its first Turkic literature; and a Buddhist refugee dynasty from China, the Qara-Khitai, fled west and ended up ruling a Muslim Central Asia with conspicuous tolerance until the Mongols swallowed all of it. This is the bridge the reader is missing: how the Turkic world became literate, urban, and Muslim, and why the administrative machinery of the Mongol Empire (Mongol Empire) was, almost to the loanword, Uyghur.

**Status:** First draft — Chapter 1 (voice-calibration sample). Chapters 2–8 follow after the register is locked.

A note on names and pronunciation: the people in this book wrote little about themselves, and what they did write used scripts and titles that need flagging up front. A **qaghan** (pronounced "KHA-gahn," the same word that later wears down to "khan") is a steppe emperor, the ruler of rulers; lesser kings under him are merely khans. The hardest knot is the word *Turk* itself. **Türk** with the umlaut, in this book, means the specific ruling people — the Ashina clan and their Göktürk Khaganate (Göktürk Khaganate) — whom the Uyghurs overthrew. **Turkic** is the much larger language family those Türks belonged to: a family as internally varied as the Romance languages, spoken by dozens of peoples (Uyghurs, Karluks, Kyrgyz, Naimans, Pechenegs) who were no more "the same people" than Portuguese and Romanians are. The Uyghurs were Turkic but not Türk, kin to the dynasty they destroyed the way a Spaniard is kin to a Frenchman. One more caution: the medieval Uyghurs of this book are connected to the modern people of the same name in Xinjiang by a long, broken, and politically charged thread; this is the story of the medieval khaganate and its oasis successors, not a charter for anyone's present claims, and where the link is real or strained it is said so plainly. Dates are CE unless marked BCE. Miles come first, kilometers in parentheses.

A note on "first" claims and on knowing anything at all: as in every TL in this project, "first" means "first surviving evidence of." For the steppe this caveat is not boilerplate — it is the whole problem. Nomads built few archives and fewer libraries; almost everything here is seen through the eyes of the people they fought, traded with, or frightened. The Chinese dynastic histories watched the Uyghurs as a creditor watches a debtor; Muslim geographers wrote them up from Baghdad and Bukhara; a Persian official under the Mongols, Ata-Malik Juvayni (a thirteenth-century administrator-historian who had the Qara-Khitai and Mongol records in front of him), preserves much of the late story. Against all that outside testimony the Uyghurs left a thin, precious counter-voice: a few great inscribed stones in their own runiform Old Turkic, raised on the Orkhon, and later a flood of Buddhist manuscripts from the dry sands of Turfan. When this book says the Uyghurs "did" or "believed" something, it usually means a Chinese clerk or a Muslim traveler said they did, sometimes confirmed by a stone the Uyghurs cut themselves. Where the outside and inside voices disagree, or where there is only one of them, the text says so.

# Chapter 1 — The Khaganate That Replaced the Turks

The empire this book opens with was built by a people who took someone else's throne, someone else's capital region, and very nearly someone else's name — and then made all three into something the previous owners would not have recognized.

Start with the ground. The **Eurasian steppe** is a belt of open grassland that runs, almost unbroken, from the plains north of the Black Sea to the edge of Manchuria, several thousand miles of wind-combed grass with too little rain for farming and exactly enough for grazing. A people who could turn that grass into horses, sheep, and mounted archers could field armies that no settled kingdom could catch and few could beat. The catch was that grass is not silk, iron, grain, or books: a steppe power that wanted those things had to take them from, or trade them with, the farming civilizations on its rim, above all China. Every empire in this book is some answer to that single problem — how a grass economy gets the products of a grain economy — and the answers run from extortion to conquest to, eventually, simply moving into the oasis towns and becoming the farmers and merchants themselves.

Within that vast belt, one stretch of it was the steppe's throne room: the valley of the **Orkhon** (OR-khon) River, in the heart of what is now Mongolia, sheltered by a sacred wooded massif the Turkic peoples called the **Ötüken** (UH-tuh-ken). Whoever held the Orkhon was understood, by steppe people themselves, to hold the legitimate center of nomadic power, the way "Rome" meant more than a city. The Xiongnu had ruled from near here; so had the Türks (Xiongnu & Huns; Göktürk Khaganate). To take the Orkhon was to claim the steppe's equivalent of a crown, and that is precisely what the Uyghurs did in 744.

The people who held it before them were the **Türks** — specifically the Second Türk Khaganate, ruled by the **Ashina** clan, the dynasty that had given the world the very word *Turk* and the first empire to use it as a name for itself (Göktürk Khaganate). By the 730s the Second Türk Khaganate was the steppe's reigning power but a brittle one, its later qaghans weak, its subject tribes resentful and over-taxed in the only currency nomads have, which is loyalty. The largest block of those resentful subjects was a loose grouping of Turkic-speaking tribes the Chinese sources lump together as the **Tiele** (TYEH-luh) — kin to the ruling Türks in language and way of life, as a Dane is kin to a German, but politically a conquered underclass. Among the Tiele, one tribal confederation was rising: the **Uyghurs** (OO-ee-gur, also written Uighur), led by a clan called the Yaglakar.

It is worth being honest about how little of the Uyghurs' own early story survives in their own words, and how much in their enemies'. The Chinese knew them as a Tiele tribe that had supplied cavalry, sometimes to the Türks, sometimes against them, for over a century. The Uyghurs' own account, cut into stone a generation later, naturally tells it as destiny. The plain version both can agree on is this: around 742 three of the Türks' subject peoples — the Uyghurs, the **Karluks** (KAR-luk), and the **Basmyl** (BAHS-mil) — rose together, and the Second Türk Khaganate, already rotten, could not put them all down at once.

What followed is a small masterclass in steppe politics. The three rebel peoples won, and then immediately turned on each other to decide who would actually inherit the Orkhon. The Basmyl took the title first; within two years the Uyghurs and Karluks destroyed the Basmyl. The Uyghurs and Karluks then divided the spoils, the Karluks taking the western steppe (file that away — it is the seed of Chapter 6) and the Uyghurs taking the center. In 744 the Uyghur leader **Qutlugh Bilge Köl** (koot-LOOG BILL-geh KUL) proclaimed himself qaghan on the Orkhon, and in 745 his forces killed the last Ashina qaghan, **Özmiş** (UHZ-mish), and sent his head to the Tang court in China as both a trophy and a diplomatic announcement: the management has changed; deal with us now.

That head mattered more than it seems. By delivering the last Türk ruler's head to **Tang** China — the long, brilliant Chinese dynasty that ran from 618 to 907 and was, in the 740s, at the height of its wealth and confidence (Tang–Song China) — the new Uyghur qaghan was performing a careful piece of theater. He was telling the richest empire on earth that the Uyghurs were the Türks' legitimate successors, a stable address China could trade with and hire, not just another raiding nuisance. The relationship that opened with that severed head would, within fifteen years, make the Uyghurs the creditors of the Tang and is the whole subject of Chapter 2.

Qutlugh Bilge Köl died in 747, and the empire's real builder took over: his son, who reigned as **Bayanchur Khan** (BUY-an-chur), titled El Etmish Bilge Qaghan, "the state-organizing wise qaghan," and the title was earned. Over roughly the next dozen years Bayanchur turned a victorious tribal coalition into an actual state, and he did it with two instruments that tell you immediately this was not going to be an ordinary nomad empire: a city, and an alphabet's worth of writing.

The city was **Ordu-Baliq** (OR-doo BAH-lik), "the city of the court," raised about 200 miles (320 km) west of modern Ulaanbaatar on the Orkhon and known to later ages by its Mongol name, **Karabalgasun**, "the black ruins." A walled, gridded, permanent capital with a citadel, markets, and craftsmen's quarters was a genuinely strange thing for a steppe empire to build. The whole logic of nomad power was mobility: you could not besiege a people who had no fixed place to defend. By planting a capital, Bayanchur was trading away some of that invulnerability for something he wanted more — a fixed point where tribute could be collected, foreign merchants and ambassadors received, scribes housed, and the qaghan's wealth and prestige put permanently on display. It was a bet that the Uyghur empire would make its money less from raiding than from being a stable hub that taxed and protected trade, and that bet shaped everything that followed, including the religion the court would adopt (Chapter 3) and, ultimately, the speed with which the whole thing could be destroyed when an enemy finally did have something to burn (Chapter 4).

The writing is the other tell. The Türks before them had cut monumental inscriptions in a runiform Old Turkic script, and the Uyghurs continued the practice on a grand scale. Bayanchur left at least two great inscribed stelae — known today, from where they were found, as the **Shine Usu** and **Tariat** inscriptions — that narrate his campaigns and the founding of the khaganate in the Uyghurs' own language and their own voice. These stones are the thin inside-voice the introduction promised: almost everything else in this chapter is Chinese testimony, but here, for a few hundred lines, the Uyghurs speak for themselves, in the formulaic, boastful, surprisingly moving register of steppe royal memory ("I had walls built there; I had the eternal stone inscribed"). It is the difference between a credit report and a diary, and the historian needs both.

The state Bayanchur organized rested on a tribal skeleton the sources call the **Toquz Oghuz** (toh-KOOZ oh-GOOZ), "the Nine Tribes" — the nine-clan core of the confederation, with the royal Yaglakar at its head and a fixed order of precedence beneath. This is the answer to a question steppe history constantly raises and rarely answers cleanly: when a nomad "empire" appears overnight, what actually holds it together between campaigns? For the Uyghurs the honest answer, as far as the inscriptions and Chinese reports let us reconstruct it, is the Toquz Oghuz hierarchy plus the qaghan's ability to keep delivering two things — military success and a cut of the China trade. Remove either and the confederation could come apart as fast as it had come together, which is exactly what Chapter 4 records.

It is worth pausing, as this project does at every "first" or "only," to place the Uyghur khaganate in the world of its own moment, because the steppe is usually told as if it ran on a separate clock from everywhere else, and it did not. The year Qutlugh Bilge Köl took the Orkhon, 744, sits in an unusually crowded decade. Six years later, in 750, the **Abbasid Revolution** would overthrow the Umayyad caliphs and found the Baghdad-centered empire whose Persianized, Silk Road–wired world the western half of this book eventually joins (Islamic Golden Age). The next year, 751, an Abbasid army and a Tang army collided at the **Battle of Talas**, in the Karluk country west of the Uyghurs, and the Karluks' decision to change sides mid-battle helped hand Central Asia's future to Islam rather than China — the single clearest hinge in this whole book, and it happens offstage in Chapter 1 to people who will not matter until Chapter 6. In China, the Tang dynasty was four years from the catastrophe (Chapter 2) that would nearly destroy it and make the Uyghurs rich. In Japan, the court at Nara was casting the Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji. In Europe, the Frankish mayor Pepin the Short was about to depose the last Merovingian king and found the dynasty that produced his son Charlemagne. The Uyghur Khaganate was born into the same fifteen years as Abbasid Baghdad and Carolingian France, and it would outlast neither in fame, only in consequence.

By around 760, then, the picture was set. A Turkic people who were not the Türks held the Orkhon, the steppe's throne. They ruled from a walled city, kept written records of themselves, organized power through the Nine Tribes, and had announced themselves to China by couriering it the head of the last emperor they had replaced. Everything that made this empire unusual — that it was urban, literate, mercantile, and would soon be the only steppe empire in history to adopt a scriptural missionary religion of the settled world — is already visible in embryo in what Bayanchur built.

None of it yet explains how a grass empire got rich enough to pay for stone cities and a state church. That money did not come from the grass. It came from a disaster four years downriver, in the heart of Tang China, where a general named An Lushan was about to set the richest empire on earth on fire — and the Uyghurs were about to charge an extraordinary price to help put it out.

# Chapter 2 — Creditors of the Tang

For most of recorded history the relationship between the steppe and China ran one direction: the nomads wanted what China had, and China paid them — in silk, in princesses, in gold — to go away. The Uyghurs inverted it. For about a decade in the middle of the eighth century, China did not pay the steppe to stay away; China begged the steppe to come in, and then could not afford to make it leave. How that happened is one of the strangest creditor relationships in premodern history, and it begins with the near-death of the richest state on earth.

The state was **Tang** China, and in 755 it was arguably the wealthiest, most populous, most cosmopolitan empire in the world (Tang–Song China). Its capital, **Chang'an** (chahng-AHN, the modern city of Xi'an), held perhaps a million people, more than any city in Europe or the Islamic world, and its court traded with, and borrowed fashions from, Persia, India, and the steppe. The man who nearly destroyed it was one of its own border generals: **An Lushan** (ahn loo-SHAHN), a heavyset, half-Sogdian, half-Türk frontier commander who had risen high in the emperor's favor and controlled three of the empire's most powerful regional armies on the northeastern frontier. In December 755 he revolted, marched on the capital with a battle-hardened frontier army, and inside six months had taken both Tang capitals, Luoyang and then Chang'an. The emperor fled west; on the road his own guards mutinied and forced the strangling of his favorite consort, Yang Guifei, in one of the most retold scenes in Chinese literature. The dynasty did not fall, but it came close enough that it would spend the next eight years clawing its survival back, and it never fully recovered the central control it had before. The **An Lushan rebellion** (755–763) is, by some demographic estimates, among the most lethal events in human history; the census collapse it produced is so large that historians still argue over how much was death and how much was simply the state losing the ability to count its own people. That uncertainty is itself the point: this was a catastrophe deep enough to break the machinery that measured catastrophes.

A Tang court in that condition needed cavalry it did not have, and it needed it immediately. It looked north, to the new power on the Orkhon.

The Uyghurs were, from the Tang point of view, the ideal mercenary: close, mounted, recently advertised as the steppe's legitimate management (the severed-head announcement of Chapter 1 having done its work), and motivated by something more durable than pay, which was the chance to be indispensable to the richest customer on earth. In 756 the Tang formally requested Uyghur military aid. The qaghan **Bayanchur** (Chapter 1) sent his son with a force of elite horse-archers, and in 757 a combined Tang-Uyghur army retook the eastern capital, **Luoyang** (lwoh-YAHNG), and then Chang'an. The Uyghur cavalry was, by all accounts including resentful Chinese ones, decisive: disciplined, ferociously effective shock horsemen of exactly the kind the rebellion's own frontier troops were and the rump Tang armies were not.

It is worth being honest, in the project's usual way, about a piece of this that the sources state and modern readers may want softened: the price of Luoyang was the city itself. The standard arrangement, repeated in 757 and again in 762, was that the Uyghurs would be allowed to plunder the recaptured city as their reward. In 762, when a second Uyghur intervention under the new qaghan retook Luoyang from rebel remnants, the agreed sack was so severe that the negotiated compromise — paid by the citizens to buy a halt — still left tens of thousands dead and the eastern capital gutted. This is not a modern projection of cynicism onto a medieval alliance; the Tang histories record the terms plainly, with the bitterness of people writing down a humiliation they had agreed to. The Uyghur relationship with Tang was a rescue, and it was an extortion, and the Chinese sources never pretend it was only the first.

Out of the rescue came the instrument that actually made the Uyghurs rich, and it was not loot, which is one-time, but a structural arrangement, which is forever: the **horse-and-silk trade**. The Uyghurs drove herds of steppe horses to the Tang frontier and exchanged them for Chinese silk at a fixed rate the Uyghurs set and the Tang, militarily dependent and politically humiliated, could not refuse. The rate was extortionate and the volume enormous, tens of thousands of horses a year against hundreds of thousands of bolts of silk, and the quality ran the other way: the Tang complained that the horses were old and broken-winded while the silk was new. Silk in this period was not only cloth; bolts of standard silk functioned as a high-value currency across Inner Asia, compact, durable, universally accepted. What the Uyghurs had built, in effect, was a pipeline that converted the one thing the grasslands produce in surplus, horses, into the one portable store of wealth the entire Silk Road would honor. A grass economy had found a way to tax a grain economy on a permanent schedule, and it had done it not by raiding the farms but by becoming the farmer-empire's irreplaceable bodyguard and then pricing the protection.

The diplomacy was sealed, as steppe–China diplomacy usually was, with a marriage, but here too the direction was telling. In 758 the Tang sent **Princess Ningguo** (ning-GWOH), a genuine daughter of the reigning emperor, to be a wife of the Uyghur qaghan. This mattered because the Tang, like most settled dynasties, normally fobbed nomad allies off with adopted or minor "princesses"; sending a real imperial daughter to a steppe ruler was an admission, in the only protocol that mattered, that the Uyghurs were not a tributary to be managed but a power to be courted. (Ningguo's own story is grim and brief: when the qaghan died soon after, she was expected by Uyghur custom to be sacrificed or at least to mutilate herself in mourning, refused on the grounds of Tang custom, and was eventually allowed to return home, a rare instance where the sources let a woman traded between empires speak in her own voice and say no.)

To set this against the rest of the world, because the inversion is the chapter's whole point: in these same years, the **Abbasid Caliphate**, newly founded in 750, was building **Baghdad** (begun 762) into the hub of an Islamic world economy (Islamic Golden Age); the **Byzantine Empire** was fighting for survival against that same expanding Islam (Byzantine Empire); and in the Frankish west, Pepin the Short's son **Charlemagne** would be crowned in 800 atop a far poorer, far less urban political order than either Baghdad or Chang'an. The Uyghur Khaganate, a nomad state most world histories do not mention, was in this same window functioning as the armed creditor of the largest economy on the planet. That is not how the steppe is supposed to work, and the Uyghurs knew it; the wealth it brought is what paid for everything in the next chapter.

Because money, for a steppe court suddenly swimming in Chinese silk and dealing daily with the Sogdian middlemen who ran the trade, did not stay only money. It bought, among other things, a religion — and not a steppe religion, but a literate, scriptural, missionary faith carried up the same roads as the silk. That is the strangest thing the Uyghurs ever did, and it is the subject of Chapter 3.

# Chapter 3 — A Persian Religion on the Steppe

Every empire in this book is, at bottom, an answer to the problem of how a grass economy gets the products of a grain economy. The Uyghurs had just solved the material half of that problem by becoming Tang China's armed creditor (Chapter 2). This chapter is about the cultural half, and it produced the single most unusual decision in the entire history of the steppe: a nomad emperor adopted, as the state religion of a horse empire, a bookish dualist faith invented in third-century Persia.

To see why that is strange, you have to know what steppe religion normally was. The default spiritual world of the early Turkic peoples was a sky-centered system, usually called **Tengrism** after **Tengri** (TENG-ree), the sky god, alongside reverence for the earth-and-water spirits, sacred mountains like the Ötüken (Chapter 1), and ancestor and shaman practice. It had no scripture, no founder, no missionary clergy, and no church — it was the kind of religion a mobile, pre-literate society generates and carries in its head, not in a library. It asked nothing of a ruler except that he embody heaven's favor, which a victorious qaghan automatically did. A faith like that is almost the opposite of what the Uyghurs adopted, and the contrast is the point.

What they adopted was **Manichaeism** (MAN-ih-kee-ism). It needs a real introduction, because it is now an extinct religion and the reader has likely met it only as a word. It was founded by a prophet named **Mani** (MAH-nee), who lived in Sasanian Persia in the third century CE and taught a thoroughgoing dualism: the universe is a battlefield between a realm of Light (spirit, goodness) and a realm of Darkness (matter, evil), and the human task is to release the trapped particles of Light through strict asceticism. Mani consciously designed his religion to be portable and universal — he wrote and illustrated his own scriptures so the message could not be corrupted, and framed himself as the successor to Jesus, the Buddha, and Zoroaster so that the faith could be preached in Christian, Buddhist, or Zoroastrian idiom as needed. It was, in modern terms, built for export. By the eighth century it had been persecuted nearly out of Persia but survived along the Silk Road, carried and financed above all by **Sogdian** merchants — the Iranian-speaking trading people of Samarkand and Bukhara whose caravan networks were the commercial nervous system of Inner Asia, and who, as Chapter 1 noted, were already the Uyghur court's scribes and financiers.

The conversion has a date and a scene, both recorded on the Uyghurs' own stone (the Karabalgasun inscription, below) and in Chinese report. In 762–763 the qaghan **Bögü Khan** (BUH-goo, also spelled Bügü), leading the second great intervention in the An Lushan war, occupied Luoyang, and there — in the wreck of a Chinese city his cavalry was sacking — he encountered Sogdian Manichaean **Electi**, the religion's elite of "elect" ascetic clergy. He took some of them back to the steppe, and on their counsel proclaimed Manichaeism the religion of the Uyghur state. Read that sequence slowly, because it is genuinely odd: a steppe conqueror, at the high-water mark of a war that had made his people the creditors of China, chose that moment to take on a foreign, demanding, ascetic, vegetarian, pacifist-leaning religion whose clergy forbade the killing of animals to a people whose entire economy was animals.

Why he did it is the kind of question this project flags rather than pretends to settle. The honest answer is that the sources give us motives and we have to weigh them, not that we know. The traditional explanation, and the one the Uyghur inscription itself promotes, is sincere royal conviction. Modern historians generally add structural reasons that do not require reading Bögü's heart: Manichaeism came bundled with the Sogdians, and binding the court to the Sogdians' religion bound it more tightly to the Sogdians' trade and credit networks, which were the source of the wealth Chapter 2 described; a scriptural state religion gave a sprawling tribal confederation a unifying ideology and a literate administrative class loyal to the throne rather than to the clans; and adopting a religion that was neither Chinese Buddhism nor Tang state Confucianism was a way of being China's creditor without becoming China's cultural client. These are not mutually exclusive, and the surviving evidence does not let us rank them with confidence. What is not in doubt is the result: for roughly the next eighty years the Uyghur Khaganate was the only steppe empire in history to run a foreign scriptural church as an organ of state.

The consequences ran deeper than belief, and they are why this chapter matters to the whole book. A scriptural religion needs readers, so the Manichaean state accelerated something already begun: the Uyghurs adapted the **Sogdian alphabet** into their own script, the **Old Uyghur alphabet**, a cursive, vertically written system suited to ink and paper rather than the chisel-cut runes of the older Turkic inscriptions. This is one of the most consequential alphabets in Eurasian history, and almost no one has heard of it: it is the direct ancestor of the **Mongol script** that Genghis Khan's empire would adopt wholesale (Chapter 8; Mongol Empire), and through Mongol, of the Manchu script of Qing China (Qing Dynasty). The administrative literacy the Uyghurs built to serve a Persian church became, four centuries later, the writing system of the largest land empire in history. That is the kind of consequence the steppe is never given credit for, and this book exists partly to give it.

The state church also rebuilt the capital. **Ordu-Baliq** (Chapter 1) acquired Manichaean monasteries and a resident clergy, and the religion's characteristic art — jewel-toned miniatures of the elect in white robes, among the most beautiful manuscript painting of the early medieval world — was produced there and, later and in far greater quantity, in the oasis cities of Chapter 5. The single greatest monument of the Manichaean state was a stone: the **Karabalgasun inscription**, a great trilingual stele raised at the capital around 808, which narrated the dynasty's history and the conversion in parallel **Old Turkic, Sogdian, and Chinese**. A trilingual royal monument is itself an argument. It says, in three scripts aimed at three audiences, that this was a state that intended to be read — by its own elite, by its Sogdian financiers, and by China — and that understood writing as an instrument of rule. No purely nomadic power had ever made that statement so deliberately.

It is worth placing the oddity in world terms, the way this project does at every "only." In the same century that the Uyghurs made Manichaeism a state religion, the Abbasid caliphs were making a particular strand of Islam the ideology of Baghdad (Islamic Golden Age); the Tang court was oscillating between sponsoring and persecuting Buddhism; the Byzantine emperors were tearing their own church apart over icons (Byzantine Empire); and Charlemagne was about to make Latin Christianity the binding cult of a Frankish empire (Early Medieval Europe). State-building through a chosen scriptural religion was, in the eighth and ninth centuries, something settled empires across Eurasia were all doing at once. The Uyghurs were doing it too — which is precisely the point, because they were not a settled empire, and no other steppe power joined them in it. The Türks before them and the Mongols after them ran on Tengri and the sky; only the Uyghurs, in this one window, ran on a book.

That made them, culturally, the most settled-looking nomad empire the steppe had yet produced: urban, literate, scriptural, mercantile, tied by religion and credit to the Sogdian networks and by arms and trade to China. It was a remarkable thing to have built in under a century. It was also, though no one on the Orkhon could have known it, dangerously narrow. An empire that lived off a single trade relationship, defended a fixed capital, and depended on a foreign merchant diaspora and a foreign church had traded the nomad's classic invulnerability for a settled state's classic fragilities — and it had no deep agrarian base of its own to fall back on if the trade, the herds, or the weather failed. In the 830s all three failed at once. Chapter 4 is the year it took to destroy everything this chapter describes.

# Chapter 4 — Destroyed in a Generation

Steppe empires usually die slowly, the way they are built — a confederation frays, tribes drift to a rising rival, a bad qaghan loses the mandate over a decade. The Uyghur Khaganate did not die that way. It died the way a settled state dies when its one city is taken: suddenly, in a single campaigning season, with a sacked capital and a scattered people. That difference is the whole argument of this chapter, because the speed of the Uyghur collapse is the bill coming due for the choices of the three before it.

The structural weakness was visible for a generation before it killed them. The Uyghur state, as Chapter 1 described, rested on the Toquz Oghuz hierarchy plus the qaghan's ability to keep delivering military success and a share of the China trade. From the 820s the throne stopped being able to guarantee either. The dynasty entered a familiar steppe spiral of contested successions, but with a settled state's added brittleness: a series of qaghans were enthroned and then murdered by their own ministers and generals, several within a year or two of taking power. Each killing burned legitimacy that a tribal confederation cannot easily regrow, because on the steppe authority is a thing the clans lend to a winner, not a thing an institution stores. By the late 830s the Uyghur center was a court that could no longer reliably command its own armies.

Then the weather cashed the bet. The sources — Chinese, since the Uyghur stones fall silent here, which is itself a kind of evidence — describe a catastrophic winter at the end of the 830s: extreme cold and snow (a *jut*, the steppe's specific terror, when ice seals the grass and the herds starve standing in it), followed by epidemic disease among the surviving animals and famine among the people. A true nomadic power can sometimes absorb a *jut* by dispersing and migrating; an empire pinned to a fixed capital, a single trade artery, and a population it had concentrated to serve both could not. The herds were the army's mobility, its food, and its currency all at once. When they died, all three died together.

The killing blow came, as it often does in steppe collapses, from inside. A disaffected Uyghur general, Külüg Bagha, having lost out in the court's murders, defected and guided the **Yenisei Kyrgyz** — a Turkic people of the Siberian forest-steppe to the north, longstanding Uyghur subjects and enemies — down onto the weakened heartland. In **840** a Kyrgyz army stormed **Ordu-Baliq** (Chapter 1), killed the reigning qaghan, burned the capital, and ended the Uyghur Khaganate. Not weakened it — ended it. The walled city that Bayanchur had built to display the dynasty's permanence was exactly the thing that made the dynasty mortal: it gave the Kyrgyz an address, a single point whose burning was the death of the state. The Türks before the Uyghurs had no capital to lose; the Mongols after them would conquer from saddles, not cities. The Uyghurs, uniquely, had built something that could be destroyed in an afternoon, and in 840 it was.

What followed split the Uyghur people into the three streams that the rest of this book follows. Most of the surviving tribes broke into three great migrations. One moved south toward the Tang frontier, hoping the old creditor relationship (Chapter 2) would shelter them; instead the Tang, no longer needing Uyghur cavalry and not sorry to be free of the debt, attacked and broke them at the Battle of Shahu in 843, ending any dream of a restoration on the old terms. A second stream moved west into the **Gansu corridor**, the strategic neck of oases between China and the Tarim, and there founded a successor state (Chapter 5). A third moved southwest into the **Tarim Basin** oasis towns and founded the longest-lived Uyghur state of all (Chapter 5). The empire was gone; the people, and crucially their literacy and their commercial habits, were not.

There was a religious aftershock with its own grim symmetry. The Manichaean church in China (Chapter 3) had survived only under Uyghur protection — Tang tolerated it because the Uyghurs demanded it. With the Uyghurs destroyed in 840, that protection vanished, and in 842–843 the Tang moved against the Manichaeans: temples seized, clergy defrocked or killed, property confiscated. This was the leading edge of the far larger **Huichang persecution** of 845, in which the Tang state, for fiscal and nativist reasons, turned on Buddhism and all foreign religions at once, secularizing hundreds of thousands of monks. The only steppe empire ever to sponsor a foreign scriptural church had, by dying, also doomed that church in the country where it had found it.

It is worth setting 840 in the world, because the synchronies are striking and this project insists on them. The same decade that destroyed the Uyghur Khaganate and shattered Chinese Manichaeism saw the **Treaty of Verdun** (843) partition Charlemagne's empire into the three pieces that became France, Germany, and the contested middle (Early Medieval Europe); Viking fleets moving from raiding to settling, with the Danelaw taking shape in England (Viking Age); and the Abbasid Caliphate sliding into the soldier-dominated crisis of Samarra that began its long fragmentation (Islamic Golden Age). The 840s were a decade in which several of the early medieval world's load-bearing structures cracked at once. The steppe's literate empire was one of them, and it is the one almost no general history records.

The last point is the one that matters most for what comes next. The Kyrgyz had destroyed the Uyghurs, but they did not replace them. They looted the Orkhon and withdrew north to their Siberian forests rather than rule the open steppe from it. For the first time in roughly three centuries — since the rise of the first Türk khaganate — no empire ruled from the Orkhon, the steppe's throne (Chapter 1). The center of nomadic power did not pass to a successor; it simply went dark, and it stayed dark for **366 years**, until a man named Temüjin was proclaimed Genghis Khan on a nearby river in 1206 (Chapter 8; Mongol Empire). The steppe's most consequential real estate lay vacant for longer than the entire history of the Roman Empire as an empire. The Uyghurs did not hand the steppe to anyone. They emptied it.

But emptiness on the grass is not emptiness everywhere. The Uyghurs who went west and southwest did not vanish; they did something no destroyed nomad empire had done before. They walked into the oasis cities and became the farmers, scribes, and merchants whose absence had been their fatal weakness. Chapter 5 is the strangest second act in steppe history.

# Chapter 5 — From Khaganate to Oasis

The usual fate of a shattered steppe people is absorption: the survivors are taken in by whoever beat them, intermarry, and within a generation or two are simply part of someone else's confederation, their name surviving, if at all, as a clan label. The Uyghurs did not do that. The streams that fled west and southwest in 840 (Chapter 4) did something close to unique in nomadic history: they kept their identity, their script, and their elite culture, and they did it by deliberately ceasing to be nomads. This chapter is about a people solving the problem that killed them by switching sides in the oldest divide in this book — the one between the grass and the grain — and joining the grain.

Two successor states matter. The western stream, in the **Gansu corridor**, founded the **Ganzhou Uyghur Kingdom**, controlling a vital pinch-point of the Silk Road between China proper and the Tarim and living, as the Uyghurs always had, off the trade that passed through. The far more important one was the southwestern stream's: around 866 a Uyghur leader took the Turfan oases and the towns of the northern Tarim rim and founded the **Kingdom of Qocho** (CHWAW, also written Kocho or Gaochang), centered on Turfan and Beshbalik. Qocho is the heart of this chapter. It lasted, in one form or another, for roughly five centuries — far longer than the khaganate that spawned it — and it is the clearest case in the whole book of a nomad people completing the full transformation into a settled civilization without losing the thread of who they were.

The first thing Qocho did was change religion, and the project's rule is to give the mechanism, not just the outcome. Chapter 3's Manichaean state did not survive the move into the oases; over the late ninth and tenth centuries the Qocho Uyghurs shifted to **Buddhism**, with substantial **Nestorian Christian** (the Church of the East, the Syriac-rite Christianity that had spread across Asia along the trade routes) and residual Manichaean minorities. The shift was not a decree but a settling-in. The Tarim oasis towns the Uyghurs moved into were already deeply Buddhist, with centuries of monasteries, Sanskrit and Tocharian scripture, and cave-temple patronage; an immigrant ruling class that wanted to govern, tax, and intermarry with a Buddhist agrarian population, and that no longer had the Sogdian-Manichaean trade-and-credit reason (Chapter 3) to stay Manichaean, drifted toward the religion of the people and the land it now lived on. Royal patronage followed popular practice rather than leading it. Within a few generations the descendants of Bögü Khan's Manichaean court were Buddhist kings endowing monasteries.

That patronage produced one of the glories of Central Asian art: the **Bezeklik Thousand Buddha Caves**, a complex of cave temples in a gorge near Turfan whose walls the Qocho Uyghurs filled with murals — donor portraits of Uyghur nobles in their distinctive court dress, ranks of Buddhas, and a fusion of Indian, Chinese, Tocharian, and Iranian styles into something recognizably Uyghur. (Much of it was cut from the walls and carried to Berlin by a German expedition in the early twentieth century, and a great deal of that was then destroyed by Allied bombing in the Second World War — a reminder, in this project's recurring key, that the survival of evidence is shaped by later fires the people who made it could not foresee.) The same literate machinery built for a Persian church in Chapter 3 was repurposed: the **Old Uyghur alphabet** became the vehicle for a vast Buddhist translation literature, scripture rendered into Uyghur from Tocharian, Sanskrit, and Chinese, alongside Manichaean and Christian texts. The dry sands of Turfan preserved this in enormous quantity, which is why the inside-voice problem the introduction described eases here: for the oasis Uyghurs, for the first time in this book, we have shelves of their own writing rather than their enemies' reports.

The deepest change was the one the khaganate had never managed: the Uyghurs became a farming people. The Turfan depression is one of the hottest, lowest, driest places in Asia, and the oasis Uyghurs made it bloom by maintaining and extending the **karez** (kah-REZ), a system of gently sloping underground tunnels that carries snowmelt from the mountains to the fields with almost no evaporation loss — the same engineering family as the Persian qanat, adapted to one of the harshest agrarian environments on earth. A people whose fatal weakness in Chapter 4 had been the absence of any agrarian base to fall back on had, within two generations, built one of the most ingenious irrigation economies in Asia. They also kept the commercial instincts that had always defined them: Qocho grew rich as a Silk Road entrepôt, its merchants running caravan finance and the kind of capital-pooling partnerships (the *ortoq*) that a later empire would adopt wholesale. A Song Chinese envoy, **Wang Yande**, who visited Qocho in 981–984 and wrote up what he saw, describes irrigated fields, libraries, dozens of Buddhist monasteries, and a literate court — an eyewitness portrait of a people that had been a horse-archer nomad empire within living memory and was now, visibly, something else entirely.

Set against the world, the oasis Uyghurs flourished in the same window as the high Abbasid economy and the Song commercial revolution (Islamic Golden Age; Tang–Song China), and they outlasted both as a continuous polity. But the payoff of this chapter is not the flourishing; it is the inheritance. The Qocho Uyghurs had become exactly the thing a conquering nomad empire most needs and least possesses: a reservoir of literate administrators, tax officials, and scribes who understood both the steppe and the settled world, written in a script built for the saddle and the chancery alike. In the early thirteenth century, when the steppe finally produced a unifier again, the Uyghur idiqut (the title Qocho's kings now bore) would look at the rising Mongols and make the decision that the Uyghurs, uniquely among the peoples in Genghis Khan's path, made voluntarily and early: he submitted without being conquered, and his people became the Mongol Empire's clerks, tutors, and administrative model (Chapter 8; Mongol Empire). The empire that died on the Orkhon in 840 had, by becoming a civilization instead of a confederation, made itself indispensable to the empire that would inherit the world.

That is the eastern, Buddhist, settled afterlife of the Uyghur world. There was another afterlife, to the west, that went in the opposite direction — toward Islam, toward conquest, and toward a Turkic literature of its own. The Karluks who took the western steppe in Chapter 1 are about to become the first Turkic Muslims, and that is Chapter 6.

# Chapter 6 — The First Turkic Muslims

This book has so far followed the Uyghurs east, into the Buddhist oasis kingdoms (Chapter 5). Now it doubles back to a thread planted and deliberately left hanging in Chapter 1: the **Karluks**, the third partner in the 742 revolt, who took the *western* steppe — the Ili and Chu river valleys, the country around Lake Balkhash and the Tian Shan — while the Uyghurs took the center. For a century the Karluks are a name in the margin of this story. In the tenth century they, and the confederation they led, do something that reshapes a third of the world's religious map: they become the first Turkic people to adopt Islam as a state, and then the first to write Islam in Turkish.

The confederation is called the **Kara-Khanid Khanate** (KAH-rah-KHAH-nid), formed out of the Karluks together with two other Turkic peoples, the Yagma and the Chigil, sometime in the ninth and tenth centuries around the twin capitals of **Kashgar** (KASH-gar, on the western rim of the Tarim) and **Balasagun** (bah-lah-sah-GOON, in the Chu valley).

Before Islam, the Kara-Khanids were what the eastern Uyghurs had been before the move to the oases (Chapter 5): a Turkic-speaking, pastoral, Tengri-worshipping steppe power, the Karluk inheritance of the western grasslands the three rebel peoples had divided in 744 (Chapter 1). What made them pivotal was their address. They sat directly on the northeastern frontier of the **Samanids**, the brilliant Persian Islamic dynasty then ruling Transoxiana and Khurasan from Bukhara, whose cities, scholars, and Sufi preachers the Kara-Khanid Turks raided, traded with, and increasingly lived alongside. They were the last great pagan Turkic power on the doorstep of the settled Islamic world, holding the oasis approaches of Kashgar and the Chu valley, and that position is the entire setup for what one of their princes did next. Their early history is otherwise thin and contested: the honest position is that we see the Kara-Khanids clearly only once Muslim sources start paying attention, which is precisely the moment that matters, the conversion.

That moment has a name, **Satuq Bughra Khan** (SAH-took BOO-grah), and the standard story, which the sources tell in pious hindsight and which we should therefore handle carefully, runs like this. Satuq was a Kara-Khanid prince exposed to Islam through the Samanid frontier and its Sufi missionaries and Muslim merchants — the Samanids being the Persian dynasty then ruling Transoxiana and Khurasan, the sophisticated Islamic state on the Kara-Khanids' southwestern border. Satuq converted, around 934, seized power from his pagan uncle in a war framed by later tradition as a holy struggle, and made Islam the religion of his line. How much of that is event and how much is hagiography is genuinely uncertain — Satuq became a saint, his tomb at Artush a pilgrimage site, and saints' lives are not minutes of meetings. What is not uncertain is the demographic consequence, because it shows up independently in Muslim chroniclers: in the mid-tenth century, a generation or so after Satuq, a mass conversion of the Turks of the region is recorded, the sources giving the deliberately huge round figure of **"200,000 tents"** accepting Islam. The number is a literary gesture, not a census; the scale it gestures at is real.

It is worth being precise about the "first" claim, the way this project always is. Individual Turks had been Muslim for two centuries before Satuq — as slave soldiers (the *ghilman* and later *mamluks*) inside the Abbasid and Samanid armies, as merchants, as captives. The Kara-Khanids were not the first Turkic Muslims as persons. They were the first Turkic Muslim *state*: the first time a Turkic dynasty ruling Turkic people on the steppe made Islam its own religion rather than encountering it as someone else's. That distinction is the entire significance. Conversion-by-enslavement spreads a religion to individuals; conversion-of-a-dynasty spreads it to a people and a territory and makes it heritable. After the Kara-Khanids, the default trajectory of Central Asian Turkic peoples is Muslim — which is why the Turks the Crusaders would fight, the Turks who would take Anatolia and India and run the Ottoman and Mughal worlds (Ottoman Empire; Mughal Empire), are downstream of a tenth-century prince at Kashgar and not of anything in Baghdad or Damascus.

The political payoff came fast. In **999** the Kara-Khanids took **Bukhara** and extinguished the Samanid dynasty, dividing the Persian Samanids' realm with the Ghaznavids (the Turkic-led, Persianate empire rising to the south) and bringing **Transoxiana** — Samarkand, Bukhara, the richest urban-agrarian heartland of Central Asia — under Turkic Muslim rule for the first time in history. The internal structure of their state was peculiar and worth one sentence because it explains the rest of the book: the Kara-Khanids ruled through a layered, deliberately divided system of senior and junior khans over an eastern and a western realm, a constitution that distributed legitimacy widely and centralized power poorly, which made the khanate culturally durable and politically brittle — easy for a later conqueror to vassalize piece by piece (Chapter 7).

The cultural payoff is the part that outlived the politics, and it is the mirror image of Chapter 5. The Buddhist Uyghurs of Qocho turned the Old Uyghur script into a vehicle for a Buddhist canon; the Muslim Kara-Khanids turned the Turkic language into a vehicle for Islamic high literature. Two works, both produced in the Kara-Khanid world within five years around 1070, did this. The first is the **Kutadgu Bilig** (koo-TAD-goo bee-LEEG, "the wisdom that brings happiness"), composed by **Yusuf Khass Hajib** of Balasagun around 1069: a long didactic poem, a mirror-for-princes on just rule, in Turkish but in Islamic-Persian literary form — the first major literary work of Islamic Turkic civilization, the moment a steppe language sat down at the table of scriptural high culture as an author rather than a subject. The second is even more remarkable as a source: the **Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk** (dee-WAHN loo-GHAT at-TURK, "compendium of the languages of the Turks"), compiled by **Mahmud al-Kashgari** in 1072–1074 — a vast Turkic-Arabic dictionary and ethnography, written in Arabic to explain the Turks to the Arab Islamic world, and containing the earliest known map drawn from a Turkic point of view, with the Kara-Khanid heartland at its center. One book argues that Turks can write Islam; the other argues that the Islamic world needs to understand Turks. Both are correct, and both are early symptoms of a power shift that the next several centuries of Eurasian history will be largely about.

By 1089 the western Kara-Khanids had been forced into vassalage by the Great **Seljuks**, the Turkic dynasty that now dominated the Islamic east from Baghdad to the Oxus — a reminder that the Kara-Khanids were the *first* Turkic Muslim dynasty but very far from the last, and were already, within a century of Satuq, being absorbed into the larger Turkic-Islamic order they had helped inaugurate. Set in the world, this is the same century in which the Seljuks took Baghdad (1055) and broke the Byzantines at Manzikert (1071), and in which the Latin West, alarmed by exactly that Turkic surge, launched the First Crusade (1095) (Byzantine Empire). The Turkic-Muslim power the Crusaders sailed east to fight had one of its taproots here, in a confederation most histories of the Crusades never name.

Two opposite afterlives, then, from one Uyghur-Turkic steppe world: a Buddhist, literate, agrarian east at Qocho (Chapter 5), and a Muslim, literate, conquering west under the Kara-Khanids. In the twelfth century both were swallowed by the same improbable third party — not a Muslim power and not a steppe pagan one, but a Buddhist Chinese-style dynasty in flight from the far side of Asia. Chapter 7 is the strangest regime in this entire book.

# Chapter 7 — The Buddhists Who Ruled the Muslims

The premise of this chapter sounds invented. A Buddhist dynasty, speaking a Mongolic language, carrying the institutions and reign-titles of a fallen Chinese empire, flees two thousand miles across Asia, conquers Muslim Central Asia, inflicts on the Islamic world the worst battlefield defeat it had suffered in the east, rules Samarkand and Bukhara and Kashgar for most of a century with deliberate religious tolerance and light taxation, is remembered by its Muslim subjects as a golden age, and reaches Crusader Europe as a rumor that helps create the legend of Prester John. Every clause of that is attested. The dynasty is the **Qara-Khitai** (KAH-rah kee-TY), the Western Liao, and it is the most improbable state the steppe ever produced.

It begins with a fall the reader can place from another TL. The **Liao dynasty** was the empire of the **Khitan**, a Mongolic horse-people who had ruled an empire across Mongolia and north China since the tenth century — the same Khitan whose Liao state appears, from the Korean side, as the invader in the Goryeo TL (Goryeo Korea Ch 3). In **1125** the Liao were destroyed by the **Jurchen**, a Manchurian forest people who had been Liao subjects and who founded the Jin dynasty in their place (the same Jurchen Jin that would later drive the Song court south). Out of that wreck, a Liao imperial prince named **Yelü Dashi** (yeh-LOO DAH-shr) led a few thousand followers west.

Yelü Dashi is one of the more arresting figures in this book, and worth a sentence of introduction in the project's manner. He was not a generic steppe warlord: he held the **jinshi**, the highest Chinese civil-service examination degree, the credential of a Confucian scholar-official, and he carried west not just a war-band but the idea of a Chinese-style centralized state. Over roughly the next decade he gathered Khitan and Turkic tribes, absorbed the old Uyghur and Kara-Khanid lands, and around **1132** proclaimed himself **Gurkhan** ("khan of khans"), founding the Qara-Khitai. Then, in **1141**, at the **Battle of Qatwan** near Samarkand, he met and annihilated the army of **Ahmad Sanjar**, the Great Seljuk sultan and the most powerful Muslim ruler in the east. Qatwan was not a skirmish; it was the largest defeat inflicted on a Muslim army in Central Asia to that point, and it made the Qara-Khitai the overlords of Transoxiana — a Buddhist dynasty suzerain over the Muslim heartland the Kara-Khanids had won in Chapter 6.

What they did with that position is the chapter's real subject, because it inverts every expectation the rest of this book has set up. The Qara-Khitai did not convert their subjects, did not impose their religion, did not even garrison the great cities heavily. They ruled by **indirect rule and light, regular taxation**: the Kara-Khanid khans, the Khwarazm-Shahs, the Qocho idiqut, and the city oligarchies were left in place as tribute-paying vassals, supervised by resident commissioners, taxed at a fixed and famously moderate rate, and otherwise left to their religion and their law. The Gurkhans kept Liao reign-titles and a Chinese-style salaried bureaucracy rather than parcel the empire into nomadic appanages. The result, by the testimony of the Muslim sources themselves — and here the introduction's caution about Juvayni matters, because our fullest account is a Persian Muslim official writing under the Mongols about a vanished Buddhist regime — was an era the oasis cities remembered as stable, prosperous, and just. A Buddhist empire of nomadic origin running a Muslim civilization by deliberately not interfering with it is not the way this story is supposed to go, and the sources' faint surprise is part of the evidence.

There is a famous side-effect. Distorted news of Qatwan — a great non-Muslim king somewhere east of Persia who had crushed a vast Muslim army — travelled west along the trade and pilgrim routes and arrived in the Crusader Levant and Europe as the report of a powerful Christian monarch beyond Islam who might be enlisted against it. This is one of the documented roots of the legend of **Prester John**, the imaginary Christian priest-king of the East who would haunt European geography and diplomacy for the next four centuries. The Gurkhan was not Christian (his dynasty was Buddhist, with Nestorian Christians among its subjects and officials), and he was not coming to help the Crusaders. But the Eurasian information system of the twelfth century could only parse "enemy of our enemy, in the East" as one thing, and the Qara-Khitai are the most likely seed of the most durable geographic myth of the Middle Ages.

Place it in the world and the symmetry is sharp. In the same decades that the Qara-Khitai ruled Central Asia by tolerant indirect rule, the Second Crusade was failing in the Levant, the Almohads were imposing rigorous orthodoxy in Spain and the Maghreb (Al-Andalus), and Song China was running its commercial revolution behind the Jin frontier (Tang–Song China). The Qara-Khitai sat, geographically and ideologically, in the hinge of all of it: a Chinese-institutioned, Buddhist, religiously laissez-faire empire holding the center of the Silk Road, invisible to the Crusaders fighting its distorted shadow.

It could not last, and the reason is the structural one this book keeps returning to. The Qara-Khitai ruled by a light touch over proud, intact vassals; that works only while the center can still win. By the 1190s the system was overstretched, the Khwarazm-Shahs in the southwest were strong enough to stop paying, and the empire's tolerant equilibrium was one hard shock from coming apart. The shock arrived from the east, in the person of a defeated prince fleeing a man the next several TLs in this project are about. Chapter 8 is the eve of the Mongols.

# Chapter 8 — The Eve of the Mongols

Every TL in this project ends by handing the reader to whatever comes next, but this one ends by handing the reader to the single most consequential thing that comes next for almost everyone: the Mongol Empire. The half-millennium this book has followed — Uyghur, oasis, Kara-Khanid, Qara-Khitai — does not simply get conquered by the Mongols. It gets *absorbed into the machinery* of the Mongol conquest, to the point that the empire which broke the medieval world ran, administratively, on tools this book has spent seven chapters describing. This chapter is how the forgotten half-millennium ends, and why it was never as forgotten as it looks: it is wearing the next empire's clothes.

The end has three moving parts, and they converge between 1204 and 1218.

The first is the rise of **Temüjin**. Far to the east, on the Mongolian steppe the Uyghurs had abandoned in 840 and no one had ruled since (Chapter 4), a Mongol war-leader spent two decades destroying his rivals. In **1204** he shattered the **Naiman**, a powerful (and partly Nestorian Christian, Turkic-influenced) confederation, and in **1206** an assembly on the Onon River proclaimed him **Genghis Khan**. State the synchrony plainly, because it is the spine of the chapter: the **Orkhon, the steppe's throne, had stood empty for 366 years** — since the Kyrgyz sacked Ordu-Baliq and went home (Chapter 4) — and in 1206 it had an emperor again. The interregnum this entire book occupies is the gap between the last Orkhon empire, the Uyghurs, and the next one, the Mongols. Chapter 1 opened on the people who took the Türks' throne; Chapter 8 closes on the people who finally took the Uyghurs' empty one.

The second part is the Uyghur decision, and it is the payoff of Chapter 5. When the Mongol storm reached the Tarim, the Buddhist Uyghur **idiqut** of Qocho, **Barchuq**, did what almost no one else in Genghis Khan's path did: he submitted *voluntarily and early*, in 1209, killing the Qara-Khitai resident who oversaw him and offering himself to the Mongols before he was attacked. He was rewarded accordingly — given a Mongol princess and the rank of a "fifth son" of Genghis Khan, his kingdom spared the annihilation visited on those who resisted. This was not cowardice; it was the same merchant-administrator's calculation the Uyghurs had been making since they couriered a severed head to the Tang in Chapter 1. A literate, mercantile, administratively skilled people with no illusions about cavalry it could not match chose, again, to be indispensable rather than destroyed.

The third part is Küchlüg, and it is the dark mirror of the second. **Küchlüg** (KOOCH-loog), a prince of the shattered Naiman, fled west from Genghis Khan exactly as Yelü Dashi had fled the Jurchen a century earlier (Chapter 7) — but where Yelü Dashi built, Küchlüg wrecked. He reached the failing Qara-Khitai, married into the Gurkhan's house, and around **1211** usurped the throne from within, ending the Yelü line by parasitism rather than conquest. Then he made the one mistake the Qara-Khitai had spent a century not making: a convert to Buddhism, he abandoned the dynasty's defining religious tolerance (Chapter 7) and persecuted the Muslim majority of the Tarim — closing mosques, forcing conversions, executing a famous imam of Khotan. In doing so he converted the Qara-Khitai's greatest asset, the loyalty of its taxed-lightly, left-alone subjects, into its fatal liability, almost on the eve of the Mongol arrival.

The Mongols, when they came, understood the asset Küchlüg had wasted better than he had. In **1218** Genghis Khan's general **Jebe** entered the Qara-Khitai realm with a small army and a single brilliant instrument of policy: he proclaimed **religious freedom** — the restoration of exactly the tolerance Küchlüg had destroyed. The Muslim population that Küchlüg had alienated rose to the Mongols' side; Jebe took the empire with little fighting, hunted Küchlüg into the Pamirs, and had him killed, his head carried back through the cities he had oppressed. The Qara-Khitai, the improbable Buddhist suzerain of Muslim Central Asia, ended not in a great battle but as a population changing sides to the people offering the old deal. With it, the last independent structure of the world this book has described was gone, folded into the Mongol Empire (Mongol Empire).

What flowed the other way — from this world *into* the Mongol Empire — is the reason the book exists, and it should be stated without hedging because it is the through-line of all eight chapters. The Mongols were the supreme cavalry of history and almost wholly unlettered when they began; an empire from the Pacific to the Black Sea cannot be run from the saddle. They built it, instead, on borrowed Uyghur infrastructure. The script the Mongols adopted for their own language was the **Old Uyghur alphabet** (Chapter 3), the writing system a Persian church had midwifed on the Orkhon four and a half centuries earlier; it remained Mongolian's script into the twentieth century and became, downstream, the Manchu script of Qing China (Qing Dynasty). When Genghis Khan captured the Naiman, he took their **Uyghur scribe Tatatonga**, who had kept the Naiman seal, and set him to teach the Mongol princes writing and the use of the state seal — a single captured clerk as the literal hinge between an illiterate conquest and a literate empire. The Qocho Uyghurs (Chapter 5) became the Mongol Empire's tutors, tax administrators, interpreters, and chancery clerks; the *ortoq* merchant partnerships the oasis Uyghurs ran (Chapter 5) became the empire-wide Mongol *ortogh* trade system; the model of taxing settled subjects through registered scribes rather than simply looting them — the thing the Uyghurs had been doing since Chapter 2 and the Qara-Khitai had perfected in Chapter 7 — became Mongol practice. The Mongols supplied the cavalry and the terror. The world this book describes supplied the alphabet, the clerks, the accountants, and the operating manual.

So the half-millennium between the Göktürks and Genghis Khan is not a gap. It is the period in which the Turkic world became literate, urban, mercantile, and — in its western half — Muslim; in which the steppe learned to run a state on paper; and in which the administrative and graphic technology of the largest land empire in history was invented, refined, and then handed forward, by a people who had learned the hard way (Chapter 4) that a civilization which makes itself indispensable outlives the empire that owned it. The Uyghurs lost their empire in a single year in 840. They are still being read, in a sense, every time a name is written in the Mongol script their Persian church created. The story continues, from the conquerors' side, in the Mongol Empire — but the pen they conquered with was cut on the Orkhon.
