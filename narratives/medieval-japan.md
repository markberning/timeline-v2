# Medieval Japan — Four Hundred Years of the Sword

**Goal:** Narrative for medieval Japan from 1185 to 1603 — the age of the samurai, the four centuries in which Japan ran a strange and durable experiment: it kept its emperor, sacred and untouchable in Kyoto, and handed the actual business of ruling to a warrior caste and its general, the shogun. The story opens with Minamoto no Yoritomo, who won a civil war, refused to take the throne, and instead invented a second government — a military regime called the shogunate, or *bakufu* ("tent government") — run from the eastern town of Kamakura while the emperor reigned, powerless, in the west. From there the story moves through the only invasion mainland Asia ever launched at Japan: Kublai Khan's two vast Mongol fleets, broken on the beaches of Hakata Bay and by the typhoons the Japanese remembered as *kamikaze*, the divine wind. It passes through an emperor's doomed attempt to take his power back (the Kemmu Restoration), a second warrior dynasty (the Ashikaga, ruling from Kyoto's Muromachi district), and a cultural golden age that gave the world the things it now pictures when it pictures old Japan — Zen monasteries, ink-wash landscapes, the tea ceremony, Noh theater, dry gravel gardens, the Golden Pavilion. Then it all comes apart. A ten-year war over who would be the next shogun burns Kyoto to the ground, and the country shatters into the Sengoku — the Warring States century, when *gekokujō*, "the low overthrowing the high," became the rule, and dozens of self-made warlords (*daimyō*) carved Japan into private domains and fought to the death. Out of that wreckage three men reassembled the country one ruthless step at a time: Oda Nobunaga, who broke the old order with European matchlock guns and burned the great monasteries that defied him; Toyotomi Hideyoshi, a foot soldier's son who finished the conquest, froze society into rigid classes, and then flung two doomed invasions at Korea; and Tokugawa Ieyasu, the patient survivor who won the gambler's victory at Sekigahara in 1600 and took the title of shogun in 1603 — founding the regime that would seal Japan shut for 250 years and turning the world of the warring samurai, almost overnight, into legend. This is where the samurai, the shogun, the castle, and the Zen aesthetic all became what they are. It is also a centuries-long case study in what happens to a society that keeps its sacred king but gives the real power to men with swords.

A note on names and pronunciation. Japanese names in this book follow the Japanese convention of **family name first**, given name second — so in *Minamoto no Yoritomo*, Minamoto is the clan, Yoritomo the man; in *Oda Nobunaga*, Oda is the family and Nobunaga the individual. (The little *no* in older names, as in *Minamoto no Yoritomo* or *Taira no Kiyomori*, is roughly "of the" — "Yoritomo of the Minamoto.") A handful of terms run through the whole story and are worth getting straight at the outset. The **shogun** (SHOH-gun) is the supreme military commander; the title is short for *sei-i taishōgun*, "barbarian-subduing great general," and the man who held it, not the emperor, actually ran the country. His government was the **bakufu** (BAH-koo-foo), literally "tent government," a name borrowed from a general's field headquarters — so even the word for the regime admitted it was a soldier's camp, not a royal court. The two great shogunates take their names from where they sat: the **Kamakura** (kah-mah-KOO-rah) shogunate from a town near modern Tokyo, and the **Ashikaga** (ah-shee-KAH-gah) shogunate, also called the **Muromachi** (moo-roh-MAH-chee) shogunate after the district of Kyoto its palace stood in. The warrior himself was a **samurai** (SAM-oo-rye, from a verb meaning "to serve") or, in the older and broader term, a **bushi** (BOO-shee), "warrior." A great territorial lord was a **daimyō** (DYE-myoh), "great name." The capital, **Kyoto** (KYOH-toh), held the emperor and the old court aristocracy. Two more terms name the texture of the late age: **Sengoku** (SEN-goh-koo), "the warring states," the century of all-against-all war after 1467 (the name was borrowed from a violent era of ancient Chinese history); and **gekokujō** (geh-koh-koo-JOH), "the low overthrowing the high," the upside-down social principle of that century, in which a retainer might murder his lord and take his place. Dates are CE throughout (this whole civilization is medieval and later, so there are no BCE dates to track). Miles come first, kilometers in parentheses.

A note on "first" claims and on how we know any of this. As in every timeline in this project, "first surviving evidence of" is what "first" really means, and for medieval Japan the sources have strong and specific biases worth flagging up front, because so much of the drama comes to us pre-shaped. The Kamakura shogunate kept an official chronicle, the **Azuma Kagami** (ah-ZOO-mah KAH-gah-mee), the "Mirror of the East" — invaluable, but it is the regime's own diary, written and edited by the winners, and it flatters the Hōjō regents who commissioned it. Much of what everyone "knows" about the samurai comes not from documents at all but from the **gunki** (GOON-kee), the war tales: above all the **Tale of the Heike** (HEY-keh), the great epic of the Genpei War, chanted by blind lute-priests and polished over generations into something closer to national myth than reportage, and the sprawling **Taiheiki** (tye-HEY-kee), the semi-fictional chronicle of Go-Daigo's age. These tales are gorgeous and they are *literature* — they invent speeches, sharpen heroes and traitors, and impose a tidy Buddhist moral (the proud always fall) on messy events; where this book leans on a war tale for color rather than fact, it says so. Later chronicles and family histories, written under the Tokugawa peace, often back-projected a clean, philosophical code of *bushidō* onto warriors who in their own day followed a rougher "way of the bow and horse." And then, late in the story, a wholly new kind of witness arrives: the letters and reports of the Portuguese and Jesuit visitors after the 1540s, sharp-eyed outsiders describing Japan to Europe — useful precisely because they noticed what the Japanese took for granted, and biased precisely because they were selling a mission. Against all of this stands the archaeology — castle sites, the Hakata defensive wall, the kilns, the battlefield finds — and the surviving art and architecture, which often correct the tales. Where the inside chronicle, the war epic, the foreign letter, and the spade disagree, the text will say which we are trusting and why.

# Chapter 1 — Two Capitals, Two Powers

Here is the single strange fact you have to hold in your head before any of the rest of this book makes sense: for the next four hundred years, Japan kept its emperor and gave away his power. It did not depose him. It did not replace him with a king of a new line, the way Europe did over and over. It left the emperor exactly where he was, in his palace in the old capital, sacred and ceremonial and very nearly broke, and it built a second government somewhere else to do the actual ruling. Two capitals, two powers, one country. That arrangement is the spine of everything that follows, and the man who invented it is where we start.

But first, the ground, because the geography quietly shapes the whole story. Japan is an arc of mountainous islands off the eastern edge of Asia, four big ones and thousands of small. The biggest is **Honshu** (HON-shoo), the long main island, and almost everything in this book happens on it or just off it. The country has two natural centers, and the tension between them runs through four centuries. In the west, on the broad inland plain around the **Kansai** (KAHN-sye) region, sits the old heartland: the capital city of **Kyoto** (KYOH-toh), the imperial court, the great monasteries, the aristocracy, the polish of a thousand years of borrowed Chinese refinement. In the east, across the mountains, lies the **Kantō** (KAHN-toh), a wide flat plain (where Tokyo stands today) that in the twelfth century was rough frontier country: horse pasture, warrior estates, men who fought for a living and looked down on the perfumed courtiers of the west almost as much as the courtiers looked down on them. West versus east, court versus warrior, polish versus muscle. Keep that compass in mind.

Now the emperor, and why he had so little to do. By the late 1100s the Japanese emperor (in Japanese, *tennō*, "heavenly sovereign") had reigned over Japan in an unbroken line for centuries and would go on reigning, in name, into our own time. The line claimed descent from the sun goddess **Amaterasu** (ah-mah-teh-RAH-soo), the chief deity of Japan's native religion, and that claim was the whole point of him. The emperor was less a ruler than a sacred axle the realm turned on, the one human whose ancestry connected the islands to heaven, and precisely because he was sacred he had become almost untouchable and almost useless. For two centuries before our story opens, real power at court had been held not by emperors but by a single aristocratic family, the **Fujiwara** (foo-jee-WAH-rah), who married their daughters to emperors, served as regents for the child-emperors those daughters produced, and ran the country from behind the throne. Then, in a twist that tells you how slippery power in Kyoto had become, even reigning emperors found a way to escape the job: an emperor would abdicate, hand the throne to a young son, take Buddhist vows, and then rule as a **retired emperor** (the system is called *insei*, "cloister government") with more freedom than he had ever had while formally on the throne — because the reigning emperor was bound hand and foot by ceremony and by the regents who managed him, while an abdicated father, freed of all that ritual, could direct affairs as the family's senior man. So on the eve of our story you already have a court where the man who reigns is not the man who rules, and the man who rules may be a monk who used to reign. Power in Kyoto was a hall of mirrors. Into that hall walked men with swords.

The men with swords were the **bushi** (BOO-shee), "warriors," the rough provincial fighting class who would in time be called by the more famous name **samurai** (SAM-oo-rye, from an old verb meaning "to serve"). They had grown up in the provinces over generations, originally as the armed retainers and estate-managers the court needed to keep order in a countryside the court itself could not be bothered to govern directly. By the 1100s these warrior families had become indispensable and dangerous: the court could not control the provinces without them, and increasingly could not control them either. Two warrior clans rose above the rest. The **Taira** (TYE-rah), also called the **Heike** (HEY-keh) from an alternate reading of the same name, had done something no warrior family had done before, which was to claw their way into the very center of the court itself. Their leader, **Taira no Kiyomori** (kee-yoh-MOH-ree) (the little *no* in old Japanese names means roughly "of," so this is "Kiyomori of the Taira"), made himself the most powerful man in Japan around 1160, married his daughter to an emperor exactly as the Fujiwara had done, and put his own infant grandson on the throne as the child-emperor **Antoku** (AHN-toh-koo). The Taira had beaten the courtiers at the courtiers' own game. The other great clan, the **Minamoto** (mee-nah-MOH-toh), also called the **Genji** from an alternate reading, were their bitter rivals, and Kiyomori had crushed them, killed many of their leaders, and exiled the survivors. Among those spared as children was a boy named **Minamoto no Yoritomo** (yoh-ree-TOH-moh), packed off to a quiet exile in the eastern Kantō. Sparing him was the single greatest mistake the Taira ever made.

In 1180 the country went to war. The conflict is called the **Genpei War** (gen-PAY) (the name fuses *Gen*, from Genji/Minamoto, and *Hei*, from Heike/Taira), and it ran from 1180 to 1185, a five-year civil war between the two clans for control of the realm. The grown Yoritomo raised the Minamoto banner in the east, gathering the rough horse-warriors of the Kantō who had no love for the perfumed Taira in distant Kyoto. The war seesawed, but the Minamoto's deadliest weapon turned out to be Yoritomo's younger half-brother, **Minamoto no Yoshitsune** (yoh-shee-TSOO-neh), a battlefield genius who won the war's decisive campaigns with daring that the later war tales would polish into pure legend. The end came in 1185 at the **Battle of Dan-no-ura** (dahn-no-OO-rah), a sea fight in the narrow Shimonoseki Strait at the far western tip of Honshu. Yoshitsune's fleet annihilated the Taira navy. The Taira leadership died on the water, and with them, drowned by his own grandmother rather than be captured, died the child-emperor Antoku, still a small boy, the Taira's living trophy. In the *Tale of the Heike*'s famous telling, his grandmother comforted him as she carried him down by promising that a capital waited for them beneath the waves — a line whose beauty is exactly why one should remember it comes from the epic, not a witness. The Genpei War was over, and the Taira were finished.

Now, what makes this more than a tale of two warlords is what the winner did next, and to understand it you should know how that tale comes down to us, because the source biases are unusually heavy and unusually beautiful. Almost everything vivid that "everyone knows" about the Genpei War comes not from documents but from a sung epic, the **Tale of the Heike** (the *Heike Monogatari*), the great war story of the conflict, recited for centuries by blind priests who chanted it to the music of the *biwa*, a kind of lute. It is gorgeous and it is *literature*: it invents speeches, sharpens heroes and villains, and threads everything onto an explicitly Buddhist moral that the proud are doomed to fall, which is announced in its famous opening about the sound of temple bells and the truth that the mighty must decline like a spring night's dream. The Heike is where Japan got its enduring picture of the doomed, elegant samurai, and it is roughly as reliable about hard fact as a national myth chanted and re-polished over generations should be assumed to be. Set against it stands the winners' own record, the **Azuma Kagami** (ah-ZOO-mah KAH-gah-mee), "Mirror of the East," the official chronicle the new warrior government kept of its own deeds. It is invaluable and it is also spin, the regime's own diary, written and edited by the people it flatters. So we have a losers' elegy turned into national poetry and a winners' diary turned into national archive, and almost no neutral third account. Where this book leans on the war tale for color rather than fact, it will say so.

Here is what the winner did, and it is the thing this whole civilization is built on. Yoritomo had just won the country. By every precedent he could have marched into Kyoto, overthrown the emperor, and founded a new dynasty, which is what an ambitious general anywhere else on earth would have done. He did the opposite. He stayed in the east. He never moved to Kyoto, never tried for the throne, never even spent much time at court. Instead he made his headquarters at **Kamakura** (kah-mah-KOO-rah), a then-modest town on the coast of the Kantō, near where Tokyo now sprawls, chosen precisely because it was *his* country, ringed by hills and the sea, far from the court's intrigues and surrounded by the eastern warriors loyal to him. From Kamakura he built something that had never existed in Japan or, in quite this form, anywhere: a second, parallel government, military in character, that ran the country's real business while the emperor in Kyoto kept his throne, his rituals, and his sacred glow.

The machinery of it was the key, because Yoritomo did not just declare himself boss; he wove his power into the country's bones in a way that proved durable. As early as 1185 he won from the court the right to appoint his own men across the provinces in two new offices. The **shugo** (SHOO-goh), usually translated "military governor" or "constable," was Yoritomo's man in a province, responsible for keeping the peace, mustering local warriors, and policing crime. The **jitō** (jee-TOH), "land steward," was Yoritomo's man on an individual estate, responsible for managing it, collecting its dues, and skimming a recognized cut. With shugo over the provinces and jitō over the estates, the new warrior government had eyes, muscle, and income reaching into every corner of the land, all of it answering not to the court but to Kamakura. The warriors who held these posts were Yoritomo's sworn followers, his **gokenin** (goh-keh-NIN), "honorable house-men," bound to him by a personal bond of loyalty and reward that was the heart of the whole system: they served him in war and administration, and he confirmed them in their lands and protected their interests, a relationship not unlike the lord-and-vassal bond taking shape in Europe in the same centuries (where a knight likewise swore military service to a lord in return for being granted and protected in his land), though it grew from entirely separate roots.

The government Yoritomo built has a name, and the name is a confession. It was the **bakufu** (BAH-koo-foo), which means, literally, "tent government," a word borrowed from the field headquarters of a campaigning general, the command tent in a war camp. Even the word for the regime admitted what it was: not a royal court but a soldier's camp that happened to be running a country. In English we usually call it the **shogunate**, after the title its head would carry. And that title came in 1192, when the court granted Yoritomo the rank of **sei-i taishōgun** (say-ee tye-SHOH-gun), "barbarian-subduing great general," a grand old military title once given to generals sent to fight the indigenous peoples of the north (the Emishi, the non-Japanese tribes of the northern frontier whom the early court spent centuries subduing). Shortened, it is the word the world now knows: **shogun** (SHOH-gun). The title was real, granted by the emperor, and that was exactly the genius of it. The shogun did not pretend to outrank the emperor; he served the emperor, formally, as the emperor's supreme general. But because the shogun commanded the warriors and the warriors commanded the land, the shogun held the real power, and the emperor, in granting the title, was essentially signing over the country to the man who had already taken it, while keeping the sacred dignity that was all he had left. It was a two-headed system, sacred head in Kyoto and governing head in Kamakura, and it would last in one form or another for nearly seven hundred years, longer than the gap between Columbus and the present day.

It is worth pausing on how unusual this was, because the cross-civilizational angle is the whole interest of it. Plenty of societies have had a powerful warrior caste, and plenty have had a figurehead monarch shadowed by a real strongman, the late Roman emperors propped up by their generals, the Frankish kings of early medieval France and Germany reduced to "do-nothing" puppets while their chief stewards (the "mayors of the palace") ran the kingdom, the Abbasid caliph — the supreme religious-political ruler of the Islamic world, enthroned at Baghdad — reduced to a sacred figurehead while sultans and warlords held the swords. What Japan did that was distinctive was to make the arrangement *permanent and formal and stable* rather than a prelude to one side eating the other. In Europe a Charles Martel's heirs eventually just took the crown and made themselves kings; in Baghdad the caliph was eventually swept aside. In Japan the figurehead emperor and the ruling shogun coexisted, on purpose, for centuries, neither destroying the other, because the emperor's sacredness was too useful to abolish and the shogun's power too real to deny. The emperor legitimized the shogun; the shogun protected the emperor; and ordinary life went on under a government that never once claimed to be the highest authority in the land. This is the experiment the book is about, and Yoritomo is the man who set it running.

It did not, of course, run smoothly, and the cracks showed before Yoritomo was even cold. The most famous early casualty was his own brother. **Yoshitsune**, the battlefield genius who had actually won the war at Dan-no-ura, became a problem the moment the fighting stopped, because he was popular, brilliant, and had accepted court titles from Kyoto without Yoritomo's leave, exactly the kind of independent warrior glory Yoritomo's whole system was built to prevent. Yoritomo turned on him. Yoshitsune fled north, was hunted across the country, and in 1189 was cornered and forced to kill himself, betrayed at the last by the lord who had been sheltering him. The pattern would repeat itself for centuries: the man who wins the war is more dangerous to the new order than the enemy he beat. Yoshitsune's tragedy fascinated the Japanese ever after, and his legend grew larger than his cautious, calculating brother's, the romantic loser outshining the ruthless winner, which is itself a very Japanese verdict and a theme this book will meet again and again.

So by the time Yoritomo died, in 1199, the architecture was in place: a sacred emperor reigning in Kyoto, a warrior government ruling from Kamakura, a shogun at its head, shugo and jitō reaching into every province, and a warrior class bound by loyalty and reward. What no one yet knew was that the family that built it would be off the stage within a generation, and that the real power would slide, almost at once, into the hands of people who never held the title of shogun at all. Power behind the power behind the power: that is the next chapter.


# Chapter 2 — The Power Behind the Power

The Kamakura shogunate had a design flaw that surfaced almost the instant its founder died, and the flaw is a clue to how power actually worked in medieval Japan. Watch what happens to the title of shogun over the next few decades, and you will see the most peculiar feature of the Japanese system, which is its bottomless appetite for ruling through someone else. The emperor reigned but did not rule; a shogun ruled in his name. Then the shogun, too, became a figurehead, and someone ruled in *his* name. Power behind power behind power, like a set of nesting dolls, each official puppet concealing the puppeteer who would, in turn, be revealed as a puppet. By the end of this chapter the country will be run, effectively, by the regent of a regent of a general who served an emperor who did nothing. And the people who pulled it off were not even a famous clan. They were the family Yoritomo had married into.

That family was the **Hōjō** (HOH-joh). To understand how they took over you have to understand Yoritomo's widow, who is one of the most formidable figures in Japanese history. **Hōjō Masako** (mah-SAH-koh) was the daughter of a minor eastern warrior, **Hōjō Tokimasa** (toh-kee-MAH-sah), and she had married the exiled Yoritomo when he was a nobody with a price on his head, a love match against her father's wishes, the story goes. When Yoritomo died in 1199, their elder son **Yoriie** (yoh-ree-EE-eh) became shogun. He was young, headstrong, and, crucially, surrounded by his mother's relatives. Within a few years the Hōjō had sidelined and then disposed of him (he was deposed, confined, and eventually murdered), replaced him with his more pliable younger brother **Sanetomo** (sah-neh-TOH-moh), and made themselves the power behind the shogun's seat. The office they invented for this was the **shikken** (SHIK-ken), usually translated "regent," though "regent" undersells it: the shikken was, in practice, the actual ruler of Japan, governing in the name of a shogun who was increasingly a child, a puppet, or both. From around 1203 onward the Hōjō held the shikken's office as a family inheritance, passing real power from Hōjō father to Hōjō son for over a century. They never took the title of shogun. They did not need it. They had the power, and they let someone else carry the name.

Masako herself is the hinge of the takeover and earned the nickname later ages gave her: the **ama-shōgun** (AH-mah SHOH-gun), the "nun shogun," because after Yoritomo's death she took Buddhist vows and became, formally, a nun, while remaining in fact one of the most powerful people in the country. (Taking religious vows did not mean retreating from the world; for the powerful it was a normal late-life move, half piety, half a change of legal status, and a nun or monk could go right on running affairs, as the retired emperors did from their cloisters.) When her second son Sanetomo, the puppet shogun, was assassinated in 1219, cut down on the steps of a shrine by his own nephew in a tangle of family revenge, the Minamoto main line that Yoritomo had founded was simply extinguished. There were no more Minamoto shoguns. The Hōjō, rather than seize the title themselves, did something cleverer: they imported a small child of noble Kyoto blood to be a figurehead shogun, a decorative infant with a grand pedigree and no power at all, and went on ruling as shikken behind him. From 1219 on, the shogun was an ornament and the Hōjō shikken was the government. Masako held it all together through the crisis, and her steadiness in 1221 saved the regime, as we are about to see.

Because in 1221 the other head of the two-headed system tried to take the country back, and the result settled, permanently and brutally, which head was really in charge. The challenger was a **retired emperor**, **Go-Toba** (goh-TOH-bah). Recall from Chapter 1 that a retired emperor, having abdicated and taken vows, could wield more real freedom than a reigning one, ruling from his cloister over the puppet on the throne. Go-Toba was talented, ambitious, and bitterly resentful of the upstart warriors in the east who had stripped the court of its power. With the Minamoto line dead and a mere child in the shogun's seat, he judged the moment ripe. In 1221 he issued a call to arms declaring the Hōjō regent an outlaw and summoning the warriors of the land to destroy Kamakura. This was the **Jōkyū War** (JOH-kyoo) (named, like most events in this book, for the year-name in the calendar then in use). Go-Toba was betting that the warriors' ultimate loyalty was to the sacred throne, and that called upon by the imperial house itself, they would abandon the eastern usurpers.

He lost the bet completely, and the moment the bet failed is one of the great set-pieces of the age, courtesy of the Azuma Kagami (the regime's own chronicle, introduced in Chapter 1, so read its drama as flattering its patrons). With the eastern warriors wavering at the terrible prospect of marching against an emperor, the aging nun Masako is said to have gathered them and reminded them, in a speech the chronicle preserves and surely improved, of everything Yoritomo had given them: their lands, their standing, their rescue from the contempt of the court. The debt they owed the Minamoto house, she argued, outweighed any call from a Kyoto that had only ever despised them. The warriors held. Kamakura did not wait to be attacked; it sent three armies west at once, overwhelmed the imperial forces, and took Kyoto within a month. Then it did the unthinkable. The Hōjō exiled the retired emperor Go-Toba to a remote island, where he died years later still composing exquisite poetry. They exiled two other retired emperors as well. They deposed the reigning child-emperor and installed one of their own choosing. And they confiscated the estates of every courtier and warrior who had backed the rebellion, some three thousand of them, and handed those lands to their own followers as rewards.

It is hard to overstate how far this broke the old rules. Warriors had just defeated, deposed, and banished emperors, and the sky had not fallen. The sacred throne, it turned out, could be overruled, exiled, and reshuffled by men with swords, and the realm went on. After 1221 nobody could pretend the court was a coequal power; the warrior government in Kamakura was now plainly the master of Japan, and to make the mastery permanent it planted a permanent office in the capital, the **Rokuhara Tandai** (roh-koo-HAH-rah tahn-DYE), a pair of Hōjō deputies stationed in Kyoto to watch the court, run western affairs, and make sure no second Go-Toba ever got ideas. The emperor kept his sacred function (this is the crucial point, the throne was humbled but never abolished, because its sacredness was the very thing that made the shogun legitimate), but the political question was closed. The Jōkyū War is the moment the medieval settlement locked into place.

With the country firmly in hand, the Hōjō did something that tells you what kind of rulers they meant to be: they wrote down the law. In 1232 the regent **Hōjō Yasutoki** (yah-soo-TOH-kee), Masako's nephew and one of the ablest of the line, issued the **Jōei Code** (JOH-ay), formally the *Goseibai Shikimoku*, the first law code written by and for the warrior class. It is a revealing document precisely because it is so practical and so unpretentious. It did not try to be a grand philosophical statement or a copy of the elaborate Chinese-derived law the court used (the dense, scholarly legal codes Japan had imported from imperial China centuries before, written in classical Chinese and built for a centralized bureaucracy). It was a working handbook for the problems warriors actually had: who inherits an estate, how to settle a land dispute between two jitō, what a shugo may and may not do, how long you have to press a claim, what counts as a crime and what the penalty is. Yasutoki himself reportedly said it was written for rough warriors who could not read the court's classical Chinese codes, a set of plain rules in plain terms so that disputes could be judged fairly and predictably rather than by favor. That last bit mattered enormously. The shogunate's deepest source of legitimacy with its warrior followers was that it was a reliable judge of their land claims, an honest broker in a world where land was wealth, status, and survival. The Jōei Code made that brokerage systematic, and it became the foundation of warrior law for centuries; later military codes were built on top of it the way later European law built on Roman precedent (the legal rules of ancient Rome, which medieval and modern Europe revived and used as a foundation).

To see why land law was the beating heart of the system, you have to understand the strange way medieval Japan actually held its land, because it was nothing like a simple map of "this lord owns this field." Most agricultural land was tied up in **shōen** (SHOH-en), private estates that had grown up over centuries as a way of dodging the central government's taxes. A shōen was not owned by one person; it was a tangle of overlapping rights stacked on top of each other, each layer entitled to a slice of the harvest. A working farmer held the right to cultivate; above him a local manager held a right to a cut; above the manager a warrior steward (the jitō) held another; above the steward a distant aristocratic or temple "owner" in Kyoto held the top right and the prestige; and the relationships could be layered three or four deep, each party drawing income from the same rice paddy without anyone "owning" it outright in our sense. Untangling who was entitled to what out of any given estate was the central legal headache of the entire age, and it is why a court of honest judges in Kamakura, applying a clear code, was worth more to a warrior than almost anything else. Power in medieval Japan was, at bottom, a vast and endless argument over slices of rice fields, and the side that judged the argument fairly held the loyalty of the men who fought.

It also helps to step back and see the whole shape of how power was distributed, because "the shogun ran everything" would be far too simple. Historians describe medieval Japan as a system of **kenmon** (ken-MON), "gates of power," a handful of great institutional blocs that shared rule and checked one another, none able to simply dominate the rest. There was the **court** in Kyoto, sacred and prestigious, still the source of titles, rank, and legitimacy, still owning huge tracts of shōen, even after the Jōkyū War humiliation. There were the great **religious institutions**, the wealthy Buddhist monasteries and Shinto shrines, which held enormous estates, ran their own affairs, commanded the spiritual anxieties of the powerful, and, as we will see, fielded their own armed monks. And there was the **warrior government** at Kamakura, with its shogun, its Hōjō regents, its shugo and jitō and law code. These three blocs, court, temple, and warrior, overlapped and competed and depended on one another; a great monastery might appeal to the court against a warrior steward, a warrior might lean on the shogunate against a grasping aristocrat, the court might bless or curse any of them. It was a system held in tension, which is one reason it was so stable for so long and also why, when one leg finally weakened, the whole thing eventually tipped over. For now, in the calm decades after 1232, the tension held, and the Hōjō regents presided over the most orderly stretch the warrior government would ever know.

Underneath the law and the institutions, the warrior himself was being forged into the figure later ages would romanticize, and it is worth meeting him as he actually was rather than as the legend made him. He was not yet a swordsman following a written philosophical code; the famous word *bushidō*, "the way of the warrior," and its polished ethics are largely a later, peacetime invention, back-projected onto these centuries by writers living under the calm of the regime that ends this book. The Kamakura warrior followed something rougher and more concrete, which the period itself called the **way of the bow and horse** (*kyūba no michi*). His defining weapon was not the sword but the bow, fired from horseback; his ideal was the mounted archer, and his prized skills were riding and shooting. His ethic was a hard code of personal loyalty to his lord, fierce concern for his own and his family's honor and reputation, and a terror of shame that could outweigh the fear of death. It was practical, violent, and tied to land and lineage rather than to any abstract philosophy. Out of this period and the wars to come would harden the warrior's most chilling practice, **seppuku** (sep-POO-koo), ritual suicide by cutting open one's own belly, chosen over the dishonor of capture, a way of dying that turned defeat into a final assertion of will and honor. The polished philosophy came later. The raw material, loyalty, shame, the bow, the horse, and the readiness to die, was being shaped now.

So as the thirteenth century wore on, the picture was one of remarkable durability: a sacred emperor in Kyoto, a puppet shogun in Kamakura, a Hōjō regent actually ruling, a clear law code, a humbled but surviving court, powerful monasteries, and a warrior class learning the habits that would define it. The Hōjō had taken Yoritomo's invention and made it stick. Their regime was about to face the single greatest external test any Japanese government would face for the next six hundred years, an enemy from across the sea larger than anything the islands had ever imagined, and it would pass that test in a way that became national myth. But first, while the warriors settled their land disputes and the regents perfected their grip, something just as transformative was happening away from the battlefields and the law courts, in the temples and the hearts of ordinary people. Japan was getting a new kind of religion, and that is the next chapter.


# Chapter 3 — A New Religion for a New Age

Step away from the warriors and the regents for a moment, because something was happening in these same decades that would shape Japan as deeply as anything Yoritomo or the Hōjō did, and it was happening in temples and on roadsides rather than on battlefields. Religion was being reinvented from the ground up. The Buddhism that the medieval period inherited was, for most ordinary Japanese, almost useless: an elaborate, expensive, scholarly, monastery-bound affair, conducted in difficult texts by an elite clergy on behalf of the aristocracy, full of rituals and study and discipline that a farmer or a soldier had no way to perform and no time to learn. Over the span of this chapter, a generation of restless monks blew that open. They created forms of Buddhism so simple that anyone could practice them, and in doing so they handed faith to the common people for the first time. This wave is called the **Kamakura new Buddhism**, and it is, with the rise of the warrior government itself, the most important thing that happened in the age. It also handed the warrior class its own beloved religion, the one the modern world most associates with old Japan, which arrived in this very period along with a hot drink. We will get to the tea.

First, the basics, because a reader who has never met Buddhism needs a foothold. **Buddhism** is a religion that began in India around five centuries before the common era with the teachings of the **Buddha** (the "awakened one," a prince who renounced his rank to seek release from suffering). At its core is the diagnosis that ordinary life is bound up with suffering, that suffering arises from craving and attachment, and that one can in principle escape the whole weary cycle of death and rebirth and attain a state of liberation. Over the centuries Buddhism spread across Asia and split into many schools, and it reached Japan from China and Korea around the sixth century, arriving as the religion of the court and the elite. By the medieval period the dominant established schools, with their great monasteries on the mountains above Kyoto, were learned, ritually complex, and politically powerful, and entirely out of reach of common people. To be saved, in the old understanding, you more or less had to be a monk with years of study and discipline behind you, or rich enough to pay monks to perform rituals for you. The new Buddhism's radical move was to say: no, you don't.

The first and most popular of the new movements made salvation almost shockingly easy, and it is worth understanding the mechanism because the whole appeal turns on it. The monk **Hōnen** (HOH-nen), beginning around 1175, taught a faith called **Pure Land Buddhism** (*Jōdo-shū*, the "Pure Land school"). The idea rested on a particular Buddha named **Amida** (AH-mee-dah), a celestial Buddha who, the scriptures said, had vowed countless ages ago to save anyone who sincerely called on him by bringing them at death into his **Pure Land**, a paradise from which final liberation was guaranteed. Hōnen's revolutionary teaching was that in this fallen, late age of the world, ordinary people could no longer hope to save themselves by their own efforts, by study or discipline or good works, and did not need to. They needed only to rely, in complete trust, on Amida's vow, and the way to do that was to recite a short formula of devotion, the **nembutsu** (nem-BOO-tsoo) — the words *Namu Amida Butsu*, meaning roughly "homage to Amida Buddha" — repeated with sincere faith. That was it. No literacy, no monastery, no wealth, no Sanskrit. A farmer in a field, a fisherman, a dying old woman, anyone who chanted the nembutsu with a trusting heart would be reborn in the Pure Land. You can see at once why it swept the country. It was the first form of Buddhism the common people could actually do.

Hōnen's most consequential follower took the logic even further. **Shinran** (SHIN-rahn), his disciple, founded around 1224 a movement called **True Pure Land Buddhism** (*Jōdo Shinshū*, "the True Pure Land school"). Where Hōnen said recite the nembutsu, Shinran said that even the recitation was not really *your* doing; it was Amida's compassion working through you, and a single sincere moment of trusting faith was enough, because salvation came entirely from Amida's grace and not at all from human effort. (There is a striking rhyme here with a much later Christian idea: the Protestant Reformation that the German monk Martin Luther launched in 1517 turned on the claim that a person is saved by faith alone, through God's grace, and not by good works or religious effort — the same move Shinran made, independently, on the other side of the world three centuries before Luther was born.) Shinran also broke with monastic tradition in his own life: he married openly and ate meat, insisting that a married layman with a family was no further from salvation than a celibate monk, since salvation came from faith and not from discipline. His True Pure Land school became the largest and most populist Buddhist movement in Japan, sinking deep roots among farmers and townspeople. Hold onto it, because three centuries later its militant congregations would arm themselves, overthrow a warlord, and run a province as a self-governing "peasants' kingdom," a story for a later chapter. The gentlest faith of the age grew the fiercest political teeth.

A third populist preacher took an utterly different and far angrier path, and he matters both for his ideas and for an uncanny prophecy. **Nichiren** (NEE-chee-ren), beginning around 1253, founded a movement built not on Amida but on a single revered scripture, the **Lotus Sutra**, which he held to be the Buddha's highest and final teaching, containing all truth in itself. His simple practice was the chanting of a formula of devotion to the Lotus Sutra rather than to Amida. But Nichiren was no gentle universalist. He was a thunderous, intolerant prophet who denounced every other school of Buddhism as false and dangerous, attacked the Pure Land teaching by name, and warned that Japan's tolerance of false doctrines would bring divine punishment in the form of internal strife and foreign invasion. The authorities exiled him more than once for the offense his attacks gave. And then, as the next chapter will tell, the foreign invasion he had predicted actually arrived, in the shape of the largest seaborne force the world had yet seen, which did wonders for his movement's credibility. His followers persist as a distinct and combative strand of Japanese Buddhism to this day.

Now the religion the warriors loved, which is the one that left the deepest mark on Japanese art and taste. **Zen** (a Japanese pronunciation of a word that traveled from a Sanskrit term for meditation, through Chinese as *Chan*, into Japanese) is a school of Buddhism that put meditation at the very center and was deeply suspicious of words, scriptures, and intellectual study as paths to enlightenment. Its claim was startling: that enlightenment is not something you reason your way to or read about, but a direct, wordless insight into your own nature that can break upon you suddenly, and that the way to it is rigorous seated meditation and the discipline of an unflinching teacher, not the memorizing of texts. Zen reached Japan as a fresh import from China in exactly this period, in two main forms. The monk **Eisai** (AY-sye), returning from study in China in 1191, founded the **Rinzai** (RIN-zye) school of Zen, the more abrupt and confrontational variety, famous for its use of the **kōan** (KOH-ahn), a baffling riddle or paradox ("what is the sound of one hand?") that the master assigns the student to wrestle with until rational thought exhausts itself and a sudden breakthrough of understanding can occur. A generation later the monk **Dōgen** (DOH-gen), who had also studied in China, founded around 1244 the rival **Sōtō** (SOH-toh) school, which played down the riddles and emphasized "just sitting," long, patient, silent seated meditation (*zazen*) as itself the practice and the point, not a means to some separate goal. Dōgen settled his community at the remote mountain temple of **Eihei-ji** (AY-hay-jee) and wrote one of the masterworks of Japanese philosophy, the *Shōbōgenzō* ("Treasury of the True Dharma Eye"), a dense and luminous meditation arguing that practice — the act of sitting itself — is not a path toward enlightenment but already its full expression. Rinzai for the sudden flash, Sōtō for the long sit: between them they planted Zen permanently in Japanese soil.

Why did the warriors take to Zen so hard? The fit was almost too neat, and it is worth spelling out the mechanism rather than just asserting the affinity. Zen's discipline mirrored the warrior's: it prized self-control, direct action, and the silencing of the chattering, fearful mind, exactly the qualities a man wants when facing death in battle. Its suspicion of scripture and book-learning suited a class that had always defined itself against the bookish court aristocrats and their classical studies; here was a profound religion that did not require you to be a scholar to be a master of it, which flattered men who fought rather than read. And its emphasis on confronting death and impermanence directly, without flinching, spoke to people whose trade was death. The Hōjō regents themselves became Zen's great patrons; the regent who organized the defense against the Mongols, whom the next chapter will meet, founded a major Rinzai temple at Kamakura and made Zen the regime's favored faith. Through that patronage Zen became the religion of the ruling warrior class, and because it was the religion of the rulers it became the channel through which a flood of Chinese culture, painting, architecture, garden design, poetry, and a particular austere aesthetic, poured into Japan, with consequences for art that later chapters will spend whole chapters on.

And the tea, as promised, because the most famous of all Japanese cultural rituals begins here, in a Zen temple, as a stimulant. When Eisai came back from China with Rinzai Zen in 1191, he also brought tea seeds and the Chinese monastic practice of drinking **matcha** (MAH-chah), powdered green tea whisked into hot water into a frothy, intensely bitter, caffeine-rich drink. In the Chinese monasteries tea served a strictly practical purpose: it kept monks awake and alert through long hours of meditation. Eisai planted his seeds, promoted tea as a health-giving aid to the religious life, and even wrote a treatise praising its virtues. From this small monastic beginning, tea-drinking spread first among the Zen clergy and then, over the following centuries, into the warrior elite, where it would be transformed step by step into the elaborate, philosophical, aesthetically loaded ritual the world now calls the **tea ceremony**, the slow, deliberate, exquisitely choreographed preparation and serving of matcha as a kind of moving meditation. That full flowering belongs to much later chapters; here it is enough to plant the seed, literally, and note that one of the supreme symbols of Japanese refinement entered the country as a Zen monk's way of staying awake.

The new age also reshaped how the Japanese pictured the holy, and the period left behind two of the most arresting Buddhist images ever made. The sculptor **Unkei** (OON-kay), working in the early 1200s, led a workshop that broke with the serene, idealized, faintly bloodless Buddhist statues of the older court style and carved figures of muscular, dynamic, almost frightening realism, full of motion, weight, and force. His masterpieces are the colossal pair of guardian kings, the **Niō** (NEE-oh), that stand snarling in the great gate of an ancient temple at **Nara** (NAH-rah), the old imperial capital just south of Kyoto that had been Japan's seat of government and great-temple center centuries before, each over twenty-six feet tall, carved from joined blocks of wood, their muscles bulging, their faces contorted in fury, hands flung out to ward off evil, the very embodiment of the new warrior age's taste for power, energy, and immediacy over the old court's delicacy. And around 1252 the people of Kamakura cast in bronze a giant seated figure of the Buddha Amida, the **Great Buddha of Kamakura** (*Daibutsu*, "great Buddha"), some forty feet tall, a serene, downcast, contemplative colossus that still sits today exactly where it was made. It once stood inside a great wooden hall, but the hall was swept away by storm and wave in the centuries after, and the Buddha was left to sit in the open air, weathering quietly under the sky, which is how the world knows it now and which suits the age's Buddhist sense that all things, even the halls of giants, pass away.

So in the same decades that the warriors were locking their government into place and the Hōjō were perfecting their grip, ordinary Japanese were being handed a faith they could finally practice, the warrior class was adopting the meditative discipline that would shape its art and its taste for centuries, and the country was acquiring, almost as a side effect, both Zen aesthetics and a frothy green drink. This religious revolution will pay dividends in nearly every chapter that follows: the Zen temples will become the engines of a cultural golden age, the tea will become a ceremony and then a tool of politics, and the gentle Pure Land faith will grow the militant congregations that fight warlords. But the immediate future held something far less serene than a meditating monk. The fiery prophet Nichiren had warned of a foreign invasion, and across the sea the largest empire the world had ever known was about to make him look like a true prophet. That is the next chapter, and it is the great test of everything the Hōjō had built.


# Chapter 4 — The Divine Wind

In 1268 a letter arrived in Japan that no Japanese government had ever received before: a polite, chilling demand from the most powerful man on earth that the islands submit to him or be destroyed. The man was **Kublai Khan** (KOO-bly KAHN), grandson of Genghis Khan, ruler of the Mongol Empire, which by then was the largest land empire in the history of the world, sprawling from Korea and China across all of Central Asia to the plains of Russia and the edge of eastern Europe, which Mongol armies had ravaged a generation earlier. Kublai had conquered or was conquering China itself, where he would rule as emperor of a dynasty he named the **Yuan** (yoo-AHN); he had subjugated Korea; and he now turned his attention to the small chain of islands off the Asian coast that had never bowed to anyone abroad. His envoys carried a message addressed, with deliberate arrogance, from the "Emperor of Great Mongolia" to the "King of Japan," inviting Japan to enter into friendly relations, which everyone understood to mean tributary submission, and noting that no one would wish to resort to arms. It was the opening move in the only foreign invasion that the Asian mainland would ever throw at Japan, and the way Japan survived it became one of the most powerful myths in the nation's history, with an afterlife so dark it reached into the twentieth century.

The Hōjō regent who had to answer the Khan was a young man named **Hōjō Tokimune** (toh-kee-MOO-neh), who took up the office of shikken (the regent who actually ruled, Chapter 2) in his teens, just as the Mongol threat was rising, and who would spend his entire short life dealing with it. Tokimune was a devout patron of Rinzai Zen (Chapter 3), and the steel that the discipline was supposed to instill seems to have been in him: faced with the most powerful empire in the world, he refused. Japan returned no answer to Kublai's demands, and when the Khan sent further envoys, Tokimune eventually had them beheaded, an unambiguous reply and a calculated insult, signaling that Japan would not so much as negotiate. He then did the prudent thing alongside the defiant one: he ordered the warriors of western Japan, especially those of the southern island of **Kyushu** (kee-OO-shoo), the part of Japan closest to the Asian mainland and the obvious landing place, to prepare for war. It came in 1274.

The first invasion, which the Japanese name the **Bun'ei** invasion after the year-period, sailed from Korea in the autumn of 1274: a fleet of hundreds of ships carrying a combined Mongol, Chinese, and Korean army of perhaps twenty to thirty thousand. They seized two outlying islands with great brutality, then landed in **Hakata Bay** (hah-KAH-tah), the broad harbor on the north coast of Kyushu that was the natural gateway to Japan and would be the focus of both invasions. What followed was a brutal shock to the Japanese, because the two sides fought in completely different ways, and the Japanese way was, on that day, the worse one. The samurai had been raised on the way of the bow and horse and on a warrior ethic of individual glory; their style of battle, idealized in the war tales, centered on the single mounted warrior who rode out, announced his name and lineage in a formal challenge, and sought worthy individual opponents for honorable single combat. The Mongols had no interest whatsoever in any of that. They fought in tight, disciplined, coordinated mass formations, advancing and wheeling to the beat of drums and gongs; they fired clouds of arrows and bolts from powerful short bows and crossbows in volleys; and they threw, for the first time in Japanese experience, exploding **bombs**, primitive gunpowder weapons that burst with a terrifying flash and noise. A samurai who rode out to announce his pedigree and offer single combat was simply shot down by a faceless mass that did not even understand the gesture. The samurai, badly outmatched in tactics, fought hard and gave ground, and the defense was in serious trouble by nightfall.

And then the Mongols got back on their ships, and overnight a storm wrecked much of the fleet, and the survivors sailed home. This is the version the tradition enshrined, and it is the first appearance of the legend this chapter is named for, so it has to be handled carefully, because the first storm is exactly the part historians most doubt. The traditional story is that the Mongols, having won the day's fighting, inexplicably withdrew to their ships for the night and were then destroyed by a sudden providential typhoon. Modern scholars are divided and generally skeptical about the 1274 storm: it is possible the fleet really was caught by bad weather, but it is at least as likely that the invaders, having scouted the defenses and perhaps run low on arrows and supplies, simply chose to withdraw and were exaggerated by later legend into the victims of a miracle. What is not in doubt is that the first invasion failed and the Mongols left, and that the Japanese, far from assuming the gods had permanently saved them, took the threat with deadly seriousness and prepared for the rematch they were sure was coming.

That preparation produced one of the great engineering feats of medieval Japan and one of the reasons the second invasion played out so differently. Beginning in 1276, on Tokimune's orders, the warriors and laborers of Kyushu built a **defensive wall** around the vulnerable inner shore of Hakata Bay, a barrier of stone and packed earth running for miles along the beach, roughly six to nine feet high, its seaward face steep to stop a charge and its top wide enough to fight from. The Japanese name it the *genkō bōrui*, the "Mongol-invasion rampart." Its purpose was specific and clever: it denied the enemy the open beach where their massed formations and cavalry would be deadliest, forced any landing to fight uphill against a prepared defensive line, and gave the defending samurai a fixed position to hold rather than a wide shore to be flanked on. Stretches of it survive and have been excavated, which is one of the ways we can check the war tales against the spade. The wall, plus a standing system of coastal guard duty rotated among the western warriors, meant that when Kublai Khan came again, Japan was as ready as a medieval society could be.

He came again in 1281, and this time the scale beggared belief. The second invasion, the **Kōan** invasion, was assembled in two enormous fleets, one sailing from Korea and a far larger one from southern China, carrying a combined force that the sources put at something like 140,000 men — a figure that, if anything near true, would make this one of the largest seaborne invasions attempted anywhere in the world up to that time, a claim often made for it. Against this the samurai held the wall. The Mongol and Chinese troops could not break the prepared defenses at Hakata Bay; the wall did its job, the coordinated samurai defense did its job, and small fast Japanese boats even rowed out at night to raid the anchored fleet. For weeks the invasion was stalled offshore, unable to establish a secure beachhead, its vast army penned aboard a crowded fleet riding at anchor in the late-summer sea off Kyushu. Late summer off Kyushu is typhoon season.

The typhoon that struck in the late summer of 1281 was real, it was catastrophic, and it broke the invasion. This second storm, unlike the doubtful first one, is well attested and not seriously questioned; a great typhoon caught the enormous fleet at anchor, where it could not run for open water, and smashed it against the coast and against itself, drowning a huge part of the invading army and destroying most of the ships. (Marine archaeology off Kyushu has since recovered wreckage of the lost fleet from the seabed, hard physical confirmation of the disaster.) The shattered survivors who could still sail fled for the mainland; those stranded ashore were hunted down. One of the largest invasions the world had yet seen had been annihilated, partly by a wall and a stubborn defense and partly by the weather, and Japan was never invaded from the mainland again.

It was the storms, especially the great one of 1281, that the Japanese remembered, and the name they gave them is the one this chapter carries: **kamikaze** (kah-mee-KAH-zeh), the "divine wind," winds sent, as people understood it, by the gods of Japan to protect the sacred land of the gods from foreign defilement. The interpretation was natural and, for the religious culture of the time, almost inevitable: the great shrines had been ordered to pray for divine aid against the invaders, and when the invaders were destroyed by a heaven-sent storm, the prayers seemed answered and the conclusion seemed obvious, that Japan was a divinely protected land that no foreign enemy could ever conquer. This is the place to be honest about the mechanism and its myth at once. The wind was real and it really did help wreck the fleet; but the *kamikaze idea*, the belief that Japan enjoyed a special supernatural immunity to invasion, was an interpretation laid over a real meteorological event, and that interpretation had an extraordinarily long and ultimately dark afterlife. It nourished a strain of belief in Japanese uniqueness and divine protection that resurfaced again and again over the centuries, and in the final desperation of the Second World War, nearly seven hundred years later, the name *kamikaze* was deliberately revived for the suicide pilots who crashed their planes into enemy ships, young men sent to be the "divine wind" that would once again, it was hoped, save Japan from invasion. The myth born on the beaches of Hakata Bay in 1281 came home to roost in the worst possible way in 1945. Few historical metaphors have ever traveled so far or done so much harm.

How do we know what the fighting actually looked like, as opposed to what the legend made of it? Here the age handed us an extraordinary document, and it comes from an entirely self-interested source, which is part of what makes it trustworthy. A samurai named **Takezaki Suenaga** (tah-keh-ZAH-kee soo-eh-NAH-gah), who had fought in both invasions, commissioned around 1293 an illustrated scroll, the *Mōko Shūrai Ekotoba*, the "Illustrated Account of the Mongol Invasion," depicting his own deeds in the war. The scroll shows him charging the enemy, his horse pierced by arrows, an exploding Mongol bomb bursting above him, the disciplined enemy formations and the individual samurai pitched against them, vivid and specific in a way no chronicle is. And Suenaga did not pay for it out of pride alone; he paid for it as evidence, a visual record of his service to press his claim for a reward, which gives us the scroll's deeper lesson. He had risked his life and ruined his fortune fighting the Mongols, and he wanted, reasonably, to be paid for it, and getting paid was turning out to be the great unsolved problem of the whole victory.

This is the bitter twist on which the chapter, and the fate of the whole Kamakura regime, turns. The warrior system that Yoritomo built ran on a simple exchange (Chapter 1): warriors fought for the shogunate, and the shogunate rewarded them with land, almost always land confiscated from the losers. After the Jōkyū War the system had worked beautifully, because there were three thousand defeated rebels' estates to hand out. But the Mongol wars produced no conquered land at all. The enemy came from across the sea, was beaten, and went away (or drowned); there were no defeated Japanese rebels to dispossess, no new provinces seized, nothing to give. Yet thousands of western warriors had spent years on coastal guard duty, had borne the staggering cost of arming and feeding themselves and their men through two campaigns, had in many cases mortgaged or sold their lands to pay for it, and they came out of the great victory expecting reward, as the bargain promised, and there was almost nothing to give them. The **reward crisis** that followed slowly poisoned the loyalty that held the regime together. Warriors who had impoverished themselves saving Japan felt cheated, and they were not wrong. The shogunate's deepest asset had always been its credibility as the honest broker that rewarded service and judged claims fairly; the Mongol victory, paradoxically, was the thing that wrecked that credibility, because for the first time the broker could not pay.

The Hōjō tried to manage the wreckage and made it worse. As more and more warriors slid into debt, mortgaging their estates to moneylenders and merchants just to stay afloat, the shogunate reached in 1297 for a desperate remedy: the **Einin Debt Cancellation Edict**, a decree (the general term is *tokusei*, a "virtuous government" act of debt relief) that simply cancelled the debts of the gokenin warriors and ordered their pawned and sold lands returned to them. In the short run it relieved some indebted samurai. In the longer run it was a disaster, because it taught every lender in Japan that a warrior's debt might be erased by decree at any moment, so lenders stopped lending to warriors or charged ruinously to cover the risk, drying up the credit the warriors increasingly depended on and shredding what was left of the regime's economic credibility. A government that voids contracts to rescue its core supporters discovers that no one will sign a contract with its supporters again. The edict became a byword for a well-meant policy that backfires, and the precedent of demanding debt cancellation would echo down the next century in the uprisings of farmers and townspeople who learned to demand *tokusei* of their own.

So the Kamakura shogunate ended the thirteenth century in a strange and dangerous condition. Outwardly it had achieved the impossible: it had defied and defeated the greatest empire on earth, twice, and survived where no one expected it to. The victory entered legend, the divine wind entered the national imagination, and the Hōjō regents could claim to have saved the sacred islands. Inwardly the very same victory had hollowed the regime out. The warriors who won it were embittered and impoverished, the loyalty that bound them to Kamakura was fraying, the credit system was broken, and the shogunate had no answer to a crisis that grew worse every year, because it had defeated an enemy it could not loot. The crack ran straight through the foundation of the warrior government, hidden under the glow of triumph. It would take a generation to widen, and the man who pried it open was the one party in the whole arrangement who had been sidelined for over a century and had never stopped resenting it: the emperor himself. An emperor who wanted his power back, and who saw in Kamakura's exhaustion his chance to take it, is the subject of the next chapter.


# Chapter 5 — The Emperor Who Wanted It All

For nearly a century and a half, the system held. An **emperor** (the sacred sovereign of Japan, descended in legend from the sun goddess) reigned in the western capital of **Kyoto** (KYOH-toh), wrapped in ritual and prestige, while the real business of ruling — taxing, judging, mustering armies, settling who owned which field — was done four hundred miles to the east by a separate military government. That government was the **bakufu** (BAH-koo-foo, literally "tent government," named for a general's field camp), set up at the eastern town of **Kamakura** (kah-mah-KOO-rah) and headed in name by the **shogun** (the "barbarian-subduing great general," supreme military commander). In fact, as the last two chapters showed, even the shogun was a figurehead by now: the Kamakura regime was run by hereditary regents called the **shikken** (SHIK-ken), drawn from the **Hōjō** (HOH-joh) family, who governed behind a series of puppet shoguns the way the shoguns governed behind the emperor. Power behind power behind power, and it had worked, more or less, for over a hundred years. It had beaten back two of the largest seaborne invasions the world had yet seen (Chapter 4). And it was about to be destroyed by a single furious man who refused to accept the deal that everyone else had learned to live with.

His name was **Go-Daigo** (goh-DYE-goh), and he became emperor in 1318. To understand why he was such a problem, you have to understand the peculiar trap the imperial family had been maneuvered into. By Go-Daigo's day the throne was not even reliably passed from father to son. Two rival branches of the imperial house — historians call them the **senior line** and the **junior line** — had been forced, partly under Kamakura's thumb, into an awkward arrangement where they took turns supplying the emperor, alternating the throne between the two branches. The bakufu liked it that way. A divided imperial family, with each branch dependent on Kamakura's goodwill to get its turn, was a weak and manageable imperial family. Emperors under this system were expected to reign briefly, abdicate young, and hand off to a cousin. They were ornaments, scheduled and rotated like crops.

Go-Daigo had no intention of being an ornament. He came to the throne already a grown man (most emperors were enthroned as children), he was learned, willful, and — the chronicles agree on this even when they disagree on everything else — genuinely convinced that an emperor was meant to *rule*, not merely to exist. He looked back past the whole age of warrior government — and past the centuries of Fujiwara regents and retired-emperor rule before it (Chapter 1) — to a half-imagined golden age in the seventh and eighth centuries, when emperors were believed to have actually governed, and he decided to bring it back. Almost from the moment he took the throne, he began to plot the destruction of the Kamakura shogunate.

It is worth pausing on how we know any of this, because the picture we have of Go-Daigo's age comes pre-shaped. The Kamakura regime kept its own official chronicle, the **Azuma Kagami** (ah-ZOO-mah KAH-gah-mee, the "Mirror of the East"), but that record was the bakufu's own diary, flattering to the Hōjō who commissioned it, and it does not cover these final years from the inside. For Go-Daigo's reign and the wars that followed, the great narrative source is instead the **Taiheiki** (tye-HEY-kee), a sprawling, semi-fictional war chronicle written down over the following decades. It is gorgeous and it is *literature*: it invents speeches, sharpens heroes into saints and enemies into devils, and stamps a tidy Buddhist moral on everything, that the proud are always brought low. Much of what "everyone knows" about this period — the noble loyalists, the brilliant guerrilla, the tragic last stand — comes to us through the Taiheiki's storytelling lens. Where this chapter leans on it for color rather than hard fact, it says so.

Go-Daigo's first attempts at rebellion were almost comically inept. He plotted twice in the 1320s, and twice the conspiracies leaked. The first time, in 1324, his agents were caught and his closest advisers exiled, while the emperor himself escaped blame by insisting, with a straight face, that he had known nothing about it. The second plot, in 1331, blew up in his face entirely. The Hōjō, finally out of patience with this troublesome sovereign, did the unthinkable: they sent an army, drove him from the capital, and in 1332 exiled the reigning emperor of Japan to the **Oki Islands**, a scatter of rocks in the Sea of Japan off the western coast. To make the throne tidy again, they installed a cooperative emperor from the rival senior line in his place. As far as Kamakura was concerned, the Go-Daigo problem was solved. A deposed emperor on a windswept island a hundred miles offshore could do no harm.

They had badly misjudged the moment, and here the deeper crack in the regime matters. The Mongol wars of the previous generation had been won — the invasions broken on the beaches of Hakata Bay and, the tales said, by the typhoons remembered as the **kamikaze** (kah-mee-KAH-zeh, "divine wind") (Chapter 4). But victory over a foreign invader is paid for differently from victory in a civil war. In a domestic war you defeat a rival lord and divide up his lands among the warriors who fought for you; there are spoils. The Mongols conquered no Japanese land, so there were no spoils. The Hōjō had nothing to give the thousands of **samurai** (SAM-oo-rye, the warrior class, from a verb meaning "to serve") who had ruined themselves with years of guard duty, fortification, and fighting, expecting the usual reward of land and never receiving it. By the 1290s the regime had grown so worried about its impoverished, indebted warriors that it tried to fix the problem by decree, simply cancelling their debts — a measure that briefly relieved the samurai but wrecked the moneylenders and shredded the regime's credit (Chapter 4). The result, two generations on, was a warrior class full of resentful, land-hungry, cash-strapped men who had concluded that the Hōjō took everything and protected nothing. Go-Daigo did not create that resentment. He simply offered it a banner.

Because Go-Daigo, it turned out, did not stay quietly on his island. In 1333 he escaped the Oki Islands — smuggled out, the Taiheiki says, hidden under a pile of dried fish in a boat — and raised his standard again on the mainland. This time men came. And one of them became the story's first great hero.

He was **Kusunoki Masashige** (koo-soo-NOH-kee mah-sah-SHEE-geh), and he was nobody. Not a great lord, not a member of a famous clan, but a minor warrior from the hills of **Kawachi** province near modern Osaka, a man of the kind of obscure local gentry that the proud old families barely noticed. What he had was a gift for the one kind of war a poor man could win against a rich one: he refused to fight the Hōjō armies in the open field, where their numbers would crush him, and instead held a string of small mountain fortresses, using the terrain, ambush, and a steady stream of nasty surprises to tie down forces many times his size. The Taiheiki delights in his tricks — rolling logs and boulders down on attackers, pouring boiling water and filth from the walls, building dummy soldiers out of straw to draw enemy volleys, then mowing the attackers down when they charged the decoys. How much of this is fact and how much is the chronicle's love of a clever underdog is impossible to separate now, but the strategic reality is solid enough: Kusunoki's tiny garrisons, especially the fortress of **Chihaya**, pinned huge Hōjō armies in the mountains for months and proved to every watching warrior that the great Kamakura war machine could be defied. He became, and remains, Japan's model of the perfect loyal warrior — the man who served his emperor with total devotion against impossible odds. (That reputation would be put to a terrible test before the chapter is out.)

While Kusunoki held the mountains, the regime made the mistake that finished it. To crush the rebellion once and for all, the Hōjō sent west one of their most powerful and trusted generals, a great eastern lord named **Ashikaga Takauji** (ah-shee-KAH-gah tah-kah-OO-jee). The Ashikaga were no minor gentry; they were a major warrior house, distant kin to the Minamoto, the long-defunct shogunal line of Yoritomo (Chapter 1), and exactly the sort of family that had every reason to wonder why it should keep taking orders from the Hōjō. Sent to destroy Go-Daigo, Takauji weighed the situation, the resentment running through the warrior class, the embarrassing fact that the mighty bakufu could not even take Kusunoki's mountain forts — and switched sides. In 1333 he declared for the emperor, turned his army around, and instead of attacking the rebels he attacked the bakufu's own command center in Kyoto, capturing the capital for Go-Daigo. The general sent to save the regime had just stabbed it in the heart.

The killing blow came almost simultaneously from another defector. A warrior named **Nitta Yoshisada** (NIT-tah yoh-shee-SAH-dah), also turning against the Hōjō, raised a force in the east and marched on Kamakura itself — the supposedly safe heart of the regime, the town that had run Japan for nearly a century and a half. The city was naturally protected, ringed by steep wooded hills with only a few narrow passes, and screened on its open side by the sea. The legend, recorded in the Taiheiki, is that Nitta found the land approaches impassable and so prayed at the shore and threw his sword into the waves as an offering, whereupon the tide drew back and let his army around the headland along the exposed beach. Strip away the miracle and a low tide along the coast is a perfectly ordinary thing; what is not in doubt is that Nitta's men broke into Kamakura in the summer of 1333 and the regime collapsed in fire and blood. The last Hōjō regent and several hundred of his clansmen retreated to a family temple and, rather than be captured, killed themselves — disembowelment, the warrior's chosen death over the dishonor of capture, a practice (later formalized as **seppuku**, sep-POO-koo) that hardened during these wars into the supreme proof of a samurai's resolve. With that mass suicide, 148 years of Kamakura rule ended. The first warrior government Japan had ever built was simply gone.

Go-Daigo had won. An exiled emperor smuggled out under dried fish had, in the space of a few months, brought down the entire military regime. And now he made his great mistake, the one this whole chapter is built around: he tried to take all of it.

What followed is called the **Kemmu Restoration** (KEN-moo, named for the era-name Go-Daigo adopted), and it lasted barely three years. The idea was breathtaking and, in hindsight, doomed from the first day. Go-Daigo would not appoint a new shogun. He would not rebuild the bakufu under a friendlier family. He would abolish the whole two-headed system that had run Japan since 1185 and *rule directly* — emperor as actual government, issuing decrees, settling land disputes, controlling appointments, the way (he imagined) emperors had governed in the deep past. For the first time in a century and a half, there would be no tent government shadowing the throne. There would only be the throne.

It failed for reasons that, laid out, look almost inevitable. The first was administrative. Land in medieval Japan was a nightmare of overlapping claims — most of it tied up in tax-exempt private estates called **shōen** (SHOH-en) layered with so many overlapping rights of ownership, management, and income that untangling who really controlled any given field was the central legal headache of the entire age. The warrior government had spent generations building the machinery and the law (it had even issued the first warrior law code, a practical rulebook for samurai land disputes) to adjudicate these quarrels. Go-Daigo swept that machinery aside and announced that henceforth *every* land claim required his personal confirmation. The result was instant paralysis: a tidal wave of petitions, contradictory grants, decisions reversed on a whim, and an emperor and his court aristocrats — men with no experience whatsoever of governing warriors — drowning in paperwork they did not understand.

The second reason was money, and it was fatal. Go-Daigo, with a court to dazzle and a sense of imperial grandeur to restore, launched expensive projects, including a grand new palace, and tried to pay for them by squeezing the country. But the deepest failure was that he forgot who had actually won him his throne. The Restoration had been carried by warriors — by Takauji, by Nitta, by Kusunoki, by thousands of ordinary samurai who had risked everything against the Hōjō. They had done it expecting the only reward that meant anything to a warrior: land, and the recognition of their status. Instead Go-Daigo lavished honors and estates on court nobles and favored temples, treated the warriors as crude instruments now that their work was done, and rewarded them thinly and capriciously. He had, in other words, recreated exactly the grievance that destroyed the Hōjō — a warrior class that felt it had given everything and been given nothing — except faster, and with less excuse.

Above all, he failed to manage Takauji. Here was the most powerful warrior in Japan, the man whose defection had toppled Kamakura, a figure with vast lands, immense prestige among the samurai, and the obvious credentials to be the new shogun. Go-Daigo would not make him shogun; the whole point of the Restoration was that there *would* be no shogun. He gave Takauji honors, including a character from his own name (Takauji's birth name was different; the "Taka" was a gift from Go-Daigo), but he denied him the one office that would have institutionalized warrior power, because granting it would have admitted the Restoration was already a fiction. So the most dangerous man in the realm was left ambitious, slighted, and idle, with an army of equally slighted warriors looking to him as their natural leader. It was a situation that could only end one way.

It ended in 1336. A revolt in the east gave Takauji his pretext. He marched off to suppress it — on the emperor's behalf, supposedly — defeated the rebels, and then, having gathered an army, simply did not come back to court. When Go-Daigo ordered him to return and Takauji refused, the break was open. The general who had brought Go-Daigo to power now turned on him. Takauji's reasoning was not subtle: if the emperor would not build a warrior government, the warriors would build one without him.

What stands at the emotional center of this whole episode, and what later Japan would venerate above almost anything else, is the death of Kusunoki Masashige. As Takauji's forces advanced on Kyoto, Go-Daigo's loyalists had to decide how to fight him. Kusunoki, the brilliant guerrilla, argued for the sensible course: abandon the capital, let Takauji occupy it, and bleed his army with the kind of patient hill-and-ambush warfare that had worked against the Hōjō. The court nobles, who found a tactical retreat undignified, overruled him and ordered him instead to meet Takauji's huge army head-on in open battle at the **Minatogawa** (mee-nah-toh-GAH-wah), a river near modern Kobe — exactly the kind of fight Kusunoki knew he could not win.

He obeyed anyway. This is the part that made him immortal. Knowing the order was militarily insane, knowing he was being sent to certain death by the vanity of men who understood nothing about war, Kusunoki marched to Minatogawa in 1336 because his emperor had commanded it. His small force was surrounded and destroyed. Wounded and beaten, Kusunoki and his brother killed themselves on the field; the Taiheiki gives the brothers a famous parting exchange, vowing to be reborn seven times over to keep fighting the emperor's enemies — almost certainly the chronicle's invention, and exactly the sort of polished line that turned a real death into a legend. Whatever the brothers actually said, the shape of the thing was real and unbearable: the most loyal warrior of the age, ordered into a hopeless battle by the emperor he served, going to it with open eyes and dying as commanded. Loyalty unto death, even death you know to be pointless. Centuries later, when Japan went looking for the purest model of the warrior spirit, this is the man it chose.

With Minatogawa lost and the loyalist cause broken, Takauji took Kyoto. He did not, however, abolish the imperial institution — no warrior leader ever dared do that. Instead he reached for the old lever the Hōjō had used so well: he installed a compliant emperor from the rival senior line and prepared to rule, as shogun, behind him. Go-Daigo, refusing to the end to admit he was beaten, slipped out of the capital, fled south into the rugged mountains of **Yoshino** (yoh-SHEE-noh), and there declared that *he* was still the true emperor, that the man Takauji had enthroned in Kyoto was a usurper, and that the genuine imperial line was the one in exile with him.

So Japan, which a moment earlier had two governments and one emperor, now had two governments and *two* emperors — each insisting the other was a fraud, each with a court, a claim, and warriors willing to die for it. The Kemmu Restoration, the great experiment in turning the clock back to direct imperial rule, was over in three years, and it had proven the thing it set out to disprove: the clock could not be turned back. The age of the warrior had not been an aberration to be corrected. It was now simply how Japan worked, and an emperor who tried to govern without the warriors found himself ruling a stretch of mountains and a beautiful idea. Out of that fracture would come fifty-six years of civil war between the two courts, and a second warrior dynasty that would last almost as long as the first. That is the next chapter.


# Chapter 6 — Two Emperors, One Throne

At the end of the last chapter Japan had managed something genuinely strange: it had two emperors at once. The ambitious sovereign **Go-Daigo** (goh-DYE-goh), who had destroyed the Kamakura military government in 1333 and then ruined his own attempt to rule directly, had fled south into the mountains of **Yoshino** (yoh-SHEE-noh) and declared that he remained the one true emperor of Japan. Meanwhile in **Kyoto** (KYOH-toh), the western capital, the great warrior lord **Ashikaga Takauji** (ah-shee-KAH-gah tah-kah-OO-jee), who had betrayed first the regime and then the emperor, had installed a cooperative rival emperor from a competing branch of the imperial family. Two men, two courts, two claims to be the sacred descendant of the sun goddess. This chapter is about the long, grinding war between them — and about how, in the shadow of that war, Japan got its second warrior government and the quiet beginnings of the warlord age that would eventually tear the country apart.

Historians call this era the **Nanboku-chō** (nahn-boh-koo-CHOH), the period of the **Northern and Southern Courts**. The names are simply geography: Takauji's puppet emperor in Kyoto was the **Northern Court**, and Go-Daigo's exile regime in the mountains of Yoshino, well to the south of the capital, was the **Southern Court**. From 1336 to 1392 — fifty-six years, longer than most modern lifetimes of the day — these two courts and their warrior backers fought across central Japan over which of them was legitimate. It was not one continuous battle but a chronic, flaring civil war, dying down and reigniting for two generations, fought in shifting alliances by warriors who often cared far less about which emperor was the real one than about which side would confirm them in their lands.

That last point is the key to understanding the whole period, so it is worth slowing down on. The deep reason the war could drag on for half a century was not the strength of the Southern Court — by any rational measure Go-Daigo's mountain regime should have been crushed in a year. It dragged on because the two courts gave every ambitious warrior in Japan a *choice of masters*. A lord who lost a land dispute under the Northern Court could defect to the Southern and have his claim honored; a warrior passed over for reward in Kyoto could ride south and be welcomed. Loyalty was for sale, and both courts were buying with the only currency that mattered, recognition of land and rank. The result was a country where allegiances flipped constantly, where a powerful warrior might be courted by both sides at once, and where the imperial split functioned less as a war of principle than as a permanent auction for warrior support. This is the soil in which the later warlord era would grow.

But first, the government. Takauji's immediate problem in 1336 was that he had won a capital and a tame emperor but had no legitimate basis to rule. He solved it the old-fashioned way. In 1336 his new regime issued a brief set of governing principles, signaling that the warriors were back in charge and that the experiment in court rule was finished, a pragmatic statement of intent after Go-Daigo's chaos. Then, in 1338, the Northern Court emperor he had installed returned the favor and granted Takauji the title he wanted: **sei-i taishōgun**, "barbarian-subduing great general" — **shogun**. With that, Takauji founded the second warrior government in Japanese history, the **Ashikaga shogunate**. Because the Ashikaga shoguns built their headquarters in the **Muromachi** (moo-roh-MAH-chee) district of Kyoto, it is just as often called the **Muromachi shogunate**, and the era it presided over the Muromachi period.

That choice of location was itself a statement, and a fateful one. The first warrior government, the Kamakura bakufu, had deliberately planted itself four hundred miles east of the court, the better to keep its rough warrior culture uncontaminated by the soft, scheming, status-obsessed world of the Kyoto aristocracy. Takauji did the opposite. He set his government down in the capital itself, cheek by jowl with the imperial court he was supposedly serving. The advantage was control: the Ashikaga could keep their puppet emperor under their eye and bask in the prestige of the capital. The cost would take a century to come due, but it was real. A warrior government living in Kyoto gradually became a Kyoto institution — courtly, cultured, absorbed in the capital's intrigues and its exquisite arts, and increasingly disconnected from the distant provinces where its real power was supposed to live. The Ashikaga would produce the most dazzling culture of medieval Japan and lose its grip on the country at the same time, and the seed of that double fate was planted the day Takauji chose to govern from Kyoto.

The Southern Court, meanwhile, was losing the war but winning the argument — at least on paper. In 1339, the year after Takauji became shogun and the same year the embittered Go-Daigo finally died in his mountain exile, a Southern loyalist named **Kitabatake Chikafusa** (kee-tah-BAH-tah-keh chee-kah-FOO-sah) sat down in a besieged fortress and wrote a book to prove that *his* court was the legitimate one. It was called the **Jinnō Shōtōki** (jin-NOH shoh-TOH-kee), roughly "Chronicle of the Direct Descent of the Divine Sovereigns," and it is one of the most important political documents in Japanese history. Its argument was theological as much as historical: Japan, Chikafusa wrote, was a divine land, uniquely sacred because it alone had been ruled in an unbroken line by the descendants of the gods since the dawn of time, and legitimacy flowed from that divine descent and the possession of the sacred imperial regalia — the three sacred treasures, a mirror, a sword, and a curved jewel, said to have been handed down from the sun goddess herself as the proof of a true emperor — which (he insisted) the Southern Court held. It was political theology dressed as history, written to delegitimize the Northern emperor as a usurper. It failed to win the war. But its central idea, that Japan's emperors were divinely descended in a single unbroken line stretching back to the sun goddess and that this made Japan uniquely sacred among nations, sank deep roots and would be quoted by Japanese nationalists six hundred years later, long after anyone cared who had sat on which throne in the 1300s.

Now step away from the war and the propaganda to the institution that gave the Ashikaga age its peculiar beauty, because it ran on a parallel track to all this fighting and it mattered just as much. The Ashikaga, governing from Kyoto, became extraordinary patrons of **Zen** (a school of Buddhism centered on meditation, brought from China in the previous era, which had captivated the warrior class with its discipline and its disdain for scripture and ritual — Chapter 3). The man who fused Zen to Ashikaga power was a remarkable monk named **Musō Soseki** (MOO-soh soh-SEH-kee), who became spiritual adviser to Takauji himself. Musō was three things at once: a revered Zen teacher, a brilliant garden designer (his moss-carpeted garden at the temple of **Saihō-ji** in Kyoto is one of the masterpieces of the form), and a shrewd institution-builder. It was largely on his advice that the Ashikaga organized the leading Zen temples of the realm into an official, ranked hierarchy.

That hierarchy was the **Gozan** (GOH-zahn), the "Five Mountains" — a name borrowed from a Chinese model, "mountain" being an old word for a major monastery. The Gozan was a network of the top Zen temples, ranked in formal tiers under shogunal patronage and control, and it became far more than a religious body. Because Zen monks were literate, disciplined, and in constant contact with China, the Gozan temples turned into the intellectual and commercial nerve center of the age. They were Japan's universities, its diplomatic corps, its bankers, and its artists' studios all at once. Monks of the Gozan handled the shogunate's correspondence with China, lent money, ran estates, and produced the era's prized art form: monochrome ink painting, the Chinese-derived style of rendering whole landscapes in nothing but graded washes of black ink, which the Gozan monks imported and would, over the next century, make unmistakably Japanese (Chapters 7 and 8).

The Gozan also did something blunter: it made money for the government. The shogunate's relationship with **Zen and China** was as much financial as spiritual. The clearest case came early. To raise funds for building a great new Zen temple in Kyoto — **Tenryū-ji**, founded to pray for the soul of the dead Go-Daigo, in a striking gesture of reconciliation toward the very emperor Takauji had ruined — the Ashikaga in 1342 fitted out official trade ships and sent them to China to turn a profit on the voyage. These "Tenryū-ji ships" revived and regularized a lucrative trade with the continent that had lapsed during the wars, and they set a pattern: the Ashikaga would fund their grandest projects, religious and otherwise, on the back of the China trade. The full, spectacular flowering of that trade, and the price the shogunate paid for it, belongs to the next chapter.

While the courts fought and the temples flourished, two slower developments were reshaping the country underneath, and both would have enormous consequences. The first was the quiet mutation of the provincial governors into warlords. The shogunate could not personally administer every one of Japan's sixty-odd provinces, so it appointed military governors called **shugo** (SHOO-goh) to keep order, raise troops, and enforce the bakufu's authority in each one. The office was meant to be a delegation of central power. In practice, across the second half of the 1300s, it became the opposite. The shugo used their positions to put down local roots — accumulating land, binding the province's lesser warriors to themselves as vassals, making the office hereditary in their own families, and skimming the taxes that were supposed to flow up to Kyoto. A shugo who had originally been Kyoto's man in the province gradually became the province's own master, owing the shogun loyalty in theory and very little in fact. Historians call these transformed governors the **shugo-daimyō** (SHOO-goh DYE-myoh), the shugo who had become **daimyō** ("great names," great territorial lords). They are the larval stage of the warlords who would later carve Japan into private kingdoms. The Ashikaga shogunate's deepest structural weakness was that it had no independent power base of its own; it ruled by balancing these great regional lords against one another, and the day the balance broke, the whole thing would come down (Chapter 9).

The second development was out at sea, and it embarrassed the shogunate for two centuries. With central authority weak and the coasts of western Japan full of armed, desperate men, bands of sea-raiders began plundering the coasts and shipping of Korea and China. They were called the **wakō** (WAH-koh), a name meaning roughly "Japanese pirates" — though in truth the wakō were a mixed lot, including plenty of Chinese and Korean raiders sailing under the same flag of convenience, a chronic maritime crime wave rather than a national navy. The raids were a serious diplomatic problem: foreign governments held the shogun responsible for the depredations of "his" pirates, and the shogunate, which could barely control its own provinces, certainly could not police the open sea. The wakō would matter directly to the diplomacy of the next chapter, because suppressing them became the price Ming China demanded for trade.

Which brings us, at last, to the man who ended the war. By the 1360s the Southern Court was fading — out-resourced, isolated in its mountains, its great founders dead. The third Ashikaga shogun took office in 1368, and he turned out to be the most capable ruler the dynasty would ever produce. His name was **Ashikaga Yoshimitsu** (yoh-shee-MEE-tsoo), and he became shogun as a child, with regents governing in his name; but as he grew into the office he revealed a political talent that none of his predecessors had matched. He systematically broke or cowed the over-mighty shugo who threatened the shogunate, brought the great lords to heel, and made the Ashikaga government, for one brief generation, genuinely strong.

And in 1392 he closed the fifty-six-year wound. Yoshimitsu negotiated an end to the schism by persuading the worn-out Southern emperor to abdicate and return the sacred imperial regalia to Kyoto, on the promise that the two imperial lines would thereafter take turns supplying the emperor, alternating the throne between them as they had before. With that, the Northern and Southern Courts were reunited, and Japan had a single emperor again for the first time in over half a century. The promise to alternate the lines was, as it happened, quietly broken — the Northern line simply kept the throne, and the Southern claim withered into a grievance that would resurface now and then for centuries. But the war was over. Yoshimitsu had reunified the imperial institution, tamed the warlords, and inherited a treasury fattened by the China trade.

He stood, in the 1390s, at the absolute peak of shogunal power — richer, stronger, and more secure than any warrior ruler before him. He had also developed expensive tastes. He was about to spend that power and that money on the most dazzling display of culture in medieval Japanese history, on a humiliating deal with China, and on a building covered in gold. That is the next chapter.


# Chapter 7 — The Golden Pavilion

The man at the center of this chapter is the closest thing medieval Japan ever produced to an absolute monarch, and he was not the emperor. He was the third **shogun** (the supreme military commander; the title is short for *sei-i taishōgun*, "barbarian-subduing great general") of the **Ashikaga** (ah-shee-KAH-gah) dynasty, the warrior family that had ruled Japan from the **Muromachi** (moo-roh-MAH-chee) district of the capital, **Kyoto** (KYOH-toh), since 1338. His name was **Ashikaga Yoshimitsu** (yoh-shee-MEE-tsoo), and by the time he was done he had ended a fifty-six-year civil war, humbled the great warlords, made himself the most powerful man in Japan, swallowed his country's pride to get rich off China, built a retirement villa sheathed in gold, and bankrolled the birth of one of the world's great theatrical traditions. This is the high-water mark of the whole age — the one moment when warrior government in Japan was strong, solvent, and culturally radiant all at once. It would never be so again, and that is exactly why it is worth dwelling on.

Recall where the last chapter left him. Yoshimitsu became shogun in 1368 as a boy, with regents running the government for him, and grew into a ruler of formidable political gifts. He broke the power of the over-mighty provincial governors — the **shugo** (SHOO-goh), the military governors of the provinces who had been steadily turning their offices into private warlord domains — bringing the great lords to heel where his predecessors had merely balanced them. And in 1392 he achieved what no one had managed in half a century: he reunited the **Northern and Southern Courts**, the two rival imperial lines that had each claimed to hold the true emperor since 1336 (Chapter 6). He did it by coaxing the exhausted Southern emperor to abdicate and come home to Kyoto, on a promise (quietly broken afterward) that the two lines would alternate the throne. With the schism healed, Japan had one emperor again, and Yoshimitsu had no rival anywhere in the realm.

What he did with that supremacy was extraordinary, and it tells you how the relationship between shogun and emperor had drifted. Yoshimitsu was not content to be merely the most powerful warrior. He wanted to stand above the entire structure of the realm, court and warrior government alike. So he climbed the ladder of the imperial court itself, accumulating the highest court ranks and titles — offices that no warrior had ever held — until he reached the very top of the aristocratic hierarchy, the office of grand chancellor (the highest post in the imperial court, the senior minister who in principle stood at the head of the whole civil government). A shogun was supposed to be the emperor's military servant. Yoshimitsu maneuvered himself into a position where he outranked virtually everyone at court and treated the emperor almost as a junior partner, sometimes presiding over imperial ceremonies as though he himself were the sovereign. He blurred the line between warrior ruler and court monarch so thoroughly that some historians suspect he was angling, near the end of his life, to put his own son on the imperial throne, or to be treated as a retired emperor himself. Whether he truly aimed that high is debated and unprovable; what is certain is that no warrior before or after came as close to merging the two halves of Japan's divided sovereignty into one person.

The most famous monument to all this was a building, and it is the one most people picture when they picture old Kyoto. In 1397, near the peak of his power, Yoshimitsu began building a lavish retirement estate in the **Kitayama** (kee-tah-YAH-mah, "northern hills") district on the edge of the capital. Its centerpiece was a three-story pavilion set beside a reflecting pond, and the top two stories were covered, inside and out, in gold leaf. It is the **Kinkaku-ji** (kin-KAH-koo-jee), the **Golden Pavilion**. The building is a deliberate, almost showy fusion of styles — each of its three floors built in a different architectural mode, blending the manner of an aristocratic court residence, a warrior's hall, and a Zen temple, so that the building itself embodies Yoshimitsu's gathering of court, warrior, and religious authority into one. Glittering above its own reflection in the pond, it announced, in the most literal way imaginable, that the shogun's wealth and refinement now eclipsed the emperor's. (A grim footnote outside our period: the pavilion standing in Kyoto today is a faithful reconstruction. The original survived for over five centuries, through every war that followed, only to be burned to the ground in 1950 by a disturbed young monk.)

The gold for that gold leaf, and for everything else, came in no small part from a deal that cost Yoshimitsu something far more valuable than money — and this is the most revealing single episode of his reign, because it shows the limits of even the strongest shogun's pride. In 1368, the same year Yoshimitsu became shogun, a new dynasty had seized power in China: the **Ming** (the native Chinese dynasty that overthrew the Mongol rulers of China and would govern it for nearly three centuries). The Ming, like Chinese dynasties before them, ran their foreign relations through the **tributary system** — a framework in which China sat at the symbolic center of the world and foreign rulers acknowledged the Chinese emperor as supreme, sending him "tribute" and receiving lavish gifts and trading rights in return. To trade officially with Ming China, in other words, a foreign ruler had to formally accept a junior, subordinate status under the Chinese emperor.

Yoshimitsu wanted that trade badly. China was the source of copper coins (which Japan used as its currency; the Japanese court had given up minting its own coins centuries earlier, so the country ran on imported Chinese cash and never had enough of it), silk, books, porcelain, and the prestige goods the Kyoto elite craved. So in 1404 he did the thing no proud Japanese ruler had ever been willing to do: he formally accepted from the Ming emperor the title **"King of Japan"** — a title that, by the logic of the tributary system, ranked him as a vassal of China. In exchange he got the **tally trade**, named for the **kangō** (KAHN-goh), the official paper tallies or licenses that Ming authorities issued to legitimate Japanese trade ships, distinguishing them from the **wakō** (WAH-koh), the pirates plaguing the China coast (Chapter 6). A licensed ship carried half of a split tally that had to match the half held by the Chinese port — a simple, forgery-proof way of separating sanctioned merchants from raiders. The trade was enormously profitable, and suppressing the pirates was the other half of the bargain.

It is worth sitting with how startling this was. The emperor in Kyoto was, by his own subjects' deepest beliefs, a living descendant of the sun goddess, the sacred sovereign of a divine land — the very claim the Southern Court's great manifesto (Chapter 6) had insisted made Japan unique and superior. And here was the shogun, the man who actually ran that divine land, bowing his head to call himself the "King of Japan" and the subordinate of a foreign emperor, for the sake of copper coins and silk. Many Japanese were appalled; Yoshimitsu's own son and successor would later repudiate the arrangement precisely because the subordination was humiliating, before later shoguns quietly restored it because the money was too good to refuse. It is a perfect illustration of the gap between Japan's sacred self-image and its practical hunger, and of the peculiar freedom of the shogun: because he was *not* the sacred emperor, Yoshimitsu could do the unthinkable and stomach the humiliation that the emperor never could have. The two-headed system, for once, had a use beyond confusion.

The China trade did more than fill the treasury. It poured Chinese goods, books, paintings, and ideas into Yoshimitsu's Kyoto, and the Gozan Zen temples (the official ranked network of meditation monasteries that served as the era's universities and trade agents, Chapter 6) acted as the filter and the studio. This is the period when monochrome **ink-wash painting** — the Chinese-derived art of rendering entire landscapes in nothing but graded tones of black ink on white paper, mountains dissolving into mist, a single fisherman's boat lost in empty space — took deep root in Japan and became the most prized visual art of the age. Brought in and practiced by Zen monks, it suited the Zen sensibility perfectly: spare, suggestive, more concerned with emptiness and what is left unsaid than with color or detail. The greatest of the Japanese ink masters were still a generation or two off (Chapter 8), but the soil was laid here, in Yoshimitsu's China-flooded capital.

Yoshimitsu's most lasting cultural legacy, though, was not a painting style or a building. It was a kind of theater, and the story of how it was born turns on the shogun's own eye for talent. In the countryside and at temple festivals, troupes of performers had long put on a rough, popular variety entertainment called **sarugaku** (sah-roo-GAH-koo) — a mixed bag of comic skits, mimicry, song, and dance, closer to a village fair than to high art. In the 1370s one such troupe came to perform before the young shogun, and its leading actor electrified him. The actor's name was **Kan'ami** (kahn-AH-mee), and he was a performer and playwright of real genius who had begun refining the rough sarugaku into something deeper, weaving in elegant dance and dramatic storytelling. Yoshimitsu was captivated — by the art, and, as the surviving evidence suggests, by Kan'ami's beautiful young son, who performed alongside him. The shogun took the troupe under his personal patronage, lifting it in a single stroke from village entertainment to the orbit of the most powerful man in Japan.

That beautiful boy grew up to be one of the towering artists in Japanese history. His name was **Zeami** (zeh-AH-mee), and under Yoshimitsu's patronage he transformed his father's refined sarugaku into the art we now call **Noh** (NOH) theater — and then wrote down its secrets. Noh is slow, spare, and intensely concentrated, the near-opposite of the boisterous variety show it grew from. Its principal actors wear carved wooden masks; they move with an extreme, almost glacial stylization across a bare stage; a chorus chants and a small ensemble of drums and flute carries a haunting, irregular music; and the plays themselves often turn on ghosts, memory, longing, and the Buddhist sense that all things pass away. A Noh play does not so much tell a story as distill an emotional and spiritual essence, frequently summoning the spirit of someone long dead to relive a moment of attachment or grief. Zeami did for Noh what no founder of a theatrical tradition usually manages: he not only perfected the performance but wrote a series of treatises analyzing its aesthetics — works long kept secret within his family — that remain among the most penetrating writings ever produced on the art of acting, centered on an elusive ideal he called **yūgen** (YOO-gen), a quality of subtle, profound, mysterious beauty, the half-seen depth beneath the surface of things — the feeling, say, of the ghost of a long-dead court lady moving slowly across the bare stage to relive a single grief, more suggested than shown, leaving the deepest things unsaid. Noh is still performed today, on stages built to Zeami's specifications, by lineages that trace back to his troupe. It is the oldest major theater tradition in the world still performed in something close to its original form, and it exists because a shogun fell for an actor's son.

Step back and look at what Yoshimitsu had assembled by the early 1400s. A reunified country under a single emperor. The great warlords humbled. A treasury swollen with Chinese copper and silk. A capital glittering with imported art and ideas. A gold-covered pavilion reflected in its pond. A new theater being born under his own roof. The court and the warrior government fused, very nearly, in his single person. No Ashikaga shogun before him had been so powerful, and — this is the quiet tragedy that shadows the whole brilliant scene — none after him ever would be. When Yoshimitsu died in 1408, the machinery he had mastered did not pass intact to weaker hands; it began, slowly at first and then catastrophically, to come apart. The shugo he had cowed crept back toward independence. The fusion of court and warrior power dissolved. Within two generations the shogunate would be a spectator at its own dissolution.

But the culture he had midwifed did not die with the power. If anything, it deepened, refined itself, and turned inward, even as the government that paid for it rotted. The next chapter is about that strange and beautiful contradiction: the moment when the Ashikaga grip was visibly failing and the Ashikaga court, almost in defiance of its own decline, produced the tea ceremony, the dry garden, and the ink landscapes that the world would come to call "classical" Japan. Beauty and decay, in the same breath.


# Chapter 8 — Tea, Ink, and Emptiness

Here is one of the strangest facts in the whole history of Japan, and this chapter is built around it: the moment that produced the things the world now thinks of as the very essence of Japanese taste — the tea ceremony, the raked gravel garden, the spare ink landscape, the cult of understated, melancholy beauty — was also the moment the government fell apart. The art and the rot happened at the same time, in the same place, under the same man. To understand classical Japanese aesthetics, you have to understand that they flowered on a rotting branch.

The man was the eighth **Ashikaga** (ah-shee-KAH-gah) **shogun** (the supreme military commander who, not the emperor, actually ran the country), and his name was **Ashikaga Yoshimasa** (yoh-shee-MAH-sah). He was the grandson of the great **Yoshimitsu** (yoh-shee-MEE-tsoo), the shogun who had reunited the country, gilded the Golden Pavilion, and bankrolled the birth of Noh theater at the peak of Ashikaga power (Chapter 7). Yoshimasa inherited that peak and presided over its collapse. He came to office around the middle of the 1400s as a young man, and he is one of history's great cautionary tales about putting the wrong person in the right job: a man of exquisite taste and real artistic vision who had no aptitude for and no interest in the brutal work of governing, set at the head of a government that was beginning to need governing more desperately than ever.

To see how badly things were already going, look at what had happened to two of Yoshimasa's recent predecessors. The sixth shogun, his uncle, had been a fierce and tyrannical ruler who tried to reassert shogunal authority by terrorizing the great lords — and in 1441 one of those lords, fearing he was next on the chopping block, invited the shogun to a banquet and had him murdered there. A reigning shogun, assassinated at a party by one of his own vassals. The killing exposed how hollow shogunal authority had already become: the man who was supposed to command every warrior in Japan could be butchered by a nervous subordinate, and the response was confusion rather than overwhelming retribution. Meanwhile, lower down, the common people were stirring. Repeatedly through these decades, leagues of farmers, porters, and local strongmen — bound together in sworn associations called **ikki** (IK-kee), village leagues for mutual action — rose up in armed protest, marching on the capital, storming the pawnshops and moneylenders, and demanding **tokusei** (toh-koo-SEH, "acts of virtuous government"): edicts cancelling their debts. That ordinary villagers could organize, arm themselves, march on Kyoto, and force a beleaguered government to wipe out debts by decree is a measure of how far real power had drained away from the center. The country was slipping its leash from the top and the bottom at once.

Into this Yoshimasa retreated — and the word is exact. Faced with a government he could not control and did not enjoy, he turned his attention almost entirely to art, taste, and pleasure, leaving administration to others and to drift. The contrast with his grandfather is the whole point: Yoshimitsu's culture had been the *overflow* of a strong ruler's wealth and confidence; Yoshimasa's culture was an *escape* from a weak ruler's helplessness. Same family, same patronage of beauty, opposite meanings.

And the beauty Yoshimasa cultivated was different in kind from his grandfather's, which is why it, rather than Yoshimitsu's gold, became the template for "classical" Japanese taste. Where Yoshimitsu had sheathed a pavilion in gleaming gold leaf to announce his supremacy, Yoshimasa's whole sensibility ran the other way: toward restraint, muted color, asymmetry, rusticity, and a deliberate, refined *poverty* of means. This aesthetic — austere, understated, suffused with a quiet awareness that all things fade — is the one the world came to recognize as quintessentially Japanese, and it crystallized under Yoshimasa's patronage in the cultural movement historians name for the hills where he eventually built his retreat: the **Higashiyama culture** (hee-gah-shee-YAH-mah, "eastern hills"), the refined arts of Yoshimasa's circle in the late 1400s.

Its signature building is the deliberate answer to the Golden Pavilion. In his retirement Yoshimasa built himself a villa in the eastern hills of Kyoto whose centerpiece was a modest two-story pavilion beside a garden. It is called the **Ginkaku-ji** (gin-KAH-koo-jee), the **Silver Pavilion** — and here is the telling thing: despite the name, it was never covered in silver. Whether Yoshimasa ran out of money (the government's finances were a wreck and a great war was about to consume the city) or simply preferred the bare, weathered dark wood, the unsilvered Silver Pavilion became, by accident or by taste, the perfect emblem of the new aesthetic. Against the literal gold of the grandfather's pavilion, the grandson's "silver" pavilion was plain wood that stayed plain wood, and that restraint was precisely the point. The world's idea of refined Japanese taste descends not from the gold building but from the one that was named for silver and never got any.

The deepest expression of all this was a way of drinking tea. Tea had come to Japan centuries earlier from China, brought back along with **Zen** Buddhism (the meditation-centered school whose disdain for ritual and ornament shaped this whole sensibility, Chapter 3) by monks who drank powdered green tea, **matcha** (MAH-chah), as an aid to staying awake during long meditation. Among Yoshimasa's circle, tea-drinking was refined into something far more deliberate, and the man usually credited with pointing it in its decisive direction was a monk and tea adviser to the shogun named **Murata Jukō** (moo-rah-tah joo-KOH). Jukō turned tea away from the lavish, competitive, show-off tea parties that the rich had been throwing — gatherings where the host displayed expensive imported Chinese utensils to impress his guests — and toward an ideal he and his successors called **wabi** (WAH-bee). *Wabi* is hard to translate and central to everything in this chapter: it is the beauty of the plain, the worn, the rustic, the imperfect, and the humble — a cracked and unassuming tea bowl preferred to a flawless precious one, a small bare room preferred to a grand hall, quiet preferred to spectacle. The wabi tea gathering was small, hushed, and deliberately austere: a few guests in a tiny, plain room, the host preparing and serving the tea with concentrated, ritualized attention, every gesture and every humble object charged with meaning precisely because nothing was lavish. This was the spiritual root of what would become the full **tea ceremony**, brought to its austere perfection a century later (the master who completed it appears in Chapter 13). It is Zen turned into a social art: meditation you could serve to a guest.

The same sensibility produced the era's painting. The monochrome **ink-wash** style imported from China through the Zen temples (Chapter 7) — whole landscapes built from nothing but graded washes of black ink, vast empty spaces left deliberately blank — reached its Japanese summit in this period in the hands of a painter-monk named **Sesshū Tōyō** (seh-SHOO TOH-yoh). Sesshū was a Zen monk who actually traveled to **Ming** China (the native Chinese dynasty then ruling the continent) to study the source of the style firsthand, and came home to do something more interesting than copy it. Where the Chinese masters tended toward soft, misty, atmospheric distances, Sesshū developed a starker, more powerful, more sharply structured manner — bold, almost slashing brushstrokes, strong dark accents, a sense of rock and pine rendered with blunt force. He bent the borrowed Chinese idiom into something recognizably Japanese, and in doing so became the painter later Japanese tradition would revere above all others. His landscapes are the visual equivalent of the wabi tea room: maximum meaning from minimum means, the empty paper doing as much work as the ink.

And then there is the garden, which may be the purest distillation of the whole aesthetic into physical form. The Zen temples of Kyoto developed a radical kind of garden in this era called **karesansui** (kah-reh-SAHN-soo-ee), the **dry landscape** garden: a garden with no water and almost no plants, made instead of carefully raked gravel or sand standing in for water, and a few placed rocks standing in for mountains or islands. The most famous of all sits at the temple of **Ryōan-ji** (ree-OH-ahn-jee) in Kyoto, laid out late in the 1400s: a flat rectangle of raked white gravel, walled on three sides, holding just fifteen rocks arranged in small groups on patches of moss, and nothing else. There are no flowers, no pond, no path to walk through it; you sit at its edge and look. It is built to be contemplated, not entered — a meditation device in stone and gravel, so spare and so suggestive that people have argued for five centuries about what, if anything, it "means," which is rather the point. (A famous feature, often noted: from any single spot at its edge, you can see at most fourteen of the fifteen rocks; one is always hidden. Whether the designers intended that as a Zen lesson about the impossibility of grasping the whole, or whether it is a coincidence later readers loaded with meaning, no one can prove.) The dry garden is wabi made of rock: emptiness arranged so carefully that the emptiness becomes the subject.

Even the era's poetry ran on the same principle of collaborative restraint. The fashionable literary game of the age was **renga** (REN-gah), **linked verse** — a poem composed not by one author but by a group taking turns. One poet would offer an opening verse; the next would add a linked verse that built on it while twisting it in a new direction; the next would link to *that*, and so on, the poem moving like a chain through shifting images and moods, no single person controlling the whole. It was social, improvisational, and governed by elaborate rules about how each link could relate to the one before, and at its best — in the hands of the great traveling master **Sōgi** (SOH-gee), who raised renga to high art in exactly these decades — it produced sequences of real beauty out of many hands working in turn. Like the tea gathering and the dry garden, renga was an art of restraint, suggestion, and the spaces *between* things: what mattered was the leap from one verse to the next, the link the reader had to feel rather than be told.

Behind all of it lay a single mood, and it had a name. The Japanese aesthetic sense of this whole era — and arguably ever since — is shot through with a tender, melancholy awareness of impermanence: the conviction that things are beautiful *because* they do not last, that the cherry blossom is most exquisite precisely as it falls. The classic statement of this sensibility had actually been written a century or so earlier, by a reclusive monk named **Yoshida Kenkō** (yoh-shee-dah ken-KOH) in a collection of musings called **Tsurezuregusa** (tsoo-reh-zoo-reh-GOO-sah), usually translated *Essays in Idleness* — loose, beautiful jottings on beauty, taste, change, and decay that became a touchstone for the medieval Japanese sense of what was elegant. Kenkō wrote that things left unfinished are more beautiful than things complete, that a frayed scroll and a worn binding have a charm a pristine one lacks, that the most moving moment of the moon is not the full moon but the moon glimpsed through cloud or longed for behind the hills. Reading him is like reading the philosophy behind everything in this chapter: the cracked tea bowl, the unsilvered pavilion, the gravel that stands for water, the hidden fifteenth rock, the blank space in the painting. Beauty in the incomplete, the imperfect, the passing.

It is impossible to miss the irony, and the people of the time did not miss it either. While the shogun perfected the appreciation of impermanence, his government was busy demonstrating it. The administration drifted and rotted; the great lords schemed; the treasury emptied; the villagers rioted for debt relief; a shogun could be murdered at a banquet. Yoshimasa, contemplating the falling blossom from his unsilvered pavilion, was presiding over a state that was itself falling, and on some level he seems to have known it and to have preferred the blossoms. There is a real philosophical fit between the aesthetic and the moment: a ruling class that had lost the ability to control events found a profound, consoling beauty in the idea that nothing lasts and nothing is meant to. Decline became, in their hands, almost a kind of art.

But decline is not actually beautiful when it arrives as fire. The same Yoshimasa whose taste defined classical Japan made one catastrophic, mundane political blunder — a botched decision about who would succeed him as shogun — and that blunder, colliding with the rivalries of the great lords he had failed to control, lit a fuse. The result was a ten-year war that would burn the very capital of Kyoto, with all its temples and gardens and treasures, to the ground, and shatter the central government beyond repair. The age of impermanence was about to learn exactly how impermanent it was. That war, and the wreckage it left, is the next chapter.


# Chapter 9 — The Capital Burns

Here is the strangest thing about the war that ended medieval Japan's old order: when it was over, after ten years and the burning of the most beautiful city in the country, almost nothing had been decided. No side won. No question was answered. The two great lords who started it were both dead before it ended, and the prize they had been fighting over had quietly become worthless while they fought. And yet the **Ōnin War** (OH-nin, named for the calendar era it began in) is the single most important event in this whole book, because it is the moment the center of Japan simply stopped working. Before it, there was a government in Kyoto that the provinces at least pretended to obey. After it, there was not. The war decided nothing except that there was no longer anyone in charge of the country, and that turned out to be the only thing that mattered.

To see how the powder got laid, go back a few decades to the regime that should have prevented this. The **Ashikaga** (ah-shee-KAH-gah) shogunate — the second of Japan's two great warrior governments, ruling from the Muromachi district of Kyoto since the 1330s (Chapters 6 and 7) — had reached its peak under the third shogun, Yoshimitsu, the man who gilded a pavilion and humbled himself to China for trade (Chapter 7). But a shogunate built on one brilliant man's grip is only as strong as the men who inherit it, and the men who inherited it were not Yoshimitsu.

The trouble was structural, and worth getting straight because it is the engine of everything that follows. The Ashikaga did not rule the provinces directly. They ruled through the **shugo** (SHOO-go), the military governors they appointed over each province to keep order, collect dues, and raise troops for the shogun. In theory the shugo were the shogun's officers, removable at his pleasure. In practice, over a century, they had turned their offices into hereditary family estates, accumulating land and warriors and tax rights until a handful of the biggest shugo houses — among them the **Hosokawa** (ho-so-KAH-wah), the **Yamana** (yah-MAH-nah), the **Hatakeyama** (hah-tah-keh-YAH-mah), and the **Shiba** (SHEE-bah) — between them controlled most of the country. (The Hosokawa and Yamana are the two that will matter most below; the others mainly supplied the quarrels these giants fought through.) The shogun sat at the top of this pyramid, but he balanced on it rather than commanded it. He stayed shogun because the great shugo wanted a shogun there to legitimize their own positions and to referee their endless quarrels over land. The moment they stopped wanting that referee, the office would mean nothing. The Ōnin War is the story of that moment arriving.

The first sign of how thin the ice had grown came in 1441, and it was shocking. The sixth shogun, **Ashikaga Yoshinori** (yoh-shee-NOH-ree), was a hard, suspicious, vindictive man who had clawed his way to the office by lottery (he had been a monk; the succession was literally drawn by lot at a shrine) and ruled by terror, destroying lords on a whim and confiscating their lands. One of the lords he had marked for destruction, a shugo named Akamatsu Mitsusuke, decided to strike first. He invited the shogun to his Kyoto mansion for a banquet and a Noh performance — and there, in the middle of the entertainment, had him murdered. This was the **Kakitsu Incident** (kah-KEET-soo, again named for the era). A reigning shogun, the supreme military authority of Japan, assassinated at dinner by one of his own vassals. The regime put together an army, hunted Akamatsu down, and destroyed him, so order of a kind was restored. But the lesson did not unhappen. Everyone in the country had now watched a vassal kill a shogun and very nearly get away with it. The aura of untouchability that had protected the office since Yoshimitsu's day was gone. It is exactly the inversion that the next century would make its rule: the low striking down the high.

Beneath the lords, the ground was shifting too, and this is the part the old samurai-centered histories tend to skip. Ordinary people had become a political force. Through the early 1400s, the villages of central Japan — bound together in self-governing leagues called **ikki** (EE-kee, "leagues" or "uprisings," sworn associations of farmers, local warriors, and porters) — had learned that if they gathered in the thousands and marched on the capital, they could frighten the government into giving them what they wanted. What they usually wanted was a **tokusei** (toh-KOO-say, "virtuous government," the polite name for a debt-cancellation decree). The medieval Japanese economy ran on credit; peasants and low samurai alike borrowed against their next harvest from pawnbrokers and moneylenders, and in bad years the debts crushed them. A tokusei wiped the slate. From the 1420s on, debt-cancellation uprisings hit Kyoto again and again, the crowds storming the pawnshops and the sake brewers (who doubled as the city's bankers) and burning the loan records. The shogunate, unable to suppress them and desperate for the cut it took from issuing the decrees, often simply gave in. A government that cancels debts because a mob demands it is a government already half-dissolved. The center was rotting from the bottom and the top at the same time.

Now the spark. The eighth shogun was **Ashikaga Yoshimasa** (yoh-shee-MAH-sah), who took the office as a boy in 1449 and grew into one of the most consequential failures in Japanese history — a man of exquisite taste and no appetite whatsoever for governing. The art he sponsored was sublime (it is the subject of Chapter 8: the Silver Pavilion, the tea aesthetic, the dry gardens). His statecraft was a disaster, mostly because there was none. By his thirties he wanted only to retire and devote himself to beauty, and he had a practical problem: no son. So in 1464 he persuaded his younger brother, a monk named Yoshimi, to leave the monastery, return to lay life, and become his heir, promising him the succession. The brother, reasonably wary, made Yoshimasa swear that even if a natural son were later born, the boy would be sent into the priesthood and Yoshimi's claim would stand.

You can guess what happened. The next year, in 1465, Yoshimasa's wife, **Hino Tomiko** (HEE-no toh-MEE-ko) — a formidable, money-shrewd woman who has often been blamed, perhaps too neatly, for what followed — gave birth to a son, Yoshihisa. And now there were two heirs to the shogunate: the brother, with a sworn promise, and the infant son, with a mother determined to see him rule. This is the **succession dispute** that lit the fuse. By itself it was a family quarrel. What made it catastrophic was that the two greatest lords in the country lined up on opposite sides of it, each for his own reasons.

On one side stood **Hosokawa Katsumoto** (ka-tsoo-MOH-toh), head of the Hosokawa house and the sitting *kanrei* (KAHN-ray, the shogun's deputy and the highest office under the shogun himself). On the other stood **Yamana Sōzen** (SOH-zen), called the Red Monk for his complexion and his shaved head, head of the rival Yamana house and Hosokawa's own father-in-law. The two men were already locked in a long cold rivalry over land and influence, already entangled in a separate succession quarrel inside the Hatakeyama house (one of the great shugo families above) over which heir would inherit it — a dispute that had already brought rival armed retainers flooding into the capital. The shogunal succession was simply the largest of several disputes onto which they could hang a war they were halfway to fighting anyway. Hosokawa backed the brother Yoshimi; Yamana backed the infant son. (The alignments would later switch, in the confused way of the whole conflict, which tells you how little the original cause finally mattered.) Around these two giants every other great house in Japan chose a side and brought its army to Kyoto.

By the start of 1467 there were something like a hundred thousand troops camped in and around the capital, an unarmed civilian city built of wood, and no authority on earth able to send them home. The shogun Yoshimasa, the one man who might have ordered both sides to stand down, did essentially nothing — issued a feeble ban on fighting that everyone ignored, then withdrew toward his arts. In the spring the skirmishing began. The Hosokawa forces, calling themselves the **Eastern Army**, dug in around the shogunal palace in the northeast of the city; the Yamana forces, the **Western Army**, fortified the northwest. The two armies turned Kyoto into a battlefield neighborhood by neighborhood, throwing up walls, watchtowers, and ditches across the streets, fighting from fortified mansions, and — because neither side could force a decision — settling into a grinding urban siege in the middle of a living city.

What it did to Kyoto is the heart of the chapter. This was the imperial capital, the cultural heart of the realm for nearly seven hundred years, home to the emperor's palace, the great temples, the libraries and treasure-houses of the court aristocracy, the workshops, the markets. It burned. Fire was the inevitable weapon and the inevitable result: armies set blazes to clear fields of fire, to deny buildings to the enemy, and out of sheer chaos, and in a city of wood and paper the fires ran. Whole districts went up. Temples that had stood for centuries, with the manuscripts and treasures of generations inside them, were lost in single nights. The aristocrats fled to the provinces or huddled in the ruins; some of the oldest noble families of Japan were ruined outright, their estates seized and their records burned. Contemporary accounts — and we should remember these are partly literary, prone to heightening a disaster into a moral lesson about the vanity of the world, a very medieval Japanese instinct — describe the capital reduced to scorched fields where foxes and thieves moved through the ash. The physical core of classical Japanese civilization was gutted, and much of what was lost has simply never come back to us; a great deal of what we don't have from before 1467 we don't have because of this war.

And then it just went on. For ten years the two armies sat in the wreckage of Kyoto and fought to no purpose. Yamana Sōzen and Hosokawa Katsumoto, the two principals, both died in 1473 — within months of each other, of illness, their war unwon — and still it continued, the armies now led by lesser men who could neither win nor admit there was nothing to win. The original cause had evaporated: at one point the shogun, the brother, and the son had all shuffled their allegiances so thoroughly that the war's nominal stakes made no sense at all. By the mid-1470s the great lords had grasped a hard fact. While they sat in Kyoto burning the capital, their own provinces back home were slipping out of their control — being seized by their deputies, their relatives, their ambitious retainers. The real prize was no longer in Kyoto. It was at home, and it was being stolen. One by one, from 1477, the armies simply packed up and went home to defend what was left of their domains. The Ōnin War did not end in a victory or a treaty. It ended because everyone stopped showing up.

Tally the result and it is almost nothing, which is precisely the point. The succession question was never really resolved; the boy Yoshihisa eventually became the ninth shogun, for what that was now worth. The Hosokawa and Yamana houses were both wrecked. Kyoto lay in ashes. And the office of shogun — the supreme command that Minamoto no Yoritomo had invented three centuries earlier (Chapter 1), that had run the country through the Mongol invasions and the schism of the two courts — was revealed to be empty. There was still a shogun. He still lived in Kyoto, still held audiences, still issued documents. But out in the provinces, no one was listening anymore, because the war had taught every lord in Japan the one lesson that mattered: the center could not protect you, could not command you, and could not punish you. You were on your own. Whatever you could hold, you held; whatever you could take, you could keep.

That realization, spreading outward from the smoking capital, is the true product of the Ōnin War, and it has a name. The age it opens is the **Sengoku** (SEN-goh-koo, "the Warring States"), the century of all-against-all war that the Japanese named, with grim self-awareness, after a famously violent period of ancient Chinese history. It is conventional to date it from 1467, the year the burning began. The country that came out the other side of these ten years was not a kingdom in crisis. It was no longer a kingdom at all — just a map of opportunities, waiting for the men ruthless and capable enough to seize them. Who those men were, and how they built private states out of the wreckage of a public one, is the next chapter.


# Chapter 10 — A Country of Warlords

When the armies finally left the ruins of Kyoto in 1477 (Chapter 9), they went home to a country that no longer had a working government, and that absence is the most important thing to understand about the hundred years that followed. There was still an emperor in Kyoto, sacred and broke. There was still a shogun, going through the motions in his half-burned palace. But the thing they had once sat on top of — a structure of command that reached from the capital out into the provinces — was gone, and it did not come back for a century. Into that vacuum stepped a new kind of man and a new kind of state. This chapter is about the world they built: how you ran a country with no country, how power was seized and held and counted, and how the very nature of war changed in the process.

Start with the principle that defined the age, because the Japanese gave it a name and the name explains everything. It is **gekokujō** (geh-koh-koo-JOH), "the low overthrowing the high." In the old order, your place was your birth: you served the lord your father had served, the great houses stayed great, and authority flowed downhill from emperor to shogun to shugo to retainer. Gekokujō is that ladder turned upside down. In the Sengoku, a retainer might murder his lord and take his domain; a deputy might shove aside the governor he served; a man of no birth at all might end up ruling a province. It was not an occasional scandal but the basic operating logic of the century, the thing everyone expected and most ambitious men attempted. The old shugo houses — the great military-governor families that had run the provinces for the Ashikaga and fought the Ōnin War — were among the first casualties. Many were destroyed from below by their own underlings during the very years they were away burning Kyoto. The men who replaced them are what we mean by the **daimyō** (DYE-myoh, "great name") in the Sengoku sense: warlords who held territory not because anyone above them had granted it, but because they had the swords to take it and keep it.

The textbook example, and the man often called the first true Sengoku daimyō, is **Hōjō Sōun** (HOH-joh SOH-oon). His career reads like the age's founding myth, and we should be a little careful with it, because the legend of his obscure origins was sharpened by later storytellers into something neater than the truth — recent scholarship has shown he was rather better connected than the rags-to-rule story claims. But the shape of the thing is real. Starting from a modest position in the late 1400s, with no great inheritance, he maneuvered, schemed, and fought his way into control of a castle, then a district, then the strategic town of Odawara on the broad **Kantō** (KAHN-toh) plain (the wide eastern lowland around modern Tokyo, the largest stretch of flat farmland in Japan and therefore one of its richest prizes). From that base his family — confusingly called the **Later Hōjō**, because they adopted the name of the long-extinct regents of Kamakura (Chapter 2) to borrow a bit of prestige, with no actual descent from them — built one of the largest and best-run domains in the country. A man who began with little ended his life a great lord, and his descendants ruled a kingdom in all but name. That was gekokujō working exactly as advertised.

So what did one of these warlords actually rule, and how? Here the Sengoku turns out to be not just chaos but a laboratory of state-building, because a daimyō who could not govern his domain did not keep it. The new lords wrote their own laws. These were the **bunkokuhō** (boon-koh-koo-HOH, "domain codes" or "house laws"), each one a little legal system for a single warlord's territory, taxing the farmers, settling disputes between retainers, regulating marriages and inheritance, and — tellingly — often binding the lord himself to his own rules, because a domain run by pure whim flew apart. Some are remarkably thorough; the code of the Imagawa house (a powerful warlord clan in the provinces southeast of Kyoto, whom we will meet again in Chapter 12 as Nobunaga's first great victim), for instance, reads like the constitution of a small independent country, which in practice is what each domain had become. There was no national law anymore, so each lord made his own, and the most successful warlords were as much administrators as fighters.

They also rebuilt where people lived. The signature settlement of the age is the **castle town**, the *jōkamachi* (JOH-kah-MAH-chee, literally "town below the castle"). A daimyō would plant his fortress on a defensible hill or rise, and then deliberately gather around its base the things he needed: his warriors, pulled off their scattered rural lands and concentrated where he could watch and command them; the artisans who made his weapons and armor; and the merchants who supplied his domain. The result was a planned town clustered protectively under the castle walls, with the lord's power literally looming over the marketplace. This was not just defense. It was a tool of control — a way to pry the warrior class off the land, where they tended to grow independent, and tie them to the lord's person. The pattern stuck so hard that a startling share of modern Japanese cities, including some of the biggest, began as Sengoku castle towns. The whole geography of where Japanese people live was partly drawn by warlords deciding where to put a fort.

Not everyone, though, lived under a warlord. In the cracks of the broken country, two remarkable experiments in non-warlord rule grew up, and both are worth meeting because they show how genuinely open the century was. The first was the merchant city. The port of **Sakai** (SAH-kai), on the coast near modern Osaka, grew rich on the trade with Ming China and governed itself — run not by a lord but by a council of its leading merchants, defended by its own moat and militia, a self-ruling commercial city in a land of warlords. Foreign visitors later compared it to the free merchant cities of Europe, like Venice (the Italian port-republic that governed itself and grew rich on seaborne trade, answering to no king), and the comparison is fair: here was a place where money, not blood, was the source of authority, an island of bourgeois self-rule in the middle of the samurai century. (It was an island that could not last; the unifiers of the chapters ahead would not tolerate a city that armed and governed itself, and Sakai's independence was eventually broken.)

The second experiment was stranger and, to the warlords, far more alarming: a province run by its peasants. This was the work of the **Ikkō-ikki** (EE-koh-EE-kee), the "single-minded leagues," armed associations built on a particular kind of Buddhist faith. To explain them we have to reach back to the religious revolution of Chapter 3. One of the new sects of that era was **True Pure Land Buddhism** (Jōdo Shinshū), founded on the teaching that ordinary people — farmers, the illiterate, the poor — could be saved simply by sincere faith in the Buddha Amida, with no need for monastic discipline or aristocratic learning. It was a faith made for the common people, and by the late 1400s its congregations had organized into something the founders never intended: leagues of believers, knit together by their faith, armed, disciplined, and willing to fight. In 1488, in **Kaga** (KAH-gah) province on the Japan Sea coast, these leagues did the unthinkable. They rose against the shugo who governed them, drove him to suicide, and took over the province themselves, running it through their temples and congregational assemblies. Kaga became, in the phrase of the time, "a province held by peasants," and it stayed that way for nearly a century. A religion of farmers had overthrown a warlord and replaced him with itself. To the daimyō, the Ikkō-ikki were a nightmare: an enemy that was everywhere, that fought without fear of death because death was salvation, and that could not be beheaded because it had no single head. They would become one of the hardest things the unifiers ever had to break (Chapter 12).

Underneath all this politics ran money, and the Sengoku economy was booming even as the country bled. The clearest sign was silver. From the 1520s and 1530s the **Iwami** (ee-WAH-mee) silver mine, on the Japan Sea coast, was opened up and then worked with a new refining technique imported from the continent, and it turned Japan into one of the great silver producers of the entire world. For a time in the sixteenth century, by some estimates Japanese mines may have been pouring out a large share of the world's silver — at any rate a genuinely global quantity, often compared to the silver then flooding out of Spanish America (Spain's vast colonial empire in the New World) on the other side of the planet. That silver paid for everything: it funded the warlords' armies, it bought the foreign goods and, soon, the foreign guns, and it pulled Japan into a world trade network that reached from the China coast to Europe. A century of political collapse was, paradoxically, also a century of economic growth — more land cleared, more trade, more money — because the warlords competing for survival had every incentive to make their domains rich. War is expensive, and the daimyō who could not pay lost.

Finally, the way war itself was fought changed, and this is the development that quietly doomed the romantic old samurai world even before the guns arrived. For most of the medieval period, the ideal warrior had been the mounted archer — a man of good family on horseback, expensively armored, trained from childhood in the "way of the bow and horse" (Chapter 1), fighting in a style that prized individual skill and individual glory. Battles had a flavor of the duel about them. The Sengoku killed that. The decisive force on the new battlefield was the **ashigaru** (ah-shee-GAH-roo, "light feet"), the mass-recruited foot soldier — commoners, often farmers, pulled into the army in large numbers, drilled to fight not as individuals but as disciplined blocks. Armed first with long pikes wielded in dense formations, and soon afterward with the new matchlock guns (the weapon that arrives in Chapter 12), the ashigaru in their hundreds and thousands could simply overwhelm the elite mounted archer, no matter how nobly born or beautifully trained he was. War became a matter of numbers, drill, and logistics rather than individual heroics. The change cut deep into who the samurai even were: the warrior class swelled with men of humble birth who had proved themselves in the ranks, and rank on the battlefield increasingly meant command of these foot-soldier blocks rather than personal prowess with the bow. The age that produced the most vivid images of individual samurai glory was, underneath, the age that was making individual glory militarily obsolete.

That tension — between the warlord world's romance and its grinding reality — is the subject the next chapter takes up directly, through the men who embodied the romance at its most dazzling, just as the forces that would sweep them all away were gathering at the edges of the country.


# Chapter 11 — Tigers and Dragons

If you ask someone today to picture a samurai, the image that rises — the mounted lord in lacquered armor under a fluttering banner, the duel of honor, the warrior who would rather die than be shamed, the master of the sword — comes very largely from this chapter's world: the high Sengoku, the mid-sixteenth century, the warring-states century at its most vivid and most legendary. It is the romantic peak of the samurai age. It is also, and this is the irony that runs underneath the whole chapter, the moment just before everything in it was made obsolete — by guns, by mass armies (Chapter 10), and by three unifiers who would put the broken country back together and end the world of the independent warlord forever (Chapters 12 to 14). We are meeting these men, in other words, at the last possible moment they could exist.

Before the men, the system they lived inside, because their glamour rested on a very hard economic floor. By the late Sengoku, a lord's power was measured in rice. The unit was the **koku** (KOH-koo), defined as roughly the amount of rice thought to feed one person for one year (about five bushels). Everything was counted in koku: a domain's worth, a lord's rank, the size of the army he could field, the stipend a retainer received. A warlord was, very literally, worth a number of koku — the annual rice yield of all the land he controlled — and that number told you, more reliably than any title, how powerful he was, because rice fed soldiers and soldiers were power. This is the dry skeleton beneath the romance. The banners and the duels and the codes of honor all sat on top of an account book measured in bushels of rice, and the warlords never forgot it even when later legend did.

Now the warriors. The two who stand at the center of the Sengoku legend, fittingly, are best understood as a matched pair of rivals, and their nicknames tell you how Japanese memory framed them: the Tiger and the Dragon.

The Tiger was **Takeda Shingen** (tah-KEH-dah SHIN-gen), lord of the mountainous inland province of **Kai** (KYE) — the rugged region around modern Yamanashi, southwest of the Kantō plain. Shingen was the complete Sengoku daimyō: a ferocious battlefield commander, a shrewd administrator who organized his domain and built flood-control works to tame its rivers and grow more rice (more rice meant more koku meant more soldiers, exactly as Chapter 10 laid out), and the master of a cavalry force feared across central Japan. His battle standard carried four phrases borrowed from Sun Tzu, the ancient Chinese general and military thinker (active around the fifth century BCE) whose treatise *The Art of War* was read across East Asia, and they became the most famous war motto in Japanese history: *Fū-Rin-Ka-Zan* — "swift as the **wind**, silent as the **forest**, fierce as the **fire**, immovable as the **mountain**." Like many warlords of the age, he had begun his rise with an act of pure gekokujō: he took his domain by deposing his own father. He spent his life expanding it, and for a stretch of the 1560s and early 1570s he was arguably the single most dangerous man in Japan.

The Dragon was **Uesugi Kenshin** (oo-EH-soo-gee KEN-shin), lord of **Echigo** (eh-CHEE-go), the snowy province on the Japan Sea coast just north of Shingen's mountains. Kenshin was Shingen's great rival and his temperamental opposite — a devout Buddhist who took religious vows and styled himself a living incarnation of Bishamonten, the Buddhist guardian god of warriors, a man with a reputation (carefully burnished by later tellers, so treat the halo with caution) for a stricter sense of honor than most of his contemporaries. The two of them, Tiger and Dragon, locked horns over a contested patch of borderland on the plain of **Kawanakajima** (kah-wah-nah-kah-JEE-mah), where their territories met. Between 1553 and 1564 they fought there five separate times. The **battles of Kawanakajima** became the most storied military rivalry of the entire Sengoku — and here the historian has to step carefully, because Kawanakajima is exactly the kind of event that the war tales and later popular culture have buffed into pure legend. The most famous episode, from the fierce fourth battle in 1561, has Kenshin breaking through the lines on horseback to attack Shingen personally, the seated Takeda lord parrying the Dragon's sword strokes with nothing but his war fan — the *gunbai*, a stout iron-ribbed signaling fan a commander used to direct his troops, sturdy enough to double as an emergency shield — until his guards drive the attacker off. It is a magnificent scene and it may well be largely a later invention; the contemporary evidence is thin, and the single-combat-of-two-great-lords motif is precisely what storytellers love to add. What is solid is that the two men fought repeatedly over the same ground, that neither ever destroyed the other, and that the rivalry ended not in a decisive victory but in the deaths of both men within a few years — Shingen in 1573, Kenshin in 1578 — their long duel a strategic draw. As a piece of history it is inconclusive. As the defining image of the noble samurai rivalry, two great lords who respected each other across years of war, it is immortal, and the gap between those two facts is itself one of the lessons of this book.

The age had other giants, and a couple are worth meeting because they round out the picture. Far to the west, in the provinces along the Inland Sea, **Mōri Motonari** (MOH-ree moh-toh-NAH-ree) rose from a single small domain to dominate the whole western end of the main island of Honshu, by a combination of cunning diplomacy, well-timed treachery, and battlefield skill that made him one of the age's great strategists. He is remembered for a famous parable he is said to have taught his three sons: handed a single arrow, each could snap it easily; handed three arrows bound together, none could break the bundle — so they must stay united. (The lesson took less well than the parable suggests; his descendants fell out anyway, in the human way of such things.) Far to the northeast, in the wild frontier region of **Ōshu** (OH-shoo), the young **Date Masamune** (DAH-teh mah-sah-MOO-neh), the "one-eyed dragon" — he had lost an eye to childhood smallpox — carved out one of the largest domains in the country late in the Sengoku, a warlord arriving almost too late to the game, who would spend his long life adjusting to the unified Japan the unifiers built around him, and who in 1613 — long after the events of this chapter, once the wars were over — sent a samurai envoy and a shipload of retainers clear across the Pacific, on to Mexico, and finally to the courts of Spain and the Pope in Rome, one of the few Japanese embassies ever to reach Europe before the country closed.

What knit all these men into a single recognizable culture was a code, an aesthetic, and a set of tools, and these are what the modern image of the samurai is really made of. Begin with the weapon. The **katana** (kah-TAH-nah), the curved single-edged sword that is the very symbol of the samurai, reached its technical and cultural peak in these centuries. Japanese smiths had developed an extraordinary method of forging it: folding and hammering the steel many times to work out impurities, then hardening the cutting edge much harder than the spine, so the blade could take a razor edge and hold it while the back stayed springy enough not to shatter. The faint wavy line where hard edge meets soft body, visible on a fine blade, is the signature of this differential hardening and was prized as a thing of beauty in itself. The sword became far more than a tool; it was held to embody the warrior's soul, surrounded with ritual and reverence. We should be honest, though, that on the actual Sengoku battlefield the sword was a sidearm — the killing was done with pikes, bows, and increasingly guns — and that much of the katana's mystique was elaborated later, under the long peace that followed, when samurai had swords but few wars. The cult of the blade is real; its starring battlefield role is partly retrospective.

Then the code of death. The practice for which the samurai are most grimly famous is **seppuku** (sep-POO-koo, also called *harakiri*), ritual suicide by cutting open one's own abdomen. A defeated or disgraced warrior, rather than be captured and dishonored, would kill himself in a prescribed and deliberately agonizing way, often with a second standing by to behead him and shorten the suffering once the cut was made. By the Sengoku this had hardened into the supreme proof of a warrior's honor and courage — the ultimate refusal to be shamed. A word of caution belongs here, the same one the framing of this book keeps raising: the polished, philosophical "way of the warrior," *bushidō*, that later ages attached to all this was substantially a creation of the peaceful centuries that came afterward, when scholars looked back and tidied a rough fighting ethic of loyalty, shame, and survival into a clean moral system. The Sengoku warrior's real code was closer to the older "way of the bow and horse" (Chapter 1) — and, in an age of gekokujō, was frequently honored in the breach, since betraying your lord was, after all, the surest way to get ahead. The samurai of legend were more loyal and more serene than the samurai of fact.

Around the edges of the warlord's world moved figures the legend has made famous out of all proportion. The **ninja** (NIN-jah), the shadow-warriors of countless later stories, have a real historical kernel: bands of irregular fighters from the **Iga** (EE-gah) and **Kōga** (KOH-gah) regions near Kyoto, professional spies, scouts, saboteurs, and assassins (the period word for them was *shinobi*) whose services a warlord could hire for the dirty work that open battle could not accomplish. They were not the black-clad supermen of film — that costume and most of the magic came later — but the actual trade of espionage and infiltration in a country full of warring lords was real, valuable, and dangerous, and the Iga and Kōga families made a specialty of it. And then there were the warrior-monks. The great temples and Buddhist sects of medieval Japan were not otherworldly retreats; many were armed powers in their own right, fielding their own troops, the **sōhei** (SOH-hay, "monk soldiers"), and holding fortified temple-complexes that could defy any warlord. Some, like the True Pure Land leagues of Chapter 10, ran whole provinces; others manufactured their own guns and fielded thousands of fighters. Religion in the Sengoku came armed.

So here is the picture at the romantic peak of the samurai age, around the 1560s: a country with no center, divided among dozens of warlords; a balance of regional power in which giants like Shingen and Kenshin could fight for years without anyone winning; a warrior culture of swords and honor and ritual death at its most vivid; armed monks and shadow-warriors in the margins; and the whole thing counted, paid for, and made possible by rice. It is a world that feels eternal — the kind of balance of mighty rivals that could seem like it might go on forever.

It did not go on at all. Even as the Tiger and the Dragon fought their last battles at Kawanakajima, two things were arriving on Japan's shores that would shatter the whole arrangement faster than anyone alive could have imagined: a new weapon from across the world, and a new religion to go with it, both carried on the same black-hulled ships. And waiting to use them was a young lord whom most of the country had dismissed as a fool. That story — the guns, the missionaries, and the man who broke the warlord world to begin putting Japan back together — is the next chapter.


# Chapter 12 — The Black Ships and the Demon King

For three hundred years Japan had been fighting with itself in a world it understood: emperors and shoguns, sword and bow, the gods of the mountains and the buddhas of the temples. Then, almost at the same moment, two things arrived from over the horizon that Japan had no category for at all. One was a weapon that could kill a mounted nobleman from across a field without the killer having spent a day of his life learning to fight. The other was a god who claimed to be the only one. The first was the matchlock musket. The second was Christianity. Both came on European ships, and both landed in the lap of the most disruptive man Japan would ever produce — a warlord his enemies called the Demon King, who used both of them to take a hammer to the entire medieval order.

His name was **Oda Nobunaga** (OH-dah noh-boo-NAH-gah; Oda the family, Nobunaga the man), and to understand why he matters you have to remember the world the previous chapter left you in. By the 1540s Japan had been carved into dozens of warlord domains, each ruled by a **daimyō** (DYE-myoh, "great name," a great territorial lord) who answered to no one. The emperor in Kyoto still reigned, sacred and broke. The **shogun** (the supreme military commander, the man who was supposed to actually run the country) was an Ashikaga puppet with no army and no money. The principle of the age was **gekokujō** (geh-koh-koo-JOH), "the low overthrowing the high" — retainers murdering lords, lords ignoring the throne, the whole social ladder flipped upside down. Into this snake pit, in 1551, a young man inherited a minor domain in **Owari** (oh-WAH-ree), a province on the Pacific coast near modern Nagoya. He was about seventeen. He dressed strangely, behaved outrageously, and was so widely written off as a half-wit that one of his own senior retainers committed suicide in protest at his behavior, hoping the shock might reform him. The nickname people used for the young Nobunaga translates roughly as "the great fool of Owari."

He was not a fool. He was a different kind of animal entirely, and the rest of Japan had not yet learned to read him.

## Guns from a storm

Eight years before Nobunaga inherited his lands, in 1543, a Chinese trading junk blown off course by a storm washed up on **Tanegashima** (tah-neh-gah-SHEE-mah), a small island off the southern tip of Kyushu, Japan's southwesternmost main island. Aboard were a few Portuguese — the first Europeans, as far as the surviving record shows, ever to set foot in Japan. They carried something the local lord had never seen: the **matchlock musket**, a smoothbore gun fired by lowering a smoldering cord onto the powder. The Japanese word for the weapon became simply **tanegashima**, after the island where it landed. (The exact date is recorded as 1543 in the main Japanese account, written decades later; a stray Portuguese source hints at 1542, and historians have argued over it, but 1543 is the conventional and best-attested year.)

What happened next is the part that should be impossible. The lord of Tanegashima bought two of the guns at a reportedly enormous price, handed one to his swordsmiths, and told them to copy it. Within roughly a decade Japanese smiths were not only reproducing the matchlock but manufacturing it in volume, and by the 1550s and 1560s guns were spreading through the warring provinces faster than anywhere in the world. This was not luck. Japan already had, in its sword-smiths, some of the finest metalworkers on the planet, men who for centuries had folded and hammered steel into the curved **katana** (kah-TAH-nah, the famous single-edged samurai sword). Pointed at a new problem, that skill solved it astonishingly quickly. The one hard part — cutting a gas-tight screw thread to seal the breech plug at the base of the barrel — they reverse-engineered. Within a generation a country at the far edge of the Eurasian world had become one of the most gun-saturated societies on earth, by some accounts producing firearms on a scale that rivaled or exceeded the major European states.

Hold that fact against the cliché. The popular image of old Japan is a land of swords that recoiled from the gun as dishonorable. The reality of the sixteenth century is the reverse: the Japanese took to the matchlock with an enthusiasm that startled the Europeans who sold it. The romance of the sword-only samurai was a later invention, polished under the long peace that this whole book is building toward — when the wars were over and nobody needed to shoot anybody. In the actual fighting century, the warlords wanted guns, and they wanted them by the thousand.

## A god who came to convert

Six years after the guns, in 1549, a second kind of European stepped ashore at Kagoshima on Kyushu: **Francis Xavier**, a Spanish-born Jesuit priest (the Jesuits being a then-new, intensely disciplined Catholic missionary order founded in 1540 to win souls for Rome). Xavier had already preached across India and the East Indies, and he came to Japan convinced he had found the most promising mission field in all of Asia — the Japanese, he wrote home, were the finest people his order had yet encountered. He stayed about two years, struggled with the language, and argued theology with Buddhist monks — who pressed him with hard questions a single all-powerful, all-good creator-god raised: if such a God existed, why was there evil and suffering, and why, in all the ages before the missionaries came, had he left the Japanese ancestors no way to be saved? He left behind the seed of something that grew with startling speed. Over the following decades the Jesuits converted, by their own counts, hundreds of thousands of Japanese, including a handful of daimyō (the so-called **Christian daimyō**, or *kirishitan*, from the Portuguese *cristão*). For a few decades there were more practicing Christians in Japan than in some European countries.

Why did it spread so fast? Partly genuine conviction, partly something more worldly. The European missionaries and the European merchants arrived on the same ships, and a daimyō who welcomed the priests was courting the traders too. This was the **Nanban trade** (NAHN-bahn, "southern barbarians," the Japanese name for the Portuguese and Spanish, who arrived from the south aboard their "black ships"). Out of Macau, the Portuguese base on the south China coast, came Chinese silk, the matchlocks, exotic goods, and the priests; back went Japanese silver, which the warring lords were mining in unprecedented quantities. A lord who let the Jesuits build a church might find Portuguese ships choosing his harbor, and a Portuguese ship in your harbor meant guns and silk and customs revenue. Religion and commerce rode together, and the most clear-eyed warlords understood the bargain perfectly.

The most clear-eyed of all was Nobunaga, and his attitude toward Christianity tells you exactly how his mind worked. He was almost certainly not a believer. But he loathed the established Buddhist monasteries as political and military rivals (more on that shortly), and a foreign religion that undercut them was a useful tool. He let the Jesuits preach, received them with curiosity, and enjoyed the strange gifts they brought. He was using them, the way he used everything.

## Okehazama: the great fool wins

But all of that — guns, priests, silver — was background to a career that began with a single reckless gamble. In 1560 a vast army marched on Nobunaga's little domain. It belonged to **Imagawa Yoshimoto** (ee-mah-GAH-wah yoh-shee-MOH-toh), one of the most powerful warlords in central Japan, and it reportedly numbered tens of thousands of men — against which Nobunaga could field a few thousand at most. Every rule of warfare said Nobunaga was finished. His own advisors counseled surrender or a defensive siege that would end the same way.

Instead Nobunaga did something that defined him for the rest of his life: he attacked. Learning that Imagawa's huge army had halted to rest in a narrow wooded gorge called **Okehazama** (oh-keh-hah-ZAH-mah), with the overconfident commander celebrating early victories, Nobunaga led a small force on a hidden march, used a sudden thunderstorm to cover his approach, and fell on the enemy headquarters in a screaming downpour. In the chaos Imagawa Yoshimoto was killed — by some accounts before he understood he was even under serious attack. The colossal army, leaderless, simply dissolved. It is one of the most famous upset victories in Japanese history, and where the war tales sharpen it into a perfect storybook ambush, the bones of it are solid: a hopelessly outnumbered minor lord destroyed a major one in an afternoon, and overnight the "great fool of Owari" became a man the whole country had to take seriously.

One detail of the aftermath matters for the rest of the book. Among the lords freed from Imagawa's control by his death was a young man named Matsudaira Motoyasu, who soon allied himself with Nobunaga. You will know him by his later name: **Tokugawa Ieyasu** (toh-koo-GAH-wah ee-eh-YAH-soo), the patient survivor who would eventually outlast everyone and win the whole game (Chapter 14). From 1560 onward, Ieyasu was Nobunaga's loyal ally — the steadiest relationship of Nobunaga's violent life.

## Taking the center

Most warlords fought to enlarge their own domain. Nobunaga fought, increasingly openly, to take the whole country — *tenka*, "the realm under heaven." And he understood something his rivals out in the provinces did not: that the road to ruling Japan ran through Kyoto, where the powerless emperor and the puppet shogun still conferred a legitimacy nobody could manufacture in the field.

In 1568 he marched into the capital. He did not abolish the shogunate; he captured it. He installed **Ashikaga Yoshiaki** (ah-shee-KAH-gah yoh-shee-AH-kee), a member of the old shogunal family, as the new shogun — and as his own puppet. For a few years it was a convenient fiction: Nobunaga ruled, Yoshiaki signed. But a puppet who realizes he is a puppet usually starts pulling at his strings, and Yoshiaki began secretly building a coalition of Nobunaga's enemies against him. So in 1573 Nobunaga simply drove him out of Kyoto into exile. With that expulsion the **Ashikaga shogunate** — the second of Japan's warrior governments, founded in 1338 (Chapter 6) — came to its formal end after well over two centuries. Japan would have no shogun at all for the next thirty years, until Ieyasu took the title in 1603. The institution that had defined the medieval age was, for the moment, gone, knocked aside by a man who never bothered to claim it for himself.

## Burning the Buddhas

Here is where the nickname comes from. Nobunaga's most shocking acts were against not warlords but monks, and to understand why he turned on the great temples you have to remember what they had become.

The big medieval Buddhist establishments were not quiet retreats. The Kamakura-era explosion of new sects (Chapter 3) had grown into something formidable. The oldest and grandest monasteries had spent centuries accumulating land, tax-exempt estates, private armies of **warrior-monks** (*sōhei*), and the kind of political weight that let them face down emperors. The greatest of them was **Enryaku-ji** (en-RYAH-koo-jee), the vast headquarters of the Tendai school perched on **Mount Hiei** (hee-AY) overlooking Kyoto — a temple-fortress complex of thousands of buildings and thousands of armed monks, which had loomed over the capital for seven hundred years and was used to getting its way by marching its monks down the mountain to intimidate the court.

In 1571 the warrior-monks of Mount Hiei sheltered Nobunaga's enemies. His response was total. He surrounded the mountain and burned the entire complex to the ground, killing — by the accounts that survive, which may exaggerate — thousands of monks, along with women and children who had taken refuge there. It was an atrocity that horrified contemporaries precisely because Mount Hiei had been sacred and untouchable for seven centuries; no warlord had ever dared. And it worked. The political power of the great monasteries, which had checked secular rulers since the Kamakura age, broke at Mount Hiei and never recovered. This is the act that earned Nobunaga his reputation as the **Demon King** (*Dairokuten Maō*, a name from Buddhist cosmology for the demon-king who obstructs the path to enlightenment — a title Nobunaga, with characteristic black humor, is said to have rather enjoyed applying to himself).

The monks fought back longest in another form. The **Ikkō-ikki** (ee-KOH ee-KEE) were armed leagues of the True Pure Land Buddhist sect — fanatically devout farmers and warrior-monks who believed that to die fighting was to be reborn in paradise, and who in some regions had overthrown their warlord governors entirely and run whole provinces as a kind of peasants' kingdom (Chapter 10). Their fortress-cathedral, **Ishiyama Hongan-ji** (ee-shee-YAH-mah HONG-gan-jee), stood at Osaka behind enormous fortifications and was supplied by sea. Nobunaga besieged it for ten grinding years. It finally surrendered in 1580 — not stormed, but worn down and negotiated out — and with it fell the last great independent religious-military power in Japan. The site would not stay empty long: Nobunaga's successor would build the largest castle in the country directly on top of it (Chapter 13).

## Volleys at Nagashino

If Mount Hiei showed Nobunaga's ruthlessness, the battle of **Nagashino** (nah-gah-SHEE-noh) in 1575 showed his cold technical brilliance — and became the most famous gunpowder battle in Japanese history.

His opponent was the army of the late **Takeda Shingen** (Chapter 11), the "Tiger of Kai," whose mounted warriors were the most feared cavalry in Japan. Shingen himself had died in 1573; his son **Takeda Katsuyori** now led the clan, and in 1575 the Takeda army was besieging a fortress allied to Nobunaga and Ieyasu. The two allies marched to relieve it, and on the plain near Nagashino, Nobunaga did something methodical and devastating.

He had brought a large body of **ashigaru** (ah-shee-GAH-roo, lightly armed foot soldiers, the commoner infantry who had become the backbone of Sengoku armies) armed with matchlocks — by the traditional figure, three thousand of them, though the real number is debated and probably lower. He fortified his position behind wooden palisades, stakes, and a stream, specifically to break a cavalry charge. And he is said to have organized his gunners into rotating ranks: one rank fires while the others reload, so that the slow-loading matchlocks could pour out something closer to continuous fire instead of one ragged volley. When the Takeda cavalry charged the palisades, they were shot down in waves. The flower of the Takeda clan — the cavalry that had terrorized central Japan for a generation — was destroyed in an afternoon by foot soldiers with guns behind a fence.

Two cautions, because Nagashino is exactly the kind of battle that legend improves. The neat "rotating volley" system is partly later embroidery; how rigidly it actually worked is uncertain, and modern historians treat the famous three-thousand-gun, three-rank picture as stylized rather than literal. And gun-armed infantry had been used in Japan for years before 1575, so Nagashino did not invent gunpowder warfare. What it did was demonstrate, unforgettably, that prepared positions plus massed firepower could annihilate the proudest cavalry in the land — and that the mounted samurai archer, the very ideal the warrior class had been built around for four centuries, was finished as the decider of battles. The future belonged to the commoner with the gun, and Nobunaga had read the future first.

## The tower at Azuchi

By the late 1570s Nobunaga controlled the center of Japan and was grinding outward toward the rest. To announce what he had become, he built, between roughly 1576 and 1579, a castle unlike anything Japan had seen: **Azuchi Castle** (ah-ZOO-chee), on a hill overlooking Lake Biwa, the great lake just northeast of Kyoto. Earlier Japanese fortifications had mostly been hilltop stockades. Azuchi was a monument — a soaring multi-story tower-keep (*tenshu*) rising perhaps seven levels, its interior reportedly blazing with gold and with screen paintings by the leading artists of the day, the swaggering gold-ground tigers and landscapes of the **Kanō school** of painters that became the signature art of the unifiers' age. Azuchi was not just a fortress; it was propaganda in architecture, a vertical statement that one man now stood above all others. It gave its name to the era — the Azuchi-Momoyama period — and it set the template for the great Japanese castle. Within a generation that template would produce the white towers of Osaka and Himeji (Chapters 13 and 14). Azuchi itself would not survive its builder by even a month.

## The fire at Honnō-ji

In the early summer of 1582 Nobunaga was closer to ruling all Japan than anyone had been in over a century. His armies were campaigning on multiple fronts; the west was nearly his. He himself was resting in Kyoto, lightly guarded, at a temple called **Honnō-ji** (hon-NOH-jee), waiting to move to the front.

He had sent one of his most capable generals, **Akechi Mitsuhide** (ah-KEH-chee mee-tsoo-HEE-deh), to reinforce another army in the west. Instead, Mitsuhide turned his troops around, marched on Kyoto in the dark, and surrounded the sleeping temple. Why he did it is one of the great unsolved questions of Japanese history — personal grievance, ambition, fear, principle, some combination have all been argued, and the honest answer is that we do not know. What is certain is the result. Trapped and hopelessly outnumbered, Nobunaga fought briefly, then withdrew into the burning temple and died — by the traditional account, taking his own life as the flames rose. His body was never found. The man who had broken the monasteries, expelled the shogun, mastered the gun, and nearly unified Japan was destroyed not by any of his great enemies but by one of his own officers, with the unification all but in his grasp.

This is the most shocking single event of the age, and it is *gekokujō* in its purest distilled form: the retainer murdering the lord, the low overthrowing the high, at the very summit. Nobunaga had ridden that principle to the top by breaking everyone above him. In the end it broke him too.

The realm he had three-quarters conquered was suddenly without a master, and the prize lay open. The man who seized it was not another great lord. He was a foot soldier's son, a man of no birth at all, who happened to be commanding Nobunaga's western army when the messenger arrived — and who would do something even Nobunaga had not managed. He would finish it. That is the next chapter.


# Chapter 13 — The Peasant Who Ruled Japan

When the messenger reached the western front in June 1582 with the news that Nobunaga was dead and Kyoto in the hands of the traitor Mitsuhide, the general who received it did the most important thing of his entire life in the space of about two weeks. He was besieging an enemy castle hundreds of miles away. He kept the news secret, swiftly made peace with the enemy he had been fighting, turned his whole army around, and raced back toward the capital on a forced march so fast it passed into legend. Within roughly eleven days of Nobunaga's death he had caught and crushed the murderer Mitsuhide at a place called **Yamazaki** (yah-mah-ZAH-kee), near Kyoto. Mitsuhide, master of Japan for less than a fortnight, fled and was killed. The man who had avenged Nobunaga and was now holding the dead lord's bloodied banner had begun life with no surname, no land, and no rank whatsoever.

His name — one of several he used across a life of relentless self-promotion — was **Toyotomi Hideyoshi** (toh-yoh-TOH-mee hee-deh-YOH-shee). He is the most extraordinary social ascent in all of Japanese history, and arguably one of the most extraordinary anywhere. He was born around 1537 to a peasant family in Nobunaga's home province of Owari — a farmer's son, possibly the son of a part-time foot soldier, with no claim to samurai status at all. He entered Nobunaga's service as a lowly attendant, a sandal-bearer by the famous story, and rose purely on ability: clever, charming, tireless, a brilliant organizer and an even better judge of men. By the time Nobunaga died, the sandal-bearer was one of his top generals. Within a year of avenging him, he was the most powerful man in Japan.

If you have been keeping the thread of this book, you can see what he is. Hideyoshi is **gekokujō** — "the low overthrowing the high," the upside-down principle of the whole Sengoku century — taken to its absolute logical extreme. The Ōnin War had shown lords ignoring the shogun; Sōun and the self-made warlords had shown men of small birth seizing provinces; Nobunaga had shown a retainer killing his lord. Hideyoshi went further than any of them. A man born a peasant, who in a normal century would have died a peasant, climbed all the way to the top of the entire country. No samurai pedigree, no noble blood, nothing but talent and ferocious ambition. He is the Sengoku's purest product and, as we will see, the man who slammed the door shut behind him so no one could ever climb the same ladder again.

## Finishing the conquest

Avenging Nobunaga gave Hideyoshi the moral high ground; outmaneuvering Nobunaga's other generals and heirs over the next two years gave him the real power. By 1583 he had defeated or absorbed his chief rivals among the dead lord's lieutenants and stood, unmistakably, as Nobunaga's successor. (The one major figure he could not simply crush was Nobunaga's old ally Tokugawa Ieyasu, with whom he eventually came to terms instead — filing that rivalry away for later, Chapter 14.)

Then he did what Nobunaga had not lived to do: he finished the unification, domain by domain, mixing war with diplomacy in a way the all-conquering Nobunaga rarely bothered with. Hideyoshi preferred to overawe an enemy into surrender rather than exterminate him, offering generous terms to lords who submitted and keeping them on as vassals. By the late 1580s only one great independent power remained — the **Later Hōjō clan** (no relation to the earlier Kamakura regents of the same name; this was a Sengoku warlord house ruling the Kantō plain in the east from their immense castle at **Odawara** (oh-dah-WAH-rah)). In 1590 Hideyoshi brought a colossal army, by some accounts over two hundred thousand strong, and simply surrounded Odawara. Rather than storm it, he settled in for a deliberate, drawn-out siege; by the traditional accounts he turned the encampment into something like a court — sending for his own household and entertainers — letting the defenders' will collapse rather than spend his men storming the walls. Odawara surrendered. With it, the last holdout fell — and for the first time in over a century, since before the Ōnin War burned Kyoto in 1467, all of Japan answered to a single authority. The Sengoku, the warring-states century, was over. The peasant's son had reassembled the country.

## Regent, not shogun

Now came a peculiarly Japanese problem. Hideyoshi ruled Japan, but he could not become **shogun**. The title of "barbarian-subduing great general" had, by long tradition, to go to a man of the right aristocratic lineage — specifically a descendant of the Minamoto, the warrior clan that had founded the first shogunate back in 1185 (Chapter 1). A peasant's son could conquer the country, but the country's oldest rules said he could not hold its highest military title. Pedigree, it turned out, was the one fortress Hideyoshi's armies could not take.

So he went around it. In 1585 he had himself appointed **kampaku** (KAHM-pah-koo), the imperial regent — an ancient and supremely prestigious court office, traditionally reserved for the highest court nobility, through which one governed in the emperor's name. It was, if anything, grander than shogun. To make it possible he arranged to be adopted into a great noble family, papering over the peasant birth with borrowed aristocracy. And he leaned, as every warrior ruler in this book had leaned, on the sacred and powerless emperor in Kyoto — ruling in the throne's name while the throne did as it was told. The two-headed system that opened this whole book, the sacred emperor reigning while the strongman ruled, held even for a farmer's son. Hideyoshi had found a third way to wear the old crown.

## The tea bowl and the knife

Power on this scale needed a stage, and Hideyoshi built two of them — one of stone, one of silence.

The stone one was **Osaka Castle**, begun in 1583 on the very site of the Ikkō-ikki fortress-cathedral Nobunaga had finally broken (Chapter 12). It was the largest castle in Japan, a vast complex of massive stone walls and a towering keep, deliberately built to dwarf everything that came before and to advertise that the country now had a single master. Where Nobunaga's Azuchi had announced a man rising, Osaka announced a man arrived.

The silent stage was the tea room, and here the story turns strange and a little dark. The **tea ceremony** — the slow, exacting ritual of preparing and serving powdered green tea — had been developing for centuries (it traces back to the tea Eisai brought from China for monks in the twelfth century, Chapter 3, and to the rustic-simplicity aesthetic of the previous age, Chapter 8). Under Hideyoshi it reached its most refined form, and its great master was **Sen no Rikyū** (sen noh ree-KYOO). Rikyū perfected what is called **wabi-cha** (WAH-bee-chah), a tea aesthetic built on *wabi* — a deliberately austere, rustic, imperfect simplicity, the beauty of a rough handmade bowl and a tiny bare room rather than gold and ornament. He shrank the tea room to a space barely big enough for a few people, with an entrance so low that even a great lord had to bow his head and crawl in. He worked with the potter Chōjirō to develop **raku** (RAH-koo) ware, hand-shaped, low-fired tea bowls whose lumpy irregularity was the whole point. In the violent, gold-drenched world of the unifiers, Rikyū made a cult of restraint.

And tea was politics. To be invited to take tea with Hideyoshi was a mark of supreme favor; the tea room was where deals were struck and intimacies traded. Rikyū, as Hideyoshi's tea master, became one of the most influential men in Japan — an arbiter of taste with the ruler's ear. Which makes what happened next so chilling. In 1591, at the height of his power, Hideyoshi ordered Sen no Rikyū to commit **seppuku** (SEP-poo-koo, ritual suicide by disembowelment, the warrior's prescribed way to die with honor). The reasons are still debated — a quarrel over a statue of Rikyū placed above a temple gate, a clash of wills, court intrigue, Hideyoshi's deepening paranoia, or some tangle of all of them; no surviving source settles it. Whatever the cause, the most revered cultural figure of the age was forced to kneel and cut himself open at the command of a former peasant. It is the clearest possible sign of how absolute, and how unpredictable, Hideyoshi's power had become.

## Freezing the world he had climbed through

Here is the deep irony of Hideyoshi, and the most consequential thing he ever did. A man who had risen from the bottom to the top — the living proof that a peasant could become the ruler of Japan — spent his power making sure no one would ever do it again. He took the fluid, ladder-climbing Sengoku society that had made him, and he froze it solid.

He did it with two enormous administrative measures, and they are arguably more important to Japanese history than any battle.

The first was the **sword hunt** (*katanagari*) of 1588. By decree, Hideyoshi ordered all weapons — swords, spears, and especially guns — confiscated from farmers, monks, and commoners. The stated reason was piety: the collected metal, the edict claimed, would be melted down for the bolts and nails of a giant statue of the Buddha, so the donors would be blessed in this life and the next. The real reason was control. For a century, peasants had armed themselves, joined village leagues, formed the Ikkō-ikki, and risen in revolt. Hideyoshi disarmed the countryside in one stroke. From now on, carrying a sword was the privilege of the **samurai** alone. The bright, hard line between the warrior and everyone else — which the warrior class would treasure and the world would later mistake for something timeless — was drawn here, deliberately, by a man who had crossed it himself.

The second was the **land survey** (*taikō kenchi*, after *taikō*, the honorific title Hideyoshi took in retirement). Over several years his officials measured and registered every rice field in Japan, assessing each one by its yield in **koku** (KOH-koo, the standard unit of rice — one koku, very roughly, the amount of rice to feed one person for a year). For the first time the entire country's wealth was counted in a single currency of grain. This did several things at once. It told Hideyoshi exactly what every domain was worth, and so exactly how many soldiers each lord owed him. It fixed each farmer to a registered plot and a tax obligation. And combined with the sword hunt and a related edict in 1591 that forbade people from changing their occupation — a samurai could not become a farmer, a farmer could not become a townsman or a soldier — it locked Japanese society into rigid, hereditary classes: warrior, farmer, artisan, merchant, each in its place, each forbidden to move. The churning social fluidity that had defined the Sengoku, and produced Hideyoshi himself, was abolished by Hideyoshi. He pulled the ladder up behind him and welded it to the wall. The class system the Tokugawa would enforce for the next 250 years (Chapter 14) was, in its essentials, built here.

## The disaster across the sea

A man who has conquered everything available begins to look for things that are not. By the early 1590s Hideyoshi ruled all Japan, and his ambition, by now grown frankly grandiose, fixed on something no Japanese ruler had ever attempted: the conquest of China.

His plan was breathtaking in scale and delusion. He would march an army through Korea — then a unified kingdom and a tributary ally of Ming China — and on into the Ming empire itself, the largest and richest state in East Asia, ruling it from a new capital and parceling out its provinces to his generals. The first step was Korea. In 1592 he launched a massive invasion, perhaps 150,000 to 160,000 men, across the strait. This is the war Koreans call the **Imjin War** (IM-jin), and it was a catastrophe — eventually for everyone, but most of all for Korea, which was devastated, and in the end for Hideyoshi's own regime.

The opening went well for the Japanese. Armies hardened by a century of civil war and bristling with matchlocks overran much of the Korean peninsula with frightening speed, taking the capital within weeks. But then three things broke the offensive. The first was the Korean navy under Admiral **Yi Sun-sin** (yee soon-shin), one of the great naval commanders in history, whose armored **turtle ships** — low, ironclad, cannon-bristling warships — repeatedly shattered the Japanese fleets and cut the supply lines feeding the armies ashore. (Korea's own story is told in this project's Joseon Korea narrative; the turtle ships and Yi's victories are among its proudest chapters.) The second was fierce Korean guerrilla resistance behind the lines. The third was the intervention of **Ming China**, which poured armies into Korea to defend its tributary, turning a Japanese walkover into a grinding stalemate. The invasion bogged down, a truce collapsed, and in 1597 Hideyoshi launched a second, even more brutal campaign that ground to the same bloody halt. Both invasions accomplished nothing strategically except mass death — Korean civilian suffering on a horrific scale, including the carrying-off of Korean potters whose skills founded famous Japanese ceramic traditions (among them the celebrated Arita and Satsuma wares of Kyushu) — and the steady draining of Japanese lives and treasure into a war that could not be won.

It was during these years, in 1597, that another shadow fell. Increasingly suspicious of the loyalty of Christian converts and of European intentions, Hideyoshi (who had earlier issued edicts against the missionaries without fully enforcing them) had twenty-six Christians — Japanese converts and foreign friars — executed by crucifixion at **Nagasaki** (nah-gah-SAH-kee), the Kyushu port that had grown into the center of the Portuguese trade and of Japanese Christianity, and that would later become the country's single window on the West (Chapter 14). The **Twenty-Six Martyrs**, as they became known, were the first such killings in what would grow, under the Tokugawa, into a savage and ultimately near-total suppression of Christianity in Japan. The open, curious era of the black ships was beginning to close.

## A child and a vacuum

Hideyoshi died in 1598, with his armies still bleeding in Korea and his great work, the unification, intact but unguarded. And here the man who had controlled everything failed at the one thing he could not command: an heir. For most of his life he had no son. Late, improbably, he fathered a boy, **Toyotomi Hideyori** (hee-deh-YOH-ree) — but when Hideyoshi died, Hideyori was five years old.

A reunified country, a freshly frozen social order, a treasury drained by a foreign war, and a master of all Japan whose entire claim now rested on a five-year-old child. Hideyoshi knew exactly how dangerous this was. On his deathbed he extracted oaths from his greatest vassals to protect his son, setting up a council of **Five Elders** (the five most powerful daimyō) sworn to govern jointly until the boy came of age, and bound them with letters that grew almost pleading as the end neared. He died begging the most powerful men in Japan to keep faith with a child.

The most powerful of those five elders was Tokugawa Ieyasu — the patient ally who had stood with Nobunaga at Okehazama in 1560, who had fought beside him at Nagashino, who had bent the knee to Hideyoshi without ever quite submitting, and who had spent forty years watching greater men rise, overreach, and die. He had buried his rivals by outliving them. Now the last and greatest of his masters was dead, the heir was a child, and the council of oaths was a fiction waiting to be tested. Ieyasu had been patient for an entire lifetime. He would not have to be patient much longer. That is the last chapter.


# Chapter 14 — The Gambler's Victory

The whole long story of medieval Japan — four centuries of emperors who reigned without ruling, shoguns who ruled in their place, regents who ruled behind the shoguns, warlords who answered to no one, and unifiers who put it all back together by force — comes down at the very end to a single autumn morning in a fog-filled valley, and to one man's decision, made around noon, to change sides. Everything turned on it. To understand how the medieval age ends, you have to understand how it nearly didn't, and how close the man who finally closed it came to losing everything.

That man was **Tokugawa Ieyasu** (toh-koo-GAH-wah ee-eh-YAH-soo), and by 1598 he was, more than anything else, a survivor. He is the third of the three great unifiers, and he is unlike the other two in temperament in a way that turns out to be the whole point. Nobunaga (Chapter 12) was the breaker — brilliant, ruthless, impatient, the hammer that shattered the old order, and dead at the brink of victory. Hideyoshi (Chapter 13) was the builder — the peasant genius who finished the conquest and froze the society, dead with his empire intact but his heir a child. Ieyasu was the *waiter*. Born in 1543 to a minor warlord family, taken hostage as a boy and raised in another lord's household, he had been a player in this game since he allied with Nobunaga in 1560 after the battle of Okehazama. For forty years he had fought when he had to, submitted when he had to, and above all *outlasted* — watching Nobunaga rise and be murdered, watching Hideyoshi rise and overreach into Korea and die. A Japanese saying about the three unifiers captures it perfectly: confronted with a bird that will not sing, Nobunaga says "kill it," Hideyoshi says "make it want to sing," and Ieyasu says "wait." He had been waiting his entire life. His patience was about to be rewarded all at once.

## The cracking council

When Hideyoshi died in 1598, the legal arrangement was clear and the real situation was not. Officially, Japan was to be governed by a council of **Five Elders** — the five most powerful daimyō — ruling jointly in trust until Hideyoshi's son **Toyotomi Hideyori** (Chapter 13), then five years old, came of age. Each of the five had sworn solemn oaths to protect the boy. Oaths, in a country built on *gekokujō*, were not worth much.

Ieyasu was by far the most powerful of the five. From his base in the east — including the small castle town of **Edo** (EH-doh, the future Tokyo), which Hideyoshi had granted him years before — he controlled the richest bloc of territory in Japan. And almost from the moment Hideyoshi was buried, Ieyasu began doing exactly what the oaths forbade: building his own faction, arranging marriage alliances between great families without the council's approval, accumulating obligations and allies. He was not yet seizing power. He was assembling the position from which power could be seized, and waiting, as ever, for his enemies to give him a reason that would make the seizing look like a response rather than a grab.

His chief opponent saw all of it clearly. **Ishida Mitsunari** (ee-shee-dah mee-tsoo-NAH-ree) was not one of the great warlords; he was a brilliant administrator, one of Hideyoshi's most trusted bureaucrats, fiercely loyal to the dead master and to the boy Hideyori. Mitsunari understood that Ieyasu meant to take everything, and he set out to stop him by rallying the lords who still felt bound by their oaths to the Toyotomi house. By 1600 Japan had sorted itself, more or less, into two enormous coalitions: an **Eastern Army** under Ieyasu, and a **Western Army** built around Mitsunari in the name of protecting Hideyoshi's heir. The reunified country Hideyoshi had bled to assemble was about to tear itself in half over who would inherit it.

## Sekigahara

The two armies converged in October 1600 on a narrow valley in central Japan, ringed by hills and choked that morning with fog and rain, called **Sekigahara** (seh-kee-gah-HAH-rah). Each side fielded somewhere around eighty thousand men — together perhaps the largest battle ever fought on Japanese soil, and very possibly the single most consequential. Nearly every major daimyō in the country had committed to one side or the other. Whoever won this field would rule Japan.

And on paper, when the fighting began, the Western Army held the stronger position. Mitsunari's forces were well placed on the high ground around the valley, and several large contingents had not yet been committed. If they all attacked together, Ieyasu could be crushed.

They did not all attack together. This is the hinge of the entire medieval-to-modern transition, and it comes down to treachery — arranged, almost certainly, in advance. Ieyasu, the lifelong waiter, had not staked everything on a fair fight. He had been in secret correspondence with several of the lords nominally on Mitsunari's side, and the most important of them was a young commander named **Kobayakawa Hideaki** (koh-bah-yah-KAH-wah hee-deh-AH-kee), who sat with a large force on a hill called Matsuoyama, overlooking the battlefield, supposedly part of the Western Army.

For hours through the morning, as the battle below ground into a savage stalemate, Kobayakawa sat and did nothing — watching, calculating, waiting to see which way the day would go. Both sides knew his fresh troops could decide everything. According to the traditional and most-told account, Ieyasu, losing patience with his wavering would-be ally, finally ordered his own gunners to fire a volley *at* Kobayakawa's position — a furious demand to commit or be treated as an enemy. (Some modern historians doubt this dramatic detail and argue Kobayakawa moved earlier or for his own reasons; what is not in doubt is that he switched.) Around midday Kobayakawa Hideaki led his army down the hill — and charged into the flank of the *Western* Army he was supposed to be fighting for. The betrayal cascaded. Other wavering lords, seeing which way it had gone, turned their coats too. The Western Army, attacked from within, collapsed. Within hours it was a rout. Mitsunari fled, was captured days later, and was executed in Kyoto.

It was over in a single day. The battle that decided who would rule Japan, and ended the four-century age of the warring samurai, was won not by superior numbers or a brilliant maneuver but by a defection arranged in the dark beforehand — the gambler's victory, in which the patient man had quietly fixed the odds before he ever sat down at the table. **Sekigahara**, fought in 1600, is the great hinge of the whole story.

## Shogun at last

Victory at Sekigahara made Ieyasu the master of Japan in fact. Now he made it law — and he did it the old way, the way this entire book began.

In 1603 the emperor in Kyoto, sacred and powerless as ever, named Tokugawa Ieyasu **sei-i taishōgun** — "barbarian-subduing great general," the **shogun**. The title that Hideyoshi the peasant could never claim went to Ieyasu without difficulty, because Ieyasu's family could plausibly assert the necessary ancient lineage (a Minamoto descent, the pedigree the title demanded). With that grant the institution that had defined medieval Japan — the warrior government holding real power in the emperor's name — was restored, after thirty years without a shogun since Nobunaga expelled the last Ashikaga in 1573 (Chapter 12). Ieyasu set up his government not in Kyoto but at his own eastern stronghold, **Edo**, deliberately far from the imperial court, exactly as Yoritomo had built the very first shogunate far away at Kamakura back in 1185 (Chapter 1). The whole age had come full circle. A warrior had won a civil war, declined to take the throne, and built a military government at a distance from the emperor he left untouched. It was 1185 all over again — except this time it would hold for two and a half centuries.

This is the founding of the **Tokugawa shogunate** (also called the Edo shogunate, after its capital), the third and last of Japan's warrior governments and by far the most durable. The medieval age — the age of the warring samurai, of *gekokujō*, of warlords carving Japan into private domains — ends here, in 1603, on the same kind of act that opened it.

## The white towers and the closing door

Two more things mark the end of the age, one built and one sealed shut.

The built thing was the castle. The towering stone-and-plaster keeps that Nobunaga had pioneered at Azuchi and Hideyoshi had raised to colossal scale at Osaka reached their dazzling perfection right at the turn of the seventeenth century. The supreme survivor is **Himeji Castle** (hee-MEH-jee), rebuilt around 1601 onward into the soaring white complex nicknamed the **White Heron** for its elegant, pale, bird-like silhouette — a maze of gates and walls crowned by a brilliant white tower-keep, the most beautiful and best-preserved of all Japanese castles, and one of the few to survive intact to today. These great castles are the architectural monument of the unification: built for war, they ended up as symbols of a peace in which they would almost never be attacked.

The sealed thing was Japan itself. The world that the black ships had opened — guns, priests, silver, William Adams (the English navigator who washed ashore in 1600 as pilot of a battered Dutch ship, the first Englishman known to reach Japan, whom Ieyasu kept on as an advisor on Western affairs and even granted samurai rank), the whole vivid era of contact with Europe — the Tokugawa would, over the following decades, deliberately close. Fearful of the loyalty of Christian converts and of European designs, Ieyasu's heirs intensified the persecution of Christianity that Hideyoshi had begun with the Twenty-Six Martyrs (Chapter 13) into near-total suppression, and then went further: by the 1630s they had largely sealed the country off from the outside world, expelling most foreigners, banning Japanese from sailing abroad, and reducing European contact to a single tightly controlled Dutch trading post — a small artificial island called Dejima, in the harbor of Nagasaki (Chapter 13), which became Japan's lone authorized window on the West. The two doomed Korean invasions were the last time Japan would send an army overseas for nearly three hundred years.

And so the warring century stopped, all at once. The disarmed peasants stayed disarmed. The frozen classes stayed frozen. The samurai, with no more wars to fight, gradually turned from soldiers into administrators, scholars, and bureaucrats drawing rice stipends — and it was precisely in this long peace, looking back at a fighting age that was already over, that the romantic code of the warrior, the cult of the sword, and the polished myth of *bushidō* were assembled into the thing the world now pictures. The masterless wandering swordsman like the famous duelist **Miyamoto Musashi** (mee-yah-MOH-toh moo-SAH-shee), by his own account undefeated in dozens of duels and author of *The Book of Five Rings*, a treatise on swordsmanship and strategy still read today, belongs to exactly this moment — the very end of the violent age, when men who lived by the sword found the world had stopped needing them.

That is where this book closes and where the next opens. The age of the warring samurai is finished. What follows is the long, locked-down, astonishingly stable peace of **Edo Japan** — two and a half centuries under the Tokugawa shoguns, the world the closed door created, told in its own narrative (Edo Japan). Four hundred years earlier a warrior named Yoritomo had kept the emperor on his throne and taken the real power for himself, inventing a government run from a tent. At Sekigahara, on a foggy morning decided by one man's betrayal at noon, that strange and durable experiment reached the end it had been moving toward all along — and the sword, for the first time in four centuries, was finally sheathed.
